I am a graduate teaching assistant for an early modern political philosophy course, and in a couple of weeks I am responsible for leading the discussion section on Hobbes's Leviathan. While rereading the early chapters to prepare, I was struck by Hobbes's aside, in his discussion of the misuse of words and definitions, that geometry is essentially the one science human beings have so far actually managed to get right. A colleague who works on seventeenth-century natural philosophy warned me not to treat that line as a throwaway compliment: for Hobbes, she said, it was the centerpiece of an entire epistemology -- what she called his "maker's knowledge" view -- on which certain, demonstrative knowledge is available to us only for things whose construction lies within our own power. Geometrical figures are the paradigm case, since a circle or a triangle is fully specified by the very motion that generates it, but Hobbes wanted the same certainty for civil philosophy too, on the grounds that the commonwealth is likewise something we construct, by covenant, rather than something we merely discover lying out in nature -- which is presumably part of why he thought Leviathan itself could proceed by definitions and demonstrations in a broadly geometrical spirit.

That conversation sent me to Douglas Jesseph's history of the long and often vicious dispute between Hobbes and the Oxford mathematician John Wallis, and it has left me with a puzzle I cannot resolve on my own. In the final quarter-century of an unusually long life, Hobbes insisted, across a whole series of published pamphlets, that he had squared the circle, and was shown to be wrong, in specific and often humiliating detail, by Wallis again and again, without this ever visibly denting his confidence. I do not need the anecdote retold to me; I already have that from Jesseph. What I would like is a properly conceptual explanation of what is going on underneath it. If certainty in geometry is supposed to come precisely from an agent's own grasp of a construction she herself has carried out, what does it actually mean that Hobbes could be so durably and serenely wrong about one? Is there something internal to his account of demonstration -- his insistence on cashing out all mathematical reasoning in terms of motion, say, and his corresponding hostility to the more symbolic, algebraic methods that Wallis favored -- that made this kind of failure almost structurally available to him, in a way that would implicate the maker's-knowledge picture itself and, by extension, the parallel he wants to draw with political philosophy? Or would that be reading far more philosophical significance into the episode than one very stubborn man's blind spot actually warrants? I would like the explanation to engage with the structure of Hobbes's view on its own terms, since it is the underlying conceptual issue, rather than the historical gossip, that I am hoping to bring into the classroom.
