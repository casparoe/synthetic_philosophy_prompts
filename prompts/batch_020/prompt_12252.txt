In my AP African American Studies class, we recently spent a unit on the Harlem Renaissance, and one argument from that unit has stayed with me even though we only devoted a single day to it. In 1926, W. E. B. Du Bois delivered and then published, in The Crisis, his essay "Criteria of Negro Art," in which he declares that "all Art is propaganda and ever must be, despite the wailing of the purists," insisting that whatever art he had produced was always meant to serve Black Americans' claim to a full and dignified life. That same year, he ran a symposium in The Crisis titled "The Negro in Art: How Shall He Be Portrayed?," essentially asking a range of Black and white writers whether authors had an obligation to depict only the best of Black life so as not to reinforce racist assumptions. A couple of years later, Alain Locke published an essay called "Art or Propaganda?" that is usually read as a rebuttal to Du Bois, and around the same time a younger circle of Harlem Renaissance writers, including Langston Hughes, Zora Neale Hurston, and Wallace Thurman, put out the magazine Fire!! specifically to write about the messier, "disreputable" sides of Black life without any uplift agenda attached.

When we discussed this in class, we split into two camps without really resolving anything: some of my classmates thought Du Bois was just being honest that art always carries some ideological charge whether the artist admits it or not, while others thought he was making the much stronger demand that Black artists consciously subordinate their work to a political program. I don't think our teacher was being deliberately evasive, but she never told us which of those two readings she found closer to the truth, or which side of the resulting debate she considered more persuasive.

Could you write me an essay that first pins down which of these two claims (or perhaps some third option) Du Bois is actually committed to in "Criteria of Negro Art," and then weighs the strongest arguments for and against that position, drawing on the objections raised by Locke and the Fire!! circle as well as any other considerations you think are relevant? I'd like the essay to actually take a side rather than conclude that "both camps have a point" — please defend a specific, arguable verdict on whether, and in what sense, Du Bois was right that art is and must be propaganda, and explain why that verdict survives the strongest objections against it.
