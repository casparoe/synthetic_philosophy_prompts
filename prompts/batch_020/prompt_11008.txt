I spent thirty-five years running an organic chemistry lab before I retired, and I still sit on my old department's Laboratory Safety Committee, which doubles as the disciplinary body whenever a safety violation gets written up. We've got a case on the agenda for next month that's been nagging at me. Two grad students in the same lab both got into the habit of running a reaction that's known to occasionally run away exothermically, doing it on the open bench overnight and skipping the blast shield and the buddy-system rule, because the PI wasn't around after 9pm to check. As far as anyone can reconstruct, student A did this maybe a dozen times and nothing ever happened - it only came out because a safety audit found it in her notebook. Student B did the exact same thing, with the exact same knowledge of the rules and the exact same reasoning ("it'll probably be fine, I've seen others do it"), and one night the flask cracked; there was a small fire and he spent a week in the hospital with burns. It also made the campus paper, which is part of why this is suddenly a big deal.

The committee is split roughly in half between people who want to suspend B's lab access for the semester and give A only a written warning, and people who think thats absurd given that A and B were, by any measure of what they knew and intended, equally reckless - the only difference is how things happened to turn out. My grandson, who's doing a philosophy PhD, tells me this is basically the textbook problem of "moral luck" (I gather Bernard Williams and Thomas Nagel are the names to know here), and I can't stop thinking he's right, and that we're about to make a call on autopilot without ever facing the actual issue. Could you draft the memo I'd circulate to the committee ahead of the vote? I need an actual recommendation - treat A and B the same, treat them differently, or some third option, with specific proposed sanctions for each - and reasoning the whole committee could follow, not just a description of the puzzle. And please don't let it dodge the strongest argument for the outcome-based camp; deterrence and how the rest of the department reads our decision seem like real considerations, not just gut reactions dressed up as principle.
