Me and my friend Theo had a two-hour arguement last night, over beers, about what "function" even means when biologists use the word. He kept insisting a trait's function is just whatever effect it was selected for back in its evolutionary history -- so the heart's function is pumping blood, full stop, because that's the effect natural selection acted on. I was defending the other camp: a trait's function is about what it currently contributes to some capacity of the organism (or system) its part of right now, and the selective history is irrelevant. We kept circling the same worn-out examples (heart-pumping-vs-heart-sounds, the appendix, vestigial stuff) and honestly didn't get anywhere new. Can you come up with an example -- real or invented, doesn't matter -- of a trait where the etiological account ("function = what it was selected for") and the causal-role account ("function = what it currently does for the system it's part of") would actually come to different answers, ideally something meatier than the usual vestigial-organ chestnuts? And briefly explain why the two accounts pull apart on your example.
