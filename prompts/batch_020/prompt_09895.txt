Ok so I fell down a rabbit hole last night rereading old Alignment Forum posts on value learning, and I think I've talked myself into a take that basically dissolves the outer alignment problem, and I want someone to poke holes in it before I get too attached. The classic worry is that writing down a reward function which actually captures what we want is really hard — Goodhart, reward hacking, the whole list. But inverse reinforcement learning just runs that process backwards: instead of some poor researcher hand-specifying a utility function, you watch what a person actually does and infer whatever reward rationalizes it. And once you put the human properly in the loop — like Hadfield-Menell et al's cooperative IRL setup, which I think Stuart Russell just rebrands as an "assistance game" in Human Compatible — the robot starts out explicitly uncertain about the reward and updates on every single action the person takes, including corrections and demonstrations. So it seems like specification quietly stops being a philosophical problem and just becomes a statistics problem: feed the system enough interactions and the inferred reward should converge on the real one, same as any other estimator gets tighter with more samples.

I brought this up with my study group and the one guy who actually does ML for a living just shrugged and said "yeah, that tracks," which weirdly made me more suspcious rather than less, since he doesn't really follow alignment stuff closely and mostly just didn't wanna argue. I'm not in a PhD program or anything, just someone who's read way too many LessWrong posts, so I might be missing something obvious here. But if I'm right, it seems like a decent chunk of the value-learning literature is fretting over a problem that assistance games already quietly closed. Is there an actual gap here that people in the field still take seriously, or has "just learn the reward from behavior, human in the loop" basically closed the book on specification?
