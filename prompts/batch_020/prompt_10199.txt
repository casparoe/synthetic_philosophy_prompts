I'm a PhD student in economics working on decision theory under risk, and lately I've been trying to sort out a disagreement that keeps resurfacing in the literature on betting and portfolio choice without ever quite getting resolved. The mainstream expected-utility view treats the Kelly criterion as nothing special: it is just the bet-sizing rule you get when your utility function happens to be logarithmic in wealth, and an agent with a different utility function is being perfectly rational to size bets differently. The rival view, sometimes framed in terms of "geometric rationality" or ergodicity economics, treats maximizing long-run expected logarithmic growth as closer to the correction of a genuine error than a matter of taste: because wealth compounds multiplicatively across repeated plays, the ordinary arithmetic expected value of a bet describes an average over a vast ensemble of parallel copies of the bettor, almost none of which resemble the single trajectory that any one non-ergodic agent, living through time, will actually experience — and on this view, a single catastrophic loss is not merely a bad draw but an absorbing state that forecloses all future compounding, something arithmetic expectations systematically underweight.

I'd like you to write a dialogue that puts this disagreement to work on a concrete case rather than leaving it abstract. Stage it at a betting table: a bookmaker has offered two characters a bet they may repeat for as many rounds as they like, staking any fraction of their current bankroll each round, with a 60% chance of doubling whatever is staked and a 40% chance of losing it outright. Character A is a risk-neutral expected-value maximizer, prepared in principle to stake everything each round since the bet is favorable; Character B, arguing from the geometric-rationality side, insists on staking exactly the Kelly fraction (20% of the current bankroll per round) and regards anything larger as a genuine error rather than a difference in taste. I'd like them to really engage with each other's strongest moves rather than talk past one another: Character B should press Character A on the fact that staking everything leads to near-certain eventual ruin no matter how favorable the odds, and Character A should have a substantive reply available — for instance, by invoking a finite horizon, or the possibility of spreading a stake across many independent copies of the bet running in parallel rather than reinvesting sequentially, which would let ordinary laws of large numbers vindicate arithmetic expectation once again. Both characters should be equally sharp, and neither should be reducible to a strawman for the other to knock down.
