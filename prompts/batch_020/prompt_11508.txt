I have spent a good deal of time following the more Bayesian corners of the rationalist blogosphere, where E. T. Jaynes's *Probability Theory: The Logic of Science* is cited with something close to reverence, and a disagreement that came up recently in a reading group I follow sent me back to his 1973 paper "The Well-Posed Problem," in which he attempts to resolve Bertrand's chord paradox once and for all. As I understand it, Bertrand's original 1889 puzzle asks for the probability that a "randomly drawn" chord of a circle is longer than the side of an equilateral triangle inscribed in that circle, and it notoriously admits three different answers — 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4 — depending on whether the randomization is performed over the chord's two endpoints on the circumference, over a point chosen uniformly along a fixed radius (with the chord drawn perpendicular to that radius), or over the chord's midpoint chosen uniformly within the disk. Each of these three procedures appears to be an equally legitimate application of the classical, Laplacean principle of indifference: when there is no reason to favor one possibility over another, distribute credence equally among them. Jaynes's diagnosis is that the original problem is simply underspecified, and that once one fixes an actual physical generating procedure (a straw or needle tossed onto the circle from some height, without careful aim) and requires the solution to be invariant under the transformations that this procedure's symmetry obviously demands — translation, rotation, and change of scale — exactly one of the three distributions, the one yielding 1/2, survives as uniquely correct.

What I would like help thinking through is how this transformation-group maneuver actually relates to the principle of indifference in its more general, classical form, rather than merely to this one paradox. On one reading, Jaynes has vindicated the principle of indifference by showing how to apply it correctly: the paradox was never a flaw in the principle itself, only in careless, underspecified invocations of it, and "invariance under the relevant symmetry group" simply is indifference done properly. On a less charitable reading, Jaynes has quietly substituted a narrower and rather different principle — invariance under a determinate symmetry group tied to a genuine physical randomizing process — one that happens to agree with intuition in this particular geometric case but gives us no traction on the purely epistemic problems the principle of indifference was originally meant to cover, such as van Fraassen's cube-factory paradox, or the everyday Bayesian problem of choosing an "ignorance prior" over a parameter that admits several equally natural but incompatible parametrizations (a rate versus its reciprocal, say), where there is no physical process at all to which one could even appeal for the relevant invariance group. I would be grateful for your honest assessment of which of these two readings is closer to correct, and whether there is a principled way of settling the matter, or whether the answer simply depends on how much philosophical weight the principle of indifference was ever entitled to bear in the first place.
