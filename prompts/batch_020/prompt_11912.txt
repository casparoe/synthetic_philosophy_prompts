Here's the question (30 pts, three parts):

"Many grounding theorists accept that if A grounds B then B supervenes on A, yet insist that grounding cannot simply be reduced to, or analyzed in terms of, supervenience.
(a) [10 pts] Using an example, explain why supervenience is not sufficient for grounding -- i.e., why two things could stand in a relation of mutual supervenience even though grounding is supposed to be asymmetric (and irreflexive).
(b) [10 pts] Fine treats 'ground' as a sentential operator ('_ because _') rather than, as Schaffer does, a relation holding among worldly items like facts. Name one substantive thing that turns on this difference, beyond the bare fact that one uses an operator and the other doesn't.
(c) [10 pts] Sider's 'purity' thesis holds that fundamental truths should be built only out of fundamental notions. What, if anything, does purity imply about whether truths of the form 'A grounds B' could themselves be fundamental?"

And here's the student's answer:

"(a) Grounding is supposed to be asymmetric and irreflexive -- nothing grounds itself, and if A grounds B then B doesn't also ground A. But supervenience doesn't have to be asymmetric: two things can be necessarily coextensive, so that neither can vary without the other, and thus supervene on each other, even though at most one could ground the other. The classic case is Socrates and his singleton {Socrates}: necessarily, Socrates exists iff {Socrates} exists, so each supervenes on the other. But {Socrates} is grounded in Socrates and not the reverse, so grounding is asymmetric exactly where supervenience is symmetric. If grounding just were supervenience, there'd be nothing left to explain why the dependence runs one way rather than the other.

(b) One thing that turns on it is ontology. If grounding is a relation, you need relata to stand in it, so Schaffer needs facts (or objects, properties, kinds, etc.) as genuine worldly items. Fine's operator doesn't commit him to facts as a further category of entity at all -- '_ because _' embeds sentences, so it can be true or false without our having to reify 'the fact that P' as something further in the world. Its also part of why Schaffer's relational approach makes him a grounding pluralist: because grounding is just an ordinary relation, he thinks there's no single unified 'Big-G' Ground, only a lot of different domain-specific small-g relations (causal, mereological, and so on), whereas Fine wants one unified operator that covers all of these at once.

(c) Purity says fundamental truths can't contain non-fundamental ingredients. This is usually motivated as a way of blocking regresses: if grounding facts could ground further grounding facts indefinitely, reality would never bottom out at a fundamental level, and purity is exactly the principle that rules that out. So on the purity picture, 'A grounds B' can't itself be fundamental whenever the regress hasn't already terminated in something fundamental."
