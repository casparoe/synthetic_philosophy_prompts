I'm a software engineer, and for the last several months a few of us at the office have been running an informal reading group that somehow ended up on Marx: we got through the 1844 Manuscripts over the summer and are now most of the way through the first volume of Capital, largely because one of our senior engineers, Priya, kept bringing it up in Slack threads until the rest of us got curious enough to show up. Priya is leaving the company after eight years to help start a worker-owned software cooperative, and a group of us are giving her a goodbye dinner this Friday. I have been asked to give the toast.

Here is my problem. Priya's eight years here were, by every account including her own, genuinely good ones: four promotions, an excellent salary, a reputation for mentoring half the engineering org, and she says without irony that she loved the work. Her manager and our VP of Engineering will both be at the dinner. Half the room is the reading-group crowd, who will notice immediately whether I use words like "exploitation" and "alienation" the way Marx actually meant them or just as loose synonyms for "unfair," and who will be a little disappointed if I retreat into a generic "we'll miss you, you worked so hard" toast. The other half has never opened Capital and simply wants a warm send-off for a friend. I do not want to stand up in front of her boss and imply that eight well-paid, well-liked years were secretly some sort of injustice. At the same time, I do not want to blunt Marx's actual technical claims — the extraction of surplus value regardless of how fair the wage looks, and the specific fourfold alienation from the product of one's labor, the process of laboring, one's own species-being, and other people — into something toothless just to keep everyone comfortable.

Could you write me a short toast, about five minutes, that takes Marx's vocabulary of exploitation and alienation seriously on its own technical terms, including the uncomfortable possibility that both can be true of work someone genuinely experienced as good, and uses that to say something honest and warm about Priya's time here — building, if it can be done naturally, to her move into a worker-owned cooperative as the kind of resolution Marx himself would recognize? It should reward the people who did the reading without turning into a seminar, and it still needs to work as a real, moving toast for everyone else in the room, her bosses included.
