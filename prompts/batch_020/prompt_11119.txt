I'm preparing a session for my graduate seminar on late Nietzsche, and I keep coming back to how strikingly literal-minded he becomes, in Ecce Homo and parts of Twilight of the Idols—both written in that astonishingly productive final year of 1888—about matters his admirers usually prefer to read allegorically. Driven no doubt by his own migraines, eye trouble, and chronic digestive complaints, he insists that nutriment, climate and place, and one's manner of recreation matter more to the shape of a life, and even a philosophy, than anything theology or metaphysics has offered, and he backs this up with a great deal of quite specific, almost fussy prescription: he inveighs against the heaviness of German cooking and beer, praises the lighter Piedmontese kitchen he came to enjoy in Turin, and lays down a further set of finicky rules about tea, coffee, and spirits. Elsewhere he groups alcohol together with Christianity among what he calls Europe's great narcotics, so that digestion ends up being treated not merely as a precondition of thought but as something close to a moral, even metaphysical, variable, continuous with what he calls décadence and the ascetic ideal.

A colleague in our medical humanities program recently forwarded a popular longevity newsletter, joking that its author was "doing Nietzsche's homework for him," and the remark has made me want a more careful answer than the offhand one I gave at the time. I would like your help reconstructing how Nietzsche would likely have judged today's biohacking and longevity-optimization culture: people who track glucose and sleep data, adopt carnivore or extended-fasting regimens, and describe the whole project in quasi-Nietzschean terms of self-overcoming and self-authorship, often citing gut-brain-axis research that seems to vindicate exactly the physiological attentiveness he was mocked for in his own lifetime. Would he recognize this as a distant, degraded heir to his own small, disciplined experiments in living, or would his own conceptual resources lead him to diagnose it as one more disguised expression of slave morality or the ascetic ideal, now dressed up as risk management and life-extension? I am less interested in a general survey of his dietary opinions than in your best reconstruction of how the underlying logic of his physiological reductionism actually cuts on this specific, quite modern phenomenon, and in your flagging where that logic runs out, such that any honest answer would have to extrapolate beyond anything he explicitly wrote.
