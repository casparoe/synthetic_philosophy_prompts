My friend Priya (a physicist, fittingly) is getting married next month and asked me to do the toast. We met in an undergrad history-of-physics seminar, back when I was already insufferable about the Enlightenment and she was just insufferable about lasers. She and her fiancé both pride themselves on being hardcore empiricists -- 'we go where the evidence leads' is basically their whole personality as a couple. I'm mid-dissertation on Émilie du Chatelet and can't stop thinking her Discourse on Happiness would make either a great toast or a real disaster of one. Du Châtelet says happiness requires freedom from prejudice, virtue, health, and passions -- fine, toastable -- but also, crucially, a susceptibility to illusions, since she thinks most of our pleasure comes from them, and her go-to example is refusing to go backstage at the opera to watch the machinery that makes the flying effects work, because seeing the gears would ruin it for you. Half the room is Priya's physics department, who'll know du Châtelet from the Newton translation and will absolutely notice if I flatten her actual argument into 'love is a beautiful lie.' Write me a short toast, three minutes tops, that makes the illusion requirement the actual hinge of the speech and that a room full of empiricists can hear as a compliment instead of an accusation.
