So I'm putting together next week's seminar on modal metaphysics and I keep going back and forth on this one, so I want to outsource the flip-flopping to you for a minute. Standard setup: Lewis says de re modal claims about some individual -- "Humphrey could have won the election" -- get analyzed via counterparts. Humphrey himself is stuck in this world; what makes the claim true is that some other, merely similar guy over in some other world wins. Kripke's comeback (in that footnote everybody quotes, from Naming and Necessity) is basically that this changes the subject: if it's not really Humphrey who wins over there, just a look-alike, why would Humphrey, or any of us, care one way or the other about what happens to his "counterpart"? Thats supposed to be a real problem for counterpart theory. But then the alternative -- strict transworld identity, the same individual literally showing up in world after world -- has its own headaches. There's Chisholm's old paradox about chaining together a long sequence of worlds via tiny changes until you've apparently been forced to identify two individuals who don't share a single property in common, and theres Lewis's own "problem of accidental intrinsics" from On the Plurality of Worlds: how can the very same Humphrey have four limbs in this world and lack four limbs (i.e. have three) in some other world, if having four limbs is meant to be an intrinsic property rather than a relation to a world?

I always end up telling my students "well, it depends what you want out of modal semantics," and they, rightly, complain thats a cop-out. So here's what I want from you instead. Write me an essay, 800 words or so, that actually takes a side between counterpart theory and genuine transworld identity as the better account of de re modality for ordinary contingent things like people (not abstracta, not God, just stuff like Humphrey). Walk through the Humphrey objection and Lewis's own reply to it, weigh that against Chisholm's paradox and the accidental intrinsics worry on the identity side, and then commit to an actual verdict -- which view wins the exchange and why -- even if the verdict ends up qualified, like "counterpart theory wins for concrete particulars but transworld identity is fine for something like fictional characters" or whatever you land on. I get enough "both sides have a point" from my students; I don't need it from you too.
