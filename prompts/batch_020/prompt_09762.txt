I spent my working life as a physicist, and in retirement I have been filling gaps in my education by reading through the history and philosophy of science -- Newton's Principia and Opticks, along with some of the secondary literature on his private theology. I now understand that Newton was a committed anti-Trinitarian of a broadly Arian stripe, who regarded the doctrine of the Trinity as a corruption introduced into the church in the fourth century, and that this conviction is tied to his insistence, in the General Scholium, that it is 'dominion' -- rather than mere perfection or infinitude -- that makes a being God. What I have not been able to piece together is how this specifically theological commitment is supposed to have shaped his physics. I keep encountering claims that his doctrine of absolute space, and the idea (much qualified by Newton himself) of space as God's 'sensorium,' grew out of this same voluntarist theology, and that it is part of what set him against Descartes' treatment of extension and matter. Could you make that connection intuitive for me? I find it difficult to see why a dispute over the status of the Trinity should end up mattering for whether space can exist independently of body.
