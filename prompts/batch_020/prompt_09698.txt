I'm writing a novel in which two old friends from graduate school -- now an analytic philosopher and a parish priest -- run into each other at a mutual friend's funeral reception. They pick up a long-running disagreement about whether penal substitution, the doctrine that Christ bore the punishment sinners deserved, is even a coherent idea. I want the philosopher to make the case David Lewis develops in his short essay "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?" -- that punishment might work like debt, so that a substitute's suffering could discharge one's liability to be punished even while one's guilt remains untouched and entirely one's own. I want the priest -- who takes her theology seriously enough to want a good argument for it, not just any argument -- to press the obvious disanalogy: our own penal practice refuses exactly this kind of substitution (no one may serve out a stranger's prison sentence), and that refusal seems to track something essential about what punishment is for, rather than a mere technicality. Write their exchange as twelve to sixteen lines of alternating dialogue in which each line answers the specific point just made rather than restating a fixed position, ending with the priest asking what would have to be true of God, on this picture, for someone else's suffering to actually discharge what a wrongdoer owes.
