I'm writing a novel with a scene in which a present-day rationalist tries to explain the idea of "acausal trade" to Leibniz. The idea, roughly: two agents who can never make causal contact with each other — because they occupy different branches of a quantum multiverse, say, or because one exists only as a simulation the other is running — can still cooperate, each conditioning its own behavior on an accurate prediction of how the other reasons. Before I draft Leibniz's half of the exchange, I need a serious reconstruction of what he would actually say, argued from his own system rather than from generic period atmosphere. His monads, after all, are windowless and cannot causally influence one another at all; whatever agreement they display is owed entirely to a harmony God pre-established among them at creation. Would he hear in acausal trade an echo of his own doctrine with the theology quietly removed, or would he insist that "coordination" between causally sealed-off agents is either an illusion or a job only a designer could perform? And given how much his system already rests on this being the one best of all possible worlds, chosen once and for all, is there any room left in it for the kind of live, contingent strategy that "trade" implies? Answer as a careful historian of philosophy would, not as a costume-drama pastiche.
