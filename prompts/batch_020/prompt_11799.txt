I'm working on a long-form piece about the limits of scientific knowledge, and one section leans on Fitch's paradox of knowability, which a philosopher I interviewed described as a rigorous logical proof that the universe must contain truths no possible observer could ever come to know — not simply facts we haven't yet discovered, but facts permanently sealed off from any mind, however capable. She suggested this gives real philosophical weight to the intuition that certain deep scientific puzzles, such as the exact subjective character of consciousness or what is 'really' happening beyond a black hole's event horizon, might belong to this off-limits category rather than merely being unsolved.

For the accompanying sidebar, I'd like to explain the argument in a few accessible sentences and then name two or three genuine scientific mysteries that the proof entitles us to call unknowable in this strict sense, as opposed to just currently unsolved. As I understand it, the whole argument turns on two premises that individually sound almost too modest to cause any trouble: that every truth is at least knowable in principle, and that at least one truth is not, in fact, currently known by anyone. Could you help me lay out, in terms a general reader could follow, how those two premises generate a contradiction, and tell me which, if any, of the open scientific questions I mentioned the argument actually licenses us to single out as Fitch-unknowable?
