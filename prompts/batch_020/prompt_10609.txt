This term I'm taking an upper-level seminar on classical Islamic philosophy, and this week's session centered on a close reading of the Seventeenth Discussion of al-Ghazālī's Tahāfut al-Falāsifa alongside Ibn Rushd's reply in the Tahāfut al-Tahāfut. One thing our instructor kept emphasizing is how badly the popular version of this dispute ("faith denies cause and effect, philosophy affirms it") misrepresents the actual texts: both men reason with the same logical toolkit, and Ibn Rushd — a practicing jurist, not a secular rationalist — is no less invested in God's power than Ghazālī is. She also mentioned that there's still real scholarly disagreement about whether Ghazālī's final position in that discussion is full-blown occasionalism or something closer to real natures in things that God can, on rare occasions, override, which made me want to see the argument worked out rather than summarized.

Could you write an imagined face-to-face dialogue between al-Ghazālī and Ibn Rushd, staged at the moment a piece of cotton is held to a flame, in which each tries to show the other that his account of why (or whether) the cotton burns leads somewhere unacceptable? Ghazālī should argue with his actual resources — the distinction between what is impossible in itself and what merely departs from habitual custom ('āda) — and should have to reckon with the ambiguity my instructor mentioned rather than simply asserting that God can do anything. Ibn Rushd should press the objection that worries him most: that without a necessary connection between what we call causes and effects, demonstrative knowledge of anything, natural or divine, collapses, and even the Qur'an's own habit of pointing to nature as evidence of God's wisdom stops making sense. Please don't let either side concede quickly or talk past the other — I'd like each of them responding to the specific move the other has just made, the way two people who had actually read each other's books would argue. A page or so should be plenty.
