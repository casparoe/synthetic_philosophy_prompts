Last night a friend and I had a long, unresolved argument about whether two honest, rational people who trust each other's judgment can knowingly agree to disagree. I argued that they obviously can: rationality constrains how you update on evidence, not what prior beliefs you're allowed to start with, so two rational people could simply begin with different priors and never be rationally obligated to converge. My friend disagreed, invoking Aumann's agreement theorem to argue that once our respective opinions are mutually known, our continuing to disagree would itself be evidence against our rationality. Looking into it afterward, I found that the theorem's force depends on the "common prior assumption" — the stipulation that any two rational agents' beliefs must ultimately derive from the very same prior, differing only because of differing information — and that this assumption is doing far more work than I'd realized, and is actually somewhat controversial in its own right.

Could you compare the common prior assumption with the more general subjectivist Bayesian picture, on which a prior need only be internally coherent, with no requirement that it be shared with anyone else? I'm especially interested in whether there's a good argument for why rational agents should be expected to hold a common prior to begin with, rather than merely being required to update correctly from whatever prior they happen to start with, and in whether the common prior assumption is really a further, substantive commitment beyond ordinary Bayesian rationality, or something a sufficiently reflective Bayesian is already committed to regardless. Finally, I'd like to know whether any of this actually vindicates my friend's position — that our disagreement, once out in the open, would have had to be irrational.
