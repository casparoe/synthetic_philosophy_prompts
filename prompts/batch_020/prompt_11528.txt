I spent nearly forty years as a solid-state chemist, and since retiring I have been attending, somewhat belatedly, a metaphysics reading group run through the philosophy department at a university near me. Last month the group worked through the literature on material constitution — the familiar puzzle of the statue and the lump of clay from which it is made — and my first instinct, shaped by decades of thinking about atoms arranged into different crystal lattices, was that the entire debate rests on a confusion: surely the statue and the lump are simply one and the same portion of matter under two descriptions, no more distinct than a diamond and a certain arrangement of carbon atoms. That confidence did not survive contact with Gibbard's case of Lumpl the lump and Goliath the statue, or with what the literature calls the grounding problem. I no longer feel entitled to dismiss the puzzle as merely verbal, though I can no longer reconstruct exactly why not, and I would value your help thinking it through properly.

Specifically, I would like you to do three things. First, set out how the Leibniz's Law argument is supposed to force a choice between holding that Lumpl and Goliath are strictly identical and holding that they are two objects that merely share a location, and explain why Gibbard's own preferred escape — treating their identity as contingent — is generally thought to require something like counterpart theory if it is to avoid outright contradiction with the necessity of identity. Second, explain the grounding problem itself in some depth: why is it supposed to be a distinctly worse kind of brute fact for two things to share literally every microphysical, categorical property at every moment of their joint existence while still differing in their modal or sortal properties, rather than just one more unexplained fact of the sort any metaphysics eventually has to accept somewhere — and, in that connection, why a move like Lynne Rudder Baker's, which grounds the statue's being a statue in its relation to "the artworld," is widely thought to get the direction of explanation backwards. Third, since my own sympathies keep drifting toward the eliminativist response of simply denying that statues exist as objects over and above simples arranged statue-wise, I would like the strongest case against that response — why most metaphysicians resist it rather than embracing it as the simplest, most parsimonious way of dissolving the puzzle altogether.
