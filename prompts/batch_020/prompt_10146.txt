In my school's philosophy club, we spent a couple of sessions this term on metaethics, and I've been reading further on my own about expressivism: the view that a sentence like "cheating is wrong" doesn't describe a moral fact but instead expresses an attitude such as disapproval. I found the view fairly compelling until I ran into what is apparently called the Frege–Geach problem, which I have found genuinely difficult to get to the bottom of. As far as I can tell, the difficulty is that moral sentences constantly show up in places where they clearly aren't being asserted at all, such as the antecedent of a conditional ("If cheating on an exam is wrong, then pressuring a friend into cheating for you is wrong"), and yet an inference built out of that conditional together with the premise "cheating on an exam is wrong," concluding that pressuring a friend into cheating is wrong, still seems perfectly valid. If the expressivist says that the meaning of a moral sentence just is the attitude expressed when someone asserts it on its own, it isn't obvious what explains either the meaning of the embedded occurrence or the validity of the resulting argument. A friend of mine in the club is convinced this is fatal to expressivism, while I'm not so sure, and the two of us haven't been able to get much further than restating our own intuitions at each other.

Could you write a dialogue between two equally capable philosophers who disagree about exactly this question: one an expressivist who thinks the embedding problem can be solved, or at least defused, without giving up the idea that moral sentences express attitudes rather than describe facts, and the other a cognitivist who thinks the problem shows that moral sentences must have genuine truth-conditional content after all. I'd like the expressivist to actually attempt a specific, detailed strategy for explaining why a moral sentence means the same thing whether it's asserted or embedded, and what makes the resulting inference valid, rather than just insisting that a solution must exist somewhere; and I'd like the cognitivist to respond with a concrete objection to that particular strategy rather than a generic dismissal, so that the two of them are genuinely engaging with each other's specific moves instead of merely restating their opening positions. A handful of exchanges of real back-and-forth would be more useful to me than a longer dialogue that simply repeats the initial problem several times over.
