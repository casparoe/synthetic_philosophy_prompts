I spent my working life doing theoretical research in general relativity, so my own introduction to Kurt Gödel came not through the incompleteness theorems but through his 1949 rotating-universe solution to Einstein's field equations, together with the companion essay arguing that the mere existence of such solutions, with their closed timelike curves, is enough to show that the ordinary, objectively "flowing" conception of time cannot be correct. That essay became something of a preoccupation once I retired: I have since worked through Hao Wang's books on Gödel and a couple of the standard biographies, and I have grown quite taken with the private, almost theological side of his thought that never comes up in a physics curriculum.

This is where I could use some help. As I now understand it, Gödel held, on the strength of the incompleteness theorems, that the human mind cannot be equivalent to any finite mechanical procedure; and, separately, toward the end of his life, he showed a few colleagues a rigorous modal-logic proof, squarely in the Leibniz–Anselm tradition, that a being possessing all "positive properties" necessarily exists. Putting these two results together seems, to me, to more or less hand you his well-documented personal conviction that some form of afterlife awaits us: a mind that provably outruns every finite machine, and that owes its existence to a being whose own existence is logically necessary, is not the sort of thing one would expect to simply switch off at bodily death. On this reading, Gödel's confidence in immortality looks less like an article of faith than a theorem he happened never to publish as such. A friend of mine who is finishing a philosophy doctorate dismissed this as far too quick when I described it to her over dinner, though she did not have time that evening to say exactly what I was running together that should not be run together. I would be grateful for a careful diagnosis: does the incompleteness-based claim about minds and machines really license the flat "not a machine" reading I am giving it, does the ontological proof bear on the question of personal survival at all, and what, if not this chain of reasoning, was Gödel's own actual argument for an afterlife?
