I had a long, slightly tense conversation last night with a friend who manages a team of about a dozen engineers, and I still haven't settled in my own mind which of us had the better of it. She recently promoted one of her reports, Marcus, to team lead over another, Priya, who by every technical measure — code quality, output, peer-review scores — was the stronger candidate. Her reasoning was that Priya rarely speaks up in meetings unless she has already worked something out in full, whereas Marcus thinks aloud, interrupts freely, and generally "takes up space" in a room, which my friend regards as indispensable in a team lead. When I pointed out that this criterion seems to reward a fairly specific register of confidence, and that several of the quieter people on her team come from cultural backgrounds in which interrupting a group discussion, especially one involving someone senior, is considered genuinely disrespectful rather than simply diffident, she didn't budge. She said every manager inevitably brings some set of personal preferences about what leadership looks like to a decision like this, that she is entitled to weigh "command of the room" heavily even though it is not the only valid way to lead, and that relabeling her considered judgment as a "bias" the moment someone disagrees with it is just a rhetorical move to put her on the defensive rather than actually engaging with her reasoning.

We went back and forth for the better part of an hour, and I came away unable to say exactly where, if anywhere, her position breaks down. On one hand, it does seem true that people are entitled to their own views about what makes someone promotable, and that not every such view can be convicted of bias merely because it happens to correlate with some demographic pattern — otherwise no preference of any kind would ever be safe from that charge. On the other hand, something about resting a concrete, consequential decision like this on "that's just my leadership philosophy" still feels like it dodges the question rather than answers it, even though I can't quite articulate why her case ought to be treated any differently from an ordinary matter of taste. Was her defense actually adequate, or is there a real distinction between a legitimate personal preference and a disguised bias that she isn't seeing?
