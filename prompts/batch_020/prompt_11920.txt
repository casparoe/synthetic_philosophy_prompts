Professor Marguerite Calloway — my first philosophy teacher, and for the fifteen years since, one of the people I called whenever a story needed a sharp, careful quote on public shaming or political hypocrisy — died ten days ago, and her department has asked me to say a few words at Saturday's memorial service. Marguerite spent her career on the ethics of blame, working in the line that runs from G.A. Cohen's essay on who has the standing to condemn terrorists through R. Jay Wallace's work on hypocrisy and the equal standing of persons, and her own contribution to that literature, which she distilled into one line for every introductory class, was uncompromising: a hypocrite doesn't just look bad pointing out a fault she shares with the person she's accusing — she forfeits the standing to raise it at all, however right she is on the merits. She called this being "out of order," a phrase left over from her college debate team that she never really put down.

The complication, which every philosopher in the room will be thinking about whether or not anyone says it aloud, is that Marguerite was constitutionally incapable of starting anything on time. In thirty-one years on the faculty, she never once opened a lecture, a committee meeting, or her own office hours within ten minutes of the posted time, all while cheerfully eviscerating — in faculty meetings, and in at least one op-ed I helped her place — colleagues who kept students waiting, which she called "a small daily theft." Half of Saturday's room will know this history in detail: her graduate students, her longtime co-authors, a man she'd been arguing with in print since 1994. The other half is her book club, her sister, three grandchildren, and the woman who ran the philosophy building's coffee cart for twenty years, none of whom has ever heard the phrase "standing to blame" and shouldn't need to in order to recognize the eulogy as true.

Marguerite left typed instructions for the service, and one line reads: "Do not let anyone up there call me punctual. I would rather be laughed at than lied for." I want to honor that. I don't think the honest way to do it is to tell one warm anecdote about her lateness and move on; I'd like the eulogy to actually reason through her own distinctions, on her own terms, to work out whether bringing this up today counts as the kind of blame she spent thirty years arguing almost no one has the standing to deliver — or whether a eulogy, addressed to someone who can no longer answer for herself, is doing something else entirely. Could you write it? Something in the five-minute range, warm rather than glib, that lets the philosophers in the room recognize their own field being taken seriously, and lets everyone else simply hear a true and loving account of who she was.
