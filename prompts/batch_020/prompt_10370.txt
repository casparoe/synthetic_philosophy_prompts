Here's the question as posed (Q3, worth 20 of the exam's 100 points):

"The fundamental dynamical laws of physics — classical mechanics, Maxwell's electrodynamics, and (setting aside measurement) the Schrödinger equation — are all time-reversal invariant: for any physically possible process, its time-reverse is equally possible according to the laws. Yet the Second Law of Thermodynamics says the entropy of an isolated system increases (or stays constant) toward the future and never toward the past.
(a) [8 pts] Explain how Loschmidt's reversibility objection and Zermelo's recurrence objection each threaten Boltzmann's attempt to derive the Second Law from the mechanics of colliding particles (the H-theorem).
(b) [8 pts] Explain what the 'Past Hypothesis' is supposed to do in contemporary responses to this problem, and how it differs from the response Boltzmann himself gave to Zermelo.
(c) [4 pts] Some philosophers argue the thermodynamic arrow explains the 'psychological arrow' — the fact that we remember the past and not the future. Sketch the argument and note one difficulty with it."

And here's the student's answer, copied exactly as turned in:

"(a) The trouble is that the microdynamics — ordinary Newtonian mechanics for colliding particles — is time-reversal invariant: if you take any solution and flip every particle's velocity at some instant, you get another equally lawful solution. Loschmidt's objection uses exactly this fact against the H-theorem: for every trajectory along which Boltzmann's H decreases (entropy rises), time-reversal symmetry guarantees a second, equally lawful trajectory — the velocity-reversed one — along which H increases (entropy falls) as the system evolves forward. So it looks like the mechanics can't possibly force entropy to rise, since it's exactly as happy to make it fall. Zermelo's objection is a different kind of problem: by the Poincaré recurrence theorem, any mechanical system confined to a bounded region of phase space must eventually return arbitrarily close to any earlier state, including low-entropy ones. So on top of Loschmidt's worry that entropy-decreasing trajectories are just as lawful, Zermelo shows that in a system like this, entropy literally cannot keep rising forever — it has to come back down eventually, no matter what.

(b) Boltzmann's own answer to both objections was to stop treating the H-theorem as an exceptionless mechanical law and read it statistically instead: entropy tends to rise because the number of microstates compatible with high entropy vastly outnumbers the number compatible with low entropy, so starting from a low-entropy state the system is overwhelmingly likely to move toward higher entropy, and any recurrence back down would take a length of time that dwarfs the age of the universe. Once you accept that, both objections are essentially answered: entropy-lowering trajectories and eventual recurrences are technically consistent with the dynamics, but they are so overwhelmingly improbable that for any realistic system entropy will simply keep climbing indefinitely, which is exactly the Second Law. This statistical move is essentially the idea that David Albert later formalized as the 'Past Hypothesis' — the claim that the universe (or system) simply started out in a very low-entropy state, which is what licenses the expectation that entropy keeps rising from here. Boltzmann's own argument already needed some such assumption to get its probabilistic conclusion off the ground in the first place, so the Past Hypothesis is less a genuinely new response to Zermelo than a later formalization of an assumption Boltzmann's argument was already relying on.

(c) The idea is that a physical record of a past event — a memory, a footprint, a photograph — is a correlation between a system's present state and an earlier event, and this kind of reliable correlation can only be produced going forward in the direction of increasing entropy, because building it requires exactly the sort of low-probability, dissipative interaction that, thanks to the Past Hypothesis, was common in our past but has no mirror-image counterpart in our future. That's supposed to explain why we accumulate traces of the past and not the future, and hence why we remember one and not the other. One difficulty is that this at best explains why our epistemic access is asymmetric; it doesn't obviously explain the further, more basic-seeming impression that time itself passes or flows, rather than merely being asymmetric — you could apparently have a static block universe with exactly this entropy gradient and exactly this knowledge asymmetry, with nothing in it actually 'moving' from past to future, so something about the felt passage of time still seems to be left over."
