I'm a senior philosophy major finishing a seminar this term on Gödel's unpublished philosophical writings -- we've spent most of the semester working through Hao Wang's reconstructions of material from the Nachlass alongside the correspondence volumes of the Collected Works -- and three weeks ago my great-uncle Henryk, a retired logician who did his graduate work at Princeton in the late 1950s and always described his handful of encounters with Gödel at the Institute as the formative experience of his career, died at eighty-nine. His department is co-hosting a memorial gathering with the family, and I've been asked to speak. I'd like help writing what I say.

The complication is that Henryk held, explicitly and by his own account for decades, a version of an argument he attributed to Gödel. It is not the modal ontological proof of God's existence that most people have vaguely heard of, but a separate and much more personal line of reasoning that Gödel apparently pressed on his own mother in letters: survival of death, on this view, is not a matter of religious faith but follows with something close to rational necessity from the premise that the world is fundamentally rational rather than a meaningless accident, since it would be strange design indeed for something as elaborately built up as a mature human personality -- cultivated over sixty or seventy years and barely put to full use -- to be annihilated the moment it is finished. Henryk treated this as his own considered view, held with the confidence of an argument rather than the confidence of faith, and more than once -- long before he was ill -- asked me not to let anyone flatten it, if the day came, into ordinary religious comfort about being "in a better place" or "watching over us," on the grounds that this would misrepresent a position he had arrived at on quite different terms.

Could you write the eulogy? It should run five or six minutes aloud, and it has to satisfy two audiences at once: several of his former colleagues will recognize precisely what is being invoked and will wince if the argument is garbled or quietly converted into generic theism, while most of the family has never heard of Gödel beyond the incompleteness theorems and needs the idea to console on its own terms, without the eulogy turning into a seminar. I've also spent enough of the semester reading the standard objections to this kind of argument -- above all, that "the world is rational" is carrying enormous weight and is never itself independently secured -- that I don't want the speech to claim more certainty for the view than either Gödel or Henryk himself ever claimed for it; I'd rather it stay honest about how speculative the reasoning is while still functioning as comfort rather than as a caveat.
