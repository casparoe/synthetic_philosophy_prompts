I spent most of my career as a marine biologist working on colonial invertebrates -- siphonophores mostly, the group that includes the Portuguese man o' war -- and since retiring a couple years back I've fallen into reading philosophy, which is a whole new world for me. My nephew, who's finishing a philosophy MA, put me onto Peter van Inwagen's book on material beings, and I got completely stuck on what he calls the "special composition question": basically, under what conditions do a bunch of smaller objects add up to make one bigger object? His own answer (organicism, apparently) is that this only happens when the little things' activities "constitute a life," so a dog is a genuine single object built out of particles, but a rock or a chair isn't really one thing at all, just particles arranged rock-wise or chair-wise. My brother in law, who's more of a common-sense type, thinks this is needlessly complicated and that the right answer is just universalism: any collection of objects whatsoever, however scattered, composes some further object, full stop, even if that "object" is as gerrymandered as my coffee mug plus the far side of the moon.

Here's where my old career won't leave me alone though: van Inwagen apparently rules out every "moderate" answer to the composition question (things compose something when they're stuck together, or touching, or whatever) because he thinks that would make composition, and therefore existence itself, a vague, matter-of-degree affair, which he takes to be flatly impossible. Fair enough, except "constituting a life" strikes me as exactly that sort of fuzzy-at-the-edges category, and not hypothetically -- I built a career on organisms that sit right on the fence between being one animal and being a colony of many, so this isnt some cute thought experiment for me, it's Tuesday. So could you compare universalism and van Inwagen's organicism as answers to the special composition question? Lay out the vagueness argument he uses against the in-between views properly, and then tell me straight: does the messiness of biological life sink organicism by that very same argument, or can "constitutes a life" definately be sharpened up enough to survive it in a way "stuck together" can't? Don't give me a wishy-washy "both views have something going for them" answer -- pick a side and defend it.
