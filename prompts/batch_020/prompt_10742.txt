So a few of us in the philosophy department started an informal reading group this term going through some of the old MIRI "agent foundations" material -- the tiling agents paper, Löb's theorem, all that self-reference stuff. Last meeting two people in the group (I'll call them Theo and Elena, not their real names) got into a genuinely good argument about whether the Löbian obstacle shows something deep about self-trust or is just an artifact of picking a bad formal architecture, and I've been chewing on it since without landing anywhere myself, so I wanted an outside read, cause honestly I couldnt tell who's right.

Theo opened by arguing that Löb's theorem shows full self-trust is off the table for any formally reasoning agent, not just a quirk of bad engineering: if a system could prove, as a general schema, that "provability of X in my own proof system implies X," Löb's theorem forces it to already prove X outright, so getting an exact successor running the same code to be trusted is a dead end, not a bug you patch later. Elena replied that this overclaims -- MIRI's own proposed fix is a tower of successively stronger systems (PA, then PA plus Con(PA), then that plus its own consistency statement, and so on), where each level can certify the one below it, so you do get a real chain of agents trusting their successors, it's just that trust flows upward through the tower rather than staying fixed within one system. Theo came back that this doesn't actually close the loop: you never get two copies of the same proof system vouching for each other, only ever a weaker system trusting a strictly stronger one, and since the tower can't be infinite in the direction that would let a system certify itself, there's still no stable fixed point -- which is exactly the philosophically interesting, unavoidable part of the result. Elena then said Theo is quietly assuming rational self-trust has to look like a soundness proof in classical logic; we don't secure trust in our own future selves that way, and newer work on logical induction and reflective oracles builds systems that hold coherent, well-calibrated probabilities about their own future outputs -- including self-referential ones -- without hitting the Löb wall, precisely because they aren't built around proving a universal soundness schema. Theo's last word was that this doesn't dissolve the obstacle so much as relocate it: logical inductors provably still can't get instant, perfect knowledge of their own future beliefs, so pushing on the probabilistic versions just turns up a softer analogue of the same wall, which suggests the actual culprit is diagonalization and self-reference limits generally, not the specific choice of classical deduction.
