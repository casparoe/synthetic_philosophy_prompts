So I was talking with a friend who's way more into comparative philosophy than I am, and she brought up this Jain concept called anekantavada, which is basically the doctrine that reality is "many-sided" (anekanta) and that no single statement about it can ever capture the whole truth, only a partial standpoint (naya). Apparently the Jains built a whole logical apparatus around this, syadvada, where every claim is supposed to get prefixed with "syat" (roughly "in a certain respect"), and there's this sevenfold schema (saptabhangi) where something like "the pot exists" ends up being true, false, both, or even inexpressible depending on which respect your looking at it from. I only half followed the details and she admitted she was simplifying a ton too, but the general vibe was that most disagreements (and, on the more ambitious Jain view, even violence) trace back to ekantavada -- people mistaking their own partial standpoint for the whole, unconditioned truth.

Anyway this got me thinking about this never-ending argument I have with my dad, where the specific topic changes every Thanksgiving but the underlying dynamic never does -- neither of us budges an inch, and we both walk away thinking the other one is just being dense. Assuming anekantavada is meant as a serious, non-wishy-washy position and not just a fancy way of saying "everyone's a little bit right," what are the most important implications of it for how I should actually handle persistent disagreements like that one? Like, concretely, does it change what I should say or how confident I should act, or is it more of a background metaphysical attitude that doesn't really cash out into any specific behavior? And while I'm at it -- isn't "no single standpoint captures the whole truth" itself just one more standpoint claiming to be the whole truth? Curious how a serious defender of the doctrine would wriggle out of that one.
