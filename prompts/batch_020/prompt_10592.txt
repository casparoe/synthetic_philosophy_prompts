I write about science and technology for a living, and for the past few weeks I've been reporting on a product-safety dispute I can't stop turning over in my head. I don't think it's really the engineering disagreement both sides claim it is. A mid-sized manufacturer of home security panels is facing a recall petition after independent testers found that one component — a relay that closes the circuit triggering the siren whenever a door sensor is breached — has a wiring defect that, tested in isolation, fails to close roughly once in twenty attempts whenever it warms past a certain temperature. And yet, across hundreds of thousands of units in the field over several years, not one customer's alarm has ever actually failed to sound, because every panel also contains a supervisory chip that watches the relay's temperature and intervenes at the instant it would start to misfire.

The trouble is that the manufacturer's own engineers can't agree on what the chip does. Some describe it as simply rerouting the trigger signal around the bad relay, leaving the relay exactly as flawed as it always was; others describe it as sending a corrective pulse that briefly brings the relay back within spec, so that for the fraction of a second when it matters, there is arguably nothing wrong with it at all. The company's lawyers say this settles the matter: a component that never fails in the field isn't defective, whatever might be true of it on a test bench. The recall petitioners call that a dodge — the relay, they insist, is still faulty regardless, and the chip amounts to a patch rather than a cure. When I described the case to a colleague who covers medical devices, she said it reminded her of an old dispute over a pacemaker lead with a similar self-correcting quirk, which makes me suspect this is a recurring structural issue rather than a one-off.

Both sides agree on every physical fact about what's happening inside the panel; they disagree only about what to call it, which makes me think there's a genuine puzzle sitting underneath the legal one. Is the relay actually faulty, given that it never fails? Does it matter which of the two engineering stories is correct — rerouting around the flaw versus momentarily curing it? And is there some principled basis for answering that, rather than just adopting whichever definition of "defect" happens to be convenient for one side?
