I spent about thirty-five years as a paleontologist before I retired, mostly working on Permian extinction boundaries, and now that I've got time on my hands I've been slowly working through some philosophy of religion, which is how I fell down a Leibniz hole this winter (the Theodicy, plus some secondary reading on the monadology, since the original is rough going in places). What strikes me is how load-bearing his three-way split of evil is for the whole system: metaphysical evil is just the built-in imperfection of anything that isn't God, physical evil (suffering) mostly flows from that or from moral evil, and moral evil is sin, full stop. He's also committed, via the best-possible-world and plenitude reasoning, to creation being as full and continuous as it possibly can be -- no needless gaps, nothing permanently left out that could exist. Reasonable enough for a guy writing in 1710, when, as I understand it, most naturalists still assumed a species couldn't really vanish for good -- Jefferson was apparantly still hoping Lewis and Clark might stumble on a living mammoth out west, decades later, on more or less that same reasoning.

Thing is, my old field didn't exist yet, and it isn't kind to that picture. Extinction wasn't scientifically respectable until Cuvier, roughly eighty years after Leibniz died, and once you accept it you're stuck with five or six mass extinctions that permanently erased whole branches of the tree rather than preserving every possible form, sitting on top of a few hundred million years of ordinary animal suffering in a world with no humans and nothing resembling a moral agent anywhere in it -- long before there was any sin to be punished for or any soul being built through hardship. So here's what I actually want from you: reconstruct, as carefully as you can, how Leibniz himself would try to fold this into his system rather than just abandon it. Does his three-way division of evil have any real resources for suffering that predates all moral agency? Does plenitude just quietly die once permanent extinction is on the table? Is there some move available to him -- pre-established harmony, locating "perfection" in relations across the whole world-series rather than in any one creature's fate, or something else -- that genuinely holds up? Don't just tell me he'd find it troubling; give me the specific moves you think he'd actually make, and say plainly if you think it's ultimately a loosing hand for him.
