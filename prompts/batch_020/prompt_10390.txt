I never studied economics or mathematics beyond high school, but over the last several months I've been teaching myself voting theory after a ranked-choice ballot measure in my area got me wondering why "fair" elections turn out to be so hard to design. Working through things on my own, I got as far as Arrow's impossibility theorem, which I think I understand reasonably well: with three or more options on the table, no procedure for turning individual rankings into a single social ranking can satisfy unrestricted domain, the Pareto principle, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship all at the same time.

What's tripping me up now is the Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem. A friend who took a game theory course waved it off as "basically Arrow's theorem again, just dressed up differently," which annoyed me, because on the face of it these look like different results — Gibbard-Satterthwaite is about single-winner voting rules and about whether voters can do better by misreporting their preferences, not about building a full social ranking at all. I've also come across a few passing remarks online to the effect that either theorem can be derived from the other with only minor added assumptions, which would seem to back up my friend's dismissiveness, but I'd like to actually understand whether that's true and, if so, in what sense. Concretely, I'd like help seeing what "manipulability" in Gibbard-Satterthwaite really has to do with "independence of irrelevant alternatives" in Arrow's setup, since on the surface those axioms look like they're about entirely different things — one about the honesty of reported preferences, the other about whether unrelated alternatives can affect the ranking between two given options. And is the "dictator" that turns up in both theorems provably the same kind of formal object in each, or is that just a reuse of convenient terminology? I would rather come away with a real sense of how deep the connection runs than with a comforting but shallow line about both being "impossibility results about voting."
