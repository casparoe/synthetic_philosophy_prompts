So my little post-retirement reading group (mostly ex-chemists plus one very stubborn retired geologist) just worked through Cicero's De Senectute, and as an old biochemist I got kind of fascinated by how tightly the argument is built. Cato basically lists four "symptoms" that supposedly make old age miserable — it pulls you out of active life, it wrecks the body, it strips away nearly all pleasures, and its right next to death — and then knocks each one down in turn, like he's testing four hypotheses one by one and rejecting the null.

That sent me back to fill a gap in my classical education, so I finally read the opening of Plato's Republic, where old Cephalus, sitting there garlanded after a sacrifice, gets asked by Socrates what old age is actually like. His answer is way shorter and less systematic than Cicero's — basically that most men blame old age itself for their misery, but he thinks it's really down to a man's character or temperament all along, and he tosses in that anecdote about Sophocles being asked whether he can still make love to a woman, and Sophocles saying he's delighted to finally be free of all that, like a slave who's escaped a harsh master. Cephalus also drags money into it, saying wealth makes old age alot easier to bear for a decent man, though it won't fix anything for a bad one.

Could you actually compare these two treatments in some depth, rather than just noting "both talk about old age"? I'm curious where Cicero and Cephalus really agree once you strip away the rhetorical packaging — e.g. this shared idea that character matters more than circumstance — and where their underlying commitments actually pull apart, say on desire and pleasure, or on what wealth is doing philosophically in each account. I'd also like to know whether you think Cicero's much longer treatise can be read as quietly answering, or complicating, what Cephalus only gestures at in a page or two. And if you think one of the two holds up better under scrutiny as genuine philosophy of aging rather than just consolation literature, say so plainly and defend it.
