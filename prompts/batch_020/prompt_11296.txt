A few weeks ago I was having dinner with a friend who works as a quant at a trading firm, and we ended up in a long argument about a hypothetical bet that I still haven't been able to shake. The bet was simple: flip a coin — if it lands heads, you lose $200; if tails, you gain $240. I said I would turn that down every single time, no matter how much money I already had saved up, since losing $200 for no reason would bother me more than winning $240 would please me, even though I can see the expected value is positive. My friend didn't dispute that this is a common reaction. Instead, he did some quick math to make a sharper point: he said that if I would really decline that exact bet at every possible wealth level, then, by the logic of standard economic theory, I am also committed to declining a second bet — flip a coin: if it's heads, you lose $1,000; if tails, you win some enormous prize, a million dollars or a billion, it wouldn't matter which. His argument was that rejecting the first bet at all wealth levels forces a very particular shape onto how much extra money is worth to me as I get richer (I believe the term he used was "utility function"), and that no prize in the second bet, however large, could ever rescue it from that shape. Since I would obviously take the second bet once the prize got large enough, he concluded that my instinct about the first bet has to be some kind of mistake rather than a coherent preference.

This has been bothering me ever since, because as a software engineer I default to trusting whichever side has the tighter argument, and I followed his math well enough to believe it's basically valid — yet his conclusion still doesn't sit right with me. Both reactions still feel entirely sensible to me at once, and this isn't purely academic either: I've turned down small, positive-expected-value gambles in real life before (friends' spread-betting apps, mostly) for exactly the reason described above. I don't have any background in economics or philosophy, so I genuinely can't tell whether there's a subtle flaw in my friend's reasoning, whether he's caught me in an actual inconsistency that I should simply accept, or whether there's a more sophisticated way of thinking about risk and rational choice under which both of my reactions could be defensible at the same time. Is my friend right that I'm simply being irrational, or is there a better way to make sense of what's going on here?
