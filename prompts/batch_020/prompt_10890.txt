Then explain what the presumptuous philosopher case is supposed to show, and which of the two assumptions it's usually raised against." (20 pts)

student's answer:

"SSA says that when I don't know which observer in some reference class I am, I should reason as if I'd been picked at random from that class -- e.g. the class of all humans who will ever have lived. SIA adds a further move on top of that: hypotheses on which more observers exist in your reference class should get a proportionally bigger prior to begin with, since your own existence is less surprising under those hypotheses than under the ones with fewer observers.

Bostrom's doomsday argument runs on SSA. Say there are two hypotheses about how many humans will ever live: 200 billion total, or 200 trillion total. If I find out my birth rank is around 70 billion, SSA tells me to update strongly toward the smaller hypothesis, because a birth rank of 70 billion is unremarkable if only 200 billion humans ever live, but wildly atypical -- too early -- if there turn out to be 200 trillion. So finding out roughly where you fall in birth order pushes you toward thinking humanity's total population, and lifespan, will be smaller than you'd otherwise guess: toward doom sooner.

SIA is usually presented as the fix for this. Since SIA already gave the 200-trillion hypothesis a much bigger prior to begin with (200 trillion observers means someone like you was much more likely to exist at all under that hypothesis), that boost exactly offsets the update you get from conditioning on your birth rank. The two effects cancel and you're left with your original, non-anthropic estimate of how long humanity will last -- so under SIA the doomsday argument has no force.

The presumptuous philosopher case is aimed at SSA, though, not SIA. Two cosmological theories are on the table, one saying the universe contains a trillion observers and the other a trillion trillion, and physicists are about to run an expensive experiment to tell them apart. Since SSA is the assumption that inclines you to favor whichever theory packs more observers into your reference class, a philosopher who took it seriously could announce the answer from their armchair before the experiment even ran, which seems like an absurd amount of cosmological information to extract from pure self-location. That's supposed to show something has gone wrong with treating your own existence as a random sample in the SSA way."
