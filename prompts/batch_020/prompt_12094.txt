So a coworker sent me this old Aeon article a while back about Kurt Godel's letters to his mom, and I've been chewing on it ever since. Turns out the incompleteness theorem guy also had this whole personal argument for why an afterlife basically has to exist -- something like: the world is rationally structured and full of meaning (you can tell because science keeps finding order everywhere), human lives get cut short before all that meaning/potential gets fulfilled and are full of totally unresolved suffering, and it'd be irrational for a rational universe to work that way, so there must be some further stage where it all gets sorted out. This wasn't just an offhand comment either, it's from actual letters he wrote his mother Marianne in 1961.

What's been nagging me is that Godel also has this seperate, way more famous argument from a 1951 talk (the Gibbs lecture), where he says either the human mind can't be fully captured by any Turing machine / mechanical procedure, or there are absolutely undecidable math problems out there -- and he leaned hard towards the first option, i.e. minds aren't reducible to machines. As a programmer that's the part I actually get on a technical level, and it's got me wondering: if you put those two beliefs of his side by side, what would he make of stuff like mind-uploading, whole-brain emulation, "backing up your consciousness" to a server -- the kind of digital-immortality thing people in tech circles love speculating about? Would he see that as basically delivering on his own rational-world afterlife argument, or would his anti-mechanism stuff mean he'd think an uploaded copy is just a really elaborate puppet show, not an actual continuation of the person? I'd love it if you could reason through this using his actual positions rather than just surface-level vibes, and tell me where you think he'd actually land.
