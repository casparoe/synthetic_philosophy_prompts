So this happened at one of my local rationalist-diaspora meetups a couple weeks ago (the kind of group where half the room has actually read Chalmers and the other half just absorbed p-zombies through years of LW osmosis), and two people, Dave and Wren, got into a genuinely good back-and-forth about zombies and physicalism while the rest of us just watched. I wrote down the gist right after because I wanted a second opinion on it, so let me lay out the exchange move by move -- bear with me if I flub some detail, this is filtered through my notes and my memory both.

Dave opened with the standard Chalmers move: he can conceive, without contradiction, of something physically identical to him down to the last quark but with "no one home" phenomenally, and since it's conceivable it's metaphysically possible, and if it's possible then physicalism is false, because you'd have two worlds with identical physical facts and different phenomenal facts. Wren pushed back with the Kripke move: we can also seemingly "conceive" of water not being H2O, or Hesperus not being Phosphorus, and those turn out to be necessary a posteriori truths anyway -- so maybe "this brain state = this conscious state" only feels contingent for the same reason, and conceivability is misleading us here too. Dave came back with the two-dimensional semantics reply: the water/H2O case only works, he said, because "water" has a primary intension (roughly, "whatever the clear drinkable stuff around here turns out to be") that comes apart from its secondary intension (H2O) -- but a phenomenal concept like "pain" doesn't have that kind of gap, since it just picks out the feel directly regardless of how the world turns out, so the Kripke move doesn't defuse the zombie argument the way it defuses water/H2O. Wren answered with the phenomenal concept strategy: maybe phenomenal concepts are just a special, recognitional kind of concept that rigidly picks out physical states without any a priori analysis of them ever being available to us, which would fully explain the epistemic gap without needing an actual ontological one. Then Dave brought up the move that got the most reaction out of the room: the Yudkowsky-style point that if all this is right, your zombie twin would still write papers about the hard problem and say "I know I'm conscious," for exactly the same physical reasons you do -- meaning your own belief that your conscious has nothing to do with you actually being conscious, which seems like an absurd coincidence, or worse, means the belief was never justified in the first place. Wren's comeback was that this is a real cost, sure, but it's a cost of epiphenomenalism, which Chalmers openly signs up for (she pointed out he's got a whole section basically named after this exact worry, "the paradox of phenomenal judgment") -- not a proof that zombies are inconceivable. Weird isn't the same as incoherent, she said, and Dave hadn't actually produced a contradiction.

Nobody at the meetup wanted to declare a winner and honestly, neither do I.
