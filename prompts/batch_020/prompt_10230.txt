I'm a PhD student in personality and social psychology, currently auditing a graduate seminar on virtue ethics in the philosophy department — mostly because so much of the empirical literature I work with gets invoked secondhand in these debates, and I wanted to see how philosophers actually handle it. During the discussion section following the week on Gilbert Harman's paper on virtue ethics and the fundamental attribution error, two of the philosophy students, Callum and Ines, ended up in a genuine disagreement that the rest of the room mostly just watched play out, and I still haven't been able to settle for myself who came out ahead.

(1) Callum opened by arguing that Aristotelian virtue ethics needs people to have robust character traits — courage, honesty, and so on — that produce consistent behavior across relevantly similar situations, and that decades of social psychology say this doesn't hold up: in Milgram's studies, ordinary participants delivered what they believed were dangerous shocks under nothing more than an experimenter's calm insistence that they continue, and in Darley and Batson's seminarian study, simply being told they were in a hurry predicted whether someone stopped to help a stranger in apparent distress far better than anything about the seminarian, including whether they were on their way to give a talk on the parable of the Good Samaritan. If a variable as trivial as being late outpredicts whatever we want to call character, Callum said, virtue ethics is presupposing traits the evidence doesn't support. (2) Ines answered that this targets a strawman: Aristotle explicitly treats full virtue, as opposed to mere continence or ordinary habituation, as rare and achieved only through the right upbringing plus practical wisdom, so finding that most people's behavior is situationally malleable is close to what his theory already predicts once you remember he thought moral maturity was a minority achievement. (3) Callum pushed back that this move is too convenient: if every inconsistency can be absorbed by saying the people involved simply weren't virtuous yet, the theory stops predicting anything, and the seminarians were about as favorable a population as one could ask for — primed with the parable's content, some literally about to preach on it — and they still did worse than you'd expect if character were doing much of the work. (4) Ines's final response introduced a different consideration: there's a non-ad-hoc reason to expect exactly this pattern even where traits are real, since single actions are noisy measures of an underlying disposition, and noisy measures look weakly correlated with anything until you average over many occasions — a fact about measurement, not special pleading for virtue ethics. She added, almost in passing, that the single most famous study in this literature, Zimbardo's Stanford Prison Experiment, has actually aged the worst of all of them, since later interviews and recordings suggest the guards' cruelty was considerably coached rather than spontaneous.

Neither conceded anything and the section ended without resolution. Could you go through the exchange move by move — (1) through (4) — and say who has the better of each specific point, including whether the empirical and interpretive claims each of them leans on actually hold up, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also be curious what you think both Callum and Ines are missing that bears on whether the situationist evidence is really a problem for virtue ethics.
