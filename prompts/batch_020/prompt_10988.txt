I spent about thirty-five years doing experimental particle physics before I retired, and a few months ago I fell into a little philosophy reading group with some old colleagues, mostly to keep the brain from rusting. Last month we worked through Wigner's 1960 essay on the "unreasonable effectiveness" of mathematics, and it hit me harder than I expected going in. The example that kept nagging at me afterward was Dirac, who wrote down his equation in '28 basically just because he wanted something linear and relativistically consistent, and got these embarrassing negative-energy solutions out of it that he hadn't asked for -- and a few years later there's an actual positron showing up in Anderson's cloud chamber. That always struck me as borderline spooky, like the math got there before the physics did.

Frank, the resident hardnosed empiricist in our group, just shrugs the whole thing off. His line is that we only remember the equations that hit and quietly bury the ones that don't (he'd dug up some old rebuttal to Wigner from a guy named Hamming making basically that same point back in 1980), that mathematics was abstracted from the physical world to begin with so it's no shock it fits back onto it, and that if the fit were really as deep as Wigner makes out it should work just as well in biology or economics as it does in particle physics -- which it very definately does not. We went round and round over coffee for a good hour and got precisely nowhere, which I gather is how these arguements usually go.

So here's my actual request: could you write me a dialogue between two people who hold these positions competently -- say a mathematical Platonist (or whatever the correct technical label is for "the effectiveness points to something real and mind-independent, not just a convenient human tool") going up against a selection-effects skeptic like Frank -- actually arguing it out rather than trading slogans past each other. I want each of them forced to deal with the other's strongest case instead of dodging it: the Platonist has to say something real about why this supposedly deep effectiveness goes missing in biology and economics, and the skeptic has to actually engage with the Dirac/positron case rather than filing it under coincidence, since that's the one that got under my skin in the first place. Set it as two old colleagues going at it after a seminar or something along those lines, whatever feels natural. Neither one should come off as the obvious loser -- I genuinely don't know which way I lean on this, so I'd like to come away from reading it actually having been pushed somewhere.
