I'm a novelist working on a novel that alternates between the perspectives of two infantrymen on opposite sides of a war between neighboring states: one defending his homeland against invasion, the other conscripted into the invading army under threat of imprisonment. While researching just war theory for the book, I've become interested in the tension between the traditional "moral equality of combatants" thesis associated with Walzer and the revisionist challenge to it developed by McMahan and others, which denies that combatants fighting for an unjust cause have the same permissions as those fighting for a just one, while still allowing that many such combatants are excused rather than culpable for what they do. Could you set out the most important implications of this revisionist position for how I write the invading soldier's moral psychology and self-justifications, so that his situation comes across as genuinely different from the defender's, without either collapsing into straightforward moral equivalence or turning him into a cartoon villain?
