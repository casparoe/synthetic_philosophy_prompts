Prepping for my epistemology seminar this week and im stuck on how two bits of On Certainty fit together, figured I'd ask you before I have to face 15 grad students tomorrow and embarass myself. Here's the passage:

"341. That is to say, the questions that we raise and our doubts depend on the fact that some propositions are exempt from doubt, are as it were like hinges on which those turn."

and then seventeen remarks later:

"358. Now I would like to regard this certainty, not as something akin to hastiness or superficiality, but as a form of life. (That is very badly expressed and probably badly thought as well.)
359. But that means I want to conceive it as something that lies beyond being justified or unjustified; as it were, as something animal."

In 341 the hinges still sound like propositions to me — things that could in principle be doubted or justified, they just as a matter of fact never get questioned. But 358-9 seem to want something stronger: that this "certainty" isn't even the kind of thing that can be justified or unjustified, i.e. maybe not propositional at all, more like a brute animal reflex. Those feel like two different pictures of what a hinge is to me. Am I overreading the tension (he does call 358 "badly thought" himself), or is this an actual fault line in the text that people who write on hinge epistemology have to pick a side on? And if it's the latter, which reading do you find more defensible?
