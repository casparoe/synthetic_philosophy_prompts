I'm a PhD student in philosophy, and this year I'm the graduate-student representative on my university's Academic Integrity Board, which adjudicates alleged violations of the honor code. I've been asked to draft the recommendation memo for a case the Board is finding surprisingly hard to resolve, and the timing is fitting: I'm also taking a seminar in comparative ethics this term, and we just spent two weeks on Confucian role ethics as Roger Ames and Henry Rosemont develop it, including the Analects passage (13.18) in which Confucius rejects the "uprightness" of a son who reports his father for stealing a sheep, and Mencius's thought experiment about what the sage-king Shun should do if his own father, Gusou, were guilty of murder. The case before the Board: a graduate teaching assistant in the biology department learned, purely through a family conversation, that his younger sister — an undergraduate chemistry major with no connection to any course he teaches or grades — had fabricated data in a lab report. Our code requires "any member of the university community who becomes aware of a suspected violation to report it to the Board within ten business days"; instead, he handled the matter within the family, pressing her to disclose the fabrication to her professor herself and redo the assignment for a reduced grade. The fabrication only reached the Board months later, via a routine data-integrity audit, which is how we learned that the TA had known all along and never filed a report. Roughly half the Board wants to sanction him for the reporting failure on straightforwardly impartialist grounds — that a tacit family exception would hollow out the rule and invite exactly the favoritism it exists to prevent — and I don't think that worry is silly, even though working through the role-ethics reading has left me more sympathetic to him than I expected to be.

Could you draft the memo I'll circulate before the Board meets next week? It should reach one concrete recommendation on what happens to the TA — sanction, no sanction, or something in between — and a second, forward-looking recommendation on whether the reporting rule itself needs an explicit exception, an interpretive footnote, or nothing at all. I'd like the reasoning to genuinely engage what's distinctive about role ethics — the idea that the TA's obligations here are constituted by his relation to his sister rather than derived from some prior, relation-independent self answering to a universal rule — rather than gesturing vaguely at "family loyalty can matter," and I'd like the impartialist objection answered on its merits rather than conceded and quietly set aside.
