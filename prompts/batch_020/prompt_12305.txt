I spent thirty years as an experimental physicist, and like most of my generation I came to Bayesian methods through E. T. Jaynes — his maximum-entropy techniques were how we coaxed clean spectra out of noisy data long before anyone called it machine learning. Since retiring I have joined a philosophy reading group at the local university, mostly to be humbled by people forty years younger than me, and we recently spent two sessions on Jaynes's treatment of Bertrand's paradox in *Probability Theory: The Logic of Science*. The paradox is simple to state: a chord is drawn "at random" across a circle, and one asks for the probability that it is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. Depending on which quantity you choose to distribute uniformly — the two endpoints, the midpoint, or the perpendicular distance from the center — you get three different, perfectly rigorous answers: 1/3, 1/4, or 1/2.

Jaynes's way out is to insist that only one of these procedures yields a distribution invariant under the transformations the problem's own statement leaves unspecified — translations, rotations, and rescalings of the circle — and he even reports a physical straw-tossing experiment whose measured frequency lands on that procedure's answer of 1/2. I find this genuinely persuasive as physics, but as epistemology I keep tripping over the same objection: deciding that those particular symmetries are the relevant ones already presupposes something about the physical process that generates the chord, which is exactly what "complete ignorance" is not supposed to hand you for free. One member of our group, an unreconstructed subjectivist, put it to the rest of us that the whole apparatus of invariance groups is just the principle of indifference wearing a lab coat.

I would like to see this fought out properly rather than adjudicated for me. Please write a dialogue, staged at a seminar-room whiteboard still covered in the three classic diagrams, between a convinced Jaynesian objective Bayesian and a thoroughgoing subjectivist in the de Finetti mold who denies that an "objective" probability for this kind of event exists prior to some specified sampling procedure. Give the Jaynesian a real answer to the charge that the choice of invariance group is itself smuggled-in information, rather than letting the straw experiment do all the work for her; and make the subjectivist reckon honestly with why the invariant answer keeps surviving contact with the physical world, rather than simply repeating that indifference is arbitrary. I don't want either of them to be a pushover for the other — let the exchange end in genuine, sharpened disagreement rather than in a concession.
