I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science, and this semester's formal epistemology seminar has spent several sessions on the problem of old evidence: the objection, pressed by Clark Glymour in *Theory and Evidence* against Bayesian confirmation theory, that the ratio formula P(H|E) = P(E|H)P(H)/P(E) makes it impossible for evidence E that a scientist already accepts with certainty (P(E) = 1) to confirm any hypothesis H, since conditionalizing on something already certain leaves every credence exactly where it was. This seems to falsify the actual history of science: when Einstein showed in November 1915 that general relativity entails the correct anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion (roughly 43 arcseconds per century, known and unexplained since Le Verrier's calculations in 1859), essentially everyone, Einstein included, treated this as powerful confirmation of the theory, even though the perihelion data itself was decades old and already certain. Our professor mentioned, without elaborating, that she considers every standard fix for this puzzle unsatisfying in its own way, and that remark has stuck with me.

Two of the standard Bayesian rescues for this puzzle make, as far as I can tell, quite different trade-offs, and I'd like your help thinking them through carefully. The Garber-Jeffrey-Niiniluoto (GJN) strategy keeps the scientist's actual credence in E pinned at 1 but denies that she is logically omniscient: what really confirms general relativity, on this view, is not the perihelion data itself but her newly acquired knowledge of the logical fact that the theory entails that data, and conditionalizing on this discovery can move her credence in the theory even while her credence in the observation never budges. The counterfactual strategy associated with Colin Howson instead preserves logical omniscience and relocates the confirmation in a counterfactual quantity: the credence the scientist would have assigned to the perihelion anomaly had she not already known it, computed from the rest of her background beliefs, with this merely hypothetical credence -- rather than her actual one -- entering the confirmation calculation. Could you reconstruct both solutions precisely enough to make clear exactly what each one gives up, and then say which offers the more defensible account of why the Mercury case really did constitute strong evidence for general relativity? Please reach and defend an actual verdict rather than simply cataloguing considerations on both sides, and note whether the case exposes a cost that the two solutions end up sharing regardless of which one you prefer.
