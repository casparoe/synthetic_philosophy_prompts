As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I'm drafting a piece on the philosophy of mathematics, and I keep encountering a shorthand according to which "intuitionistic logic" and "mathematical constructivism" are effectively synonymous, as though rejecting the law of excluded middle were all it takes to count as a constructivist. Before repeating that shorthand in print, I'd like to pressure-test it. Please compare intuitionistic logic with mathematical constructivism as a broader program, addressing in particular: (a) how Brouwer's own intuitionism went beyond the bare logic into further commitments — choice sequences, the continuity principle, the fan theorem — that yield results genuinely inconsistent with classical mathematics, whereas Errett Bishop's constructive analysis was deliberately built as a "common core" valid under classical, recursive, and intuitionistic readings alike; and (b) whether predicativist or finitist restrictions on existence proofs deserve the label "constructivist" even though they need not reject excluded middle at all. On balance, is intuitionistic logic constructivism's common denominator, one historically contingent implementation of it among several, or a formal tool that can be adopted for reasons that have nothing to do with constructivist philosophy of mathematics?
