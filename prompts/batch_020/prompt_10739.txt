My younger sister and I inherited our father's lake house about a year and a half ago, and we still have not settled who is to keep it. I would like to buy out her share; she would rather take the cash and let the property pass to me outright. The difficulty is that neither of us wants to be the first to reveal how much the house is really worth to us: if I admitted that I would pay almost anything to keep it, she would naturally ask for more than I can afford, and if she admitted that she urgently needs the money, I would offer her less than I otherwise might. So for months we have exchanged guarded, not entirely honest offers through a mediator, paying his hourly fee throughout, while the house itself sits empty and continues to accrue property taxes.

I described all this over dinner last week to a friend who works in consulting and has a background in decision theory, and her response led to a long and rather heated argument. She claimed that even though neither my sister nor I is willing to disclose honestly what the house is worth to us, or what our finances actually look like, it should still be possible to design a rule that we both commit to in advance — one that requires neither of us to reveal anything, nor to trust that the other is being truthful about anything — and that is nevertheless guaranteed to leave each of us at least as well off as our fallback option of simply selling the house and splitting the proceeds evenly, while leaving room to do better than that if our two valuations really do turn out to differ substantially. I could not see how this was possible: either the rule makes use of information about our actual positions, in which case whichever of us is willing to misrepresent that information has an incentive to exploit it, or it makes no use of that information at all, in which case it seemed to me that it could never do any better than simply splitting things down the middle. My friend maintained that this was a real, well-studied possibility rather than a sleight of hand, but she was unable to fully convince me before we had to leave the restaurant.

I would like to know which of us was actually right, not least because my sister and I are due back at the mediator's office next week. Is there a genuine, non-trivial sense in which we could reach an arrangement that is guaranteed never to leave either of us worse off than an even split, yet could still sometimes improve on it, without either of us ever having to trust the other's honesty or reveal our hand? Or was my instinct correct that "requires no trust whatsoever" and "can still improve on the fair default" are simply incompatible demands to place on the same arrangement? If my friend is onto something real, I would also be curious what a workable version of such an arrangement might actually look like for two people in our position.
