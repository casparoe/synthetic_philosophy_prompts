I work in formal verification, where branching-time logics such as CTL* are the basic tool of the trade: a formula's truth depends on quantifying over a tree of possible future execution paths. I only recently discovered that this apparatus descends from Arthur Prior's tense logic, developed largely to make sense of Aristotle's sea battle problem in De Interpretatione 9, and I now find it hard to use the formalism without wondering which side of an old metaphysical dispute it has quietly built in. Prior offered two rival semantics for future-tense statements in a branching model, which he called Ockhamist and Peircean, and the debate was later complicated by Thomason's supervaluationist treatment and by the contested "thin red line" proposal. Could you give me a properly technical explanation -- not the usual "the future is open" hand-waving -- of what actually separates these positions: why Ockhamism has to relativize truth to a history rather than a bare moment, why Peirceanism avoids that move only by treating nearly every future contingent as false rather than truth-valueless, and why supervaluationism is supposed to count as a genuinely distinct third option rather than Peirceanism wearing a different label for the gaps? I'd also like to know exactly what critics find incoherent about the thin red line, since it seems to be the move every later proposal is trying to avoid making.
