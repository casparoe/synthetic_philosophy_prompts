I'm a software engineer. The one philosophy class I ever took in college happened to be the class where I met the woman who is now my oldest friend. I remember almost none of the material, but I remember her: she stuck with it all the way through a philosophy PhD and now teaches it, while I went into software instead. Next month I've been asked to give the toast at her wedding.

As far as I can tell, she didn't just study Richard Rorty's pragmatism, she organized her outlook on life around it: the idea that language and theories aren't mirrors reflecting some pre-existing reality, but tools a community builds and revises for getting by together, and that things like truth and meaning are made this way rather than discovered as facts already sitting out there waiting for us. She has some phrase for the specific words a person ends up using to describe what matters most to them (I think she calls it a "final vocabulary"), and her point is that a vocabulary like that is something you build and can always revise, not something you find. Half the guests will be people from her department, including a colleague she has spent a decade happily arguing with about whether truth is really just correspondence to how things are; the other half, including her fiancé's family, have never heard any of this discussed in these terms. She has told me, half joking and half not, that she will visibly wince if my toast leans on the usual wedding material about soulmates, fate, or the two of them being "meant for" each other, since that's more or less exactly the picture of truth as discovery that her whole career has been an argument against.

I want to get this right for her, but the philosophy is honestly a bit beyond me, and I don't trust myself to thread this needle alone. Could you write that toast for me? I would like something that runs about four or five minutes read aloud, is genuinely warm and a little funny rather than a philosophy lecture, and gets her actual view right enough that her colleagues in the room recognize it and appreciate a nod or two aimed at them, while still giving her family and new in-laws something moving they can follow with no background at all. What I most want is a toast that finds its own honest, positive way of describing what she and her fiancé have built together, one that would actually satisfy her on her own terms, rather than a version that just carefully tiptoes around the words she doesn't like.
