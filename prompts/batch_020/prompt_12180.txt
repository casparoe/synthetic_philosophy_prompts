I'm taking a seminar this term on Hegel's political philosophy, and we've just reached the part of the *Elements of the Philosophy of Right* dealing with what he calls the state's "external sovereignty" — the section where he discusses war. For our next response paper we have to work through §324 together with its Addition, and while I can parse the individual sentences, I'm having real trouble seeing how the argument actually holds together as an argument rather than just a string of assertions. Here is the passage as it appears in our course reader:

"§324. This determination [i.e., that the state, as an individual, stands in relation to other states, and so possesses in them its own actual, if negative, relation to itself] contains within it the further ethical moment that the finitude of property and of life — which within civil society counts as the fixed and secured basis of personality, as something simply to be preserved — is itself posited as what it is in truth, namely as ideal, standing under a claim higher than itself. For this reason war is not to be regarded as an absolute evil, or as a merely external contingency whose ground is in turn merely contingent, whether this be sought in the passions of rulers or of peoples, in injustice, and so on — in short, in something that simply ought not to be; rather, it lies in the very nature of finite things that their contingency should fall due and show itself as fate. *Addition.* In peace, civil life expands ever further in all directions; its several spheres wall themselves in and settle into fixed habit, and in time human beings become stuck fast within them, their particular characters growing ever more rigid and, eventually, ossified. But the health of a body depends on the unity of its members being preserved through their motion, and the same holds of the ethical health of nations. Just as the movement of the winds preserves the sea from the stagnation that a lasting calm would produce, so a lasting — or indeed 'perpetual' — peace would produce a like corruption among nations."

What I can't quite pin down is what kind of claim Hegel is making here. My first instinct was to read this as the fairly crude thought that conflict is good for a society because it toughens people up and keeps them from getting complacent, but that reading doesn't seem to earn its keep against words like "finitude," "contingency," and "ideal," which look like they're importing technical weight from earlier in his system rather than functioning as ordinary vocabulary — and presumably "ethical" here is doing the specific work of *Sittlichkeit* rather than meaning "morally good" in some loose sense. I'm also unsure how the sacrifice of "property and life" is supposed to connect back to his account of civil society and the "system of needs," where people relate to each other largely as self-interested, separate individuals: is the idea that this sacrifice is precisely what binds a person to the state as a universal, in a way ordinary peaceful civil existence never could on its own? And is Hegel making a normative claim (that war is good, or that a state which never fights is somehow deficient), or something closer to a structural claim about what must remain true of any system of distinct, sovereign states, independent of whether a given war is desirable? I gather that not much later in this same section he explicitly rejects Kant's proposal for a league of states securing perpetual peace, and I suspect this passage is meant to do the setup work for that rejection, but I can't yet see the connecting logic. Could you help me work through what's actually going on here, sentence by sentence if necessary, rather than handing me the one-line "Hegel thought war preserves the ethical health of nations" gloss I keep running into?
