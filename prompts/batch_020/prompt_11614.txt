For the novel I'm currently drafting, I want a pivotal scene to hinge on a genuine philosophical difficulty rather than a comforting platitude about good intentions. Kant held that an act has moral worth only when it is performed from the motive of duty, and not merely in conformity with it. Nomy Arpaly and others have pushed back against this with the case of Huckleberry Finn: Huck's sympathy for Jim overrides his sincere, if badly mistaken, conviction that helping an enslaved man escape is theft and a sin, and Arpaly argues that his action nonetheless has moral worth, because he is genuinely responsive to Jim's humanity whatever his explicit principles say.

I need a wholly different scene with the same underlying structure — not Huck Finn — in which my character does the right thing for what are clearly the right-making reasons, while sincerely, if wrongly, believing that what she's doing is forbidden. Once you've built the scene, tell me whether it really vindicates Arpaly's more permissive standard of moral worth, or whether it also exposes a way her account could mistake mere personal loyalty for genuine moral responsiveness.
