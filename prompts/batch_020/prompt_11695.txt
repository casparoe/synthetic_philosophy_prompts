I'm a novelist working on a book set partly at Oxford in 1956, around the real controversy over the university's decision to award Harry Truman an honorary degree. The philosopher Elizabeth Anscombe opposed it in a pamphlet, "Mr. Truman's Degree," that called him a mass murderer for ordering the bombing of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. Her opposition failed and Truman received the degree anyway, but I want a scene in which two of my fictional dons refight that argument at high table, and I'm determined that neither of them comes off as a strawman for the other. To test the material, I described the historical dispute at a dinner party, and two friends of mine who have nothing to do with the book ended up having almost exactly the argument I'm trying to dramatize -- in earnest, and without resolving it.

(1) Nathan opened by saying that Anscombe had simply been right: Truman chose to destroy two cities, overwhelmingly full of civilians, as his actual means of forcing Japan's surrender rather than as a side effect of hitting some legitimate target nearby, and no downstream benefit, however real, can convert intentionally killing the innocent into anything other than murder. (2) Ingrid answered that this proves too much: the standard postwar estimates of the planned invasion of the Japanese home islands put its likely toll, Japanese and American, well above the combined death toll at Hiroshima and Nagasaki, so refusing to drop the bombs would foreseeably have killed more innocent people, not fewer -- and a moral theory that tells you to prefer the option that predictably kills more people is a theory you should start doubting, not a stick to beat Truman with. (3) Nathan replied that this is exactly the move Anscombe spends the pamphlet refusing to allow: it treats the dead as fungible units to be weighed against a hypothetical chosen after the fact, when the real distinction is between killing a city's residents as your means of extracting surrender and merely foreseeing that civilians will die as the side effect of hitting a legitimate target -- collapse that distinction and there is no atrocity that couldn't be laundered by inventing a sufficiently dreadful counterfactual. (4) Ingrid's rejoinder was that Hiroshima did contain a genuine army headquarters and troop concentrations, so Truman's intention could just as well be described as "breaking Japan's remaining capacity and will to resist," an intention Anscombe would presumably tolerate if pursued by, say, bombing a munitions factory with civilian workers nearby, with the residents' deaths a horribly foreseen rather than strictly intended cost; and if the verdict turns entirely on which of these two descriptions of Truman's state of mind is correct, she wasn't sure the doctrine of double effect was doing any real work, as opposed to dressing up whichever conclusion you'd already reached.

Neither would give any ground, and I've been turning the exchange over ever since, partly for my own sake and partly because I don't want my two dons to be any less sharp than Nathan and Ingrid actually were. Could you go through their four moves individually and adjudicate them: who has the better of it at each step, and what, if anything, does each of them miss?
