In his correspondence with Samuel Clarke, Leibniz argued against Newton's absolute space and time with what is now called the shift argument: if the entire material universe had been created a few feet to the left of where it actually sits, or several years earlier than it actually was, nothing about the result would be observably different, so treating the shifted version as a genuinely distinct possibility posits a difference with no sufficient reason behind it. Two centuries later, Einstein's hole argument -- revived by philosophers of physics in the late 1980s -- raises a strikingly similar worry from inside general relativity itself. If the points of the spacetime manifold have identities of their own, independent of the metric field, then a diffeomorphism that acts as the identity outside some small region but smoothly redistributes the metric inside it yields a distinct, equally valid solution of Einstein's equations, one that agrees with the original everywhere outside the region yet differs within it. Since nothing in the physics itself picks out one solution as the real one, this commits the substantivalist to a radical indeterminism that looks entirely spurious. Suppose Leibniz were presented with the argument in full technical detail, along with its standard escape routes (denying that diffeomorphic models represent distinct physical possibilities, or retreating to a "sophisticated" substantivalism that strips spacetime points of any primitive identity). Would he simply claim posthumous vindication for his relationalism, or would his other commitments complicate that victory lap -- in particular, a principle of sufficient reason that leaves him little room to shrug off physical indeterminism as a brute fact, and his considered view that space and time are ultimately phenomenal, grounded in non-spatial monads rather than real relations among bodies? Reconstruct the verdict you think Leibniz would actually reach, and defend it.
