My school has an Effective Altruism club that I joined this year, largely because I liked the idea of arguing about ethics with snacks provided, and a few weeks ago we read a short piece on something called "moral trade": the idea that two people who disagree about ethics can still strike a deal that leaves each of them better off by their own lights, the way a committed vegetarian might pay a meat-eating friend to cut back on meat even though the friend was never persuaded that eating meat is wrong. When I brought it up at lunch, most of my friends said it just sounded like paying someone to behave, which I don't think is quite right, although I had a hard time explaining why.

That question turned out to matter sooner than I expected. My older sister is getting married in three weeks and has asked me to say a few words at the reception, where roughly half the guests will be friends of hers from a campus ethics reading group who will recognize the term the moment I use it, and the other half, including both sets of grandparents, have never heard it and just want a nice story about two people who love each other. As it happens, she and her fiancé have basically been practicing moral trade for their entire relationship without ever naming it: she is a committed environmentalist who refuses to fly, and he travels constantly for work and goes deer hunting with his father every November, and over the years they have worked out arrangements like him funding the carbon offsets for every work trip in exchange for her coming along, rifle in hand, on opening weekend. I would like the toast to use that idea as its actual backbone rather than as a passing joke: it should explain, in terms simple enough for the grandparents but precise enough that the reading-group half does not wince, why what my sister and her fiancé have built is different both from simply agreeing to disagree and from one of them quietly giving in, and why it should not collapse into "paying someone to be a little less wrong." My sister's one condition is that it cannot read like a club presentation: it has to work first as a toast about two particular people, no more than three minutes long, warm and a little funny, with the philosophy doing real work without ever announcing itself. Could you write that toast for me?
