I serve as the resident philosopher on my university medical center's Institutional Review Board, and a protocol has come before us that I would like help thinking through in advance of Thursday's vote. A psychiatry team is seeking approval for a psilocybin-assisted therapy trial for treatment-resistant depression. Their consent form discloses the standard medical risks and adds, accurately enough, that the drug session "may produce profound and lasting changes in mood, personality, outlook, and sense of self," and asks participants to consent to this in advance. A bioethicist on the board considers the form adequate as written, on the grounds that it discloses everything there is to disclose.

I have raised an objection I have taught for years in my decision theory seminar but never seen collide with an actual protocol before: if, as L.A. Paul argues, some experiences are epistemically transformative -- teaching you something about their own value that you had no way of knowing beforehand -- and often personally transformative besides, reshaping the very preferences by which you would later judge whether they were worth having, then no amount of additional disclosure closes the gap, because the participant is being asked to authorize an outcome she cannot yet access, on behalf of preferences she may not keep. Two other members reply that this objection, taken seriously, would also unravel consent for chemotherapy, bariatric surgery, and deep-brain stimulation for Parkinson's disease, all of which are already known to reshape a person's values and sometimes personality in much the same way, and for which we require nothing beyond the disclosure already on offer.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before the meeting. It should recommend, concretely, whether we approve the consent process as drafted, require specific revisions to it, or decline to approve the protocol until the process is redesigned, and it should say what the board ought to do the next time a protocol raises the same issue, not just this one. I want the "proves too much" objection engaged with directly rather than set aside: either say what, if anything, distinguishes this case from chemotherapy, bariatric surgery, or deep-brain stimulation in a way that should matter for consent, or concede that nothing does, and follow that concession through to what it then implies for how the board treats those other protocols.
