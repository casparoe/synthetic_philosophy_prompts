So I've been chasing something down for a piece on paradoxes of infinity and decision theory, and I keep getting stuck on this thing called the Pasadena paradox, which a couple of philosophers apparently built specifically to be a nastier cousin of the St Petersburg paradox. Both are these coin-toss games where you flip till you get heads and the payout balloons with how many flips it takes, but from what I can tell the St Petersburg payout is just straightforwardly infinite in expectation, wheras the Pasadena version alternates the sign of the payout so the sum only "conditionally" converges -- and because of that you can supposedly reshuffle the very same possible outcomes and get the expectation to equal any number you like, or diverge, or refuse to settle at all. A friend who did a PhD in this stuff described it to me over dinner and now I can't let it go.

What I haven't been able to get straight is why philosophers treat "the expectation is undefined" as such a categorically bigger problem than "the expectation is infinite" -- isn't infinite already about as broken as a decision rule can get? Could you actually compare the two paradoxes properly for me: what's doing the damage in Pasadena that isn't already present in St Petersburg, in terms of the actual convergence/rearrangement math, not just the vibe of 'one is infinite and one is undefined, spooky.' And then give me your own judgment -- I've seen vague references to fixes involving some kind of "relative" or comparative notion of expectation, or a distinction between "weak" and "strong" expectations -- do any of those actually dissolve the problem Pasadena raises, or are they just clever-sounding ways of declining to answer it?
