I'm a junior at my high school and was elected as the student representative on our district's Educational Technology Advisory Committee, a joint parent-teacher-administrator-student body that makes recommendations to the school board. Since September the district has been piloting an "early warning" system that assigns every student a weekly risk score for being off track to graduate on time, based on attendance, grades, and behavior referrals, so that counselors know which students to check in with first. The committee has to vote in three weeks on what to recommend to the board, and our last two meetings have stalled on a fairness dispute that I don't think either side will simply talk itself out of.

Parent representatives, citing data the district itself released, point out that among students who qualify for free or reduced-price lunch and who never actually went on to struggle that year, the system flagged them as "high risk" far more often than it flagged other students who were equally fine — to them, that gap in false alarms shows the tool is biased against lower-income students and shouldn't be adopted as is. The vendor and most of the teachers counter that the tool is nonetheless accurate exactly where it matters: at any given score, say 70 percent, roughly 70 percent of the students who receive that score really do go on to struggle, and this holds up equally well whether or not a student qualifies for free or reduced-price lunch. Both sides have presented their numbers to the committee, and as far as I can tell neither is misrepresenting the data or making an arithmetic error. Out of curiosity, I did some outside reading on my AP Statistics teacher's suggestion, and I now suspect these two complaints might be in genuine tension with each other whenever the two groups don't struggle at exactly the same underlying rate to begin with — but I don't fully understand why that would be, and more importantly, I don't know what it should mean for what our committee actually does.

Could you write the memo I'll circulate to the committee ahead of the vote? I'd like it to recommend one concrete option — adopt, reject, or adopt with specific conditions — along with a timeframe for acting on it, and to explain plainly, in a way both camps could follow, why they may not be able to get everything they are asking for at once. I lean toward not wanting to discard a tool that could genuinely help counselors reach struggling students sooner, but I don't want the memo to wave away the parents' objection as a misunderstanding, since I don't believe it is one. Please also spell out the strongest form of the case against whichever option you end up recommending.
