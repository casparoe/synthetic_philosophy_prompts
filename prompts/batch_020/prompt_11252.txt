Question: In "The Possibility of Parity," Ruth Chang argues against what she calls the Trichotomy Thesis: the view that, with respect to any covering consideration, two items must be related as better than, worse than, or equally good. (a) Reconstruct her Small Improvement Argument against the Trichotomy Thesis, using an example of your choice. (b) Chang maintains that this argument establishes incommensurability but not incomparability, and that this is exactly where the notion of two items being "on a par" does its work. Explain what is at stake in this distinction. (c) On Chang's view, what is the significance of "hard choices" — cases in which options are on a par — for practical reason and human agency?

Student's answer:

(a) The Trichotomy Thesis says that whenever two things can be evaluated at all, one must be better, one must be worse, or the two must be exactly equal. Chang's Small Improvement Argument attacks this using cases like Raz's choice between becoming a lawyer and becoming a clarinettist: neither career seems better than the other. Chang then asks us to imagine "improving" the legal career slightly, say by adding a small pay increase, to get a new option, Lawyer+. If Lawyer+ now strikes us as clearly the better option overall, that shows the original two careers could not have been exactly equal — a real tie would not have been so easy to break with such a small change. Since they also weren't better or worse than each other to begin with, the only conclusion left is that they must have been on a par.

(b) It's important to see that "incommensurable" and "incomparable" mean the same thing for Chang: two items are incommensurable, i.e. incomparable, exactly when none of the three trichotomous relations holds between them. Parity is Chang's word for the rough, imprecise kind of near-equality that two items can have once we accept that they are incomparable in this strict, cardinal sense; it's what allows us to say that, even though there's no precise fact about which career is better, choosing either the law or the clarinet is still a reasonable thing to do, rather than a mistake.

(c) Chang thinks that in a genuinely hard choice — one where the options are on a par — reasons alone cannot dictate an answer, so we are free to commit to one of the options and, by that very act of commitment, make it the case that this is the option we now have most reason to pursue. On her view this is what makes us distinctively rational agents: rather than simply being pushed around by antecedently given reasons, in hard choices we get to author our own values and commitments. This is quite different from thinking that such choices are arbitrary, since after committing we have created a real reason to stick with our choice going forward.
