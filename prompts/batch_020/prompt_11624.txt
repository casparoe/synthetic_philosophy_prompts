I was arguing about this with a friend who's doing a DPhil in the ethics of war, and it sent me down a bit of a rabbit hole with Anscombe's old pamphlet "Mr Truman's Degree" -- the one she wrote in 1956 after Oxford voted to give Truman an honorary degree and she was one of the only ones who voted "non placet" (the old Oxford "no") over it. What I like about her argument is that its not the usual "do the numbers add up" utilitarian tally everyone does with Hiroshima -- its this much sharper, narrower claim.

Her line is basically: choosing to kill innocent people as a means to some end is murder, full stop, however good the end turns out to be, and that's a totally different thing from bombing a munitions factory or a naval dockyard as carefully as you can, even knowing as a statistical certainty that civilians nearby will die (she's explicit she's not a pacifist -- that second kind of killing is fine by her). Hiroshima and Nagasaki, on her view, were aimed at the civilian population itself, to terrorize Japan into surrendering -- she makes a lot of the fact that the second bomb fell only three days after the first with no fresh ultimatum in between. So for her, "it ended the war faster and saved lives overall" just can't convert that into something other than murder. She's also got this extra move where she says the whole "bomb vs. a much bloodier land invasion" comparison is rigged, because the only reason invasion was even the alternative on the table is that the Allies had separately committed to demanding unconditional surrender, which she thought was its own bit of folly -- so the consequentialist case is smuggling in an earlier, avoidable policy decision as though it were just a fixed fact about the world.

Could you write me a dialogue between two Oxford philosophers around the time of that vote -- one defending Anscombe's actual argument pretty much as she lays it out, the other a committed consequentialist who thinks Truman's decision was justified and thinks the whole intending/merely-foreseeing distinction is basically a dodge? Neither one should be a pushover. The consequentialist needs to press hard on things like: how would anyone even verify what Truman "really" intended versus merely foresaw, and why should one and the same physical act count as murder or not depending on a fact about someone's head that's this hard to pin down. And the Anscombean shouldn't just restate the doctrine -- they need to actually answer that, and also deal with the consequentialist turning her own unconditional-surrender point back on her ("fine, that demand was unjust too, but Truman inherited that situation in August '45 -- he didn't get to relitigate it"). I want genuine back-and-forth where each one is responding to what the other just said, not two people taking turns giving speeches.
