I'm taking a graduate seminar on theories of well-being, and this week's discussion of Nozick's experience machine turned into a genuine disagreement between two other students, Nadia and Owen, that neither of them managed to resolve. For context: the thought experiment asks whether you would plug into a machine that could give you, for the rest of your life, any sequence of experiences you wanted, indistinguishable from the real thing, and Nozick takes the fact that almost no one says yes as evidence against hedonism about well-being. I only listened and would rather not referee it myself, so I'm hoping you can go through what they actually said.

(1) Nadia opened by arguing that the thought experiment refutes hedonism: if pleasant experience were all that mattered to a good life, everyone should want to plug in, since the machine can guarantee more and better experiences than real life ever will; that almost no one is willing to do so shows we also care about actually doing things, about actually being a certain kind of person, and about staying in contact with a reality we didn't simply manufacture for ourselves. (2) Owen answered that this shows less than Nadia thinks, since refusing to plug in might just be ordinary status quo bias rather than a verdict about what's valuable: people are generally reluctant to make large, irreversible changes regardless of content, and the thought experiment stacks the deck by asking someone to give up an entire life they already have. He suspected that if you instead asked people to choose, in advance and with no attachment to either outcome, between a machine-life and a real one for someone who doesn't yet exist, the preference for reality would soften considerably. (3) Nadia granted that some status quo bias was probably in play, but didn't think it did the work Owen wanted from it: even in that depersonalized, choose-in-advance version, she said, most people still pick the non-machine life, so something besides mere inertia must be driving the verdict; and in any case, bias and a substantive preference for reality could both be operating at once, so establishing the first doesn't rule out the second. (4) Owen shifted ground: granting all of that, he said, a hedonist isn't forced to treat every pleasant sensation as equally valuable regardless of its source. On something close to what's called attitudinal hedonism, how valuable a pleasure is depends partly on whether what you're pleased about is actually true -- pleasure taken in a real achievement counts for more than pleasure taken in an indistinguishable illusion, even though the two feel identical from the inside. On this view hedonism survives intact; the machine merely cannot deliver the quantity of value its pleasures seem to promise. (5) Nadia's reply was that this rescues the word but not the substance: if the truth of what a pleasure is about affects how valuable that pleasure is, then truth is doing evaluative work of its own, not reducible to or explained by the pleasure itself -- and crediting some independent factor like that is exactly what it means to give up hedonism, not defend it.

Could you work through the exchange move by move, saying who has the stronger case at each numbered step and flagging anywhere a move is only partly right? I'm especially interested in whether Owen's appeal to attitudinal hedonism in (4) actually saves hedonism from the thought experiment or just relocates the disagreement, and whether there's a consideration -- about Nozick's argument or about hedonism generally -- that both Nadia and Owen overlook and that would change the shape of the exchange. Please close with a brief overall verdict on whose position comes out ahead once all five moves are weighed together.
