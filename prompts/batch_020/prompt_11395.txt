So I've spent the last year or so falling down various philosophy rabbit holes after forty-odd years doing bench science, and the latest one has genuinely thrown me. I always knew Gödel as "the incompleteness guy," a towering rigorous mind, but I recently learned he spent a chunk of 1961 writing long letters to his elderly mother, trying to rationally convince her that they would see each other again after death.

As I understand it, his argument goes roughly like this: science itself presupposes that the world is rational and orderly rather than arbitrary -- basically the principle that everything has a sufficient reason, the same idea behind assuming everything has a cause. If you grant that the world really is rationally organized in that deep sense, then it makes no sense for it to build something as elaborate as a human being, loaded with capacity for growth and relationships, only to let nearly all of that potential go permanently to waste at death, like pouring a houses foundation and then abandoning the whole project forever. A properly rational world basically has to include some further existence where all that unfinished business gets completed. He also argued his own incompleteness results told against materialism and against minds being reducible to brains acting as finite machines, which he took to knock out the obvious objection that a ruined brain must mean a ruined mind.

I genuinely can't tell if this is a serious argument that I'm dismissing out of sheer empiricist reflex, or a brilliant man's wishful thinking dressed up in the vocabulary of rigor. Could you write me an essay that actually works through it premise by premise, engages with the strongest version of the reasoning, and then lands on a real verdict about whether it holds up -- not just a "well, reasonable people could disagree" shrug?
