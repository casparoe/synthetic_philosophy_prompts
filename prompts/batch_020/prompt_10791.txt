Here's the student's answer, verbatim: "Walzer's moral equality of combatants thesis holds that ordinary soldiers on both sides of a war are moral equals, regardless of whether their side's cause is just (jus ad bellum) — a soldier fighting for an aggressor state is not a murderer just because his side lacks just cause, so long as he fights within jus in bello (discrimination and proportionality). This is tied to Walzer's independence thesis: jus in bello is logically independent of jus ad bellum, so the rules of conduct apply equally to just and unjust combatants. Walzer's argument rests partly on the idea that soldiers are rarely well-positioned to know which side's cause is actually just, and partly on the fact that soldiers, simply by being combatants, pose a lethal threat to one another and so have each forfeited their right not to be attacked — it's the reciprocal imposition of risk, not the justice of the cause, that licenses killing. McMahan rejects MEC: on his 'responsibility account,' liability to be killed tracks moral responsibility for contributing to an unjust threat, not mere combatant status, so unjust combatants are typically liable to attack while just combatants generally are not. But since most unjust combatants don't know their cause is unjust, McMahan concludes that they aren't actually liable to be killed, only excused for what they do, since their ignorance is usually non-culpable — so an unjust combatant who sticks to discrimination and proportionality still does wrong in killing enemy soldiers, but isnt liable for that wrong, because he acts wrongly yet permissibly."
