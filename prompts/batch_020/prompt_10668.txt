I write about science and the ideas that shape it, and lately much of that work has meant covering the replication crisis in psychology. Partly on the strength of a piece I wrote on p-hacking and the preregistration movement, a psychology department recently invited me onto its newly formed Open Science Committee as the outside, non-specialist member. The committee has just run into its first genuinely hard case, and both factions have asked me to draft the memo, since neither entirely trusts the other to state its opponent's position fairly.

Two years ago the department pre-registered a well-powered Registered Report attempting to replicate a well-known ego-depletion effect, committing in the Stage 1 protocol to a one-tailed t-test at α = .05 with a specified sequential stopping rule. The finished study lands at p = .058 — just outside the pre-registered threshold, which by the letter of the protocol makes it a null result and nothing more. The complication is that one committee member, a Bayesian statistician, reran the same data (a reanalysis that was not itself part of the pre-registered plan) and reports a Bayes factor of roughly 8-to-1 favoring the null. She wants the write-up and any press release to describe the effect as actively disconfirmed rather than merely "not significant," and points out that her method isn't even sensitive to the stopping rule the frequentist plan specified so carefully in advance. Two other members insist we report exactly what was pre-registered and nothing else, arguing that introducing a different statistical framework after seeing the data reopens precisely the interpretive latitude Registered Reports exist to close off, whatever that framework's merits in the abstract.

Could you draft the memo I'll circulate to the department chair before we meet with the full faculty next month? I need a specific, defensible recommendation for how this result should be characterized and reported, ideally a standing rule the committee could apply the next time this situation comes up, and real engagement with the Bayesian statistician's argument rather than a default retreat to "the protocol is the protocol."
