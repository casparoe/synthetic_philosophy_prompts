"In What Is Art?, Tolstoy argues that art is fundamentally a matter of the 'infection' of an audience with feeling, rather than a matter of beauty. Explain (a) why Tolstoy rejects beauty-based definitions of art, (b) his 'infection' criteria for distinguishing real art from counterfeit art, and (c) how he further distinguishes good art from bad art among works that already pass the infection test. And here's the student's answer, cleaned up for spelling but otherwise exactly as submitted:

"Tolstoy begins What Is Art? by working through the history of aesthetics -- Baumgarten, Kant, Hegel, Schopenhauer, and so on -- and rejecting every definition that makes beauty the essence of art. His main complaint is that 'beauty' just collapses into 'whatever gives disinterested pleasure to people of refined taste,' and since there's no non-circular way to say whose taste is the correct one, defining art via beauty just quietly ends up ratifying the taste of whatever class currently has the leisure to cultivate refined pleasure -- in practice, the wealthy. So the beauty theory isn't really a theory at all, just a class prejudice dressed up as philosophy.

In place of beauty, Tolstoy defines art as a means by which one person transmits a feeling they have actually lived through to another person, by external signs (words, sound, color, movement), such that the audience is 'infected' by that same feeling. Whether a work counts as real art, rather than 'counterfeit' art produced for money or through technical imitation of art's outward forms, depends on three things: the individuality of the feeling conveyed, the clarity with which it's conveyed, and the sincerity of the artist -- whether the artist actually felt an inner need to express it. Of the three, sincerity is the one Tolstoy leans on hardest; a technically dazzling piece that doesn't come from real inner necessity will fail to infect no matter how individual or clear it is.

Among works that clear this first bar, Tolstoy thinks we still need a further standard to separate good art from bad, and he ties this to what he calls 'the religious perception of the age.' Because the religious perception of his own age is Christian, Tolstoy's view is that a work has to take explicitly Christian devotional subject matter -- love of God, prayer, the sacred -- to count as good art, which is why he keeps coming back to hymns and icon painting as his models and is so suspicious of the secular novel as a form. Bad art, by contrast, is infectious art whose content is exclusive, divisive, or merely sensual (pride, vanity, patriotism, sexual desire), even if it's genuinely felt and genuinely infects its intended audience. That's the standard behind his infamous verdicts on Wagner, Baudelaire, late Beethoven, and Shakespeare: not that they fail to move anyone, but that what they move people to, and who they can reach, fails the test. He's consistent enough to turn this on himself, writing off War and Peace and Anna Karenina and allowing only a couple of his own late, simple stories into the category of good art.

Collingwood's theory in The Principles of Art is usually filed alongside Tolstoy's as another 'expression theory,' and the family resemblance is real: both throw out beauty and technical skill as the mark of art, and both make emotion, not representation, the essential thing. But if anything, Collingwood's version is the more demanding of the two on the question of communication. For Collingwood, expressing an emotion is a way of coming to know your own mind: you start with a vague, unindividuated disturbance and work it into clear, articulate form through the imaginative activity of composing, and Collingwood thinks that clarification is only really completed once an audience can 're-enact' that same imaginative activity for themselves and recognize the feeling as one they share too. So communication to a real audience turns out to be even more central for Collingwood than for Tolstoy, if anything -- an expressive act nobody else ever understood would be an incomplete, failed act of self-knowledge on Collingwood's view. Which means the two theories actually converge here rather than diverge: a private sketch or tune that the artist never shows to anyone wouldn't be a finished work of art on either account."
