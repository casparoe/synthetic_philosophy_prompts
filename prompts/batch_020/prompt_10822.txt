My uncle Marek died a couple weeks ago and I've been asked to give the eulogy, which is terrifying for reasons beyond the usual ones. He was an actuary, not a philosopher by training, but sometime in his 40s he fell into a Leibniz reading group and never really left it — forty-odd years of genuinely, unironically believing that this is the best of all possible worlds, through two layoffs, a stroke, and this last year's brutal cancer. He was never glib about it, either. He'd get visibly annoyed when people flattened it into "everything happens for a reason" and launch into this whole spiel about compossibility — how God didn't pick this world because every single thing in it is individually good, but because this particular combination of things that can actually coexist together adds up to more good, net, than any rival combination on offer, and that the suffering in it is real suffering, not some disguised blessing in costume. He told my aunt straight out, more than once, that if anyone stood up at his funeral and started talking about him "being in a better place now, watching over us," he wanted them corrected on the spot, gently, because thats not a Leibnizian thought and he found the conflation lazy.

The problem is the room is going to be almost entirely non-philosophers — his kids, his old coworkers from the firm, the neighbors — except for two or three guys from that reading group who will absolutely clock it if I flatten Marek's actual view into either generic inspirational-poster comfort or the Voltaire/Pangloss punchline he complained about for decades. Could you write the eulogy? It needs to genuinely comfort people who've never heard of Leibniz, without saying anything he would've considered sloppy — which I think means it has to deal honestly with why "it happened for a reason" and "he's watching over us now" aren't quite the right kind of comfort on his view, and figure out what the right kind actually is once you take the "best of all possible worlds" thing seriously rather than as a punchline.
