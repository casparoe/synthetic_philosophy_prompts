Been down a bit of a rabbit hole on accuracy-first epistemology lately, starting from old LW posts on calibration and scoring rules and ending up in Jim Joyce's papers on probabilism. His argument is basically that having probabilistically incoherent credences is irrational because they're always 'accuracy-dominated' -- there's some coherent credence function that's guaranteed to score better on accuracy no matter how the world turns out. But to even define 'accuracy' you have to pick a scoring rule (Brier score is the classic choice), and apparently there are other scoring rules that look just as resonable on paper where that dominance result stops holding. Feels like that makes the whole case for probabilism kind of rigged, like the conclusion got smuggled in through the choice of scoring rule rather than actually being forced on you. Can you walk me through how people in this literature try to answer that arbitrariness worry (is there some principled reason to rule out the 'bad' scoring rules?), and give me your honest read on whether probabilism actually survives it, or whether this is a bigger crack in accuracy-first epistemology than its defenders usually let on?
