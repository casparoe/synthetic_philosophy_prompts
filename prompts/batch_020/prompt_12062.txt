At work, our small ML team was recently asked to build a credit-risk scoring model for a lending product, and a design review over this has surfaced a disagreement I haven't been able to resolve since. Our data scientist wants the model to satisfy "equalized odds" (equal false positive and false negative rates across the demographic groups we're required to monitor), our compliance lead insists on "calibration" (a predicted score of 20% should correspond to an actual default rate of about 20% within every group), and our product manager keeps asking, reasonably enough from her perspective, why we can't simply have both of these together with roughly equal approval rates across groups. Digging into this over the weekend, I found that there's apparently a proven result, associated with work by Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan and, separately, by Chouldechova, showing that calibration and equalized error rates cannot both hold whenever the base rate of the outcome differs across groups -- which is exactly our situation, since default rates do differ across the groups in our data.

I can follow the mathematics of why this impossibility holds, but I'm having a much harder time figuring out what to actually do with it, or how to explain it to my team in a way that goes beyond "trade-offs exist, pick one." Could you lay out the most important implications of this kind of impossibility result for a situation like ours? I'd particularly value your take on what the result does and doesn't tell us about whether our data or underlying process is itself unjust, whether there is any principled basis -- as opposed to mere convenience -- for prioritizing one fairness criterion over another for a product like a credit model specifically, and what it implies for how honestly we can present our eventual choice of metric to regulators or to the customers affected by it, given that an equally defensible choice would have treated some of them differently.
