For a graduate seminar on just war theory, my instructor has set us an unusual exercise. We're asked to imagine ourselves as a member of Oxford's Convocation in 1956, in the weeks before the University is due to confer an honorary Doctor of Civil Law on Harry Truman at Encaenia, in recognition of his wartime leadership. Elizabeth Anscombe, then a fellow of Somerville, has just circulated her pamphlet "Mr Truman's Degree," arguing that the University is about to honor a man guilty of mass murder. On her account, the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki did not merely foresee civilian deaths as a side effect of striking some legitimate military target; they chose the destruction of whole civilian populations as the very method by which Japan's surrender was to be procured. Under the doctrine of double effect, this makes the killing intentional rather than merely foreseen, and therefore murder, regardless of how many lives the bombing is thought to have saved overall. Anscombe is circulating a non placet (dissenting) motion, trying to persuade enough members to force a real debate rather than let the degree pass "on the nod," as these things usually do.

I have to write the short opinion I would circulate to fellow members of Convocation before the vote, recommending how we should proceed and why. My instructor has been explicit that two easy outs will not be accepted: quietly noting that the degree did in fact pass comfortably, or taking a generic show of hands on "was Hiroshima justified" that never actually engages Anscombe's intended/foreseen distinction. She also wants the strongest opposing consideration taken seriously rather than just gestured at: namely, that the civilian deaths at Hiroshima and Nagasaki might be redescribed as the foreseen-but-unintended cost of destroying Japan's capacity and will to keep fighting, exactly as we would redescribe the civilian deaths from any conventional bombing campaign, so that Anscombe's charge of murder either has to be extended to most Allied strategic bombing as well, or resisted by a description of the act that a defender of Hiroshima could invoke just as easily. Could you draft this opinion for me? Please give a clear recommendation on the vote, engage Anscombe's argument directly on its own terms rather than dismissing it as legalistic hair-splitting, and give a fair, non-strawmanned statement of whichever side you conclude against: the case for blocking the degree, if you end up recommending we proceed, or the strongest case for proceeding anyway, if you end up recommending we block it.
