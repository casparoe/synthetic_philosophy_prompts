I'm about two-thirds through a novel with a subplot set at Oxford in 1956, built around the real fight over giving Harry Truman an honorary degree, back when G.E.M. Anscombe tried to block it by writing a pamphlet literally titled "Mr Truman's Degree," calling him a mass murderer over Hiroshima and Nagasaki (it didn't work, Oxford gave him the degree anyway). My philosopher character is only loosely based on her, but I want the actual philosophy in the book to hold up rather than just gesture at "ethics stuff," so I've been reading into the doctrine of double effect and the just-war reasoning behind her real argument. Bad idea to bring this up over wine with my writing group, because two friends, Nina and Callum, ended up arguing about it for most of the evening while I just topped up glasses and took notes.

Nina opened by saying Truman obviously made the right call full stop, since every serious estimate of a land invasion of Japan puts the death toll way above what the bombs caused, so the outraged-philosopher routine is moralizing that just ignores the actual arithmetic. Callum said that's not even engaging with what Anscombe argued, because her objection was never about the body count — it was that Truman intended the deaths of huge numbers of civilians as the actual mechanism for ending the war, and deliberately killing innocent people as a means to your goal is murder no matter what it buys you afterward, that's the whole reason "murder" is a category rather than just another line item in a cost-benefit sheet. Nina shot back that the intend-versus-foresee line is doing alot of work for something so slippery: if you drop a weapon you know for a stone-cold fact will kill huge numbers of civilians, saying "I only intended to end the war" sounds like a technicality, and anyway Anscombe apparently has no problem with soldiers who intentionally shoot enemy soldiers as a means of winning, so "kills people as a means" clearly isn't sufficient on its own for murder, meaning something else has to be doing the real work. Callum answered that the cases aren't actually parallel, because combatants count as legitimate targets in virtue of being part of the threat, while civilians don't, and crucially the bomb wasn't killing civilians as a regrettable side effect of hitting some other target — mass civilian death was the entire mechanism the plan depended on, which is presumably why a demonstration blast over open water or empty ground was considered and rejected beforehand. Nina's comeback was that conventional firebombing raids earlier in the war, aimed explicitly at civilian morale, had already killed comparable or even bigger numbers with a fraction of the moral outrage, which makes her suspect the bright line people draw is really tracking how dramatic and futuristic the weapon looked, not any principled difference in kind.
