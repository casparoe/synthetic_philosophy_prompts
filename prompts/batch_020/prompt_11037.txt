I'm a senior philosophy major finishing up a seminar this term on collective intentionality, where we've spent most of the semester on the disagreement between Bratman's account of shared intention and Gilbert's rival view. Bratman builds shared intention entirely out of each person's individual intention that the group act a certain way, together with conditions of mesh between everyone's sub-plans, mutual responsiveness in intention and action, and common knowledge of all this, with nothing further required. Gilbert, in a paper literally titled "Walking Together," uses the example of two people walking down a street side by side to argue that genuinely joint activity requires an actual joint commitment generating real obligations and entitlements between the parties — something she insists no amount of merely individual, however well-meshed, intending could ever add up to. Our department is holding a small reception for graduating majors the week before commencement, maybe two dozen people in the department lounge, and the chair has asked me to give about five minutes of remarks on behalf of the six of us graduating this year.

The trouble is that the seminar has ruined the easy version of this speech for me, the one where I just declare that we "went through it together" and sit down. On Gilbert's view, mere co-enrollment isn't enough for that: her whole point is that two people can walk the same route side by side for years without ever walking together in her sense, and I honestly don't know that all six of us were ever jointly committed to anything as a group, as opposed to separately committed to finishing the same major. On Bratman's view the bar is lower but still real — I'd need actual mesh and mutual responsiveness among our individual plans, not just overlapping transcripts. Three of us did walk to our 9am seminar together most Tuesdays junior year, and I have genuinely caught myself wondering whether that counts. I'd like the speech to do this work rather than skip it — earning whatever it ends up saying about us as a "we" instead of just asserting it — while still landing warmly for my parents and the other families in the room, who will be delighted and completely lost the moment the phrase "joint commitment" leaves my mouth, and while still satisfying my professor and the two other seminar classmates graduating alongside me, who will be sitting right there and will notice immediately if Bratman's or Gilbert's view ends up doing no real work in the speech.

Could you write the speech for me? I'm picturing something close to five minutes read aloud, warm and a little funny in places, built around the walking-together case (or some other suitably concrete image) as its actual spine rather than as a name-check, and honest about the fact that not everyone in that graduating class felt equally bonded to everyone else — without letting that honesty curdle into something sour or too heavy for what is, after all, a happy occasion.
