So last night a friend and I got into a two-hour arguement that started because I mentioned I'd been reading Émilie du Châtelet's little essay on happiness (the one from the 1740s, Discours sur le bonheur) for a seminar I'm auditing. In it she lays out basically five things you supposedly need to be happy: freedom from prejudice, virtue, good health, strong tastes and passions, and — this is the part that set us off — a certain susceptibility to illusion. She's pretty blunt that most of the pleasure we get in life comes from illusion rather than clear-eyed truth, things like overrating how much our own work or love or life matters in the grand scheme of things. But she also spends big chunks of the same essay telling you to free yourself from prejudice and always follow reason. My friend says that's just a flat contradiction, that "illusion" is only a nicer word for self-deception and she can't have it both ways. I tried to argue there's a real difference between an illusion and an outright error or false belief, and that she's noticing something true about happiness that plain truth-and-reason advice misses, but I honestly couldn't back that up on the spot and it's been bugging me since.

Could you write me an essay that actually settles this? I want you to dig into whether her idea of "beneficial" illusion can be made consistent with the more rationalist side of her thinking — is there a principled way to seperate the illusions she thinks we need (about love, our own importance, glory, etc) from the plain errors she condemns elsewhere — and more broadly, whether she's right that some amount of illusion is genuinely necessary for happiness, rather than just a crutch for people who haven't gotten to the truth yet. Go through the strongest version of both sides, but don't land on some mushy "both have a point" ending — I want an actual verdict, something I can go back and win the argument with, not a shrug.
