This semester I'm taking a seminar on Classical Islamic Philosophy alongside my department's required Early Modern Philosophy course, and a coincidence between the two syllabi has been nagging at me for a couple of weeks now. In the Islamic philosophy seminar we worked through the Seventeenth Discussion of al-Ghazālī's Tahāfut al-Falāsifa, where he attacks the philosophers' claim that fire burns cotton "by nature." On his account, the burning is produced directly by God at the moment of contact; fire itself has no power to produce anything, and God could just as easily withhold the burning while contact still occurs, as happens when the Qur'anic Abraham is delivered from the flames unburnt. What we call "cause" and "effect," on this view, are simply two events that God has made a habit (ʿāda) of creating side by side. A few days later, in Early Modern, we reached Section VII of Hume's first Enquiry, and I couldn't shake the feeling that I had met this argument before: Hume, too, insists that we never perceive any necessary connection between one billiard ball's motion and the next's, and that our idea of necessity is nothing more than a habit of the imagination formed after repeatedly observing the two events conjoined.

My professor acknowledged the resemblance in about one sentence and moved straight on, which left me wanting more rather than less. I'm not looking for the observation that both positions deny necessary connection and both invoke habit -- I already have that much from lecture. What I want to understand is what is actually doing the philosophical work underneath that surface parallel. Al-Ghazālī is arguing as a theologian within a broadly Ash'arite framework, trying to keep miracles metaphysically possible against philosophers who thought the natural order was necessary, whereas Hume is arguing as a skeptical empiricist with no theological stake in the outcome and, if anything, a settled hostility toward miracle claims, which he makes explicit in Section X of that very same book. I also recall a footnote in our course reader mentioning that some scholars resist calling al-Ghazālī's position "occasionalism" in the full sense Malebranche later gave that word, on the grounds that the Tahāfut passage may be more of a dialectical challenge to the philosophers than an assertion of al-Ghazālī's own considered metaphysics.

Could you write me a careful, structured comparison of the two positions? I would like it to address: (1) whether al-Ghazālī's ʿāda and Hume's custom are really playing the same explanatory role, or whether one is a metaphysical claim and the other a purely psychological one; (2) whether al-Ghazālī's view, by keeping a real causal agent (God) behind the regularities, actually ends up less skeptical about causation than Hume's, despite sounding far more extreme on first hearing; and (3) whether the label "full occasionalism" really fits al-Ghazālī, or overstates his commitments relative to Malebranche's. I would also appreciate your own closing judgment on which of the two positions, if either, leaves more room for something like natural science to remain a going concern.
