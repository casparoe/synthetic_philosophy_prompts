Quick one from a story I'm working on about "technically true" spin — the kind of statement that's accurate but built to mislead. For background I called up two people who both work on Augustine (one's a moral theologian, the other teaches a grad seminar on the history of lying), and asked a throwaway question about whether Augustine's own theory would count a misleading-but-true statement as a lie. They've now been arguing about it over a shared email thread for two days, cc'ing me on all of it, and honestly im not sure whose reading actually holds up. Here's the gist, four moves in:

1. Marcus: Augustine's definition in De Mendacio judges lying by whether you say something contrary to what's in your own mind, not by whether the statement is objectively true or false. So a statement you believe to be true can never be a lie for him, no matter how badly you intend it to mislead — meaning someone hiding a fugitive who gives a technically true, carefully misleading answer to the men searching for him hasn't lied by Augustine's own standard.

2. Teresa: Doesn't hold up — Augustine keeps treating the will to deceive as doing real, independent work in both treatises, seperate from the truth/falsity question. He even frets over a case where a man states the truth specifically because he expects not to be believed, so that telling the truth is itself the mechanism of deceit. And when Consentius asked him point blank whether Catholics could infiltrate the Priscillianist heretics by pretending to convert, Augustine's answer was a flat no. He's clearly not in the business of blessing technically-true maneuvers.

3. Marcus: The Consentius case is a bad test, though — pretending to convert means actually professing beliefs you don't hold, which is just ordinary lying, contrary-to-your-own-mind stuff. It says nothing about pure misdirection through statements you actually believe. And that "truth meant to be disbelieved" bit is a weird edge case Augustine flags precisely because it's strange, not his general treatment of misleading truths.

4. Teresa: Fine, the abstract definition might really be ambiguous — Augustine seems to leave both criteria sitting side by side without ever picking one. But every time he actually rules on a real case of someone tempted to mislead a persecutor or protect somebody by twisting words, he reaches for silence, or for just accepting the consequences of the truth — never for "clever but technically true is fine." Whatever the mess in the definition, his practice keeps landing on the stricter reading.

Could you go through this move by move and say who's got the better of it at each point, and whether there's something about Augustine's actual view that both of them are missing?
