Lately I've been chewing on Karl Friston's free energy principle (FEP) and the whole predictive processing program that's grown up around it. Some of the maths felt oddly familiar - variational free energy, entropy, all the stat-mech vocabulary I used to use for completely different systems back in the lab - which is probably why I got sucked in to begin with. But the deeper I read, the more serious-sounding objections I run into from philosophers and cognitive scientists, and I honestly can't tell anymore which of these are fatal problems and which are just people being cranky about a framework that happens to be fashionable.

So I'd like your help sorting them out. Below are five objections to FEP that keep coming up in what I've read. I want you to rank them from most to least damaging to FEP's claim to be a genuinely explanatory, empirically substantive scientific principle (as opposed to, say, a mathematical truism dressed up in biology-sounding language). Use tiers S down to D, where S means "this objection alone is basically fatal to FEP as currently formulated" and D means "this objection is confused or trivially answered and shouldn't worry anyone."

1. The dark-room problem: if organisms just minimize free energy/surprise, why don't they seek out the dullest, most predictable environment available and stay there forever, instead of exploring?
2. The unfalsifiability charge: any system that persists in a steady state can be redescribed after the fact as "minimizing free energy," so the principle never actually rules anything out.
3. The Markov blanket boundary problem: treating the statistical Markov blanket in the maths as if it picks out the literal physical boundary of an organism or a self, when its really just a modelling device chosen by whoever is doing the describing.
4. The "old wine, new bottles" charge: FEP doesnt add anything empirically new to the Bayesian brain hypothesis, homeostasis, and autopoiesis, it just relabels them in fancier vocabulary.
5. The scope problem: the maths applies just as well to thermostats, crystals, and hurricanes as it does to brains, so it can't be what explains anything distinctively mental or alive about cognitive systems.
