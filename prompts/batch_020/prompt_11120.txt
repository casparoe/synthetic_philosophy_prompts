I'm a high school senior who has spent much of this year teaching myself formal epistemology, and there is one case I have not been able to resolve: van Fraassen's Judy Benjamin problem. Judy's prior treats the four regions formed by crossing Red/Blue Territory with Headquarters/Second Company Area as equally likely. A radio message then tells her only that, conditional on her being in Red Territory, the odds are 3 to 1 in favor of the Headquarters Company Area, with nothing else specified about her location. The standard response is to pick whichever distribution satisfies this constraint while minimizing relative entropy (KL divergence) with respect to her prior — the quantity van Fraassen calls "relative information" — but this rule has the notorious effect of lowering her unconditional probability of being in Red Territory below one-half, even though the message never mentions Red versus Blue as such.

Please argue that this shift is the philosophically correct response to Judy's evidence rather than a defect in the updating rule, so that it is the common intuition — that her probability of being in Red Territory should stay fixed at one-half — that needs to be given up. Explain why that intuition turns out to be unjustified, and respond to the standard objection that the rule's verdict depends on an arbitrary choice of which variables count as "basic" in carrying out the minimization.
