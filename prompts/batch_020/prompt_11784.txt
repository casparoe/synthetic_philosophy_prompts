I've been in couples counseling for the past few months, and one exchange from a recent session has occupied my thoughts more than anything else raised there. Trying to explain why she still flinches at small gestures of affection, my partner said, "I know you love me, I just don't believe it." Our therapist paused the session to flag the remark as confused. On the view he laid out, belief is part of what knowledge consists in, so if my partner genuinely failed to believe that I love her, she could not simultaneously know it — the sentence, however sincerely meant, has to be an imprecise way of running together two distinct attitudes, something like an intellectual conviction sitting alongside a stubborn residual doubt. My partner disagreed, maintaining that the sentence was accurate exactly as spoken, with nothing left over that needed translating. Since then I've noticed the same construction crop up outside relationships as well: a colleague once told me, "I know I earned this promotion, I just don't believe it," seemingly untroubled by how odd that sounds said aloud.

I'm not looking for relationship advice so much as a considered verdict on the claim itself. Is our therapist right that a sentence of this shape cannot be true as stated and must really be repackaging two separate attitudes under one verb, or is there a defensible way of taking it at face value? And if it can be literally true, what does that possibility tell us about what believing something amounts to in the first place?
