I'm about a third of the way into a novel built around a single act of cowardice: my protagonist, a primary-school teacher named Marion, watches a colleague be quietly humiliated in front of the whole staff room and says nothing, even though everyone who knows her, herself included, would have predicted she'd intervene. In the chapters that follow she becomes fixated on the psychological literature on situationism as a way of making sense of, and I suspect excusing, what she did, and I want to give her a dinner-table scene in which she rehearses that case against her older sister Iris, who teaches moral philosophy and isn't having any of it. My own grasp of this material comes mostly from a couple of podcast episodes and a seminar I audited years ago, which isn't a sturdy enough foundation to let either character stake her credibility on, so before I trust this scene enough to keep it in the manuscript, I'd like your comments on the exchange below.

Marion begins: "It isn't just that people are inconsistent, it's that the inconsistency runs all the way down. Harman's whole argument starts from the fundamental attribution error—we're wired to over-explain behavior by character and under-explain it by circumstance—and he thinks virtue ethics has basically dressed that bias up as metaphysics. Doris backs it up with the data: Milgram's subjects, Zimbardo's guards, the seminarians in the Good Samaritan study who stepped over a man doubled up in a doorway, and here's the detail I can't get past, only about one in ten helped when they'd been told to hurry, compared with something like two in three when they hadn't, and it barely mattered that they were literally on their way to give a talk on that parable. Even Isen and Levin's dime-in-the-phone-booth result, where finding ten cents in the coin return made people wildly more likely to help a stranger pick up dropped papers, says the variance in a life is mostly weather, not character. Mischel had already shown that a trait score barely predicts behavior from one situation to the next, something on the order of a .3 correlation, which is closer to noise than to a soul." Iris answers: "That's Harman's rhetoric, and it isn't even Doris's considered claim. Doris goes after what he calls 'globalist' traits, the kind that would make you act the same in a rigged experiment and a burning building, and he's explicit that something narrower and more local might survive all this. Kamtekar's reply holds even if you grant every number in his book: Aristotle never promised raw, untrained impulse would be stable, only that phronesis, the practical wisdom that actually perceives what's morally salient in a situation, would be. So don't ask whether the seminarians behaved consistently. Ask whether, hurrying, they perceived the man in the doorway as someone who needed them, or just as forty seconds they didn't have. Annas would go further and say most of us are amateurs at this the way most people are amateurs at an instrument, and you don't disprove virtuosity by pointing out that beginners fall apart under pressure."

Setting the philosophy aside for a moment, what I need from you is threefold. First, is Marion's version of the argument a fair statement of the situationist case, or have I let her caricature Harman and Doris in ways Iris, who is supposed to be the sharper of the two, would actually have caught immediately? Second, is Iris's reply really the strongest one available, or is there a more decisive rejoinder I should be arming her with instead? I have a vague memory of objections to how the underlying trait studies are designed in the first place, and, separately, of an argument that virtue is sustained socially rather than residing purely inside one person; either might land harder than an appeal back to Aristotle's own texts. Third, and quite apart from getting the philosophy right: does the exchange read like two people who have actually metabolized this material, or does it still smell of two encyclopedia entries arguing with each other?
