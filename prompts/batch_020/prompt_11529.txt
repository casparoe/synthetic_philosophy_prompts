A few months ago I lost the better part of a week chasing a distributed-consensus bug at work, which sent me down an unexpected path into the "coordinated attack problem" -- the classic result that two generals trying to arrange a simultaneous attack over a channel where messages can go missing can never be certain, no matter how many rounds of acknowledgment they exchange, that they are both certain the attack is on. That in turn pulled me into the wider theory of common knowledge in game theory and epistemic logic, and I got hooked enough that I now run a small podcast interviewing academics who work at the intersection of computer science, economics, and formal philosophy. I don't have any formal training in philosophy or economics myself, so I rely on questions worked out in advance more than most hosts probably need to.

Next week's guest is a game theorist who works on the epistemic foundations of rational choice. She's best known for defending the philosophical weight of Aumann's agreement theorem and, more controversially, for arguing that common knowledge of rationality is exactly what forces the backward-induction outcome in games like the centipede game. Whenever she's pressed on how unrealistic it is to suppose that real, embodied, non-omniscient agents ever achieve genuine common knowledge of anything, she reliably retreats to the same line: that common knowledge is a modeling idealization, "like a frictionless plane," and idealizations can be false of everything and still do indispensable explanatory work.

I'd like eight to ten interview questions that go beyond letting her restate the theorems for a lay audience. Please include at least one question, plus a follow-up keyed to whatever answer she's most likely to give, on each of: (1) why a public announcement of some fact can generate common knowledge -- and thereby change what a group will do -- even when every individual already privately knew that fact beforehand, the way it plays out in muddy-children-style puzzles where every child can see everyone else's dirty forehead but not her own; (2) how much of the "you cannot agree to disagree" conclusion is really being carried by the common-prior assumption rather than by rationality or common knowledge as such, and what, if anything, she thinks is lost if two perfectly honest Bayesians are simply allowed to start from different priors; and (3) her defense of treating common knowledge of rationality as holding even at the nodes of a game that a fully rational population would supposedly never reach, against the charge that this leaves off-path play resting on an incoherent epistemic state. Then build to a closing sequence that makes the "frictionless plane" line progressively harder to lean on -- for instance by raising the tension between no-trade theorems and the plain existence of real speculative markets, or the result from the "electronic mail game" showing that a vanishingly small, ineliminable chance that a confirmation message failed to arrive is enough to stop two players from ever coordinating on the efficient outcome, no matter how many rounds of confirmation pass -- and end with a question that loops back to the distributed-systems problem that got me into this: given that her own framework implies common knowledge is provably unreachable over any channel that can fail, what would she need to observe to conclude that treating human coordination as though it rested on common knowledge is doing more distorting than explaining?
