As a software engineer, I've spent my spare evenings over the last couple of years building small game-playing bots: minimax with alpha-beta pruning for tic-tac-toe and Connect Four, and, more recently, some tinkering with chess endgame tablebases, which as I understand it are really just backward induction pushed to its logical extreme—you solve every position by propagating outcomes back from checkmate. So when I recently ran into the "centipede game" in an introductory game-theory chapter, backward induction already felt like second nature to me: evaluate the leaves of the game tree first, propagate values upward, and the resulting policy is guaranteed to be the best you can do against a fully rational opponent—essentially Zermelo's theorem, if I'm remembering the name right. In the centipede game, two players alternate choosing between "take" (ending the game and pocketing a lopsided but growing pot) and "pass" (letting the pot keep growing for a possible later split), and the standard backward-induction argument concludes that the only rational outcome is for the first player to take immediately, even though both players would end up far richer if they kept passing for a while.

My first reaction was that this isn't really a paradox at all, just the exact same logic as my chess bots applied to a different payoff table: since backward induction is mathematically guaranteed to yield the optimal strategy in a finite, perfect-information game, "take immediately" simply is the objectively correct move, and the fact that human subjects in actual lab experiments keep passing for several rounds just shows that people are bad at this particular game tree, the same way a weak chess player might miss a forced mate: a performance failure, not evidence of anything conceptually interesting. When I mentioned this to a friend who's taking a decision theory seminar, she told me I was "importing an assumption that doesn't survive the move from chess to the centipede game" and that I was missing the actual puzzle that's kept game theorists and philosophers arguing about this for decades. I don't really follow what assumption she has in mind, since backward induction seems like backward induction no matter what numbers you put into the payoff table. Could you spell out what's wrong with treating "take immediately" as optimal in the same sense that my chess engine's moves are optimal, and what the genuine, still-debated conceptual issue in the backward-induction/centipede-game literature actually is, if it isn't simply that people fail to reason as many steps ahead as the theory demands?
