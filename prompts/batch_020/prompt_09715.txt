We spent a chunk of my epistemology seminar last week on Fitch's paradox of knowability, and I've been turning it over since because I'm pretty sure it basically ends verificationism / anti-realism about truth as a serious option, but my professor talked about it like the debate is still wide open, which I don't get. The proof itself is short: suppose, like a verificationist wants to, that every truth is at least possibly knowable. Now take any truth nobody happens to know yet (there's gotta be at least one, we're obviously not omniscient) and glue it into one sentence: "p is true, and nobody knows that p." That whole sentence is itself true, so by the knowability assumption it must be possible for someone to know it. But if anyone ever did know it, they'd know p (that's half the conjunction) and also know that nobody knows p (the other half), including, at that very moment, themselves. That's a flat contradiction, so the sentence can't possibly be known by anyone, ever. Which means, running the same knowability assumption backwards, the sentence can't be true either, so there's no such thing as an unknown truth, i.e. everything true is already known by somebody. Since that's obviously false, something upstream has to give, and the only thing that looks remotely optional in the setup is "every truth is knowable," which is basically the whole verificationist/Dummett-style anti-realist thesis about truth in the first place.

Given this seems like a pretty clean reductio resting on barely anything besides logic-101 stuff (non-contradiction, knowledge implying truth, knowledge distributing over "and"), I don't see how anti-realism about truth survives as a serious position after 1963. I brought this up with a friend who's doing a philosophy PhD and she said plenty of people, including some pretty serious anti-realists, still don't treat Fitch's result as a knockdown at all, which honestly confused me more than the proof did. Am I missing something about why there's supposedly still wiggle room here, or is this just one of those cases where a technical result quietly killed a view and the textbooks havent caught up yet?
