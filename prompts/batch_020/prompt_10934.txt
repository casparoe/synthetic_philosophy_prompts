For my term paper in the formal epistemology seminar I am taking this semester, I have been working on the two-envelope paradox, and before I commit to an argument in writing I would like a sanity check on it.

The resolution given in lecture was the standard one: the "always switch" argument secretly assumes that, whatever amount x you find in your envelope, the probability that you are holding the smaller of the two amounts is exactly 1/2, regardless of x. That can only be true for every x if the amount is drawn from an improper (non-normalizable) prior, such as a uniform distribution over all positive reals; any genuine, properly normalized prior breaks that symmetry, so conditioning on what you actually see should stop "always switch" from being uniformly justified.

I tried to construct a case where this diagnosis does not save you. Let the smaller of the two amounts, M, take values 2^k dollars for k = 0, 1, 2, ..., with P(M = 2^k) = (1 - r)r^k for a fixed r strictly between 1/2 and 1 (I used r = 0.6) -- a perfectly legitimate, properly normalized prior. A fair coin then decides whether your envelope holds M or 2M. Working through Bayes' theorem, I find that for every possible amount x = 2^n you might actually observe, the expected value of the other envelope conditional on seeing x is still strictly greater than x: exactly double at the smallest observable value, and exactly 6.25% higher at every value above that. So "switch" is favored at every single observation point, not merely on average before looking, even though the prior is entirely proper. The reason, as far as I can tell, is that M has infinite expectation here (since 2r > 1), so this is really a two-envelope version of the St. Petersburg paradox in disguise. I have a nagging feeling I have seen a move like this somewhere in the literature, but I built it independently and could not track down a reference, so I would rather have the reasoning checked on its own terms.

What I cannot decide is what it shows. Does it mean the improper-prior story was never the deep diagnosis, and the real culprit is expected-value maximization under an infinite or undefined expectation, with improper priors merely the most familiar way that surfaces? Or is my example secretly just a relabeled version of the same mistake the lecture identified, so that "infinite expectation" is not really a distinct diagnosis at all? Could you check whether the derivation is correct, and then say which of those two readings you think is right, or whether there is a third option I am missing entirely?
