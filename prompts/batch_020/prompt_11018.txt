Okay, this is going to sound like a weirdly specific thing to still be annoyed about, but I had this argument with my friend Priya a few nights ago and it's been rattling around in my head since. She got back one of those genetic screening panels that spits out something like "14% lifetime risk" for some condition, and I made an offhand comment that the number doesn't really tell you anything about HER specifically, its just whatever group the company decided to average over. She got kind of annoyed and said that's true of literally every statistic anyone has ever been given and that I was being needlessly nihilistic about it. So then we spent like an hour arguing about what the "right" group to average over would even be. Everyone with her exact genetic markers? Same markers plus her age, family history, diet, zip code? At some point the group just shrinks down toward "people who are exactly Priya," which is a group of one, and then the frequency is either 0 or 1 and the 14% was kind of a fiction the whole time. She thinks I'm overthinking a basic stats question and that there's obviously some non-arbitrary best class to use (whatever the actual study measured), and I think it's arbitrary all the way down, in a way that should bother us more than it seems to bother anyone.

I looked into it afterward and apparently what I'm groping at has an actual name in philosophy of probability, "the reference class problem," and people have been fighting about it since at least Venn in the 1800s (there's a classic example of his about a consumptive Englishman that's basically Priya's situation exactly). Reichenbach's proposed fix was to use the narrowest class for which you can still get reliable statistics, but the more I think about it the more that just seems like it's naming the tension -- narrow for relevance vs. wide for reliable numbers -- rather then actually resolving it. I also found a paper by a guy named Hájek literally titled "The Reference Class Problem Is Your Problem Too," arguing it's not just a frequentist headache but infects basically every account of single-case probability, including ones like propensity theory that were partly invented to dodge it. Could you actually explain what's going on here -- why this is supposedly a deep, maybe unsolvable problem rather than an annoying technicality, why "just pick a narrower class" doesn't get you out of it, and whether Hájek's bigger claim (that literally nobody gets to feel smug about this, not even Bayesians) holds up? And if there's a genuinely good way of blocking the "it's all arbitrary" conclusion, I'd love to hear the strongest version, since Priya is going to want the receipts next time this comes up.
