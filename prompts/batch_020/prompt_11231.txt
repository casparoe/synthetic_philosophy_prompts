So I'm putting together a session for my grad seminar on metaphysics of science, and I want to try something a bit different from the usual "here's Lewis, here's Armstrong" handout, since half the room just skims those anyway. Could you write me a dialogue between a committed Humean about laws (running something like Lewis's Best System Account -- laws are whatever comes out of the deductive system that best trades off simplicity against strength over the whole Humean mosaic, no extra necessity-glue required) and a committed anti-Humean (happy to reach for Armstrong-style necessitation between universals, dispositional essentialism a la Bird, or even Maudlin's primitivism, whichever combo they find most defensible, so long as laws involve something over and above the mere pattern of particular fact)?

I want them arguing over one specific case rather than trading slogans: the old textbook pair of "no lump of enriched uranium has ever had a mass over 100kg" versus "no lump of gold has ever had a mass over 100,000kg." Both are exceptionless, unrestricted generalizations of exactly the same logical form, but intuitively only one of them counts as a law and the other's just a big accident. I want the Humean to actually explain what the simplicity/strength (or "fit," if probabilistic laws come up) balancing act is doing to sort those two apart, without quietly smuggling necessity in through the back door -- and then get hit with the classic complaint that a "law" which is really just a compact summary of the mosaic can't turn around and explain the very mosaic it summarizes (isn't that backwards?). And I want the anti-Humean to have to earn their necessitation relation or essential disposition too, specifically by answering the old Lewis-style question of what N(F,G) (or whatever the anti-Humean's favorite necessity-maker is) is actually doing that a bald regularity doesn't already do, since just naming the relation "necessitation" doesn't obviously buy you anything.

Neither one should get the last word for free -- I want real engagement, each reply actually landing on what the other just said rather than restated position statements past each other, and its totally fine if it just ends in a standoff, since that's more or less where the actual debate sits right now. Something like 700-900 words is probably the sweet spot, and please have them use the real technical vocabulary rather then just gesturing vaguely around it -- my students can smell hand-waving a mile off by this point in the term. (Feel free to just tag the speakers H and A, or give them names, whatever's easiest.)
