After forty years as a geophysicist, I retired five years ago and have spent a good part of my time since auditing philosophy courses at the local university, largely for the pleasure of finally doing something I never had time for during my career. This term's ethics course spent several weeks on the surprisingly unsettled question of why promises obligate at all. We read Hume's claim that we feel bound only because a useful social convention has trained us to, and that there is nothing intrinsically binding about giving one's word, set against T. M. Scanlon's later view that what really grounds the obligation is the wrong of leading someone to rely on an assurance and then disappointing that reliance. I did not expect any of this to be practically useful. It turns out to be exactly what I need, because my professional society has just asked its senior Fellows to staff an Ethics Panel that offers binding arbitration for collegial disputes too old, too informal, or too poorly documented ever to reach a court, and I have been handed a case that seems to me to turn entirely on which of those two views, if either, is right.

The facts are not in dispute between the parties. In the late 1990s, as graduate students sharing a grant, Dr. A and Dr. B jointly developed a new seismic sensor design; A asked B to spend two additional years building and documenting the hardware rather than pursuing his own results, and promised him, in so many words, "a fifth of whatever this is ever worth" if the design were ever commercialized. Nothing was written down. B's early publication record suffered somewhat over those two years; A's did not. Two decades later, A founded a company built on a refinement of the design, which was acquired last year for close to three hundred million dollars — a literal fifth would be sixty million. A has offered B a one-time payment of fifty thousand dollars as a gesture of thanks, arguing through counsel that a loosely worded, unwritten remark between two overworked graduate students was never meant as a precise or permanent contract, that he cannot be held to a fraction he does not recall specifying so exactly, and that the company's eventual scale owed far more to decisions, risks, and luck in which B had no part whatsoever. B maintains that he shaped two years of his career, and his whole sense of being fairly treated by his own field, around that promise, and that it should not matter to the panel that no court could ever enforce it. Our charter tells panels only to recommend "whatever most fairly reflects the parties' legitimate expectations and the discipline's own standards of collegiality," which settles nothing on its own; the panel is currently split down the middle, half inclined to defer heavily to A's account precisely because nothing was ever formalized, the other half convinced that this reasoning would make the panel pointless, since informality is exactly why the case is in front of us rather than a judge.

Could you draft the short memo I will circulate to my fellow panel members before we reconvene in three weeks? I need one concrete resolution — a specific remedy and a rough timeline for carrying it out, not a range of options — defended with reasoning both scientists could actually recognize themselves in. I would especially like it to take the strongest form of A's position seriously, namely that a vague, decades-old promise made under expectations nothing like the eventual outcome cannot simply be enforced at face value as though nothing about the situation had changed, rather than dismiss that position as merely self-serving.
