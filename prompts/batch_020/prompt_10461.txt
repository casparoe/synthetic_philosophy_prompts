Harsanyi's aggregation theorem (1955) purports to derive utilitarianism from purely decision-theoretic premises. If each individual's preferences over lotteries satisfy the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms, society's preferences over the same lotteries also satisfy those axioms, and society is indifferent between any two lotteries whenever every individual is indifferent between them, then the social utility function must be an affine (weighted-sum) combination of the individual utility functions. Harsanyi took this as proof that Bayesian rationality plus a minimal Pareto condition commits us to something like utilitarianism.

Two objections deserve serious engagement. First, each individual's utility function in the theorem is pinned down only up to that individual's own choice of zero point and unit -- a cardinalization that reflects attitudes toward risk, not obviously the kind of scale needed to weigh how much one person's gain matters against another's loss. Sen and others have pressed Harsanyi on whether this gap can be closed without smuggling in further ethical assumptions that the theorem itself does not supply. Second, Diamond's classic reply notes that the very same Pareto condition ranks a fair coin flip between two people for an indivisible good as no better than simply handing the good to one of them outright, whenever the two procedures happen to yield the same sum of expected utility -- even though the fair procedure seems, intuitively, to have something going for it that the arbitrary one lacks.

Write an essay assessing whether Harsanyi's theorem succeeds in giving utilitarianism a genuine decision-theoretic foundation. Engage substantively with both objections, including whether the second is best diagnosed as a clash between ex-ante and ex-post standards of fairness, a problem specific to treating cardinal utility as interpersonally comparable, or something else. Reach and defend a definite verdict of your own; a qualified verdict is fine if precisely specified (e.g., "the theorem is valid but ethically inert without an independent comparability assumption"), but simply noting that reasonable people disagree is not.
