I sit on the executive committee of a mid-sized manufacturing firm deciding what to do about a three-year-old breach-of-contract suit against a former supplier. We've already spent $3.5 million in legal fees; outside counsel says another $1.5 million and eight months would get us to trial, where we could win $6 million or lose entirely, and the supplier has just offered to settle for $500,000. Over dinner last night, a friend who teaches game theory argued that we're now in a real-world dollar auction: since neither side can recover what it has already spent, stopping now locks in a $3 million loss for us, while continuing offers a chance of coming out ahead at the risk of an even larger one — and the supplier faces the mirror image of that same choice, so both of us have reason to keep escalating well past the point where, in hindsight, stopping would have made more sense. A colleague's draft memo takes this to mean we should simply accept the settlement, since sunk costs are sunk; I think that's too quick, because what we should do here depends on what the supplier is likely to do next, not only on what we've already spent. Please draft the short memo I'll bring to Monday's vote: a concrete recommendation — accept, counter, or proceed to trial — with a timeframe, reasoning that actually engages the two-sided escalation dynamic my friend described rather than just invoking "ignore sunk costs" the way my colleague did, and a fair account of the strongest consideration against whatever you end up recommending.
