ok weird research question but im writing a novel where one of the characters is ABD in philosophy -- total Kant person -- and there's a scene where she has to shut down her insufferable brother in law at thanksgiving who's doing the "well ACTUALLY the categorical imperative is simple" thing. I want her comeback to be real philosophy, not hand-wavy, so I went back to the Groundwork and now I'M stuck.

After the four examples (suicide, the lying-promise borrower, the guy who lets his talents rot, and the guy who won't help anyone in distress because "what concern is it of mine?"), Kant sums up the test like this:

"We must be able to will that a maxim of our action should be a universal law. This is the canon of the moral appreciation of the action generally. Some actions are of such a character that their maxim cannot without contradiction be even conceived as a universal law of nature, far from it being possible that we should will that it should be so. In others this intrinsic impossibility is not found, but still it is impossible to will that their maxim should be raised to the universality of a law of nature, since such a will would contradict itself."

So the borrower's maxim apparently can't even be conceived as a universal law without contradiction, but the guy who refuses to help anyone can conceive of his maxim as a universal law just fine -- he just can't will it. Here's my problem: why doesn't the same move work against him? A world where literally nobody ever helps anybody seems just as hard to actually conceive of as a world with no promises, since the whole concept of "needing help" seems to assume a background where help sometimes gets given. Is there an actual non-question-begging reason the test sorts these two guys into different bins, or did Kant basically decide which duties counted as "perfect" first and reverse-engineer the contradiction story to fit? Give me the real answer, pitched at a level I can turn into her dialogue rather than a lecture.
