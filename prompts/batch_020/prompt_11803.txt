Last weekend I got into a long, increasingly heated argument with a friend of mine over dinner about Elizabeth Anscombe's 1956 pamphlet "Mr. Truman's Degree," in which she campaigned against Oxford's decision to award Harry Truman an honorary doctorate on the grounds that he had ordered the atomic bombing of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. My friend, who trained as a historian rather than a philosopher, kept insisting that the argument only sounds rigorous in the abstract, and that the intention/foresight distinction it leans on quietly collapses the moment you try to apply it to an actual, messy military campaign. I found myself defending Anscombe more than I expected to, and by the end of the night neither of us had budged.

I co-host a small philosophy podcast for an audience that already knows the basic shape of the doctrine of double effect, and next month's guest is a just war theorist who has published specifically in defense of Anscombe's argument against Truman -- not just her double-effect apparatus in general, but its application to this particular case. I don't want the episode to stop once she's restated the pamphlet's headline claim that murder remains murder whatever the balance of consequences; I want the questions to go after exactly the places where my friend and I got stuck. Could you draft eight to ten interview questions for her? I'd like one question that pushes her to specify, without just asserting the conclusion, what exactly distinguishes intending civilian deaths as the very means of victory from merely foreseeing those deaths as a side effect of striking a legitimate target such as a naval yard, with a follow-up keyed to whatever criterion she offers; one question on whether the just-war sense of "innocent" -- non-combatant status, not moral purity -- can really do the work Anscombe needs once it's applied to a civilian population manufacturing the shells and engines that keep the war effort running; one question on the apparent tension between her explicit rejection of pacifism and her absolute prohibition on killing the innocent, since it's not obvious why the latter doesn't just collapse into something close to the former once total war is underway; and, to close, one question pressing the standard reply that the bombings, terrible as they were, killed far fewer people than an invasion of the home islands would have -- I want her pushed until she either bites the bullet or tells us what, if anything, would count as a legitimate exception to the prohibition.
