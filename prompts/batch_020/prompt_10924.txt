In my democratic theory seminar this term we've been working through the "boundary problem" -- the question of who belongs to the demos, sharpened by Frederick Whelan's observation that no procedure for fixing democratic membership can itself be democratically legitimate, since any vote on membership already presupposes an answer to who is entitled to vote. Two rival responses we've read are Robert Goodin's all-affected-interests principle and Arash Abizadeh's all-subjected (coercion) principle. Please compare these two principles as solutions to the boundary problem, and say which one, in your judgment, comes closer to actually resolving Whelan's circularity rather than merely relocating it. Justify your answer with reference to a concrete case where the two principles would pick out different demoi -- for instance, immigration policy, or a diffuse transnational harm like carbon emissions.
