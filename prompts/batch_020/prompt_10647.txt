Last night at the rehearsal dinner, an old grad-school friend and I got into a real argument, much to the annoyance of everyone trying to eat dessert: whether Kant's demand that a truly moral act come from duty rather than inclination can survive Bernard Williams's complaint that it leaves us with "one thought too many," or whether Barbara Herman's fix -- that duty only has to sit in the background as a limiting condition, not as the felt motive of the moment -- actually saves Kant. I'm giving the toast tomorrow for my friend Owen, who is finishing a dissertation on exactly this moral-worth literature, and his fiancée Mira, who has sat through his rants about Kant's warm-hearted philanthropist -- the one who helps people purely for the pleasure of it, and who Kant says deserves no real moral credit for it -- more times than any person should have to. About half the room are philosophers who will catch every reference; the rest are family who have never heard of Kant and just want to hear that Owen loves her. Write me a five-minute toast built around the joke that, on a strict Kantian reading, this marriage has been dangerously low in moral worth since day one, but have it resolve into a genuine answer to the puzzle rather than a mere punchline, so that it lands as a tribute to a love that would still be there even if duty ever came knocking -- not as an insult, and not as a lecture.
