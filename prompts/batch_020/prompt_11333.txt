I spent the better part of four decades as an observational astrophysicist before retiring, and in the years since I have been filling the gap with a rather disorganized self-study of philosophy of science. Bayesian reasoning was the water I swam in professionally, fitting models to data and updating parameter estimates as new observations came in, so I assumed the philosophical literature on Bayesian confirmation theory would feel like familiar territory. Instead, I recently ran headlong into what is apparently called the "problem of old evidence," and it has unsettled something I thought I understood rather well.

As I understand it, the difficulty (originally raised by Clark Glymour) runs roughly as follows. On the standard Bayesian picture, evidence E confirms hypothesis H just in case learning E raises your credence in H. But if P(E) is already equal to 1 -- as it is whenever E is "old evidence" already sitting in the background knowledge K -- conditionalizing on E is a no-op: it leaves every credence exactly where it was, however tightly H predicts E. The textbook illustration, fittingly for someone with my background, is the roughly 43 arcseconds per century of anomalous precession in Mercury's perihelion, a data point that had sat in the astronomical record for decades before Einstein showed that general relativity entails it. Since any scientist evaluating general relativity already had P(E) = 1 for that figure, naive Bayesian conditionalization seems committed to saying the perihelion data could not have confirmed the theory at all, which flatly contradicts the near-universal judgment that it was among the most important confirmations general relativity ever received. A friend from a philosophy-of-science reading group I recently joined maintains that the puzzle is fully resolved once you drop the assumption of logical omniscience, but I do not yet understand the move well enough to know whether I agree with him.

Could you give me a careful account of why this counts as a genuine formal problem for Bayesian confirmation theory rather than a merely verbal one, and then walk through the leading proposed solutions in some depth? I would especially like to understand the Garber-Jeffrey-Niiniluoto strategy of relocating the "new" information from E itself to the logical fact that H entails E, including what is supposed to justify treating a logical or mathematical discovery as a legitimate object of Bayesian updating in the first place; and I would be curious how that strategy compares with any rival proposals that instead try to reconstruct a counterfactual, pre-evidential credence in E. Finally, I would value your own sense of whether working epistemologists regard the problem as solved, merely contained, or still a genuine embarrassment for Bayesianism.
