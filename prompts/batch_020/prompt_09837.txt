I recently reread John Maynard Keynes's 1930 essay "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren" in full, after seeing the fifteen-hour-week line quoted -- as it always is, and almost always out of context -- in an Astral Codex Ten open thread about AI-driven automation. What struck me on the full read is how much sturdier philosophical machinery is doing the work underneath that one famous prediction. Keynes distinguishes needs that are absolute from needs that are merely relative or positional; he calls the love of money as an end in itself "a somewhat disgusting morbidity"; and he argues that we must keep pretending avarice and usury are virtues for a while yet, since only they can lead us out of "the tunnel of economic necessity into daylight." He is also quite explicit that once scarcity is solved, the real difficulty becomes mankind's "permanent problem" of how to occupy freedom well, and he clearly expects the answer to lean on the Bloomsbury-and-Moore-inflected goods of his own youth -- love, beauty, disinterested contemplation -- rather than on consumption.

Keynes pegged his hundred-year horizon to almost exactly now, 2030. By most measures of capital accumulation and output per head, he was roughly right, if anything conservative. What he plainly did not anticipate is the specific shape of our situation: automation creeping into cognitive and creative work rather than just manual labor, live policy fights over universal basic income, and, sitting right alongside all that abundance, a culture that valorizes hustle and overwork rather than straining under the weight of empty leisure. Reconstructing as carefully as you can from Keynes's own premises, rather than from generic present-day common sense, how do you think he would diagnose that gap? Did he underestimate how large a share of demand is positional rather than absolute, did he underestimate how durable the psychology of "purposiveness" would prove even once it stopped being economically necessary, or is the failure somewhere else in the model entirely? And, given both his economics and his own fairly definite opinions about what leisure is actually for, what would you guess he would tell a government today trying to decide how to handle AI-driven technological unemployment and the various universal basic income proposals now on offer?
