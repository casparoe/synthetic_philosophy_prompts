And here is their answer, exactly as written:

"Whenever we try to justify a belief, we seem to do so by appealing to some further belief. If I believe there is a tree outside because I can see it, my visual experience is supposed to justify that belief; but if I'm asked why my experience justifies anything, I need to give another reason, and so on. Repeating this process generates what is sometimes called Agrippa's trilemma: the chain of justification must either go on forever, stop at some belief that itself has no further support, or loop back onto something already used, which would be circular. Coherentists are usually willing to accept some version of the circularity, but foundationalists and infinitists both try to avoid it.

Foundationalists solve the trilemma by insisting that the chain does stop, but not arbitrarily, because it ends in 'basic' beliefs that don't need any further belief to support them, typically because they are self-evident or directly grounded in experience. Since these beliefs have to do the work of stopping the regress without themselves being justified by anything else, they have to be certain and immune to error, which is what basic beliefs are for foundationalists.

Infinitists, most notably Laurence BonJour, deny that the chain needs to stop at all. On this view, an endless series of reasons is not a problem, because far enough back the series is allowed to loop back and reuse reasons already given, so a person is never actually left without an answer to 'why believe that?', even though their mind is finite.

Overall, I find infinitism more convincing, because it doesn't need to assume that some beliefs are simply beyond doubt, the way foundationalism does."
