I'm drafting a novel about a young lawyer navigating Germany's postwar legal reckoning with Nazism. While telling two friends from my writing workshop about one of the real cases I've been using as research, I watched them fall into an argument that, without either realizing it, reconstructs a famous exchange in legal philosophy almost move for move.

The case: in 1944, a woman denounced her husband to the authorities under wartime statutes (1934, 1938) that made disparaging remarks about Hitler a capital offense; he had said something contemptuous of the regime while home on leave. He was sentenced to death under those statutes; the sentence was never carried out, and he was sent to the front instead. In 1949, a West German court convicted the wife of unlawfully depriving her husband of his freedom, holding that the statute she had invoked could never have been valid law, since it was "contrary to the sound conscience and sense of justice of all decent human beings." This is the real case behind the Hart-Fuller exchange in the 1958 Harvard Law Review. Here is how my friends Owen and Nadia argued about it over dinner:

(1) Owen said the court obviously got it right: a decree that made grumbling about the Führer a capital crime was never law in any sense worth the name, so the wife can't invoke the shelter of legality for what she did under it.

(2) Nadia said that proves too much: plenty of Nazi statutes -- on taxation, conscription, confiscation -- were just as compulsory and just as wicked, and nobody says those were never law. If "monstrous" is enough to strip something of legal status, "law" just becomes a compliment paid to statutes we approve of, and what the court actually did was punish her retroactively for something legal at the time, while pretending otherwise.

(3) Owen answered that it wasn't only the statute's content that was rotten -- the whole apparatus around it was incoherent by its own lights: secret directives, arbitrary enforcement, courts and the Gestapo doing whatever served the regime regardless of what was on the books. A system that disordered forfeits its claim to be a legal order in the full sense, so singling out this statute isn't arbitrary; it's symptomatic.

(4) Nadia replied that every legal system has some gap between the rule on paper and how officials apply it, and that wartime regimes everywhere lean on secrecy and improvisation; if enough incoherence voids a law, somebody owes her a threshold, and she's never seen one that doesn't just smuggle back in "this result disgusts me." There's a more honest fix available regardless: have a legislature pass an openly retroactive statute punishing denouncers -- at least that admits it's retroactive punishment for a grave wrong, instead of a court quietly rewriting the past.

(5) Owen said honesty isn't free: telling future legislatures that retroactive punishment is available whenever a wrong feels large enough is exactly the power you don't want lying around. And "it was valid law, just wicked" is the very sentence German judges used for a decade to apply monstrous decrees without a flicker of resistance -- a jurisprudence willing to say a regime fails law on its own terms gives an official something to stand on while it's happening, not just a tool for tidying up afterward.

(6) Nadia said that cuts both ways -- it also hands every official a ready-made excuse to defy any law he personally finds outrageous. And it still doesn't explain why judges in 1949 got to decide, after the fact, that the statute had secretly never existed, rather than doing the harder and more honest thing: admitting this was law, that they were choosing to punish her anyway, and saying plainly why.
