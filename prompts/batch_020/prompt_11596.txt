This semester I'm taking a graduate seminar on formal approaches to distributive justice, and this week we're working through how Lara Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility (REU) theory bears on the Harsanyi–Rawls dispute about the original position. Harsanyi's utilitarian conclusion depends on modeling parties behind the veil of ignorance as expected-utility maximizers who assign themselves an equal chance of occupying any social position; Buchak argues that if these parties are instead REU maximizers, able to weight outcomes by more than just their probability-weighted average, they will rationally choose a form of "relative prioritarianism" that weights the interests of the worse-off more heavily without granting them Rawls's lexical priority. Please describe what you take to be the most important implications of this result for the broader project of grounding distributive principles in facts about individual rational choice under uncertainty. I would especially like to know exactly which assumption in Harsanyi's argument REU theory is denying, and whether Buchak's relative prioritarianism should be regarded as a genuinely new position in distributive ethics or as an unstable compromise that inherits objections leveled at both utilitarianism and the difference principle.
