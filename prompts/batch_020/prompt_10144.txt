I spent most of my working life as a neurophysiologist, first recording from single neurons and later building large-scale network models, so when I took up philosophy of mind seriously after retiring, Jerry Fodor's language of thought hypothesis struck me as a strange creature: a claim that thought simply must involve discrete symbols and a combinatorial syntax, argued for largely on a priori grounds that had little obvious connection to anything I recognized from the wetware. Two former colleagues and I have since started a small podcast out of our retirement reading group, and next week we are interviewing a guest who has built her career defending and updating Fodor's hypothesis, including the systematicity-and-productivity argument he made with Zenon Pylyshyn against connectionism in 1988 and his later asymmetric-dependence theory of mental content. She has an irritating habit: whenever someone raises a connectionist or deep-learning result that seems to get along fine without discrete symbolic representations, she reaches for Marr's distinction between the algorithmic and the implementational level and answers with some version of "you have shown me new hardware, not refuted the algorithm." I would rather the questions not let her simply repeat that move for the whole hour.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions for this conversation? I would like the questions touching on connectionism arranged as a short sequence: open with one that presses the systematicity-and-productivity argument specifically against the fluent, apparently productive sentences that today's large language models generate without anything resembling an explicit syntax tree, then add two or three follow-ups that make the Marr's-levels deflection progressively harder to fall back on, ending with a direct question about what specific empirical finding -- from neuroscience, developmental psychology, or machine learning -- would actually count as evidence against the language of thought hypothesis rather than simply being redescribed as implementation. Separately, I would like at least one question on Fodor's radical concept nativism, the notorious claim that even a concept like CARBURETOR must be triggered rather than learned, probing whether "triggering versus learning" is doing genuine explanatory work or is just relabeling the problem of concept acquisition. And I would like at least one question on whether the asymmetric-dependence solution to the disjunction problem really explains how misrepresentation is possible, or only restates the problem in causal vocabulary. For each of these last two questions, please add a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give.
