I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind finishing a dissertation chapter on teleosemantics, and one thought experiment keeps derailing my writing sessions rather than helping them along. On Millikan's proper-function account (and the etiological theories descending from it), a mental state has the content it does because states of that type were selected, across a lineage of reproducing ancestors, for being reliably caused by, or for reliably producing, some specific condition. The trouble, as Davidson's Swampman case and its many teleosemantic descendants bring out (Millikan and Neander each took up versions of the problem in papers from the mid-1990s), is that a being with no reproductive lineage at all — a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a person, assembled by sheer physical accident — would, on the letter of the theory, have organs and internal states with no proper function whatsoever, and so beliefs and desires that are not about anything, however convincingly the being talks, loves, or grieves. My advisor is skeptical that fiction can do any real philosophical work here, and I'd like to prove her wrong.

So here's what I'm hoping you can help me with: a short story, somewhere in the 1000-1800 word range, built around a person who dies in a freak accident over a wetland, at the exact moment that a bolt of lightning (or some other one-off physical fluke) reassembles the surrounding matter into a perfect physical duplicate of them, memories, relationships, and habits intact, who simply returns to the old life undetected. Somewhere in the story — through a conversation, an overheard argument, a document stumbled across, or the duplicate's own private unease — I'd like the idea to surface that, on views like Millikan's, none of this being's thoughts about their spouse, their work, or their own past actually represent anything at all, for want of the right history. I'm not looking for the story to adjudicate the metaphysics; I'd rather it sit with what that thought would actually do to the person having it, or to the people who love them. Invent whatever names, setting, and relationships serve the story best.
