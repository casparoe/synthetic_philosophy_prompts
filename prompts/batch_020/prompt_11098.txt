So my parents are hitting 40 years of marriage next month and, as the oldest, I've been informed that I'm giving the toast. Relevant background: my dad was a game theorist for his entire career before he retired, and his signature move at family gatherings is bringing up the centipede game completely out of nowhere -- you know, the one where two players alternate turns either taking a growing pot for themselves or passing it along, and backward induction says the first player should just grab it immediately on move one, since that's the unique subgame-perfect equilibrium, even though if both players kept passing all the way to the end they'd both end up much richer. He thinks its the funniest and most depressing result in economics, that "rational" players are supposed to unravel all the way back to zero cooperation before the game has even properly started, and he'll bring this up apropos of nothing, like if someone mentions lending a coworker twenty bucks.

I want to build the toast around the idea that he and mom have basically been playing one extremely long centipede game for 40 years and have never once taken the "rational" defect-on-move-one option. My worry is landing on the cheap version of this, something like "well, I guess love just isn't rational," which is definately the kind of hand-wave that would make the economists in the room wince. The genuinely interesting thing about backward induction, from what I've read about it, isn't just that it predicts something people don't actually do -- it's that there's a real question of whether the argument even holds together. It only works if you assume common knowledge of rationality all the way down, but if you're several rounds in and your co-player has, by the induction's own logic, already done something "irrational" by not defecting on move one, what are you even supposed to infer at that point? The whole backward chain kind of presupposes it never has to survive contact with an actual surprising move. It's got some of the same self-undermining flavor as Newcomb's problem or Parfit's hitchhiker, but it's its own paradox, and I don't want to muddy a wedding-anniversary toast by throwing three decision theory problems into one speech. Also worth knowing: roughly half the guests are dad's old department colleagues and a couple former grad students who will absolutely clock the reference, and the other half is mom's book club plus a bunch of cousins who need zero jargon and just want a nice toast about their aunt and uncle.

Could you write me the toast? Something like 4-5 minutes read out loud, concrete rather than lecture-y (please, no opening like "as you all know, in game theory..."), warm enough that mom tears up a little, and honest enough about the actual paradox that dad's old colleagues end up nodding instead of wincing. Bonus points if it lands on a line that's actually toastable, something people can raise a glass to at the end.
