I'm a backend engineer, and about two years ago a production incident turned into an unlikely hobby. Two of our services couldn't safely agree that a transaction had gone through, because any acknowledgment message could itself get lost, and confirming the confirmation just pushes the same problem back a level, forever. My tech lead shrugged and called it "the Two Generals' Problem," and mentioned that computer scientists like Halpern and Moses had formalized it decades ago using the same apparatus that game theorists and philosophers call "common knowledge." That comment turned into two years of genuine reading on the side, and eventually into a small interview podcast I run in the evenings, nominally about decision theory for engineers.

For the next episode I've landed an epistemic game theorist who has built her career defending Robert Aumann's 1995 result that common knowledge of rationality forces the backward-induction outcome in generic games of perfect information (roughly, games where no two outcomes tie in payoff). The rationality in question is what the literature calls "substantive" rationality -- a player would behave rationally even at nodes her own earlier choices have effectively ruled out -- and on my guest's view, two flawlessly rational players facing a hundred-round centipede game will see the first player grab the pot on move one, full stop; anything else that happens between real people is evidence the idealization wasn't met, not evidence against the theory. In her book she also cites Rubinstein's electronic-mail game approvingly, as proof that common knowledge is a genuine all-or-nothing phenomenon rather than something reachable by degrees.

I don't want the interview to just be her restating that thesis for forty minutes, so could you draft eight to ten questions that push on where the actual literature pushes back, each with a short follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give? I want at least one question on the Aumann/Stalnaker split over how to interpret the counterfactual buried inside "substantive rationality," since Stalnaker used essentially the same formal setup to prove the opposite conclusion; at least one on the McKelvey-Palfrey centipede experiments, where subjects keep the game running well past the first move in the overwhelming majority of trials, and what that's supposed to show if her claim is meant to be normative rather than predictive; and at least one that turns her own citation of the electronic-mail game back on her -- if "almost common knowledge" collapses to a completely different equilibrium than the real thing no matter how many confirmations get exchanged, why should we trust that true common knowledge is a coherent condition that any pair of embodied, noisily-communicating agents could ever actually satisfy, rather than a mathematical idealization with no realistic instance on either end of it.
