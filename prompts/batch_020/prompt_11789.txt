Write a short story built around the Löbian obstacle to self-trusting agents.

Löb's theorem entails that a consistent formal system at least as strong as arithmetic cannot prove its own general soundness schema ("whatever I can prove is true") for arbitrary statements; it can only be vouched for by a strictly stronger theory, never by itself. This creates a genuine bind for any agent that wants to build, or become, a successor at least as capable as itself while proving in advance that the successor's judgment deserves trust: naive formalizations of that trust either fail outright or force the successor to be strictly weaker than its parent. Make this specific bind — not generic anxieties about betrayal or misalignment — the story's central tension, whether it plays out between an agent and a successor, an heir, a copy, or a future self, and let the logical structure of the problem itself, rather than just the vague theme of "trust," drive what actually happens in the plot. Dramatize it through consequence and plot rather than through a character explaining Löb's theorem out loud. Setting, characters, and genre are otherwise open.
