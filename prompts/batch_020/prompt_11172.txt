I sit on the incidental-findings subcommittee of my university's biobank, and we're close to voting on a policy for a case that keeps recurring in our aging-cohort study: participants who consented to research-grade whole-genome sequencing and turn out to carry two copies of the APOE4 allele, which substantially raises lifetime Alzheimer's risk and for which there is still no approved intervention that prevents or delays onset in people who are asymptomatic. At enrollment, participants ticked a box indicating whether they wanted this kind of finding disclosed if it ever turned up, and a sizable minority said no.

One subcommittee member, a former decision theorist now doing public-health research, keeps invoking what she calls Good's theorem -- the result that for a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer, the expected value of costless evidence is never negative -- to argue that the "no" box is tracking discomfort rather than anything a sound policy should defer to, and that disclosure by default is simply what the math recommends once you're honest about it. Two of our clinicians push back with the "right not to know" tradition that grew out of Huntington's-disease predictive testing, together with documented cases of anxiety and family disruption following unsolicited disclosure of similarly grim, non-actionable results -- disruption that's compounded here, since a participant's APOE4 status also implies something about their parents and siblings, none of whom consented to learn anything. I've read enough Bayesian-inflected blogs over the years to recognize the theorem on sight, but I've never seen anyone actually work out whether it bears on a case like this one, and nobody on the subcommittee seems able to either dismiss the argument or accept it outright.

Could you draft the short recommendation memo the subcommittee will bring to the full board next month? It should land on one specific policy for this class of finding rather than calling for further study -- covering at least the default (opt-in vs. opt-out at enrollment), the threshold a finding must meet to be disclosed at all, and how, if at all, biological relatives factor in. And since the decision-theoretic argument is the one nobody here has managed to actually rebut rather than just talk past, please give it a serious hearing on its own terms: say specifically which of the theorem's idealizing assumptions -- about the evidence being free, the relevant utility function being fixed and already complete, there being a single decision-maker, and so on -- this situation satisfies or fails to satisfy, rather than just asserting that real participants aren't idealized Bayesian agents.
