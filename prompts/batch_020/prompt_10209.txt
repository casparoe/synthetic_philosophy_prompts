A friend and I spent much of last night arguing about a news story in which a care home was accused of causing a resident's death by failing to check on her regularly. My friend took it as obvious that the understaffing had caused the death, but when I pushed on what distinguishes "causing" from merely "failing to prevent," I got stuck: on the simplest counterfactual test -- she wouldn't have died had someone intervened -- the same seems true of every other person who could have intervened and didn't, from a rarely visiting relative to an inspector who never came. Nobody wants to call all of these people causes of her death, but it isn't obvious what principled line excludes them short of denying that omissions can ever be causes, which seems to concede too much. I gather philosophers of causation have grappled with exactly this over-generation problem. Could you explain why it counts as a genuine puzzle for theories of causation rather than just a question of blame, walk me through the main strategies that have been proposed for containing it, and say which of them, if any, strikes you as the most defensible?
