Here is the response as submitted:

"Zhuangzi's equanimity about death rests on two well-known episodes that, on inspection, turn out to make the same underlying move. First, when his wife dies (ch. 18, 'Perfect Happiness'), Hui Shi finds him drumming on a basin instead of mourning; Zhuangzi explains that she has simply undergone another transformation of the same kind that first gave her form out of formlessness, on the model of the four seasons, so grief would only show he had failed to understand fate. Second, in the same chapter, Zhuangzi pokes an old skull with his carriage whip on the road to Chu, and that night dreams that it speaks to him, explaining that death has no ruler above and no subjects below, none of the toil of the four seasons, and a freedom no king on his throne could match; when Zhuangzi, through the Arbiter of Fate, offers to restore its flesh and bones and send it back to family and friends, the skull refuses. Taken together, the two episodes commit Zhuangzi to essentially the move Epicurus makes later in the West: since death removes the very subject who could be harmed by it, there is no 'you' left for death to happen to, so fearing it is a conceptual mistake. The skull passage even goes one better than Epicurus, since it gives us a dead person's own testimony that non-existence is preferable to life, which is about as close to a first-hand report from the other side as this kind of argument could ever get. The two episodes differ in tone, one consoling and one closer to a joke, but underneath it is the same argument told twice."
