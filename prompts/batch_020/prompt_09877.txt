So this happened to me a few weeks ago and I still cant quite shake it off. My work health plan has one of those "wellness" programs where you sync up a fitness ring or watch and get a break on your premium. I skimmed the consent form and clicked agree without really reading it, same as everyone does. Then a coworker who apparently reads privacy policies for fun pointed out that the wellness vendor isnt just passing HR a yes/no "did you hit your step goal" flag - they're feeding detailed sleep and menstrual-cycle data through to the insurer's underwriting side, who fold "aggregated" versions of it into how the whole group's rate gets set at renewal. I brought it up with my sister, who's a corporate lawyer, expecting her to be as annoyed as I was, and she just shrugged and said "you agreed to it, what's the problem." Which, fine, technically true, but it still feels like an actual violation and not just an annoyance I have no standing to complain about, and I'd like to be able to say why beyond "it feels icky."

I went down a bit of a rabbit hole after that reading philosophy-of-privacy stuff - Westin and Fried on privacy as basically control over information about yourself, then Helen Nissenbaum's "contextual integrity" idea that a privacy violation is about information moving out of the context whose norms governed it in the first place, not really about secrecy or consent at all. Can you build me the strongest version of the argument that Nissenbaum's framework explains what's actually wrong with my situation better than the control/consent story my sister was implicitly running does? I want you to actually engage with two comebacks a defender of the consent view would make, not just wave at them: first, the obvious one, that I did consent, so on a theory where privacy just is control over your own information there's nothing left to explain; and second, the one that actually stumps me, that contextual integrity is supposed to work by pointing to a breach of settled, entrenched norms for a context, but wearable-to-underwriter data flows are new enough that there isn't really a settled norm yet either way, so how does Nissenbaum's account get to call this a violation rather than just a new norm quietly forming in real time. I don't want a survey of the positions, I want an actual argument that picks a side and defends it against those two comebacks specifically.
