Working on a piece about how "I know this for certain because I made it myself" arguments hold up over time, and Hobbes is turning out to be my best case study. He got so hooked on Euclid after stumbling on the Pythagorean theorem proof in his 40s (Aubrey's famous story: Hobbes reads the proposition, says "this is impossible!", then works backward step by step until he's convinced) that he decided geometry is the one genuinely certain science -- precisely becuase we ourselves stipulate the definitions and construct the figures -- and he wanted his whole civil science built on that same deductive model. (Also: he spent something like two decades insisting he'd squared the circle and never once backed down against Wallis.)

Here's what I can't work out: if Hobbes had somehow lived to see (a) non-Euclidean geometry, which showed Euclid's axioms weren't the only consistent option, and (b) Gödel's incompleteness theorems -- would either one actually threaten his specific reason for thinking geometry (and therefore his politics) is certain, or is the "we made it, so we can't be wrong about it" move weirdly insulated from both? Give me a threat score 1-10 for each, plus the reasoning behind it.
