I am researching a piece on the ethics of expert and scientific communication, and in the process I have repeatedly run into a long-standing dispute in epistemology over what actually makes an assertion appropriate: is it enough that what one says is true, or must one actually know it? Write a dialogue between two epistemologists who hold opposing views on this question: one defends the knowledge account of assertion (one may assert P only if one knows P), the other a truth account (an assertion is proper whenever P is true, whether or not one knows it). Rather than trading slogans, have them work through two specific cases together, each responding directly to what the other has just said. First, the lottery case: why does it seem improper to flatly assert that a particular ticket in a fair, million-ticket lottery has lost, even though the claim is true and justified to a probability of .999999? Second, Jennifer Lackey's cases of "selfless assertion," in which a speaker sincerely and properly asserts a well-supported claim that she does not herself believe — for instance, a science teacher who presents a well-established theory to her class despite rejecting it on religious grounds. Each philosopher should be pressed on whichever of the two cases is hardest for their own view, and the dialogue should end wherever it honestly ends, even if that is a standoff rather than a tidy resolution.
