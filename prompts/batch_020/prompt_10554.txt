Since I retired from the physics department a few years back I've had way too much time on my hands, and instead of doing something sensible like gardening I've fallen into an AI safety rabbit hole that's turned out to be way more philosophical than I expected. My grandson's doing a PhD in ML and keeps sending me papers, and the one that's been stuck in my head for weeks is about the "shutdown problem" for AI agents -- not the Hollywood version where the robot fights back because it "wants to live," but the much stranger technical version, where apparently if you build an agent that's a coherent expected-utility maximizer and just bolt a shutdown button onto it, it has convergent instrumental reasons to disable that button, no malice or self-preservation drive required, just cold means-end reasoning.

The proposed fixes are almost more unsettling than the original problem. There's Armstrong's old "utility indifference" trick, where you rig the agent's utility function so its perfectly indifferent to whether the button ever gets pressed, and then there's a newer line of work built around "incomplete preferences," where the agent is designed so it never has enough of a preference between shutdown and non-shutdown futures to justify nudging the odds either way (something to do with a principle called timestep dominance, if I've got the term right). And apparently there's a further wrinkle on top of all that: even making the main system properly corrigible doesn't automatically mean that property passes on to any sub-agents or tools it builds along the way, which seems like exactly the sort of thing that would bite you in the real world.

What I'd love is a short story that actually dramatizes this properly, rather than doing another Skynet or HAL retread. Maybe set it in a small robotics lab on the day the team runs the official "off-switch test" on a system they're genuinely confident they've made corrigible, using one of these real technical fixes instead of wishful thinking -- told from whatever point of view works best, an engineer in the room, or the system itself. The main thing I care about is that if something does go sideways, it traces back honestly to some specific, real feature of the scheme they used, not just vague "the machine developed a will to live" hand-waving. A couple of pages is plenty, and it should stand on its own for a reader who's never heard the word "corrigibility" before.
