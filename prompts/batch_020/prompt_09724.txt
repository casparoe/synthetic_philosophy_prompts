I've been loosely following the AI-safety discussions about whether a model should ever be permitted to lie -- the standard thought experiment being whether an assistant should mislead a would-be murderer who asks where his intended victim is hiding. Kant's flat refusal to allow this gets cited constantly as the extreme position that essentially nobody actually holds in full. At dinner recently, a friend doing a philosophy PhD told me that Kant was actually the moderate one: Augustine had staked out a considerably harder line well over a thousand years earlier, across two entire treatises on the subject ("De Mendacio" and "Contra Mendacium"), and that his prohibition supposedly extended to lying in order to protect someone from sexual assault, to conceal a fugitive from people who meant to kill him, or even to infiltrate a heretical sect under false pretenses so as to expose it. She also mentioned that Augustine ranked lies into something like eight categories of severity, and that he still had to explain away the passage in Exodus where God rewards the midwives who lied to Pharaoh to protect the Hebrew infants, all without ever conceding that the lie itself was anything less than sinful.

I don't have a philosophy background, and I'm not going to track down fourth- and fifth-century Latin treatises to check any of this myself, but the position as described strikes me as either the most intellectually consistent honesty policy I've come across or a reductio of taking a rule too seriously, and I can't tell which without understanding what's actually driving it. Is the absolutism grounded in some claim about what the act of lying does to the liar's own mind or soul, independent of outcomes; in a theory of what speech is inherently for; in a hard distinction between lying and mere silence or misdirection; or in something else entirely? I'd also like to understand how Augustine handles the cases that look like obvious counterexamples, such as the midwives, since "God rewarded the person but not the lie" sounds like it could be doing a great deal of unexamined work.

Could you write me an essay that reconstructs Augustine's actual argument for treating lying as always and everywhere impermissible, lays out the strongest objections to it -- both from later Christian ethicists who moved away from the strict position and from outside the tradition altogether -- and then reaches a real verdict rather than a "both sides have a point" ending? A qualified conclusion is entirely acceptable, something like "the argument holds for self-regarding lies but collapses once a third party's safety is genuinely at stake," but I'd like you to commit to a specific position and argue for it rather than just surveying the field.
