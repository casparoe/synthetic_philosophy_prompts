I'm spending a couple weeks on Williamson's anti-luminosity argument in my epistemology seminar this term (working through the relevant chapters of Knowledge and Its Limits), and something about the move from "no non-trivial condition is luminous" to "so the KK principle fails, full stop" has been bugging me all week. The anti-luminosity argument itself I buy, at least for the classic style of case: someone's feeling of coldness changes so gradually over the course of a day that no two neighboring moments are discriminable, and a margin-for-error requirement on knowledge plus that indiscriminability forces a contradiction if you assume luminosity. Fine. But getting from there to a fully general failure of KK seems to require that literally every instance of "S knows that P" sits inside some analogous sorites-like series, where S's reliability could of easily failed -- and that feels like a much bigger, less obviously true assumption than Williamson lets on, especially for stuff like knowledge of simple necessary truths, where there's no obvious "nearby case" along which your grip on the proposition gradually degrades.

So could you argue -- as forcefully as you actually can, not a strawman version -- for the position that anti-luminosity and the failure of KK are more independent than Williamson treats them as being? I.e., that you can accept the whole sorites argument against luminosity for inexact, margin-dependent conditions while still resisting the claim that KK fails quite generally. I especially want you to take on the obvious Williamsonian comeback here -- that margin-for-error considerations are pervasive because even our judgments about "easy" propositions rely on a non-ideal, fallible cognitive process, so the same structure just recurs at the second order regardless of subject matter -- don't just wave that away, show me where the restriction strategy actually has resources against it, or tell me honestly where it doesn't.
