Isaac Newton devoted more energy to theology than to physics, and much of that effort was strikingly heterodox for a man of his position. He privately concluded that the doctrine of the Trinity was a corruption introduced into Christianity long after Christ, and he drafted, though never published, a closely argued philological case that a key Trinitarian proof-text — the "Johannine Comma" in 1 John 5:7 — was a later scribal insertion absent from the earliest manuscripts, a claim mainstream textual scholarship has since largely come to accept.

At the same time, Newton spent decades treating the prophecies of Daniel and Revelation as authentic, divinely encoded predictions, using them to build a precise chronology of sacred and world history. Modern historical-critical scholarship applies Newton's own kind of philological scrutiny much further, however, and has concluded that Daniel was almost certainly composed centuries after the events it "foretells," making its prophecies retrospective rather than predictive. Given how much of Newton's theological worldview depended on Daniel's authenticity, how do you think he would have reacted to this later scholarship: as a natural, if unwelcome, extension of his own method, or as a conclusion that his deeper commitments would have compelled him to resist? Ground your answer in the specific features of Newton's theology rather than his general religiosity.
