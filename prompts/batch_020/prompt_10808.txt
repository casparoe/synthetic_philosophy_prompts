Here is the student's answer:

Elster borrows the scene in the Odyssey where Ulysses, knowing he will be overwhelmed by desire when he hears the Sirens, has his sailors tie him to the mast and stop their own ears with wax, so that he can hear the song without being able to steer the ship toward it. For Elster this models precommitment: a rational agent, foreseeing a future loss of self-control, uses present reason to narrow their own later options. Constitutions do the same thing for a polity: bills of rights, supermajority amendment rules, and judicial review all make it harder for a future majority to act on a momentary passion, the way the ropes made it harder for Ulysses to steer toward the rocks.

The main disanalogy is that Ulysses could not know in advance exactly how strong the Sirens' pull would be, so tying himself to the mast was really a hedge against his own uncertainty; a constitution, by contrast, is fixed in advance by people who assume they already know better than any future generation what that generation should want, which is a much stronger and more paternalistic claim than anything Ulysses was making about himself.

Elster's concept of a "gag rule" — illustrated by the House of Representatives' refusal to receive antislavery petitions between 1836 and 1844 — shows that removing an issue from the agenda can let an institution keep functioning rather than simply tying its hands, so a constraint can be enabling rather than restrictive. This is really a friendly amendment to the Ulysses story rather than a challenge to it: Ulysses's ropes were also enabling, since they let the ship keep moving toward Ithaca instead of onto the rocks, so the mast and the gag rule are essentially the same mechanism operating at different scales, and the precommitment picture survives largely intact.
