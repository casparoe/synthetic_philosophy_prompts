So in my science & religion seminar we just read Galileo's letter to the Grand Duchess Christina back-to-back with Bellarmine's 1615 letter to Foscarini, and now I can't decide who actually has the better epistemic principle here, not just who ended up "right." Bellarmine's move is basically: fine, use heliocentrism as a calculating device, but don't touch the traditional reading of scripture unless there's a "true demonstration" that the earth moves, and he's pretty openly skeptical that's even attainable. Galileo, leaning heavily on Augustine, argues you're obligated to reinterpret the text once something about nature is clearly known, and that clinging to the literal reading is what actually risks embarassing the faith down the line. What bugs me is that the "true demonstration" Galileo himself eventually offered (the tides argument in the Dialogue) turned out to be wrong, and we didn't get an actual proof of earth's motion until stellar aberration almost a century later. So comparing their two general principles rather than who happened to be right in hindsight: whose standard for when new knowledge should override an authoritative text's traditional interpretation is actually more defensible? Is Bellarmine's caution a sound epistemic policy or basically an unfalsifiable dodge, and does Galileo's own accommodation principle escape that same charge? Genuinely torn on this one, and please don't just give me the standard "church bad, Galileo good" story.
