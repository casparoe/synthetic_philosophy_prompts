Over a forty-year career as an experimental physicist working mainly in laser spectroscopy, I used the machinery of quantum mechanics constantly but rarely had occasion to think hard about its interpretation; Bell's inequalities registered, at the time, as little more than a mildly curious problem set from graduate school. Since retiring I have been trying to fill that gap by reading more seriously in the philosophy of physics, currently as part of a small monthly discussion group hosted by a local university's philosophy department. This term's readings on hidden-variable theories have left me with a puzzle I haven't been able to resolve on my own.

My recollection from graduate school was that Bell's theorem, together with its later experimental vindication by Aspect in the 1980s and, still more decisively, by the loophole-free tests that followed decades afterward (work for which Aspect eventually shared a Nobel Prize with Clauser and Zeilinger), had delivered a more or less final verdict against hidden-variable theories as a class. What I had not appreciated until now is that Bell's own theorem grew directly out of his engagement with David Bohm's 1952 pilot-wave reformulation of quantum mechanics (itself a revival of an idea de Broglie had proposed and abandoned decades earlier), and that this same pilot-wave approach, now developed rigorously as Bohmian mechanics by Dürr, Goldstein, and Zanghì and defended philosophically by Maudlin and others, is still treated as a serious, internally consistent hidden-variable theory, apparently undisturbed by the very theorem it helped provoke. This strikes me as close to a contradiction: either I have simply misremembered what Bell's theorem establishes, or Bohmian mechanics has found some way of quietly stepping around a conclusion to which it should, by rights, be subject. Could you clarify exactly what Bell's theorem does and does not rule out, and where precisely Bohmian mechanics parts ways with the assumptions that go into the proof? I would like to be able to explain this coherently at our next meeting, rather than simply reporting that I found it puzzling.
