A friend of mine who is finishing a philosophy PhD recently sent me an excerpt from Kierkegaard's essay "The Present Age," half-joking that someone who works in tech would find it more relevant than she originally had. She turned out to be right: although the piece is ostensibly about newspapers and pamphlets in 1840s Copenhagen, its account of what Kierkegaard calls "the public" reads like a strangely precise diagnosis of anonymous online mobs, engagement-driven outrage cycles, and the way an ordinary person can suddenly become the internet's designated target for a day. From what I've since read, Kierkegaard's objection wasn't to crowds or opinions as such, but to a specific abstraction that he thought the press manufactures: a "public" with no concrete members, incapable of ever assembling anywhere or being held responsible for anything, which lets each individual borrow its authority and dissolve their own judgment into it while still feeling entitled to an opinion on everything. I was also surprised to learn that this wasn't purely theoretical for him personally: after a public dispute with a critic, he spent months as the target of relentless caricature in a Copenhagen satirical paper called The Corsair, mocked in print and, by his own account, recognized and jeered at by strangers in the street who had never read a word he had written — which struck me as a nineteenth-century version of getting dragged online by people who've only seen the screenshot.

I'd like you to write a short story, a page or two long, that dramatizes this specific idea rather than just gesturing vaguely at "social media is toxic." Feel free to set it wherever seems natural to you — inside a platform's trust-and-safety or growth team, in the life of someone who unexpectedly becomes a target, or from the perspective of whoever benefits from the pile-on — but I would like the plot itself to make Kierkegaard's more precise claims felt: that "the public" is an abstraction with no hands and no fixed address, that it lets people borrow its authority without ever personally risking anything, and that this is close to what he meant by "leveling" — an erosion of real individuality and commitment that happens exactly because no one involved has to own their part in it. I would much rather this come through in concrete scenes and choices than have a character pause to explain the theory out loud.
