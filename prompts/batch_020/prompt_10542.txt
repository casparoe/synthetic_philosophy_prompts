So my cousin Wei is getting married next month and because I'm the "philosophy person" in the family (I'm doing a PhD in comparative/Chinese philosophy, and Ames and Rosemont happen to be on my comps list this semester) I've somehow ended up with toast duty. Her dad is old school and genuinely believes marriage is one of the wu lun, that you don't really become a full person until you're properly embedded in your relationships, ritual and all. Her fiancé's friend group, meanwhile, is going to walk in expecting the standard American toast about two independent people who found "the one" and complete each other. And Wei herself, who's finishing a JD and is not shy about her opinions, has told me more than once, half joking, that if my toast makes her sound like she's dissolving into "wife" she is never speaking to me again.

Here's what I actually want, and it's less a vibe than an argument: I want the toast to genuinely use Confucian role ethics as its spine, not just sprinkle in some Analects lines for flavor. The real claim -- that there's no discrete, pre-relational Wei who exists first and then chooses to enter a marriage contract, but that who she is gets constituted and cultivated through the roles and relations she takes up (the "focus in a field" language, if that's a useful hook) -- and that this is supposed to be a genuine third option, not liberal individualism and not just virtue ethics with chopsticks, since Ames and Rosemont are pretty insistent its not the second thing either. But the toast also has to actually answer Wei's worry using the tradition's own resources instead of just reassuring vibes -- something like yi (appropriateness) and the Mencius bit about quan, moral discretion overriding rigid li in hard cases (the drowning sister-in-law example is the standard one), to make the point that filling a role well was never supposed to mean rule-following, it takes judgment and nerve, so there's actually more room in this view for her being sharp and difficult and independent than her worry assumes.

Can you actually draft the toast, not just outline the argument -- something that reads aloud in about 4-5 minutes, has one genuinely funny (not cheesy) line in it somewhere, and works as a toast about Wei specifically rather than a seminar on Zhou dynasty kinship structures. Ideally it also quietly dissolves the dad-vs-friend-group culture clash rather than picking a side.
