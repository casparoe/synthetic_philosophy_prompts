For the past few months I have been teaching myself the basics of rational choice theory by reading whatever I can find online, and my interest in the subject actually started with the old fable about the servant who sees Death in the marketplace of Baghdad, flees on his master's horse to Samarra to escape her, and then learns that Death had an appointment with him there all along. Recently I discovered that decision theorists built a formal version of this same story, usually set in Damascus and Aleppo instead of Baghdad and Samarra, in which a man who is given a strange, searching look by Death must decide whether to stay put or flee to the other city, knowing that Death has already worked out, with near-perfect reliability, exactly where he will end up that night. From what I can gather, philosophers use this scenario, together with a good deal of expected-utility mathematics, to establish essentially the same moral as the original tale: that whichever city the man picks, the outcome has already been settled, so his deliberation cannot really change anything.

What I have not been able to work out, even after going back and forth about it with a couple of friends, is why this needed to become a serious topic in academic decision theory instead of simply remaining a cautionary tale about fate. If the point of the exercise is to show, with mathematical rigor, that facing a sufficiently reliable predictor makes deliberation pointless, doesn't that make "Death in Damascus" just a technical proof of fatalism dressed up in the language of utility functions? I would really appreciate it if you could explain what conclusion this puzzle is actually meant to establish, and why decision theorists thought it required so much formal machinery to make what looks, on the surface, like a very old point about the futility of trying to outrun one's fate.
