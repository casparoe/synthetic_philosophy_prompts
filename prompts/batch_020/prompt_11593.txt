I've spent the last few weeks reporting a feature on the strange afterlife of the continuum hypothesis, and the more set theorists I talk to, the more I suspect the real disagreement dividing them is philosophical rather than mathematical. Everyone agrees on the technical facts: Gödel's constructible universe L satisfies CH (indeed the full generalized continuum hypothesis), Cohen's forcing method produces models in which CH fails, and stronger forcing axioms such as Martin's Maximum push the size of the continuum all the way up to ℵ₂. What people disagree about is what this independence means. One camp, the universists, holds that there is a single, definite universe of sets, so CH is simply true or simply false, and its independence from ZFC only shows that ZFC is too weak to tell us which; the task ahead is to find new, well-motivated axioms — large cardinal axioms, say, or determinacy hypotheses — that will eventually decide it, much as Gödel himself suspected CH would turn out to be false once the right axioms were found. The other camp, the multiversists, following Joel David Hamkins, denies that there is any single background universe to be right or wrong about: there is instead a multiverse of equally legitimate set-theoretic universes linked by forcing extensions and inner models, so that CH is simply true in some of them and false in others, and asking for the one real verdict is as confused as asking whether the parallel postulate is really true once you've seen both Euclidean and non-Euclidean geometries.

For the piece, I'd like to dramatize this disagreement rather than just summarize it side by side. Could you write a dialogue between a committed universist and a committed multiversist, staged as an exchange over coffee right after a colloquium talk on forcing? I'd like the universist to press the multiversist on whether "true here, false there" is really an alternative answer to the continuum problem or just an elegant way of admitting there isn't one, and to invoke the track record of axioms like projective determinacy in settling other independent statements as grounds for optimism that CH could eventually be settled too. I'd like the multiversist to come back with the geometry analogy in more detail, and to ask the universist what kind of evidence could ever favor one new axiom over an equally natural rival that decides CH the other way. Both characters should be equally sharp and genuinely responsive to each other's points — no straw men — with the dialogue ending with the disagreement sharpened rather than conveniently resolved.
