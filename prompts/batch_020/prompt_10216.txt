I'm a graduate student in philosophy, and this term I'm taking a seminar on the epistemic foundations of game theory that has turned out to be far more consuming than I expected. Two weeks ago we worked through Aumann's 1995 proof that common knowledge of rationality (CKR), in a game of perfect information, entails the backward-induction outcome — so in the centipede game, both players should stop at the very first opportunity — and then spent the rest of the session on the "paradox of backward induction" that Pettit and Sugden, Bicchieri, and Binmore have each pressed against results like it: the argument seems to require each player to believe, at every node, that the other will still play rationally from here on, even at nodes that could only be reached if that same opponent had already deviated from rationality once.

Our instructor thinks Aumann's proof survives this objection. Her defense is that the rationality actually tested by a player's choices along the path of play is not the same thing as the rationality the proof needs at every node, including ones nobody ever reaches, and that the distinction isn't ad hoc, because the epistemic model fixes each player's complete contingent plan — and beliefs about the other's plan — at the very outset, before the game (and any deviation) has even happened. I have to give a short critical response to this in class next Monday, and I don't yet have a clean way of articulating what's wrong with her move, even though it still feels to me like it's begging the question against the critics.

Could you construct the strongest available argument for the position that Aumann's theorem does not actually vindicate backward induction in the centipede game — that common knowledge of rationality cannot coherently underwrite the specific beliefs about continued play, at off-path and counterfactually-reached nodes, that the backward-induction argument needs at every step? I'd like the argument to engage directly with the "fixed plan at the outset" defense described above, rather than falling back on the more familiar empirical point that experimental subjects don't actually unravel centipede games the way the theory predicts — that fact is suggestive, but it isn't itself an argument about what common knowledge of rationality can and cannot support, which is what I actually need for Monday.
