I have no formal training in philosophy or theology, but for the last year or so I have spent a good deal of my free time working through the free-will-and-foreknowledge debate on my own, starting from recorded debates featuring William Lane Craig and then following the footnotes backward into the primary and secondary literature. That is how I ended up absorbed in Molinism: the view that, logically prior to any decision about what to create, God already possessed "middle knowledge" of every counterfactual of creaturely freedom — that is, of what any possible person would freely do in any given circumstance — and that this is what allows him to providentially arrange history around genuinely free creatures instead of simply determining them outright.

The piece I cannot get past is the grounding objection, which as far as I can tell traces back to a 1977 paper by Robert Adams and was later pressed hardest by William Hasker, himself one of the leading open theist philosophers. The complaint, as I understand it, is that these counterfactuals cannot be true "in advance" of creation because there is nothing for their truth to consist in: the person in question does not exist yet, and in any feasible world God does not go on to actualize, they never will, so the very choice being described never actually takes place for anything to ground it in; and if the truth is grounded instead in that person's character or individual essence, the essence seems to either settle the outcome, which looks fatal to libertarian freedom, or fail to settle it, in which case it isn't doing any grounding work at all. I have come across the standard Molinist replies — that truths need not have truthmakers in the first place, that the objection quietly smuggles in a causal theory of free action, that the very same problem afflicts perfectly ordinary future contingents that most people are happy to treat as true or false — but I genuinely cannot tell whether these hold up or simply relocate the difficulty. A friend from my old Bible study is convinced the whole apparatus is ad hoc, and I keep wanting to defend it, but neither of us can really argue both sides well enough to settle anything.

So rather than another summary of the state of play, I would like to watch two people who actually hold these views argue it out directly. Could you write a dialogue between a Molinist and an open theist debating specifically whether middle knowledge can survive the grounding objection? I want both of them portrayed as equally capable: the open theist should press the objection in its strongest form rather than a caricature, and the Molinist should answer with specific, substantive moves rather than simply insisting that God can somehow do it anyway. At some point, I would also like the Molinist to turn the objection back on the open theist and press them on whether their own view can consistently avoid a parallel grounding problem for whatever future-tensed or counterfactual truths about free action they are still willing to affirm. It doesn't need to end with a decisive winner — I would just like both of them to have genuinely engaged with the other's strongest point at least once, rather than talking past each other.
