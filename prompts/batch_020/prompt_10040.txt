So I occasionally guest-lecture for a "reporting on science" module at a journalism school near me, and this term I set the students a short written question on the replication crisis, since half of them will end up covering some university's press release with a p-value in it someday whether they like it or not. Now I'm the one stuck grading the answers, which is a trap I set for myself, because Im not a statistician, I just play one in print sometimes — I've been circling this stuff since the Bem ESP paper back in 2011 basically dared the entire field to take its own statistics seriously. Anyway, here's the question I gave them:

"Explain, in a paragraph or two, why some methodologists think the replication crisis in psychology is at least partly a statistical problem rooted in the difference between frequentist and Bayesian inference — and why other methodologists think that framing is misleading. Your answer should touch on (a) what a p-value does and doesn't tell you, (b) 'researcher degrees of freedom' and p-hacking, and (c) whether switching to Bayesian methods would, on its own, solve the problem."

And here's the answer from one particular student that I have to grade and comment on:

"A p-value is the probability of getting data as extreme as, or more extreme than, what you actually observed, assuming the null hypothesis is true. People often confuse this with the probability that the null hypothesis itself is true given the data, but that's a posterior probability, which is Bayesian territory, not frequentist. Because p-values are so easy to game, researchers exploit what Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn labeled 'researcher degrees of freedom' in their famous 2011 paper — trying different exclusion rules, covariates, or ways of splitting the sample until p sneaks under .05 — and this kind of p-hacking, combined with journals' preference for publishing significant results, is a big reason why the Open Science Collaboration's 2015 replication project found that only around a third of the 100 psychology studies it tried to replicate actually held up.

Switching to Bayesian statistics would fix most of this, because a Bayesian credible interval tells you the actual probability that the true effect falls in that range, whereas a confidence interval, strictly speaking, doesn't. Bayesian inference also conditions only on the data you actually collected rather than on some fixed stopping rule, so it's immune to the optional-stopping tricks that inflate false positives under frequentist testing — you can't hack a Bayesian analysis just by peeking at the data and stopping early. So really, the replication crisis is substantially a frequentist problem, which lines up with the fact that fields that are already Bayesian, like physics, haven't had anything like the same crisis."

I want a real score out of 20 plus a letter grade, and then specific comments backing it up — maybe organized as a little rubric, like JSON with fields for p_value_definition, researcher_degrees_of_freedom, bayesian_solution_claim, and citations, each with its own short note, so I can see exactly where points were won or lost instead of one paragraph of general impression.
