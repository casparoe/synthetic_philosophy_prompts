So I write about AI and tech for a living, and I was at dinner last week with two researcher friends who both work on interpretability, and they got into an arguement that I haven't been able to shake since. We'd been talking about one of those papers where a team can watch a model's internal "reasoning" trace right before it gives someone a misleading answer, and the conversation turned to whether it even makes sense to say the model "decided" to mislead the person, or "wanted" to avoid some outcome - versus that just being a nice story we layer on top of what's actually happening underneath.

One friend, the more skeptical one, argued that all the goal/intention talk is basically a story told after the fact: once you fix the weights and the input tokens, the output is completely nailed down by matrix multiplications on a chip, so the psychological-sounding description isn't doing any real causal work - it's just riding along for free on top of a story that's already been fully told at the hardware level. The other friend thought that was way too strong, because you could say almost the same thing about a human brain (fix the neurons and the inputs and the behavior is "completely nailed down" too), and we don't usually conclude from that that people's reasons and beliefs never really cause anything.

Anyway, the skeptical friend's argument feels like it proves too much, but I honestly can't work out where it breaks, if it does. Is there a principled way of saying the "higher," goal-involving description is doing genuine causal work of its own, rather than just freeloading on the physical description, without having to land on either "it's nothing but physics" or some extra, slightly spooky causal ingredient? And is the AI case actually different from the brain case here, or is it the same problem twice, and I just find the conclusion easier to swallow when it's about machines than when it's about people? Would love an actual walk-through of how philosophers usually pick apart this kind of argument, not just a one-line take.
