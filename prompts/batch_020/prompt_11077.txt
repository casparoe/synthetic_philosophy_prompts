I've been slowly working my way through Wittgenstein's On Certainty since I retired (funny place to end up after 40 years in a lab, but here we are), and I'm stuck on the hinge propositions section. Around 341-343 he says our doubts and questions only make sense against a backround of things that just aren't up for doubt - he calls these "hinges" that everything else swings on. Fine so far. But earlier, around 96-99, he's got this whole river metaphor going: some propositions harden into hinges over time while others soften back into ordinary empirical claims that get tested again, and he actually writes that "the river-bed of thoughts may shift."

That's the bit that trips me up. As an old scientist that sounds exactly like what happened to Newtonian mechanics, or the fixity of species before Darwin - rock solid and basically unquestionable for ages, then suddenly back in play. So if hinges are the sort of thing that can turn into ordinary empirical beliefs and back again (kind of like Kuhn's paradigm shifts?), what's actually left of the claim that they're groundless, not answerable to evidence the way normal beliefs are? Why isn't "hinge" just a fancy word for "empirical belief nobody happens to be questioning at the moment"? Can you help me see what would block that collapse, on Wittgenstein's behalf?
