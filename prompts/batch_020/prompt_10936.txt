I'm a second-year PhD student in History, and this came out of my proseminar on nineteenth-century Black political thought last week. We'd just gone through Douglass's 1860 Glasgow speech, "The Constitution of the United States: Is It Pro-Slavery or Anti-Slavery?," and afterward two people in the seminar (not me — I mainly just listened) ended up arguing about it over drinks for like half an hour. Quick context in case it helps: Douglass spent the 1840s as a Garrisonian, agreeing the Constitution was a pro-slavery document — Garrison's line was that it was "a covenant with death, and an agreement with hell" — and that abolitionists should refuse to vote or hold office under it. Then around 1851 he broke with Garrison and flipped to arguing the Constitution, read on its actual words, is (his phrase) a "glorious liberty document," since it never once says "slave" or "slavery," only things like "other Persons" and "Person held to Service or Labour."

Here's roughly how the argument between Mara and Jae went, reconstructed from memory so it's probably a little flattened: (1) Mara said if the framers had really meant to write slavery into the fundamental law they had every chance to just say so, so the deliberate vagueness is itself evidence the enacted text doesn't secure what pro-slavery readings claim, whatever the delegates privately intended. (2) Jae said that's projecting present-day textualism backward — nobody in the room in 1787, or Douglass himself a decade earlier, thought "Person held to Service or Labour" was remotely ambiguous about who it covered, the euphemisms were about delicacy, not a hidden legal opening. (3) Mara pointed to the three-fifths clause: counting slaves at three-fifths rather than in full actually caps slaveholding states' representation relative to counting them as whole persons, so even that clause cuts against being read as a clean pro-slavery win. (4) Jae answered that "less favorable to the South then the maximalist version" isn't the same thing as "anti-slavery" — three-fifths still beats the zero that Northern delegates originally wanted, so Mara was choosing a flattering comparison class. (5) Mara said none of that matters for what the text means as law now, since enactment, not intent, is what actually binds. (6) Jae said that's precisely the move Garrisonian-era Douglass himself rejected, so the real explanation for the switch is that his politics changed — he needed a legal tool for anti-slavery action inside the union instead of disunion — and the textualism got built to fit that, not the other way around.

Neither of them budged and I honestly went back and forth on who was winning, but I couldnt land on an answer.
