I help run a small mutual aid fund in my city — just five of us volunteers pooling monthly donations (usually a few thousand bucks total) and sending one-time cash grants to people who hit an eviction notice, a medical bill, a shutoff notice, whatever. We always get 3-4x more requests than we can fund, so every single month is a rationing problem.

For the past year our policy has been a "need index": a volunteer scores each application 1-10 on income, dependents, how dire the situation sounds, etc, and we fund the top scores until the money's gone. One of our newer volunteers has been on an economics kick lately (started with a Robin Hanson post and spiraled from there) and now argues the whole index is confused at the root. His point is basically that Robbins showed back in the 30s that you can't actually compare how much a marginal $400 matters to person A vs person B — it's not an empirical fact waiting to be measured, its a value judgment wearing a lab coat, which is exactly why later economists like Kaldor and Hicks built "compensation" tests specifically to dodge ever needing interpersonal comparisons at all. So he says our 1-10 score is fake precision dressed up as data, and we should switch to a weighted lottery among everyone who clears a basic eligibility bar, so we stop pretending to rank suffering we can't actually rank. Two of the other volunteers think this is well-actually-ing an obviously decent practice — surely some need comparisons are more defensible than others, the guy about to be literally homeless vs the guy short on a phone bill isnt a close call.

I've lurked on LW and the EA forum for years and this might be the first time this stuff has had real stakes attached instead of being a trolley-problem thought experiment. We vote on next month's policy in two weeks. Could you draft the memo I'll circulate beforehand? I need it to actually land on a recommendation we could run starting next cycle, not just "here are both sides" — but I want it to genuinely wrestle with the interpersonal-comparisons objection instead of waving it off as pedantry, because I suspect he's onto something even if the lottery fix feels wrong to me.
