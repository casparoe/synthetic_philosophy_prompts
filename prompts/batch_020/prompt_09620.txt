I'm a second-year PhD student in economics, currently enrolled in a graduate seminar on information economics and social learning, where we've spent the last few weeks on the Bikhchandani-Hirshleifer-Welch cascade model and Banerjee's herd behavior paper. The result that has stuck with me most is that a sequence of individually rational people, each updating on the actions of those who moved before them rather than on their own private information, can converge on a shared conclusion that looks from the outside like a confident consensus -- even though it may really rest on only one or two people's actual signals, with everyone who follows simply, and quite rationally, copying whoever went before. The professor who teaches the seminar is retiring at the end of this term, and the department is holding a dinner at which six of her students, past and current, will each give a short toast. I'm the only current student on the list, and I've been placed fifth, after four speakers who, judging by the outlines we were all asked to circulate in advance, are each planning to open with some version of the same two stories about her: the red pen she was famous for, and the sabbatical that upended half the literature on equilibrium selection.

I'd like you to write my toast, and I'd like it to be honest about the position this actually puts me in. By the time I stand up, the room will have heard four testimonials that agree with each other closely enough to look, structurally, like exactly the kind of cascade we study in her seminar -- and I have to decide whether to add a fifth confirming data point to it, or to spend my two minutes on something closer to a private signal: a fact about her that nobody else at the dinner happens to have. I'm thinking, for instance, of the semester she spent walking me back through my own job-market paper, not by telling me what was wrong with it but by asking, again and again, what I actually knew as opposed to what I had absorbed from everyone else's models. I don't want the toast to explain informational cascades to anyone -- her own line to us, every single year, was that "a cascade is not a consensus," and I would rather that idea shape the toast than be stated in it -- but I would like one line in there that rewards the economists in the room for knowing exactly what a cascade is, without requiring her sister, her husband, or her college roommates, who will be sitting at the same table, to have read a single working paper. Could you write it for me? It should run about four minutes read aloud, and it should end up sounding like a love letter rather than a literature review.
