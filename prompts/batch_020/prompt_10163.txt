I'm a second-year PhD student in a joint philosophy-of-physics program, and this term the Humeanism/anti-Humeanism debate about laws of nature has gone from background reading to something I have to actually master under pressure. The professor who normally runs our graduate metaphysics seminar is on research leave, and I've ended up covering discussion sections despite having only a working knowledge of the literature myself. Partly to force myself to get on top of the material rather than stay one week ahead of the syllabus, I volunteered to take over hosting duties for our department's small interview podcast, and next month's guest is a philosopher who has built her reputation defending a strong version of dispositional essentialism: the view that fundamental properties like charge and mass have their causal profiles essentially, so that the laws linking them are metaphysically necessary rather than regularities imposed on properties from outside. Unusually, she also extends the analysis to spacetime itself, arguing that metric and topological structure is just as dispositional as charge or spin, which has apparently annoyed people who thought geometry was the last safe haven for categorical properties. Whenever someone presses the modal costs of her view, she tends to reach for the same move: "you're not imagining our world with different laws, you're just imagining a different world with different properties" — a tidy way of converting every counterexample into a change of subject.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that don't just give her room to restate the position? I want at least one question that presses the regress-of-powers worry: if a disposition's identity is fixed by its relations to further dispositions, what stops the whole structure from being either circular or quietly dependent on a categorical anchor after all, and does extending it to spacetime make this better or worse? I want at least one that asks her to locate the real difference between her necessitarianism and Armstrong's, given that his N-relation was explicitly contingent while hers isn't, and that presses on whether the added necessity actually earns its keep or just relocates the very inference problem van Fraassen raised against him. And I want at least one that turns her "different world, different properties" line back on itself by asking what would have to be true for a case to count as the very same property behaving differently, rather than simply being redescribed, after the fact, as a different property behaving exactly as it must. For each of these three, add a short follow-up tailored to the answer she's most likely to give. Close the list with a question asking what observation or modal argument would actually count as evidence against dispositional essentialism, since every objection I've seen her face so far seems to get absorbed by redescription rather than answered.
