I co-host a small podcast that grew out of my city's Astral Codex Ten meetup, and next month we're recording an episode with a moral philosopher whose entire research program is devoted to defending the reality and moral significance of luck against the standard Kantian rebuttal -- the "control principle," on which an agent can only be justly assessed for what was within her control. She leans heavily on Bernard Williams's two classic cases: the lorry driver who, through no fault of his own, kills a child and is nonetheless expected to feel what Williams calls "agent-regret" rather than mere spectator's sympathy, and Gauguin, whose decision to abandon his family for painting turns out, on Williams's telling, to be justified only in retrospect and only if he actually became a great painter -- something he had no way of knowing when he made the choice. Her signature move in every recorded talk of hers I have found is to treat the discomfort these cases provoke as confirming evidence for her view rather than as an objection she owes us an answer to, and I expect she will try the same move on us.

I would like eight or nine interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, that push past the point where she can simply restate Nagel's fourfold taxonomy of resultant, circumstantial, constitutive, and causal luck. Please include at least one question that forces her to distinguish agent-regret from a merely unwarranted psychological artifact -- the pang you feel after a coin flip goes against you, or survivor's guilt -- with a follow-up on what, structurally, makes the lorry driver's case different; one that presses the Gauguin case on the survivorship-bias worry, asking whether her verdict would change had he died obscure and unrecognized, and if not, what the luck is actually doing in the moral evaluation; one on whether causal luck quietly swallows the other three categories, collapsing her position into either a platitude -- nothing anyone does is ever fully non-lucky -- or something closer to Neil Levy's harder conclusion that luck undermines responsibility altogether, and how exactly she resists that slide; and one that connects this to a heuristic our listeners already take for granted, the poker-derived rule against "resulting," which says a decision should be judged by the reasoning behind it rather than by how it turned out, asking her whether punishing completed crimes more harshly than failed attempts reflects a genuine difference in blameworthiness or is merely a legal compromise driven by evidence and deterrence. Please close with a question asking her to name the single weakest of the four categories for her own position, and what would change her mind about it.
