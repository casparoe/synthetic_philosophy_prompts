I'm on my high school's math team, and our coach likes to end practice with "bonus round" tangents that have nothing to do with competition material. Last week he described sports arbitrage betting — placing bets across bookmakers whose odds are out of sync so that you profit no matter the outcome — and used it to introduce the Dutch book argument from Bayesian epistemology: the claim that if your own degrees of belief don't obey the probability axioms, a bookmaker who knew your exact odds could offer you a series of bets that each look fair to you individually, yet that together guarantee you a loss, and that this is supposed to show your credences were irrational all along. Two of my teammates, Owen and Naomi, ended up arguing about what this really establishes, and I've kept turning it over since neither one struck me as clearly winning.

Owen's opening claim was that the theorem settles things outright: since incoherent credences always admit a guaranteed-losing set of bets that you'd accept one by one, that alone proves the credences are irrational, full stop. Naomi objected that this only shows you're vulnerable if a bookmaker who somehow knows your exact credences actually shows up and builds that precise menu of bets; absent that, she said, nothing has really gone wrong for you, so the argument at most establishes a hypothetical risk rather than an actual flaw in your reasoning. Owen replied that the risk just is the flaw, whether or not it's ever cashed in, the way a bridge is badly designed if some specific, rare load would collapse it, even though that load may never actually occur. Naomi came back that the bridge comparison assumes you have to price a bet the same way whether it's offered alone or bundled with others, and that a sharp enough bettor with the very same credences would simply notice the trap partway through and refuse the last bet — which would make this an argument about a naive betting policy, not about the credences underneath it. Owen's last word was that refusing doesn't save Naomi's point: if you still privately think each bet is fair on its own but won't buy them as a set, that mismatch just is the original incoherence, relocated rather than removed.

Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who has the better of it at each point, instead of just declaring an overall winner? I'd also be curious whether some version of the Dutch book argument cleanly escapes Naomi's "sharp bettor" worry, or whether it marks a genuine, still-open weakness in using betting behavior to argue that credences must obey the laws of probability.
