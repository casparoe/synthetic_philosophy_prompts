Explain her argument for why we need a fourth basic value relation — 'parity' — alongside 'better than,' 'worse than,' and 'equally good.' And here's the student's answer: "When two options are genuinely hard to choose between, like a stable corporate job versus a risky startup offer, it's tempting to say one option secretly is better and we just can't tell, or that they're exactly tied. Chang's small-improvement argument is meant to rule out both. Suppose the corporate offer and the startup offer are equally good. Now take the corporate offer and add a small raise to it, giving us corporate-plus, which is obviously a bit better than corporate on its own. If corporate and startup were really equally good, corporate-plus should now be strictly better than startup too, since improving one side of a tie should tip the balance. But intuitively corporate-plus isn't clearly better than startup — it still feels like basically a toss-up. Since a real tie has to be broken by any small improvement and here it isn't, the original relation between corporate and startup can't have been equality. It's not better-than or worse-than either, by hypothesis, so Chang says there has to be a fourth relation, 'parity,' sitting alongside the other three — which is basically her updated version of the value pluralism Isaiah Berlin made famous, where things like liberty and equality can't be measured on one common scale. Since corporate and startup can't be put on a shared scale that lets us rank them, they're incommensurable, and the small-improvement argument is really just one symptom of that deeper incomparability. So the upshot of Chang's view is that in genuinely hard cases, rational comparison runs out and either choice is permissible, since there's no fact of the matter about which is better."
