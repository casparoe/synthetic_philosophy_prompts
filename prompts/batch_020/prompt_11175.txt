As a novelist, I find myself preoccupied by the uncompromising rigor of Tolstoy's *What Is Art?* — a theory of art strict enough that, followed through to its conclusion, it seems to condemn a great deal of what we would ordinarily call great literature, quite possibly including its own author's most celebrated novels. I'd like to see how consistently the theory actually applies when pressed.

Using the specific tests Tolstoy sets out for genuine artistic "infection" — the sincerity, individuality, and clarity of the feeling transmitted — together with his further, content-based requirement that the feeling conveyed be one capable of uniting people rather than only a leisured few, please rank the following works from most to least likely to count as true art by Tolstoy's own late standards:

- His own *War and Peace*
- His own *Anna Karenina*
- Shakespeare's *King Lear*
- A Wagner opera
- Harriet Beecher Stowe's *Uncle Tom's Cabin*
- An anonymous peasant folk tale

```json
[
  {"work": "...", "reason": "..."},
  ...
]
```

where each "reason" is no more than one sentence and names the specific criterion (or criteria) the placement turns on.
