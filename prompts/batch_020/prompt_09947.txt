As a journalist who writes about science and ideas, I'm working on a piece about a strange philosophical detour: David Lewis -- better known for modal realism and possible-worlds metaphysics -- once wrote a short paper called "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?", in which he tests the Christian doctrine that Christ was punished in place of sinners against the ordinary logic of debt: if a third party can pay off your unpaid bill and thereby discharge it, why can't a willing substitute discharge your deserved punishment the same way? I'd like to open the piece with a dialogue that dramatizes the strongest form of this disagreement for readers who will never track down the original paper. Write a dialogue between two philosophers: one who thinks the debt analogy vindicates the coherence of penal substitution, the other who thinks it fails because punishment, unlike debt, is essentially retributive and tracks the wrongdoer's own desert rather than a transferable quantity of suffering. Each should press the other on a specific case rather than simply restate an opening position: the defender needs to explain why criminal punishment isn't just civil liability by another name, and the critic needs to explain why a third party paying someone's fine, or an employer's vicarious liability for an employee's tort, doesn't already show that legal consequences can transfer more easily than the critic allows. Keep the exchange under 450 words, and let the argument, not the staging, do the work.
