Was up way too late last night arguing with my friend about Schopenhauer's chapter on music in WWR (we're both slogging through it for a seminar paper) and we hit a snag neither of us could actually settle. The headline claim is that music is totally different from the other arts -- painting, poetry, sculpture all depict the Platonic Ideas, the "grades of objectification" of the will, but music skips that whole apparatus and is a direct, unmediated copy of the will itself, running parallel to the phenomenal world rather than representing anything in it. Fine so far. But then in that same section (52, I think, near the end of Book 3) he turns around and gives this very specific structural analogy: the bass line corresponds to the lowest grade of the will's objectification, brute unorganized matter, the inner harmonizing voices correspond to the plant and animal kingdoms, and the melody -- the single voice moving freely on top of everything -- corresponds to man's rational, self-conscious striving.

My friends point was that this can't be consistent: that bass/harmony/melody mapping is literally the same hierarchy (mineral, plant/animal, human) that the other arts use to depict the Ideas, so if musics own internal structure tracks that hierarchy, it IS representing the Ideas after all, just very abstractly, and the big "no mediation" claim quietly falls apart. I tried to save him by arguing that music isn't depicting any particular Idea or instance of one, it's replicating the dynamic form of willing -- striving, brief satisfaction, renewed striving -- simultaneously at every level, which is a difference in kind from how a landscape painting depicts gravity or a portrait depicts a person, but I'll be honest, I talked myself into a circle and I'm not totally sold on my own rescue. Can you actually dig into whether this is a real inconsistency in the text, and whether there's a principled way (his own or from people who've written on this since) to keep "direct copy of the will" and "structural analogy to the grades of objectification" from contradicting each other? Even just knowing whether this is a recognized sticking point in the secondary literature, rather than something we're the first two idiots to notice, would help.
