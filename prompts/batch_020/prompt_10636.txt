I teach philosophy for a living – mostly ancient ethics, nothing mathematical – so it may be a professional hazard that I've turned a small puzzle from my own morning routine into more of a formal problem than it probably deserves. Still, it has genuinely got me stuck, and my running partner is no help. I run most mornings along a canal towpath, still half-asleep, and the route home takes me past two footbridges in quick succession that are, as far as I can tell, perceptually identical: same ironwork, same patch of nettles at the near end, same blind bend in the trees just before each one. I need to turn off the towpath immediately after the second bridge to get home the short way. If I turn off after the first bridge instead, I end up on a stretch of road with no verge and have to backtrack a good twenty-five minutes, uncomfortably close to traffic; if I fail to turn off at either bridge and keep going, I'm not lost, but I'm committed to a fifteen-minute loop around the reservoir before I can double back. Turning off correctly at the second bridge costs nothing extra.

The trouble is that by the time I am actually standing at one of these bridges I cannot tell whether it is the first one or the second – and I mean this as a genuine limitation, not laziness: I have tried counting, tried fixing other landmarks in mind, and I still lose track most mornings regardless. So a while back I settled on a rule: whenever I reach a bridge, without trying to work out which one it is, I turn off with some fixed probability that I calculated in advance, using the sweep of my watch's second hand as a makeshift randomizing device. Working out beforehand which fixed probability would make my mornings go best on average was straightforward enough. But on some mornings, standing at the bridge, I try to reason about the decision fresh instead – asking myself, given only that I am standing at some bridge wondering what to do, how likely it is that this is the second one rather than the first – and that line of thought seems to pull toward a different probability than the one I settled on in advance. I cannot tell whether I have found two genuinely different, competing pieces of reasoning, in which case I do not know which one should actually govern my hand at the bridge, or whether I am simply confusing myself and they secretly agree. My running partner insists I am overthinking a trivial problem and should just concentrate harder and count properly, but that option is not really open to me on the terms I have described, and it dodges rather than answers the question of what I should be thinking as I actually stand there. I would genuinely value your opinion: which form of reasoning should I trust, is my partner's dismissal fair, and, practically, should I go on trusting the watch-hand rule at all?
