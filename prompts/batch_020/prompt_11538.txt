I spent thirty-five years as an evolutionary biologist working mostly on the evolution of cooperative behaviour in social insects, and since retiring I have been reading my way, rather unsystematically, through the metaethics literature. Two arguments I have come across keep bumping into one another in my mind, and I would like some help working out whether the resemblance between them is more than superficial.

The first is Sharon Street's "Darwinian Dilemma" for realist theories of value: her claim that a realist who grants, as seems undeniable, that natural selection shaped the content of our evaluative attitudes must choose between an implausible "tracking" account, on which selection somehow favoured evaluative beliefs because they were true, and a far more credible "adaptive link" account, on which selection favoured them purely for their fitness effects, leaving any correspondence with independent evaluative facts an unexplained coincidence. The second is Alvin Plantinga's evolutionary argument against naturalism, which I first encountered decades ago in the rather unwelcome context of intelligent-design polemics, long before I was prepared to take it seriously as philosophy: the claim that if naturalism and evolution are both true, then the probability that our cognitive faculties are reliable, P(R | N&E), is low or inscrutable, since selection tracks survival value rather than truth, so that a naturalist who accepts evolution thereby acquires a defeater for the reliability of all her beliefs, including her belief in naturalism and evolution themselves.

On the surface, both arguments run the same basic move: a selective process tuned to fitness gives no particular reason to expect it to have also delivered true beliefs in some target domain. What I would like is a more careful comparison of their actual logical structure, not just this family resemblance. Does Street's dilemma have anything like the global, self-undermining character of Plantinga's argument, or does its scepticism stay local to moral and evaluative belief, leaving untouched the very faculties needed to state and assess the dilemma in the first place? Relatedly, some realists answer Street with a "third factor" reply, on which some further trait, such as a general capacity for practical reasoning, explains both our having certain evaluative attitudes and their reliability, without either causing the other. Is an analogous move available to the naturalist against Plantinga, or does naturalism lack the resources for it in a way that theism, on Plantinga's own view, does not? I would be grateful for a structural comparison along these lines, together with your own assessment of which of the two arguments poses the more serious problem for its target position, and why.
