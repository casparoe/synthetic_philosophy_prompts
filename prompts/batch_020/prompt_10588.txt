For thirty-five years I worked as a field ornithologist, much of that career devoted to the population dynamics of ground-nesting shorebirds, before retiring six years ago. Retirement has left me time to read a good deal of moral philosophy, a subject my scientific training never required of me, and this new reading has recently collided, rather uncomfortably, with my ongoing work as a trustee of a small reserve that protects one of the last significant breeding colonies of a critically endangered plover.

For close to two decades, the reserve has run an intensive lethal control programme against the introduced rats, feral cats, and stoats that prey on the plovers' eggs and chicks, and the trust credits this programme, quite plausibly, with having pulled the bird back from the edge of local extinction. At a recent board meeting, however, a younger trustee, an ecologist who has been reading much of the same animal-ethics literature I have, pointed out that the predators we trap and poison are just as capable of suffering as the birds we are trying to protect; that many of them, especially those taken by poison, die slowly over a period of days rather than quickly; and that the number of individual predators killed each year is now considerably larger than the number of plover chicks the programme plausibly saves. If what ultimately matters, morally speaking, is the welfare of individual sentient creatures, she asked, on what basis do we justify inflicting so much suffering on one set of animals in order to spare a smaller amount of suffering, or secure a comparatively small number of additional lives, in another? The rest of the board dismissed the question as obviously beside the point: the plover is rare, native, and in genuine danger of disappearing forever, whereas the predators are common and not native to the island at all. When I try to turn that reaction into an actual argument, though, rather than a shared intuition, I find I cannot do it without simply asserting that rarity or native status matters more than the amount of suffering involved, which feels uncomfortably close to assuming exactly what is in question.

Having spent my working life treating the protection of endangered species from introduced predators as simply what responsible conservation looks like, I am no longer sure how to think about programmes like ours now that I have started taking the welfare of individual animals seriously as a moral matter in its own right. Is there a defensible principle that could justify what the reserve is doing, or does my colleague's objection point to something the rest of us have been too quick to dismiss?
