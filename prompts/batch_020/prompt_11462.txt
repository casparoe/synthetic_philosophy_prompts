I'm a novelist finishing a book set in Turin in the autumn of 1888 — Nietzsche's last productive season before his final collapse — in which a fictional acquaintance of his tries to take seriously how much stock the philosopher put in questions of food, weather, and daily regimen. Before I dramatize any of that, I've drafted the passage below, which I expect will become the book's afterword for readers who won't otherwise meet this material, and I'd like an outside read on it before I go further. I'm after correction more than encouragement, and I especially want to know whether the closing move is real thinking or just a flourish.

Readers who know Nietzsche only through the Übermensch or the death of God are usually startled by how much room he gives, in that last sane year, to his stomach. Long stretches of Ecce Homo — in the chapters called "Why I Am So Wise" and "Why I Am So Clever" — are devoted not to metaphysics but to nutrition, place, climate, and recreation: the "little things," as he calls them, which he insists have been treated as beneath a philosopher's dignity while notions like God, soul, and virtue were indulged as though they mattered more. He singles out the Renaissance dietary writer Luigi Cornaro, famous for prescribing an extremely meager diet as the secret of a long life, as a case of a man mistaking his own unusually slow metabolism for a universal law: the ration that kept Cornaro alive would have starved a man built differently, and a cure indifferent to constitution is not wisdom but self-ignorance dressed as advice.

The same logic sits behind his contempt for the vegetarian and temperance movements of his day — which is a little rich, since he avoided alcohol, and beer particularly, for reasons that look a lot like Cornaro's: a constitution that could not handle it. What he objects to, it seems, is not abstinence itself but its moralization into a rule for everyone — the conversion of a personal physiological fact into a universal ought. Beer gets blamed, half seriously, for a certain fog over the modern German mind, as though a nation's philosophy were a symptom of its brewing habits. Behind the joke sits a claim he makes plainly elsewhere: that a philosophy is a confession, an "involuntary memoir" of the body that produced it, and that migraines, failing eyes, and bad digestion are not distractions from a thinker's ideas but part of their cause.

What I want the afterword to leave open, rather than settle, is that this diagnosis seems to catch its author in his own net. If every philosophy is a symptom, his suspicion of metaphysics — built over decades of migraines, ruined eyes, and a lifelong hunt for climates that would not hurt him — should itself be a symptom, no truer for being his. He evidently thought he had an escape: some constitutions are ascending and some declining, and the capacity to diagnose the difference is itself a mark of the ascending kind. I am not convinced that move earns what it needs to earn, and I would rather the afterword name the strain than quietly resolve it.

Could you read this with three questions in mind: first, whether I've represented Nietzsche accurately given how much I'm compressing, and flag anything that's simply wrong; second, whether the final paragraph identifies a genuine philosophical difficulty for him, or whether I've rigged his way out to be either too weak or too convenient; and third, whether the whole thing reads like something a general reader would want to finish, rather than a lecture.
