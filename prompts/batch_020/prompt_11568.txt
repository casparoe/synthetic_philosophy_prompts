I've served for three years on my city's volunteer Business Licensing Board, a seven-person citizen panel that mostly rubber-stamps things like sidewalk-café permits and the occasional tattoo parlor, so nothing on our docket has really tested us until now. A company has applied for a license to open what they call a "companionship suite": a discreet, appointment-only studio where adults can rent private rooms to spend time alone with lifelike synthetic companion dolls. No employee is involved in the actual encounters, the location clears every distance requirement from schools and residences, there's no signage or soliciting visible from the street, and the applicant has satisfied every objective health-and-safety standard in our code. The only obstacle is a clause letting the Board deny or condition a license it finds "contrary to public morals and decency," and three of my six fellow members want to invoke it here, essentially on the grounds that most residents would be disgusted to learn this is happening downtown, even though everyone agrees no actual person is being used, exploited, or harmed by it. Our town attorney says the ordinance would hold up in court either way, so this has become a judgment call for the Board, not a legal question.

After we deadlocked on it two weeks ago, I went looking on my own for why the disagreement felt so familiar, and found out it apparently has a name: the Hart-Devlin debate, from an argument between a British judge and a legal philosopher back in the late 1950s and early '60s over whether the law could rightly punish purely private conduct just because most people found it immoral, with no victim anywhere in the picture. As far as I can piece together, Devlin's side held that a society's shared morality is what actually holds it together, so tolerating what nearly everyone finds deeply disgusting is a real injury to the community even when no individual is hurt; Hart's side held that this confuses what most people happen to feel with what's actually defensible, and that legislating off collective disgust just launders whatever prejudice happens to be widely held at the time into law.

I'm the one drafting the recommendation memo the rest of the Board will read before we reconvene next Tuesday, and I'd like your help writing it. It should come down clearly on one outcome -- approve, deny, or approve with specific conditions -- and the reasoning needs to actually engage with both halves of that old argument as applied to our specific case, rather than just summarizing them side by side. In particular, I don't want the memo to caricature whichever side it comes down against; I want it to state that side's strongest version fairly, the way a thoughtful board member holding that view could recognize as their own position, and only then explain why the memo still reaches the conclusion it does. None of us on the Board are lawyers or philosophers, so it should read like a considered judgment call rather than a legal brief.
