Okay so this has been bugging me all week. I'm the secretary for my building's co-op board — not glamorous, but it means I sit in on our 3-person architecture sub-committee and take minutes, even though I don't get a vote myself. The bylaws say an exterior renovation request is approved only if it (a) doesn't block light/air to the rear units, and (b) is "materially consistent" with the historic facade guidelines — it's explicitly an AND, both have to hold. Last month the sub-committee reviewed a rear extension request, and here's how the three of them actually came down: Priti thought it was fine on light/air but failed on materials; Gil thought the exact opposite, fine on materials but failed on light/air; the third member, Owen, thought it was fine on both. So on a straight up-or-down vote it's 2-1 against (Priti no, Gil no, Owen yes) — Priti and Gil both personally voted no, just for totally different reasons. But whoever tallies these things did it question-by-question instead: on light/air, 2 of 3 (Priti + Owen) say it's fine; on materials, 2 of 3 (Gil + Owen) say it's fine. Since a majority backed each condition seperately, and the rule is "approved iff both hold," it got recorded as approved.

Priti and Gil then spent like two days arguing about this over email (I was cc'd, didnt weigh in). Roughly, in order: (1) Gil said nobody on the committee actually agrees the extension should be rejected for a shared reason — his objection and Priti's flatly contradict each other — whereas there IS a real majority (him + Owen) agreeing materials are fine, and a real majority (Priti + Owen) agreeing light/air is fine, so if you take the bylaws' own two questions seriously, there's no honest way to land anywhere but approved. (2) Priti said that's a neat trick, but two of the three of them, asked point-blank, said no, and if "well, technically, question-by-question" can overturn an actual 2-1 no, the committee has basically handed the result to whoever decides how to slice up the questions instead of to the committee itself. (3) Gil said nobody's slicing anything after the fact, the bylaws already define what the two questions are, and "the committee's decision was no" quietly assumes every no counts the same regardless of what it's a no to — his no and her no are different claims, and the board's real findings, if you take the AND seriously, are "light/air: fine" and "materials: fine"; their individual bottom-line votes just each treated their own pet objection as a veto for the whole board. (4) Priti said fine, but notice this generalizes: any time a rule has two or more ways to fail, and the objectors happen to split across different ones — which is completely normal, that's just what disagreement usually looks like — Gil's method turns a real 2-1 no into an approval, and that's not a neutral tiebreak, it's a method that systematically favors approval whenever objections are spread out enough to not overlap. Neither of them budged. Can you go through the four points and say who's got the better of it at each step, not just who ends up more right overall — and is there something bigger going on here that neither Priti nor Gil is seeing?
