I spent my career as a physicist working mainly in statistical mechanics, and it was only after retiring that I started reading moral philosophy in any systematic way. What pulled me in, oddly enough, was the debate over pandemic-era triage protocols — who gets the ventilator, who gets the vaccine first — which eventually led me to John Taurek's 1977 paper "Should the Numbers Count?" and Derek Parfit's reply to it, "Innumerate Ethics." I gather this exchange sits a little off to the side of the trolley-problem literature most people encounter, in the more specific territory of whether harms to different people can even be added together at all, and I found it unexpectedly gripping, because it looks to me like a dispute about whether badness aggregates the way certain physical quantities do — which happens to be something I have actual working intuitions about, for once.

Here is the shape of an argument I have been drafting, and I would like your unvarnished reaction before I take it any further. Taurek's case, as I understand it, involves a scarce drug that can save either one stranger, David, or a group of other strangers instead, where whichever option is not chosen costs those people absolutely everything they have — the whole of their remaining life. Taurek denies that we are entitled to say the group's deaths add up to something worse than David's death on its own, and suggests that, absent other grounds for preference, flipping a coin between the two options is actually what treats everyone with equal concern. Parfit's reply is that this cannot be right, since a policy of ignoring the numbers has consequences he thinks are clearly unacceptable once you let the size of the group vary. My own entry point has been the physicist's distinction between extensive quantities, like energy or entropy, which sum when you combine two systems, and intensive ones, like temperature, which do not — putting two rooms at the same temperature together does not give you a room at double the temperature. It seems to me that Taurek is, in effect, treating the badness of a person's loss as intensive: capped within that person's own life, and not the sort of thing that grows just because it recurs in more lives. Parfit, and later (if I am remembering the attribution correctly) Frances Kamm with a pairing or "balancing" argument, seem instead to be treating losses as extensive and summable across separate people, the way you would sum the energies of separate, non-interacting systems. Tentatively, I am inclined to think Taurek's picture is the more careful one, and that the aggregative view simply helps itself to the very assumption in dispute.

What I would like from you is not agreement but resistance. Does the extensive/intensive distinction actually track anything real in this debate, or is it borrowed vocabulary that could just as easily be run the other way — entropy, after all, is extensive, so why not say suffering aggregates like entropy rather than behaving like temperature, which would hand the numbers straight back to Parfit? I would also value a check on whether I have represented Taurek's and Parfit's actual arguments fairly, since I am working as much from secondary summaries as from the originals, and, finally, whatever you take to be the strongest existing reply — Kamm's or anyone else's — that my little analogy has not yet reckoned with.
