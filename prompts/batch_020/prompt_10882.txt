A friend and I spent most of a dinner arguing about a study I had read in which researchers decoded a paralyzed participant's inner, unspoken speech directly from speech-related motor regions of the brain — reliably enough that the team added a kind of mental "password," a phrase she had to silently rehearse before the decoder would activate, so it would not output thoughts she never intended to share. My friend argued that since the decoding is ultimately validated against what the participant later confirms she meant to think, the device shows inner speech was never "private" in Wittgenstein's sense to begin with — it is ordinary public-language use running through a new physical channel, and the private language argument has no purchase here. I argued the opposite: a decoder whose only criterion of success is the subject's own subsequent say-so reproduces exactly the predicament of Wittgenstein's diarist in the Investigations, who likewise had nothing to appeal to beyond her own impression of having got it right, so the technology dramatizes his objection rather than sidesteps it. Neither of us budged. Setting the obvious anachronism aside, how do you think Wittgenstein himself would have assessed this technology if we reason strictly from the mechanics of the private language argument — the demand for a check on correctness that doesn't simply consist in its seeming right to the subject — rather than from the slogan that "private language is impossible"? Whose reading holds up, or is there a distinction neither of us was drawing that dissolves the disagreement?
