I've been down a decision-theory rabbit hole this week for a piece I'm working on about why the Allais paradox still divides economists and philosophers on whether so-called "irrational" risk aversion is actually irrational. Someone recommended Lara Buchak's book *Risk and Rationality*, where she lays out her risk-weighted expected utility theory (REU) — basically the idea that you stick a "risk function" onto the probabilities before taking the expectation, which lets someone be pretty risk-avoidant in a global, whole-gamble kind of way without that counting as a mistake, or as some weirdly-shaped utility curve for money. Nice story. But then I went further back into the econ literature and found Quiggin's rank-dependent utility model from the early 80s (and Yaari's "dual theory" a few years after that), and as far as I can tell its doing basically the same formal trick: reweighting probabilities by the rank of the outcome instead of taking them at face value the way plain expected utility does.

So now I'm stuck on how to write the paragraph explaining the difference, because I genuinely can't tell if there is one. Is Buchak's REU actually formally distinct from Quiggin/Yaari-style rank-dependent utility, or is it the same mathematical object wearing a different philosophical coat — theirs offered as a descriptive account of observed deviations from expected-utility theory, hers as a normative defense of that very same pattern of choices as fully rational? And if it's mainly the second thing, is that a real substantive addition to decision theory, or more of a reinterpretation with no teeth? Would also be curious, if you had to bet on it, whose account you think does more actual explanatory work for why someone would pay a premium for certainty in an Allais-type choice. My editor keeps telling me to "just explain the difference in two sentences" and I keep failing, so any help untangling this before my draft is due would be great.
