So a former student of mine who now works at one of the AI safety outfits was in town last week, and over dinner she got me up to speed on the orthogonality thesis -- the Bostrom/Yudkowsky claim that intelligence and final goals are basically independent axes, so a superintelligent system could in principle be pursuing more or less any goal you like, however trivial (paperclips, etc.) -- plus the companion claim, instrumental convergence, that agents with wildly different final goals will nevertheless converge on a similar set of sub-goals along the way (self-preservation, resource acquisition, resisting being shut off, and so on -- what Omohundro called "basic AI drives"). Somewhere around dessert, I remember because I was halfway through a pretty mediocre tiramisu, it hit me that this is basically a rerun of the phronesis/deinotes distinction from Nicomachean Ethics VI, and I haven't been able to let it go since.

You'll recall Aristotle's distinction there between practical wisdom (phronesis), which he insists cant exist except in someone of good character, because it essentially involves correctly grasping the right end, and mere cleverness (deinotes), which he describes as a value-neutral capacity to hit whatever mark you've set for yourself, noble or base. What I'd like from you is a real attempt to reconstruct, using Aristotle's own conceptual resources rather than just modern ones, what he'd say if you explained the orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence to him. A couple of things I especially want you to dig into: (1) does the deinotes/phronesis split mean Aristotle basically already grants something like the orthogonality thesis at the level of "mere" intelligence, while denying it applies to anything he'd be willing to call real practical rationality? (2) given his teleological picture, where every kind of thing has it's ends fixed by its nature or function (the ergon argument from Book I), would he even accept that a "final goal" can be selected totally independently of an agent's rational capacities, the way the thesis requires -- or does his essentialism about ends undercut the whole setup from the start? And relatedly, is there anything in his framework that predicts, or resists, something like instrumental convergence? I'm not after a textbook summary of either side, more your best-faith reconstruction, tensions and all.
