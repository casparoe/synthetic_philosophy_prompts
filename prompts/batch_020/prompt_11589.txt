My uncle, a moral philosopher who spent nearly four decades working on the ethics of war, died three weeks ago at seventy-eight after a short illness. He served two tours as an infantry officer in Vietnam in his early twenties, and for most of his career held what he liked to call the "boring, decent" view he'd absorbed from Walzer: the moral equality of combatants -- that soldiers who fight within the laws of war are entitled to equal moral standing regardless of which side turns out to have had the just cause, since ordinary soldiers neither choose nor can reliably judge the wars they are sent to fight. In the last decade of his life, though, he became one of the sharper voices arguing the other way, closer to McMahan's line that a combatant's liability to be killed, and his standing to kill, actually track the objective justice of his cause -- so that the equal legal privilege we extend to both sides' soldiers is a wise institutional fiction, not a moral truth. He never worked out on paper what this later view implied about his own service, and he died before the two doctoral students extending it could finish the volume they had planned to dedicate to him.

I've been asked to give the eulogy, partly because I'm a philosophy PhD student myself (in value theory, not war ethics, though I sat through enough of his seminars to follow the argument) and partly because no one else in the family wanted the job. The problem is the room. The front pews will hold his surviving Army friends, men who have spent fifty years quietly certain that fighting honorably made them blameless, whatever the war turned out to have been; a few rows back will sit his colleagues and the two students, who know exactly which side of the debate he spent his final years defending and will notice if I sand it down into platitudes about "honoring all who served." I don't want to lie to either group, and I have about five minutes at the lectern, not a conference slot on jus ad bellum. Could you write the eulogy -- something honest about what he actually came to believe, warm enough to comfort his family and his war buddies, and careful enough that the philosophers in the back don't wince?
