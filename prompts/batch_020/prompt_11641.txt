Last night a friend and I argued for hours about Kant's notorious claim that we have no direct duties to animals, only "indirect" duties grounded in what cruelty toward them does to our own character — as when he claims that a man who has his aged, faithful dog shot once it can no longer serve wrongs no one, yet still damages in himself a sympathy on which his duties to other humans depend. My friend insisted this whole framework was a period-bound rationalization that could not have survived contact with two things Kant never saw: the industrial scale of factory farming, and the modern ethological evidence that species like corvids, elephants, and great apes possess self-awareness and rich social cognition. I countered that Kant excludes animals from the kingdom of ends specifically because they lack rational autonomy, not general intelligence, so the cognition research might leave him largely unmoved even as the sheer scale of factory farming, given his own emphasis on cultivated sympathy, might trouble him more. Reasoning strictly from Kant's own premises rather than from what we might wish he'd said, work out how he would most plausibly respond to each development, and specify exactly which of his commitments would have to bend for his conclusion to change and which would remain untouched.
