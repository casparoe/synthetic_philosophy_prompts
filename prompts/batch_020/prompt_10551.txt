My aunt Elena died a couple weeks ago and I got asked to give the eulogy, which is nerve-wracking enough on its own, but there's an extra wrinkle: she was a trauma surgeon for almost thirty years, and before medicine she'd actually finished a philosophy degree and never fully let it go, she ran an informal ethics reading group with a few other doctors for somthing like two decades. Her whole life she was convinced that Bernard Williams and Ruth Barcan Marcus were right that genuine moral dilemmas are possible: that you can end up in situations where whatever you do, you've wronged someone, and the guilt left over afterward isn't some irrational glitch, it's tracking something real, even when you objectively made the best call available. This wasn't just an abstract position for her. There was a mass-casualty night about fifteen years back, after a bad highway pileup, when there were way more critical patients than the ER had bays or blood for, and she had to make a triage call that the hospital's own review board later cleared her of completely. She never forgave herself anyway, and got annoyed when people told her she should of, since to her that would've meant denying there was ever a real conflict at all. She used to joke that people who think you can always reason your way to a guilt-free answer are just trying to "Kant their way out of it" (I gather that's a pun, I don't really get it beyond that).

The problem is that most people at the funeral are just family and old patients' families, not philosophers, but two or three people from that reading group will definitely be there and will notice immediately if the eulogy quietly reassures everyone that "she did nothing wrong," in a way she would have hated. Can you write me something like a five-minute eulogy that actually comforts a room full of non-philosophers, gets across who she was and what that night cost her, and stays honest to the fact that she thought some moral costs just don't wash out -- without it turning into a seminar on ethics?
