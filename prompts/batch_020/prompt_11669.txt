I've belonged for several years to a small private forecasting group, the kind of circle one ends up in after enough time reading rationalist blogs and forums. Two of its longtime members recently had an argument that ended up being less about their actual disagreement than about Aumann's agreement theorem itself. They'd each kept a running credence on the same standing question for months, compared notes every so often, and never once converged. As best I can reconstruct it, the meta-argument went like this.

(1) Sam said this should trouble them both: Aumann's theorem holds that two Bayesians with common priors, whose posteriors on some event are common knowledge between them, must have identical posteriors. They'd stated their numbers to each other more times than he could count, so continued disagreement meant one of them was reasoning badly.

(2) Elena said the theorem only bites given common priors, which nothing guarantees the two of them share. Different starting priors, not irrationality, could explain the whole thing, and the theorem itself says nothing about where priors are supposed to come from.

(3) Sam replied that this couldn't be the whole story, since neither of them could give any principled, non-self-serving reason their priors differed, and a prior that just happens to rationalize whatever its owner already believed looks less like an innocent starting point than like special pleading. He added that Geanakoplos and Polemarchakis's extension of Aumann's result covers exactly this case: agents who simply keep exchanging their posteriors are forced into agreement within finitely many rounds, and the two of them had exchanged theirs far more than that.

(4) Elena answered that Sam's suspicion-of-priors move proves too much, since on that logic nobody could ever reasonably go on disagreeing with anybody about anything, and that it smuggles in a substantive, independently contestable position — close to what epistemologists call the uniqueness thesis — that Aumann's theorem itself never asserts. As for Geanakoplos and Polemarchakis, she pointed out it rests on exactly the common-prior assumption she'd already flagged, plus each side updating on nothing but the bare number the other announces, with no motivated reasoning and no differing trust in how much the other actually knows about the question — neither of which describes two people trading messages for months.

Neither of them budged, and I've gone back and forth myself on who has the better of this. Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move, (1) through (4), saying who's right, who's wrong, or only partly right at each step, and flag anything you think both of them are overlooking about what Aumann's theorem can actually license two real, disagreeing people to conclude about each other? Close with a one-paragraph overall verdict.
