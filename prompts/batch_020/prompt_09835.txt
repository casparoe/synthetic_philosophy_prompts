Okay so I'm a few years into a philosophy PhD (working mostly on modality/metaphysics) and I TA our department's intro philosophy of religion course. Grading this week's papers on Molinism made me realize I might have been explaining "middle knowledge" wrong to my students for like two semesters now, so I want a sanity check before I dig myself in deeper. The way I always frame it, after reading through Flint's book and a bunch of Craig's stuff, is: prior to creating anything, God basically previews every possible timeline the universe could take depending on which world he decides to actualize -- kind of like the multiverse scene in that Doctor Strange movie, or a chess engine running out every line to see how the game ends -- and since he can see exactly how each person would freely act all the way down each of these timelines, he just picks the one timeline/world where, say, the optimal number of people freely choose salvation. That's supposedly the clever move that lets him providentially steer history without overriding anyone's libertarian free will, because he's not making anyone choose anything, he's just choosing which pre-run timeline to greenlight.

My question is: if that's basically the picture, what is middle knowledge actually doing that plain old foreknowledge isn't already doing? Either way, God is watching how the future would unfold under each option and then choosing based on what he sees, so I don't really get why Molinists treat "natural knowledge vs middle knowledge vs free knowledge" as this huge conceptual breakthrough rather then just a fancier, more thorough version of simple foreknowledge. Is the "middle" part doing any real independent philosophical work here, or is this basically a distinction without a difference and I'm missing the actual substance of the view? Genuinely not trying to be a smart-ass about it, I just want to stop repeating this to a room of undergrads every semester if its actually a bit confused.
