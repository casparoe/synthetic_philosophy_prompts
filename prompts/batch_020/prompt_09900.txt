Reading back over that study after my own dumb little moment felt way too on the nose.

The actual paper is about whether the two big virtue-ethics comebacks to this kind of finding really work, or whether they quietly gut the theory instead. First there's Annas's move that "virtue" in the full sense is rare, an achievement most people never reach — most of us are just continent or weak-willed rather than virtuous, so of course behaviour swings around with hurry or mood, that was never what virtue ethics predicted for ordinary people walking down the street. Second there's Sreenivasan's "errors about errors" argument, that the psychologists are only finding an "absence" of traits because they're quietly requiring a weirdly strict kind of cross-situational statistical consistency that we don't actually demand when we attribute traits in everyday life. (I think there's also a Kamtekar paper making a related but different point, that the situational cues are changing what people notice or find salient rather than showing there's no character at all — but I haven't read that one as closely, just seen it cited around.)

My actual thesis, which I'd love blunt comments on, is that Annas's move and Sreenivasan's move together make virtue ethics' claims about character basically unfalsifiable: messy behavioural data can always get explained away either as "not truly virtuous people anyway" or "not the right kind of consistency test," so no possible pattern of situationist results could ever count against the theory. Is that a fair reading, or am I flattening two more careful arguments into something dumber than they actually are? Is there an obvious way a virtue ethicist could block the unfalsifiability charge that I'm missing, maybe through the Kamtekar route instead? Honestly any combination of comments would help — whether I've got the psychology right, whether the philosophical argument actually holds together, and whether this is even a fresh enough angle or if I've just reinvented a point people already made to death back in the 2000s.
