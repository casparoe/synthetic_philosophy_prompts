I have spent a long time reading rationalist blogs and forums — LessWrong, Astral Codex Ten, the EA Forum — and one argument that keeps resurfacing there is the "coherence argument" for expected utility maximization: the claim that any sufficiently capable agent, human or artificial, will end up behaving like an expected-utility maximizer on pain of being money-pumped by an adversary who exploits inconsistencies in its preferences. That argument leans on agents obeying axioms like independence (or, in Savage's framework, the sure-thing principle), which sent me back to the textbook case that is supposed to show ordinary people failing exactly that axiom: Allais' paradox. Most popular treatments stop at "people irrationally prefer certainty, hence prospect theory," and I want to push past that to a more specific question: does the paradox actually establish irrationality, or does it mainly reveal how much normative work the independence axiom is quietly doing?

Here is the shape of the essay I want to write. I open with the standard version of the paradox: a choice between a certain $1 million and a gamble giving a 10% chance of $5 million, an 89% chance of $1 million, and a 1% chance of nothing, followed by a choice between an 11%/89% gamble over $1 million and nothing versus a 10%/90% gamble over $5 million and nothing — where the modal pattern (certainty in the first pair, the bigger prize in the second) violates independence, since the second pair is just the first pair with the shared 89%-chance-of-$1-million branch swapped for an 89%-chance-of-nothing branch. I mention the story, retold in a lot of decision theory writing, that when Allais put this exact choice to Savage in person, Savage's own snap answers broke his own sure-thing principle, and that Savage's considered verdict was to side with the axiom over his gut, treating the gut reaction as a kind of optical illusion rather than as evidence against the axiom. Then I turn to the money-pump argument itself and claim it proves less than advertised: an agent willing to choose "resolutely" — in the sense Edward McClennen gave that term in his work on dynamic choice, committing to a whole plan up front rather than re-optimizing at each node of a decision tree — can hold Allais-type, independence-violating preferences without ever being forced into the sequence of trades that is supposed to bleed such agents dry. If that is right, the coherence-argument case for expected-utility-maximizing AI is quietly resting on a second, much less examined premise: that rationality requires naive or sophisticated re-choice at each node rather than resolute commitment to a plan.

Could you give me detailed comments on this? I am especially interested in: whether I have the mechanics of the paradox and the Savage anecdote right, or am garbling something; whether the resolute-choice move genuinely rescues Allais preferences from being money-pumped, or whether there is a cleverer pump that catches resolute choosers too; whether the final inference — from "resolute choice blocks the pump" to "coherence arguments for AI risk are weaker than advertised" — actually holds, given that gradient descent and evolutionary selection do not obviously resemble a resolute chooser; and whether this whole line is novel enough to be worth writing up, or is just a slower rediscovery of an argument LessWrong and the EA Forum have already had many times over.
