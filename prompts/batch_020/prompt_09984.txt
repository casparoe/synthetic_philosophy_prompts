Two months ago I was made risk-management chair of my fraternity chapter after a pledge was hospitalized during a mixer, and our national organization is now requiring the chapter to rewrite its entire alcohol policy before we are allowed to host events again. I have spent most evenings since drafting rules about sober monitors, drink limits, and mandatory check-ins. Purely by coincidence, in a seminar on Greek political philosophy this same term we reached Book I of Plato's Laws, and I was startled to find the Athenian Stranger mounting a long, evidently serious defense of supervised drinking parties as an institution the lawgiver should build into the city: watching a person under the influence of wine, he argues, reveals the untrained parts of the soul in much the way that watching them under the influence of fear does, so that a properly regulated symposium, presided over by an official expected to remain sober, becomes a diagnostic and training exercise in self-command rather than a mere indulgence. This stands in considerable tension with the Plato I thought I knew from the Republic, where the guardians achieve temperance by being kept away from wine and every other appetite-loosening pleasure rather than by being deliberately exposed to it, and where the whole apparatus of the ideal city seems built on distrust of exactly the kind of test the Laws recommends.

I would like an essay that treats this tension as a genuine interpretive problem rather than resolving it too quickly. Present the strongest case that the position on drunkenness in the Laws amounts to a real revision of the view of pleasure, self-mastery, and moral education at work in the Republic, and then present the strongest case that the two dialogues are in fact consistent, once one accounts for the difference between the guardians of Callipolis and the ordinary citizens of Magnesia. Weigh these two readings against each other on their textual and philosophical merits, and end by defending a specific verdict: does Plato's considered view treat controlled drunkenness as a genuine civic and pedagogical good, or merely as a concession suited to a "second-best" city that a philosopher-king would have no need of? Do not end on a split-the-difference note. Commit to one reading as the better-supported of the two, and state plainly what would have to be true of the texts for the other reading to win out instead.
