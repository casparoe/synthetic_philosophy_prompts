I'm drafting a novel in which one character — a retired philosophy don with unfashionably Cartesian sympathies — is asked by her family to judge whether a new conversational AI actually understands anything it says, and I want her reasoning to be faithful to Descartes rather than a caricature of "animals are just machines." In Part V of the Discourse, Descartes gives two quite specific grounds for denying a rational soul to any beast or automaton. First, although a trained bird can utter words and a machine could be built to emit a fixed response to a fixed stimulus, nothing composed only of organs could rearrange words to reply aptly to whatever is said to it, the way even the dullest human being manages to do. Second, skill that arises from the disposition of organs is always narrow and task-specific — a clock outperforms us at telling time without possessing any wisdom — whereas reason, in his phrase, is a universal instrument serviceable in any situation, which is why he thought no finite arrangement of parts could make a machine behave appropriately across the whole range of circumstances a person meets in life.

Working strictly from those two criteria, and not from his general view that animals are automata, how would you expect Descartes to assess a system that holds up its end of an open-ended conversation across almost any subject raised with it? Does such a system actually pass his first test, or does his own framework still supply grounds for denying that it does? And does the second criterion — narrow, organ-bound competence versus the universality of reason — end up telling for or against treating broad conversational range as evidence of real understanding? Work through the two criteria separately before giving an overall verdict on where he would likely land.
