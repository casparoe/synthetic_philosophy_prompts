In a graduate seminar on metaethics we have been working through Peter Geach's 1965 paper "Assertion," which presses what is now called the Frege–Geach problem against emotivist and expressivist analyses of moral language. The problem, briefly: if "Cruelty is wrong" merely expresses disapproval of cruelty rather than stating a fact, it is unclear what a speaker is doing in asserting "If cruelty is wrong, then teaching children to be cruel is wrong," since uttering that conditional plainly does not itself express disapproval of cruelty (the speaker may be entirely agnostic about the antecedent). Yet the resulting modus ponens — add the premise "Cruelty is wrong" and derive "Teaching children to be cruel is wrong" — is obviously valid, which seems to require "wrong" to carry one stable content across its asserted and unasserted occurrences, exactly the sort of content expressivism denies moral vocabulary has. Contemporary expressivists such as Blackburn, Gibbard, and Schroeder have built increasingly elaborate semantic machinery (inconsistency of attitudes, hyperplan-relative validity, the "being for" relation) to meet exactly this challenge.

What our seminar has not discussed is how the problem bears on Hume himself, who is routinely invoked as the historical ancestor of the whole expressivist tradition: Blackburn explicitly presents quasi-realism as a development of Humean projectivism, and Hume's own remark that morality is "more properly felt than judg'd of" reads like a slogan for noncognitivism avant la lettre. But Hume died well over a century before Frege distinguished force from content, and nothing in his moral psychology — the general point of view, the correction of sentiment through sympathy and general rules, his sharp division of labor between reason (which fixes the facts) and sentiment (which evaluates them) — was designed with embedding or validity in mind. If you had to reconstruct Hume's most plausible response to Geach's challenge, what would it be: is there any piece of his apparatus that could do genuine work against this specific objection, or would his sentimentalism turn out to be just as exposed as Ayer's or Stevenson's cruder emotivism, merely dressed in eighteenth-century vocabulary? I would like your best reconstruction, including which Humean concept you think would have to carry the weight of an answer and where exactly you expect that reconstruction to strain or break down.
