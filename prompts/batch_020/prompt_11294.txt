Over the past few weeks I've been working through Justus Buchler's old Dover anthology of Peirce's writings alongside James's Pragmatism, partly prompted by a throwaway line in a LessWrong comment thread claiming that "the rationalist community basically agrees with Peirce and against James" on the nature of truth. That claim nagged at me, since my recollection of Yudkowsky's "The Useful Idea of Truth" and the surrounding sequence is that it treats pragmatist theories of truth in general as a kind of category error, conflating the usefulness of believing a thing with the thing's actually being so — exactly the "belief in belief" move the Sequences spend so much effort warning against. So I've started drafting a shortform post to work out whether Peirce and James really diverge sharply enough on this point for only one of them to be vulnerable to that charge, and I'd like your comments on the argument before I post it anywhere.

The core of the argument is this. James's slogan is that truth is nothing more than whatever proves expedient in our thinking, just as the right is whatever proves expedient in our behaving; taken at face value, that really does sound like it collapses truth into whatever is currently convenient to believe, tenses and all. Peirce, by contrast, defines truth as "the opinion which is fated to be ultimately agreed to by all who investigate," which is a claim about where inquiry converges in an idealized limit rather than about what's expedient today — and he was irritated enough at being lumped in with James's popularization that he coined "pragmaticism," a name he thought "ugly enough to be safe from kidnappers." On my reading, Peirce's theory is closer to a correspondence theory wearing convergence-theoretic clothing than to anything James actually says, since the whole apparatus only works if there is a mind-independent reality producing the convergent evidence in the first place. But I keep getting stuck on the standard "buried secret" or "isolated fact" objection: propositions about matters no investigator will ever look into, such as the precise number of grains of sand in a particular dune that eroded away millennia before anyone thought to count them, seem to have perfectly determinate truth values despite there being no fact of the matter about what inquiry would converge on. I'm not convinced Peirce's talk of counterfactual "would-be" investigation actually rescues him here, as opposed to just re-importing a correspondence notion of truth through the back door while insisting on convergence-theoretic vocabulary.

I'd appreciate comments on a few things in particular: whether I'm being fair to Peirce and James respectively, or flattening real subtleties in either view (especially anything in Peirce's later, more technical papers that complicates the clean "convergence theory" reading I'm relying on); whether the parallel to Yudkowsky's correspondence-theoretic stance actually holds up, or whether I'm just forcing a rationalist-blogosphere framing onto a debate that predates it by a century; and whether there's a genuinely strong Jamesian response to the isolated-fact worry that I'm missing, beyond the usual observation that James also cared about long-run, community-wide verification and wasn't as crudely individualist and present-tense as his slogan suggests. I'd also welcome a blunter verdict on whether this is worth turning into a full post at all, given how often the pragmatism-versus-correspondence debate gets rehashed, or whether I should just leave it in the comment thread where it started.
