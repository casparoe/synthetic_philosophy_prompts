So I fell down a Stanford Encyclopedia rabbit hole a while back on the "wrong kind of reasons" problem for fitting-attitude theories of value -- the classic setup where a demon says he'll torture you unless you admire him, so now you've got a clear reason to admire him, except that reason has nothing to do with him actually being admirable, its just a reason to be in the state of admiring. Ever since then I keep noticing the same shape show up whenever rationalist-adjacent people argue about whether its ever okay to believe something because believing it is useful -- growth mindset, placebo effects, "fake it till you make it," self-fulfilling prophecies, all that stuff that gets filed under instrumental vs epistemic rationality.

Can you spell out the most important implications of the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem for that debate specifically? I'm most curious whether any of the standard fixes people propose for WKR in the value case (object-given vs. state-given reasons, restricting which properties even count as reason-giving, etc) actually generalize to give the pragmatist side a principled way to say "usefulness can sometimes be a genuine reason to believe" -- or whether those same moves just end up showing that a useful-but-unsupported belief is always structurally identical to admiring the demon. And please don't just default to "well Pascal's wager is bad epistemics," I already know that take and want the actual structural point.
