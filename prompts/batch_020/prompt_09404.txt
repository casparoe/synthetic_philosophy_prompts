The Löbian obstacle names the difficulty a proof-based agent faces in certifying that its own future reasoning, or that of a successor sharing its proof system, can be trusted: by Löb's theorem, a consistent theory T cannot prove the reflection schema "if T proves P, then P" for arbitrary P without already proving P outright, which blocks naive schemes for self-trust and self-reproduction ("tiling"). Compare two strategies that have been proposed to work around this obstacle: (1) stratifying trust across a hierarchy of successively weaker proof systems, so that an agent only ever needs to certify a strictly weaker successor rather than an exact copy of itself, and (2) abandoning binary provability in favor of calibrated subjective probability, so that a reflective agent can coherently assign probabilities to sentences about its own probability assignments without triggering the Löbian derivation. Which of these two strategies comes closer to genuinely resolving the problem of self-trust, rather than merely relocating or disguising it? Justify your answer by identifying the specific price each strategy pays in order to avoid Löb's theorem.
