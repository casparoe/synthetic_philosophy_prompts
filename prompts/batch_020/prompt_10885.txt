Problem 3 (15 points). Nover and Hájek's "Pasadena game" (2004) is often introduced as a cousin of the St. Petersburg game. A fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads. If the first heads occurs on toss n, you receive a payoff of (-1)^(n-1) · 2^n / n (so the payoffs alternate between gains and losses as n increases).

(a) [5 pts] Compute the expected value of the Pasadena game by summing over n = 1, 2, 3, ... in that natural order.
(b) [5 pts] Nover and Hájek claim that, despite appearances, this "expected value" is not merely a large or infinite number but is undefined outright. State the mathematical fact about the payoff series that grounds this claim, and explain why the analogous fact does not hold for the St. Petersburg game.
(c) [5 pts] Is the difficulty the Pasadena game poses for decision theory the same difficulty posed by the St. Petersburg game, or a different one? Explain.

Student's answer:

(a) The probability that the first heads appears on toss n is (1/2)^n. Multiplying by the payoff and summing gives Σ (1/2)^n · (-1)^(n-1) · 2^n/n = Σ (-1)^(n-1)/n = 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ... = ln 2 ≈ 0.693.

(b) This number can't be trusted as *the* expected value, though, because 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - ... is only conditionally convergent: the series of absolute values, 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + ..., is the harmonic series, which diverges. The Riemann rearrangement theorem tells us that a convergent series like this one can be reordered to sum to anything at all — any target number, or +∞, or -∞ — just by changing the order in which the positive and negative terms are taken. So although lining the payoffs up by n happens to give ln 2, a different but equally legitimate grouping of the same outcomes could give a totally different answer. The St. Petersburg game avoids this specific problem, since its series never converges in the first place — but I think the two paradoxes end up in the same place: each game has an infinite expected value, so a rational agent should apparently be willing to pay any finite entry fee to play either one.

(c) For that reason, I'd say the Pasadena game is best understood as a more elegant version of the St. Petersburg problem rather than a genuinely new one: both are cases in which a well-defined, fair-sounding gamble turns out to have an infinite expected value, which is absurd, since no actual gambler would pay an unlimited fee to play. Nover and Hájek's contribution is to show that the absurdity survives even when payoffs alternate between wins and losses, which is a nice extension of Bernoulli's original point, but the lesson for decision theory — that expected-utility maximization breaks down once gambles are allowed to be unbounded — is the same lesson the St. Petersburg game already taught us.
