Okay so I've been chewing on this for a while now. Got into that whole endurantism vs perdurantism thing about how objects persist through time - like, is a thing "wholly present" at every single moment it exists, or is it really more like a long four-dimensional worm made of different temporal parts, and what we call "the object" at any one moment is just one slice of that worm. Somehow that got me thinking about the old Heraclitus river line, "you never step in the same river twice."

Except I read somewhere that the really extreme version of that - like you can't even step in it once because its already changed by the time your foot lands - is more Cratylus/Plato's spin on him, and that Heraclitus's actual point is closer to: the river stays the same river precisely because different water keeps flowing through it, like sameness and difference aren't opposites for him, more two sides of one coin.

So if that's roughly right - which side of the endurantism/perdurantism debate do you reckon Heraclitus would actually land on, once someone explained the modern version of it to him properly? And does his whole "unity of opposites" move actually get him out of the classic problem endurantists have, that a thing can't have two contradictory properties (straight, then bent, say) at different times without some kind of awkward workaround - or is that just poetic hand-waving that dodges the real question?
