I am a software engineer without formal training in philosophy, but I build recommendation systems that lean on a version of "revealed preference": we treat clicks, dwell time, and conversions as evidence of what users actually want. This led me to read about inverse reinforcement learning (IRL), particularly a result that goes beyond the familiar point that many reward functions can rationalize the same behavior. It shows that even if an algorithm jointly infers a reward function and a "planner" (a model of how rational or biased the agent is), and even if it favors simpler reward-planner pairs, simplicity still cannot single out the pair that reflects the agent's real preferences: there is always an equally simple degenerate alternative -- say, a reward function indifferent to everything, paired with a planner that just outputs whatever behavior was observed -- that fits the data equally well. Please describe the most important implications of this for how much epistemic confidence we can ever place on claims, whether from an IRL-based AI system or an ordinary analytics pipeline, that a person's behavior reveals what they truly want.
