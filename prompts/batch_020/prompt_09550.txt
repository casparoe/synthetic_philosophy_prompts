Here's the question as it was given to students. The exchange was:
Sam: "Did you get through the Kant reading for tomorrow?"
Jordan: "I made a pot of coffee, and then Alex needed a hand getting the new couch up the stairs."

(a) What does Jordan conversationally implicate here, and reconstruct the reasoning a hearer would have to go through to work it out, referencing the Cooperative Principle.
(b) Which maxim does Jordan's utterance most obviously flout, and explain the difference between flouting a maxim and violating one. Why does that difference matter for whether an implicature gets generated at all?
(c) Show that the implicature you gave in (a) is cancellable and non-detachable, and explain why these two tests together support treating it as conversational rather than conventional.
(d) Is this a generalized or a particularized conversational implicature? Defend your answer.
(e) How does this example illustrate Grice's distinction between "what is said" and "what is implicated"?

And here's the student's answer:

(a) Jordan is implicating that he did not finish the Kant reading. By the Cooperative Principle, Sam is entitled to assume Jordan's contribution is as informative and relevant as the exchange requires. On its face, listing two unrelated errands doesn't answer a yes/no question at all, so Sam has to assume there's a reason Jordan chose to say this instead of just answering, and the most likely explanation for volunteering an account of the afternoon rather than a direct answer is that the honest direct answer is "no," and that the coffee and the couch are Jordan's way of explaining why he didn't get to it.

(b) What Jordan is doing is violating the Maxim of Relation: on its face, an account of coffee and couch-moving has nothing to do with whether he's done the Kant reading. Grice distinguishes this from merely flouting a maxim, which is failing to fulfil it quietly, in a way meant to go unnoticed -- the sort of unstated corner-cutting involved in a half-truth. Violating a maxim, by contrast, is doing so openly, in a way both speaker and hearer recognize, which is exactly what licenses Sam to look for a further meaning instead of just being misled: since Jordan isn't even trying to hide that he hasn't really answered, Sam can safely assume the irrelevance is deliberate and meaningful rather than an attempt at deception.

(c) The implicature is cancellable: Jordan could go on, "...but I still got through the Kant before any of that happened," and this wouldn't be a contradiction, just a way of blocking the inference Sam would otherwise draw. It's also non-detachable: Jordan could have communicated the same implicature with a totally different sentence with the same content ("I spent the afternoon on coffee and furniture," say), so the implicature rides on what's communicated rather than the specific words used. Together these show the implicature isn't conventional: an implicature like the one carried by "but" is tied to that particular word and disappears if you swap in a word with the same truth-conditional content, like "and," whereas this one survives no matter how you rephrase it, because it's calculated fresh from context rather than attached to specific wording.

(d) This is a generalized conversational implicature rather than a particularized one, since Sam doesn't need to know anything special about Jordan or their situation to draw the inference -- almost anyone who heard this exchange would conclude Jordan hadn't done the reading, just from the shape of the response. That's different from Grice's own example of the philosophy student's reference letter, where you need independent knowledge that the writer was expected to comment on the candidate's philosophical ability in order to see the implicature that the student is weak at the subject.

(e) Grice's distinction is between what a speaker's words strictly say -- their truth-conditional content -- and what the speaker merely implicates by saying it in this context. What Jordan says is just that he made coffee and helped Alex with the couch; that's all that's needed for the utterance to come out true or false. That he didn't do the reading is no part of what he said: it isn't entailed by it, and could turn out false even if what he said is true, if it somehow emerged that he'd finished the Kant earlier and just didn't mention it. The upshot is that a lot of what gets communicated rides on top of, and separately from, what is strictly said.
