So a few weeks back my brother-in-law (he's doing a math PhD) mentioned offhand that some mathematicians don't accept proof by contradiction as a legit way of showing something exists, which broke my brain a little, so I went down a rabbit hole about "intuitionism" and this guy L.E.J. Brouwer who apparently started the whole thing. I tracked down an actual English translation of one of his early lectures (it's from 1912, translated by a guy named Arnold Dresden and printed in the Bulletin of the American Mathematical Society in 1913 — so fair warning, the prose is pretty stiff and old fashioned). Right before the bit that's got me stuck, he'd spent a paragraph rewriting a theorem of Bernstein's (the one about comparing the sizes of infinite sets by matching them up one-to-one) into a version an intuitionist could actually sign off on, and then he says he wants to try it out on a real example. This is where I loose the thread completely:

"In order to investigate the validity of this interpretation, we quote the proof: 'From the division of A into A₁+B₁+C₁, we secure by means of the correspondence γ₁ between A and A₁ a division of A₁ into A₂+B₂+C₂, as well as a one-to-one correspondence γ₂ between A₁ and A₂. From the division of A₁ into A₂+B₂+C₂, we secure by means of the correspondence between A₁ and A₂ a division of A₂ into A₃+B₃+C₃, as well as a one-to-one correspondence γ₃ between A₂ and A₃. Indefinite repetition of this procedure will divide the set A into an elementary series of subsets C₁, C₂, C₃, ..., an elementary series of subsets B₁, B₂, B₃, ..., and a remainder set D. The correspondence γc between A and A₁+B₁ which is desired is secured by assigning to every element of Cᵥ the corresponding element of Cᵥ₊₁ and by assigning every other element of A to itself.'

In order to test this proof on a definite example, let us take for A the set of all real numbers between 0 and 1, represented by infinite decimal fractions, for A₁ the set of those decimal fractions in which the (2n−1)th digit is equal to the 2nth digit; further, a decimal fraction that does not belong to A₁ will be counted to belong to B₁ or to C₁ according as the above-mentioned equality of digits occurs an infinite or a finite number of times. By replacing successively each digit of an arbitrary element of A by a pair of digits equal to it, we secure at once a law determining a one-to-one correspondence γ₁ between A and A₁. For of the element of A₁ that corresponds to an arbitrary well-defined element of A, such as, e.g., π − 3, we can determine successively as many digits as we please; it must therefore be considered as being well-defined.

In order to determine the element corresponding to π − 3 according to the correspondence γc, it is now necessary to decide first whether it happens an infinite or a finite number of times in the decimal fraction development of π − 3 that a digit in an odd-numbered place is equal to the digit in the following even-numbered place; for this purpose we should either have to invent a process for constructing an elementary series of such pairs of equal digits, or to deduce a contradiction from the assumption of the existence of such an elementary series. There is, however, no ground for believing that either of these problems can be solved." — and then there's a footnote hanging off that last sentence which says: "Such belief could be based only on an appeal to the principium tertii exclusi, i.e., to the axiom of the existence of the 'set of all mathematical properties,' an axiom of far wider range even than the axioms of inclusion."

Ok so, I think I follow the broad shape of it: he builds these sets with subscripts, keeps splitting them into buckets, and matches them up one to one. Then he plugs in an actual number, pi minus 3, and asks which bucket it lands in, and says there's no reason to think that question can be settled either way. But that footnote is where I completely lose it: he says believing otherwise "could be based only on an appeal to" this "principium tertii exclusi" thing, which I looked up and is apparently just Latin for the law of excluded middle. I don't get why though. Isn't it just plain true that either that digit pattern shows up infinitely often in pi or it doesn't, whether or not we're ever smart enough to prove which? Why does merely believing there's a fact of the matter here amount to sneaking in the law of excluded middle, and why would that be a problem specifically for classical logic, as opposed to just being a hard open problem in number theory we haven't cracked yet? I feel like there's some actual philosophical move happening in that footnote that I'm not quite catching, and I'd love help spelling out exactly what it is.
