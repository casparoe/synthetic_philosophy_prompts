In the course of my dissertation research on the units-of-selection debate, I have repeatedly run up against a worry about the multi-level Price equation that I have not seen satisfactorily resolved in the secondary literature. When the Price equation is used to partition total fitness–character covariance into a between-group and a within-group component, the resulting split depends entirely on how the population happens to be carved into groups: the very same selection episode can be partitioned so that virtually all of the covariance loads onto the between-group term (e.g., by taking maximally fine, one-individual "groups") or so that it loads almost entirely onto the within-group term instead (e.g., by taking a single, maximally coarse group), without any change whatsoever in the underlying causal process. Sober and Wilson try to defuse this worry by restricting admissible partitions to genuine trait groups—groupings that track the actual purview of individuals' phenotypic effects on one another's fitness—rather than allowing the analyst to carve up the population however one likes. Please argue for the more deflationary position that, even once this restriction is in place, claims of the form "trait X evolved because of selection at the group level" still fail to state an observer-independent causal fact about a population's evolutionary trajectory, and amount at best to a convenient redescription relative to a modeling choice. I would like the argument to engage directly with the trait-group restriction itself, rather than simply setting it aside.
