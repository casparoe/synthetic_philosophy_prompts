Condorcet's work in the 1780s contains two results that sit in real tension with each other: the Jury Theorem, which shows that majority voting among competent, independent voters converges on the truth as the size of the group grows, and the voting paradox, which shows that pairwise majority voting over three or more options can produce a cyclical collective preference even when every individual voter's own preferences are perfectly consistent. Both results grew out of his interest in whether real courts and assemblies could be trusted to reach correct verdicts.

He did not live to see a structurally related problem that later work folded into the general theory of judgment aggregation: the discursive dilemma, List and Pettit's term for a puzzle often presented as Condorcet's own paradox transposed from preferences to logically connected judgments. In its classic form, a multi-member court must rule on a conclusion — say, a defendant's liability — that is logically fixed by two independent premises, such as whether a valid contract existed and whether it was breached. Voting on each premise separately and then applying the logical rule can yield a different verdict than voting on the liability question directly, even though each judge's own verdicts remain perfectly consistent.

Given Condorcet's own probabilistic epistemology and his hopes for the Jury Theorem as a justification for collective decision-making, how do you think he would have responded to this dilemma? Would he have taken it as a reason to prefer a premise-by-premise voting procedure, on the grounds that each premise is exactly the kind of simple question the Jury Theorem was designed for, or would he have seen it as exposing a deeper problem with his hope that majority procedures could be certified as reliable quite generally?
