Since retiring from a career in field ecology, I've spent a good deal of time working through Karl Popper's writings on the philosophy of science, and one episode keeps nagging at me. In his 1976 intellectual autobiography, Popper argued that the theory of natural selection was not, strictly speaking, a testable scientific theory but rather a "metaphysical research programme" that supplies a framework for more specific testable hypotheses; a couple of years later, he partly recanted, conceding that natural selection could after all be treated as falsifiable. Popper died in 1994, roughly a decade before falsifiability became the centerpiece of American legal battles over teaching intelligent design in public schools, most notably the 2005 Kitzmiller v. Dover ruling, which held intelligent design not to be science partly because it isn't testable, while treating evolutionary theory's own scientific credentials as beyond question. Given how much trouble Popper had deciding whether Darwinism itself passed his own test, how do you think he would have reacted to seeing that same test wielded so confidently, and so one-sidedly, against intelligent design a decade after his death? Would he have taken it as vindication of his recantation, or worried that the courts were repeating, in reverse, the very mistake he had only recently talked himself out of?
