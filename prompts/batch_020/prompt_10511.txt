So I've been reading through Nietzsche's Genealogy of Morals on my own over the last couple months (no philosophy classes, just me, a cheap paperback, and a lot of late-night wikipedia-ing), and there's one bit in the second essay that's been bugging me for weeks now. Right around section 2 he introduces this figure he calls the "sovereign individual" — basically someone who has mastered himself so completely that he can actually make a promise and mean it — and Nietzsche gets almost giddy describing him: calling him autonomous, going on about his pride in his own power, this special kind of freedom he's supposedly earned. Every intro-level summary or youtube explainer I've come across treats this passage as basically Nietzsche laying out his own personal ideal of what a great human being looks like, kind of a rough draft of the overman.

Here's what trips me up though: isn't the entire rest of that essay about how stuff like guilt, conscience and "responsibility" aren't real achievements at all, just scar tissue left behind by thousands of years of brutal training that turned unpredictable animals into calculable, promise-keeping ones (that's the whole "morality of custom" thing he spends pages on right before this part)? If that's really the point, then this sovereign individual, who is literally described as the ripened fruit of that exact same training process, can't just casually be Nietzsche's escape hatch from it all. It feels like he wants the guy to be both the product of the cage AND somehow free of it, and I don't see how you get to have it both ways. I brought this up with a friend and apparently there's real disagreement among nietzsche nerds online over whether this passage is meant totally sincerely, or whether its secretly just another target of the same genealogical takedown that priests and ascetics get elsewhere in the book.

What I want is for you to actually argue the second reading, as hard as you can: make the case that the sovereign individual can NOT be Nietzsche's genuine, sincerely-held ideal, and that reading it that way is actually inconsistent with the genealogical method he uses on every other moral concept in the book. Don't just lay out both sides evenly, I can get that anywhere - really commit to the argument, and make sure you deal with the obvious objection that a guy this suspicious of conventional morality surely wouldn't describe something so glowingly unless he genuinely meant it.
