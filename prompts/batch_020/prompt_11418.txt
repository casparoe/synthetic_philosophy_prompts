After forty years as an entomologist, spent describing and classifying beetles and arguing with colleagues about exactly where one species shades into another, I retired a few years ago and have been filling the resulting gap with a rather undisciplined tour through the history of philosophy. A friend from my new reading group recently pointed me toward the School of Names (名家) of the Warring States period, and in particular toward Gongsun Long's White Horse Dialogue (白馬論). Having spent a career worrying about precisely this sort of boundary problem in a biological register—whether a dingo counts as a true dog, or the red wolf as a genuine species rather than a coyote hybrid—I found the text unexpectedly gripping, even though, or perhaps because, I cannot decide whether it is making a serious point or merely a clever one.

As I have pieced it together from a few secondary sources, the dialogue opens with one speaker asking whether it is admissible to claim that a white horse is not a horse (白馬非馬), and the other affirms this, explaining that "horse" is the name given to a shape, while "white" is the name given to a colour, and that naming a colour is not the same act as naming a shape—hence a white horse is not a horse. The text then adds a second argument: if one asks for a horse, a yellow or a black horse will do, but if one asks for a white horse, a yellow or a black horse will not do; and since what satisfies "horse" is not the same as what satisfies "white horse," the two terms cannot be picking out the same thing.

What I would value your help with is deciding how much genuine logical or semantic force this has, rather than being a piece of sophistry that trades on sliding between "is" as identity and "is" as class-membership—as though I insisted that a stag beetle is not a beetle, on the grounds that "stag beetle" specifies a narrower set of properties than "beetle" does on its own. I gather some modern scholars argue that classical Chinese common nouns behave more like mass terms ("gold," "water") than like the count-nouns that English translation forces on them, which would turn the dispute into one about parts and wholes rather than about class-membership as such—does that reading rescue the argument, or does it just relocate the equivocation to a different level? And, more broadly, how should someone like me, with no independent access to the classical Chinese, weigh the long-standing charge from Gongsun Long's contemporaries that the whole exercise confused "names" and "actualities" (名實) for the sake of display, against the more sympathetic modern readings that treat him as a genuine, if idiosyncratic, logician?
