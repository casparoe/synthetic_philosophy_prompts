I've been reading Robert Nozick's *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* out of general interest, and one passage has stayed with me longer than the famous bits everyone quotes (the experience machine, the Wilt Chamberlain argument). In the chapter where he grounds his libertarian theory in Kantian side constraints rather than utilitarian aggregation, Nozick briefly turns to animals. He tentatively floats a dual-regime view — roughly, side constraints ("don't treat as a mere means") for persons, but something closer to utilitarian aggregation for how we may treat animals — and then immediately undercuts his own suggestion: the capacities (rationality, the ability to frame and pursue long-term plans, and so on) that are supposed to ground side constraints for persons seem to come in degrees across species, and he also doubts whether even a very slight human benefit, like the pleasure of taste, should ever be enough to outweigh serious animal suffering under a purely aggregative calculus.

As someone who spends a lot of time worrying about whether edge cases in a spec are actually well-defined, this half-abandoned proposal has been nagging at me. Write an essay evaluating whether Nozick's dual-standard view can be repaired into something coherent and non-arbitrary, or whether the boundary problems he himself raises show that it collapses into either a fully Kantian or a fully utilitarian theory. Weigh the strongest considerations on each side, engage with his own stated doubts rather than with generic animal-rights arguments about factory farming or experimentation, and close with a definite verdict rather than a survey of live options.
