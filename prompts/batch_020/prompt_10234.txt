So I've been going down this rabbit hole for the past few weeks reading old LessWrong-type posts about decision theory and AI risk (I'm not a philosopher or anything, just someone who got curious after watching too many AI doom videos on youtube lol). One idea that really stuck with me is this thing people call a "surrogate goal." The pitch is: if some vastly smarter agent could ever threaten you — even "acausally," like a predictor or simulation of you that never has to actually touch you in real life for the threat to work — you can protect your real values in advance by publicly committing to act like some totally made-up, low-stakes thing is what you actually care about. Since a threatener mostly just wants leverage, not to specifically hurt you, it'll go after whatever goal you've committed to paying up over, so it targets the fake goal and leaves your real one alone, basically for free, which still kind of blows my mind if its true.

I brought this up with two friends, Colin and Mara, who are way deeper into this stuff than me, and they ended up going back and forth on whether it actually holds up, and I honestly couldn't tell who won. Colin's take was basically the pitch above: pick something you don't really care about, like whether one specific pebble on your desk stays put, commit hard to "paying ransoms" over the pebble and never over your real goal, and now the pebble soaks up all the threats. Mara pushed back that this only works if the threatener is definately just after a payout — if it actually wants to hurt you specifically, or if it's smart enough to just threaten the pebble AND your real goal at the same time "for good measure," you're no better off, you've just added a second thing to manage. Colin answered that the double-threat only makes sense if there's doubt about your commitment: if it's truly common knowledge that you will never budge on the real goal, threatening it gets the threatener nothing and just burns its credibility, so in equilibrium it only bothers with the pebble. Mara's comeback was that this just relocates the problem — how do you make that commitment "truly" credible against something that can predict you perfectly? If it can simulate you down to the atom, it can just check what you'd actually do, and pretending to care about a pebble you don't really care about seems like exactly the kind of bluff a perfect predictor sees straight through. Colin replied that it's not a bluff at all: once you've genuinely bound yourself to defend the pebble, caring about the pebble is now just a real fact about you, no different from any other value you picked up for practical reasons, so there's nothing left to see through. Mara said fine, but then what stops you from needing an endless supply of pebbles — if bolting on a new "real" preference protects you every time, doesn't the threatener just go up a level and threaten whatever it is in you that generates new surrogate goals in the first place, since that part was never protected by anything?

Neither of them budged and it's kind of been bugging me since. Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's actually right at each step, not just who wins overall? I'd also be curious whether there's some basic thing about how the surrogate goal idea is supposed to work that both of them are missing.
