And here's the answer, transcribed exactly as the student wrote it:

"Arrow's Impossibility Theorem (1951) says that once there are three or more options, there is no way to turn everyone's ranked ballots into a single social ranking that satisfies all of the following at once: unrestricted domain (the rule must accept any possible set of individual rankings as input), the Pareto principle (if every voter prefers A to B, so must society), non-dictatorship (no single voter's preferences automatically become society's regardless of everyone else), and independence of irrelevant alternatives, or IIA, which says society's preference between A and B should depend only on how individuals rank A against B and nothing else — this is actually the same independence assumption that shows up in expected utility theory, the one the Allais paradox is famous for violating, just applied to voters instead of lotteries. Arrow proves you can't have all four at once: any rule that satisfies unrestricted domain, Pareto, and IIA turns out to be a dictatorship, so the only way to avoid a dictator is to give up one of the other conditions.

You can see the trouble with a simple Condorcet cycle. Say voter 1 ranks A>B>C, voter 2 ranks B>C>A, and voter 3 ranks C>A>B. Pairwise majority vote gives A beats B, B beats C, and C beats A — a cycle, so there's no consistent winner. This is exactly the kind of IIA violation Arrow is describing: the majority verdict on A vs. B isn't stable once you factor in where C sits for each voter, so no single social ranking can be constructed.

It's worth being careful about scope, though: the theorem only concerns deterministic rules that aggregate ordinal rankings into a complete social ordering over three or more alternatives. It says nothing about randomized rules (a lottery over outcomes sidesteps the theorem entirely), and it's specifically about ordinal information — cardinal methods like approval or score voting use strictly more information than a bare ranking, which is why there's an ongoing argument about whether Arrow's result even applies to them."
