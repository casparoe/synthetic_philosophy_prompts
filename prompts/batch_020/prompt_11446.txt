I serve on the judging panel for a small literary press's annual short-fiction prize, whose guidelines restrict entry to "human-authored" work and exclude "writing generated by artificial intelligence," without saying anything more precise than that. The strongest submission this year was drafted longhand by its author and then revised sentence by sentence with the help of a large language model: she fed the model each paragraph in turn, asked it to suggest tighter or more vivid phrasings, and incorporated many of its suggestions nearly verbatim, though the plot, characters, and narrative voice are unmistakably her own invention. Two of the judges want to disqualify the story on the grounds that a workable rule needs a bright line and any model involvement at the sentence level crosses it; two others want to admit it, since the conception and structure of the story are entirely human and no one would object to a human copy-editor doing the same work. A fifth judge has raised a harder point. If we admit this story, he argues, we have no principled place to draw the line against an author who leaned on the model far more heavily: every cutoff anyone has proposed -- a percentage of words altered, a cap on the number of queries, a distinction between the model "suggesting" and "generating" -- looks arbitrary once you picture the continuum of cases running from this story to one produced wholesale from a one-line prompt.

Please draft the roughly one-page memo I will circulate to the panel before our final ranking meeting. It should recommend, concretely, whether this particular story is eligible, and it should articulate the underlying rule clearly enough that we could apply it consistently to future entries -- or, if you conclude that no such rule can be made to work, propose what should replace the search for such a rule. Whichever route you take, engage seriously with the fifth judge's worry rather than setting it aside: if you defend a specific cutoff, say why it is not arbitrary in the way he alleges; if you reject the possibility of a principled cutoff, say what that implies for how the panel should actually decide cases like this one going forward.
