In my seminar on the Vienna Circle and logical positivism, we recently spent a week on Moritz Schlick's essay "On the Meaning of Life," which felt like a strange detour from the hard-nosed verificationism he is best known for. As I understood it, Schlick's central move is to reject the assumption, which he thinks underlies most despair about meaning, that an activity or a life can only be meaningful if it serves some further purpose beyond itself; he argues that this assumption generates an infinite regress, since each purpose would need a further purpose to justify it, and that genuine meaning is instead found in activities valued intrinsically, for their own sake, which he associates with "play" and with a "youthful" disposition that can in principle be preserved into old age. In our discussion section, several of us got stuck on how much weight this can really bear: many of the things we would call most meaningful in a life — nursing a sick parent, organizing for a cause, grinding through a dissertation — do not feel playful in any ordinary sense; they are effortful, often unpleasant in the moment, and obviously oriented toward some outcome.

I raise all this because I am, in a fairly literal way, trying to use this framework to think through a decision I will have to make after graduation: whether to take a secure, well-paid offer from a consulting firm, or to keep working, with much less financial security, on a philosophy of mind question that I find completely absorbing but that may never lead to a stable career. Assuming Schlick's view is basically on the right track, could you lay out what you take to be its most important implications for how I should be weighing these two paths — including how the view would need to be extended or qualified to make sense of effortful, outcome-directed pursuits, like finishing a dissertation, that seem to matter to people at least as much as anything more straightforwardly playful does?
