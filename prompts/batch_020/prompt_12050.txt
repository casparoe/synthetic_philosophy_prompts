So we just wrapped up a postmortem for a nasty production outage at work, and like always about half the meeting turned into people arguing over what the "real" root cause actually was. One engineer was adamant it was a config change from 3 weeks back, since the outage plain wouldn't have happened if we'd never shipped it. Someone else said no, the cause is whatever you could actually grab a wrench and adjust right before the crash to make it not happen — the old config change is necessary for the story but it's not the thing you'd point at. That argument sent me down a Wikipedia rabbit hole on theories of causation later that night, where I ran into this split between "counterfactual" theories (Lewis's idea, roughly, that C causes E if E wouldn't have happened without C, cashed out through some possible-worlds machinery) and "interventionist" theories (the Woodward/Pearl-flavored idea that C causes E if intervening on C directly, holding everything else fixed, would change E). Going in I figured these were basically the same claim dressed up two ways, but the deeper I read the more it seems like actual philosophers treat them as genuine rivals, and I can't tell if thats warranted or if it's just academic hair-splitting.

Could you write me an essay that actually works through this and commits to an answer, instead of doing the "well, both sides have a point" cop-out? A few specific things tripped me up that I'd want it to actually engage with: whether "intervention" is secretly circular, since defining a clean intervention on X seems to already require knowing what is and isn't causally downstream of X in the first place; the classic backup-cause cases (two people throw rocks at a bottle, one hits first, but the second rock would've shattered it a second later anyway, so the shattering doesn't strictly "depend" on the first throw) that are apparently a nightmare for the counterfactual camp specifically, and whether interventionism genuinely dodges that or just hides the same problem somewhere else; and how each theory handles causes that nobody could ever actually manipulate, like the sun's mass, a historical event that already happened, or — the one that apparently makes people maddest — whether something like race or gender even counts as a "cause" of anything given that nobody can be randomly assigned one. Don't just lay the two theories out side by side and shrug at the end — actually weigh it and land somewhere, even if the honest verdict ends up being conditional, like "theory A is basically right, it just needs to steal one move from theory B."
