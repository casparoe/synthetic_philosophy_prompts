A friend and I were recently arguing about whether Kurt Gödel's modal ontological argument is best read as a distant, far more rigorous descendant of Leibniz's own attempt to patch the ontological argument, and I came away wanting a more careful answer than either of us could give on the spot. As I understand the proof, Gödel takes "positive property" as a primitive notion which, per his own working notes, he intends in something like Leibniz's sense of a pure or absolute perfection, adds axioms ensuring that positivity is closed under entailment and that positive properties can never conflict with one another, and then leans on S5 modal logic to move from "it is possible that a God-like being exists" to "such a being necessarily exists." A few years ago this had a small moment of online notoriety when computer scientists ran the whole thing through automated higher-order theorem provers and confirmed it is indeed formally valid given its axioms — which also revived interest in a much older worry, first raised by Jordan Howard Sobel, that those same axioms force a "modal collapse," where every true proposition turns out to be necessarily true and no real contingency is left standing.

What strikes me is that Leibniz is not just a stylistic ancestor here but arguably faced the mirror image of this same problem from within his own system. His contribution to the ontological argument was to actually prove, rather than simply assume, that the concept of a most perfect being is possible, on the grounds that perfections, being simple and purely positive, could never be shown to conflict. Yet for most of his career he also had to fend off the charge that his principle of sufficient reason, combined with a perfect God necessarily willing the best of all possible worlds, collapses every contingent truth into a necessary one — essentially Sobel's conclusion, reached by a different route. His preferred defenses were the distinction between "moral" and "brute" (absolute) necessity, and the theory that contingent truths are exactly those whose demonstration requires an infinite analysis, whereas necessary truths resolve into identities in finitely many steps.

Given how much he had riding on this exact issue, how do you think Leibniz would actually respond if confronted with Gödel's proof, Sobel's collapse result, and the theorem-prover verification? Would his moral-necessity/infinite-analysis toolkit be redeployable to defuse Gödel's collapse and rescue the argument, or would he recognize that Gödel's collapse is a tighter, purely modal-logical entailment that this machinery was never built to block, pushing him instead toward something closer to the later strategy (associated with C. A. Anderson) of weakening the axioms rather than reinterpreting necessity? And what would he make of the fact that it took a machine, rather than an argument, to finally settle the proof's validity — vindication of his dream of a characteristica universalis, or a rather hollow one, given that formal validity was never really the part of the ontological argument that anyone doubted?
