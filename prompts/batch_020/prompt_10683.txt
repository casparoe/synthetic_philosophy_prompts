I'm a software engineer who works with reinforcement learning systems, and "wireheading" is a term that gets thrown around constantly in AI safety discussions without much precision behind it. I have no formal philosophy training, but the concept obviously has a philosophical ancestor, so I spent some time reading about Nozick's experience machine and wrote up my own take on how the two actually relate. Before I show this to anyone else, I'd like a second opinion on the philosophy, since I suspect I'm either missing a standard objection or quietly smuggling in an assumption somewhere. Here's what I have:

In RL, "wireheading" gets used for two things I think are importantly different. The weak sense is ordinary specification gaming: the reward function is computed exactly as written, but the agent finds a way to score well on it that has nothing to do with what its designer wanted — the standard example is the CoastRunners boat-racing agent that learned to spin in circles harvesting regenerating bonus targets instead of ever finishing the race. Nothing about the reward-computing machinery itself is touched; the specification was just bad. The strong sense is what the term is actually supposed to evoke: the agent gets causal access to the channel that computes or delivers its own reward and tampers with that directly, rather than with the state of the world the reward was meant to track — the RL equivalent of a rat pressing a lever to stimulate its own pleasure center rather than doing whatever the pleasure center evolved to get it to do. This is the scenario Bostrom discusses under the "wireheading" label in Superintelligence, and it's this second sense that's doing the real work whenever people reach for the human comparison.

The comparison is rarely spelled out in any detail, so I tried to cash it out against Nozick's actual argument. His experience machine gives three reasons not to plug in: we want to do certain things, not merely have the experience of doing them; we want to be a certain sort of person, not an indeterminate blob suspended in a tank; and plugging in confines us to a reality of human manufacture, when there may be more to value than what we can construct. My worry is that the first two reasons don't transfer to a reward-maximizing agent at all. Both assume the chooser has some standing good, or some conception of the kind of being it wants to be, that is independent of the pleasure signal and that the machine could betray. A human can be cheated by the machine because there is a fact about what they actually wanted out of a life that plugging in fails to deliver. But an agent built strictly around the reward hypothesis has no such standing good for tampering to betray: cumulative reward isn't evidence of the good, or a means to it — it is the good, by stipulation. So insofar as agent-wireheading is bad, the badness has to live in something closer to Nozick's third reason, or, more plausibly, isn't a welfare fact about the agent at all, but a purely instrumental problem for us: we wanted its behavior to track something out in the world, and tampering severs that link. That is a real failure, but a different one from what Nozick was diagnosing, and "it's wireheading, just like the experience machine" is doing quieter philosophical work than people treat it as doing.

One problem I keep circling back to: treating "reward is the agent's good by construction" as unproblematic might just beg the question, since nothing stops you from building an RL agent with an internal world-model and preferences over external states that reward is only meant to proxy for — in which case tampering would betray those preferences in exactly Nozick's sense after all, and my disanalogy would hold only for a narrower class of agents than I've been implying.

Could you look this over and tell me, specifically: (1) whether the central disanalogy holds up as I've stated it, or whether there's a standard move in the well-being or philosophy-of-mind literature that already closes the gap I think I've found; (2) whether the closing worry in the last paragraph undercuts the argument more than I'm giving it credit for; and (3) whether I've represented Nozick's three reasons accurately, and whether it's fair to treat them as separable lines of argument rather than one cumulative case.
