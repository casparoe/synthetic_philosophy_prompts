I'm chairing a colloquium Q&A with a philosopher of mind who defends Integrated Information Theory as an identity claim -- a system's experience simply is its maximally irreducible cause-effect structure, not merely correlated with it. Whenever a counterexample comes up, she retreats to the same move: the theory reasons from axioms about experience to constraints on physical substrate, so if the substrate looks strange, it's our intuitions that need revising, not the axioms. Draft eight interview questions that keep testing that move rather than letting her simply restate it. Include one question built on the exclusion postulate's implication that a tiny change in a system's connectivity can flip which of two overlapping candidates counts as conscious (sometimes called the "boundary problem"); one drawing on Scott Aaronson's objection that the theory's own formalism assigns enormous integrated information to simple, densely-connected error-correcting-style networks that intuitively do nothing interesting; and one on the "unfolding argument" -- that any recurrent network can be unfolded into a feedforward one with identical input-output behavior but far lower integrated information -- pressing on what, if anything, is left for the theory to be wrong about. For each, add a follow-up keyed to the reply she's likely to give, and close with a question about what observation, in principle, she would accept as evidence against the theory.
