"Bradley's argument starts from the idea that if two terms, A and B, are related by some relation R, then R can't just be a further item sitting alongside A and B, because then we would need a further relation to connect R to A and another to connect R to B; but those relating relations would themselves need to be connected to what they relate, and so on without end. Since the process never terminates, R never actually succeeds in relating A and B, which is absurd, since things obviously do stand in relations. Bradley uses this to conclude that relations, taken as items distinct from their terms, are ultimately incoherent, which is part of why he thinks reality itself must be a single, all-inclusive whole rather than a plurality of genuinely related things — and it's actually this same regress that later convinced Russell and Moore, in their revolt against idealism, that relations must be treated as perfectly real and external to their terms.

This regress connects to the unity of the proposition because a proposition like 'Caesar defeated Pompey' is not just the collection {Caesar, defeating, Pompey}, since that same collection is also all there is to the false proposition 'Pompey defeated Caesar' — so merely listing the constituents can't be what makes a proposition a single unified claim with a definite truth-value; something has to explain why the parts hang together one way and not the other. But appealing to some further relation to weld the constituents together runs straight into Bradley's regress, since we'd then need to explain how that welding relation itself connects to the things it's supposed to weld.

Frege's response is to say that concepts (the sort of entity a predicate stands for) are 'unsaturated', unlike objects, which are complete in themselves. A concept like being wise has a kind of gap built into it, and slotting a complete object like Socrates into that gap automatically yields a saturated, unified thought, without needing any seperate relation to glue them together, so the regress can't even get started.

Russell's multiple relation theory handles it differently: instead of treating the unity as something the proposition has on its own, he relocates it to the act of judging. When Othello judges that Desdemona loves Cassio, his mind stands in one single multiple relation to Desdemona, to loving, and to Cassio all together, and it's this act of judging that finally welds the elements into a single unified proposition that is genuinely true or false, in a way that sidesteps Bradley's regress entirely, since the judging relation doesn't need a further relation to connect it to what it relates. The main weakness usually raised against this is that it makes the unity of the proposition totally dependent on there being a mind there to do the judging, so unjudged truths, like unnoticed mathematical facts, would apparently not be unified propositions at all until someone actually thought them, which seems like a strange result."
