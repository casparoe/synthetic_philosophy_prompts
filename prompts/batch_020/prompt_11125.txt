My closest friend is getting married in a few weeks and she's asked me to give the speech. Bit of backstory: a few of us started a totally amateur online reading group years ago (none of us went to uni for this, we just got bored one winter and started picking philosophy books at random to argue about), and the one that really stuck with everyone was Moore's Principia Ethica. My friend actually met her now-fiancé in that same group — they spent something like three weeks arguing about the chapter on "the ideal" and somehow that turned into them dating. So I want to build the speech around what Moore actually says there, that personal affection and the appreciation of beauty are basically the two best things there are, way ahead of being clever or successful or even just being a good person on your own, and especially his organic unity idea, where two people genuinely seeing and appreciating each other is worth so much more than either of their good qualities added up separately. What I don't want to do is just list her good qualities and then his good qualities, because I've realised that's kind of exactly the mistake this philosophy tells you not to make — the good isn't in the parts, its in the whole.

Maybe a third of the fifty-ish guests were in the reading group at some point and will catch every reference, the rest are just her family and coworkers who have never heard of G.E. Moore in their life. Can you write me a five minute wedding speech that actually uses this idea to decide what gets said (not just one line dropping his name and moving on), while still working completely as a warm, funny speech for everyone else — it can't turn into a philosophy lecture. If there's a natural place for it, I also vaguely remember that Virginia Woolf's whole Bloomsbury crowd basically built their social lives on this exact chapter of Moore, and Keynes wrote about it later, kind of ruefully, about how they took his religion of beauty and love but quietly dropped the actual rules about decent conduct that came right before it in the book. Feels like an honest, slightly funny thing to gesture at, that even the people most obsessed with this idea didn't fully live up to it — but don't force it in if it doesn't fit naturally.
