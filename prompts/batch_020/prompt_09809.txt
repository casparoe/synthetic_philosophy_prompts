I'm a PhD student in philosophy specializing in decision theory and formal epistemology, and I've spent the last few weeks TAing an introductory course that just finished covering the St. Petersburg paradox. When a student asked whether every "expected value breaks down" puzzle is basically the same trick (payoffs get unboundedly large, so you cap the utility function and move on), I went looking for a cleaner counterexample and landed back on Nover and Hájek's Pasadena game, which I've always thought is the more interesting case but is annoyingly hard to explain in five minutes of class time.

Here's the setup, for context: you flip a fair coin until it lands heads for the first time; if the first heads comes on toss n, you're paid 2^n/n when n is odd and you pay 2^n/n when n is even. Since the probability of first heads on toss n is exactly 1/2^n, each term of the expectation sum collapses to (-1)^(n-1)/n, so the expected value of the whole game is just the alternating harmonic series, 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + …, which converges to ln 2, but only conditionally. By the Riemann rearrangement theorem, that very same infinite set of (probability, payoff) pairs can be reshuffled to sum to any real number you like, or to positive infinity, or to negative infinity, or to nothing determinate at all, without a single fact about the underlying gamble ever changing. So the standard move against St. Petersburg — cap the payoffs, or cap utility — does nothing here: the trouble was never that a number got too big, it's that there is no well-defined number at all unless you first stipulate an order of summation.

Could you write me a short story of around 1,200 to 2,000 words built around that specific fact, rather than the more familiar "the payout is infinite, so something's wrong" moral a St.-Petersburg-flavored story would reach for? I'm picturing a protagonist whose livelihood depends on being able to price gambles — an actuary, a quant, a professional card counter, whatever you find workable — who is offered exactly this game by a counterpart able to make it look like a windfall or a catastrophe purely by choosing the order in which the running total is read aloud, without ever altering a single term of the underlying game. Set it wherever and whenever you like; something with a quietly uncanny or noir-ish register seems apt, though I'll leave the texture to you. I'd like the unease to come from the protagonist, and the reader, realizing that nothing about the gamble itself has changed between the two tellings, only the bookkeeping — and please don't let the character stumble onto a fix at the end. The obvious ones (insisting on a privileged ordering, restricting payoffs to be bounded, restricting decision theory to finite state spaces) are exactly the escape routes Nover and Hájek consider and reject in the original paper, so a tidy resolution would be less faithful to the actual state of the debate than an ending that leaves the paradox, and the protagonist, genuinely hanging.
