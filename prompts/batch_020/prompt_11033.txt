That sent me down a side rabbit hole reading about Bohmian mechanics / pilot-wave theory on my own, since apparently theres still a small but serious contingent of physicists who treat it as a live option, which honestly surprised me.

Heres the bit I can't get straight though. From what I read, Bell wrote his famous 1964 paper specifically because he'd been impressed by Bohm's 1952 hidden-variable theory and wanted to check whether you could build a version of it that got rid of the obviously weird part -- the pilot wave instantaneously guiding every particle no matter how far apart they are. And the punchline of the theorem is that you can't: any hidden-variable theory that reproduces QM's predictions has to be nonlocal, full stop. So wouldn't that mean the theorem directly refutes Bohmian mechanics, since that's the literal theory it was built out of and found "guilty" of nonlocality? But every summary I read insists Bell's theorem "doesn't rule out" pilot-wave theory, and apparently Bell himself stayed a genuine admirer of Bohm's approach for the rest of his life, which seems like a strange thing to admire if his own proof is what killed it. Am I misunderstanding what the theorem actually rules in versus rules out, or is this just physicists being sentimental about a theory their own math put in the ground?
