So my friend Dana and I got into it pretty badly last night, over some mediocre beer until like 1am, about whether Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" actually settles anything about free will and blame. I'm halfway through a seminar on moral responsibility this semester so the piece was pretty fresh in my head. Dana's take: once you really absorb that resentment, gratitude, guilt, indignation, all of that, are just built into human relationships in a way we can't opt out of, the question "but is it justified given determinism" basically dissolves, because you're demanding a justification from outside a framework that was never optional for us to begin with — so the demand itself is confused, not just unanswered. I said that's kind of a bait-and-switch: pointing out that we can't help reacting with resentment doesn't tell you whether resentment is fitting, any more than the fact that people can't help finding a visual illusion compelling tells you the illusion is accurate. That's a straightforward is-ought gap, not a dissolution of the question. Dana thinks I'm strawmanning the naturalism here, because on her reading Strawson isn't using the ubiquity of reactive attitudes as a premise in an argument for their fittingness at all — he's rejecting the idea that fittingness needs grounding in facts about determinism in the first place, so there's no inference left for an is-ought objection to grab onto.

We also got stuck on how this plays out in a harder case: someone whose capacity for ordinary fellow-feeling basically never got the chance to develop, because of a genuinely horrific childhood, who then does something terrible as an adult. Strawson's excuses ("he didn't mean it," "he was pushed") are supposed to leave the reactive attitudes intact, and his exemptions ("he's not himself," the sort of thing we'd say about a severe psychopath) suspend them wholesale in favor of the purely objective attitude. But this case seems to license both reactions depending on whether your attention is on the act or on the formation, and its not obvious that Strawson's appeal to what's "natural" for us to feel can adjudicate between them, since both reactions feel equally natural to have. I think that's actually a bigger problem for Dana's reading than mine, but she wasn't having it, and we never got anywhere.

I'd love an actual verdict instead of a "both sides have a point" cop-out: can you build the strongest possible case that my is-ought objection really does land against Strawson's argumentative strategy in "Freedom and Resentment," and that Dana's more quietist reading doesn't get him off the hook? Feel free to use the abusive-childhood case above (or a better one of your own) to show concretely where the strategy breaks down, rather than staying at the level of abstract talk about "the framework."
