I'm reporting a feature on the strange afterlife of Ludwig Boltzmann's statistical mechanics in contemporary cosmology, and one historical wrinkle keeps nagging at me. In his running dispute with Zermelo and other critics of the H-theorem during the 1890s, Boltzmann's replies included, on one hand, the observation that the recurrence time for any macroscopic system so vastly exceeds the age of the world that no observer could ever be present to witness a violation of the Second Law; and, on the other — in an idea he pointedly credited not to himself but to his assistant, Ignaz Schütz — the suggestion that the universe as a whole might rest eternally in thermal equilibrium, with our own low-entropy corner of it simply being one of the rare regions where a statistical fluctuation has produced order, since nowhere else could there be anyone around to notice. Carry that same Boltzmann-Schütz reasoning forward a century and it is essentially what generates today's notorious "Boltzmann brain" problem in eternal-inflation cosmology: on many current models, a long-lived vacuum will overwhelmingly tend to generate isolated, momentarily conscious brains through random fluctuation, in numbers that vastly outnumber observers like us, who arose the "expensive" way, through a genuinely low-entropy early universe, stars, and evolution — which threatens to turn our own orderly memories into evidence against the very theories that predict them.

A physicist I interviewed for the piece dismissed the question as pointless, on the grounds that asking what Boltzmann would make of eternal inflation is like asking what he would have made of streaming television. I'm not convinced that's fair, since the whole appeal of the question is that his own reasoning seems to generalize well beyond anything he could have foreseen — and that generalization is what I'd like your help reconstructing. If Boltzmann were briefed today on the Boltzmann brain problem, which way do you think his own instincts, including his oddly diffident attribution of the fluctuation idea to Schütz, would actually push him: toward embracing an eternally inflating multiverse as a natural extension of a picture he was already willing to entertain, or toward resisting the reasoning once it starts undermining the very evidence on which it depends? I would also like your sense of whether he would have found David Albert's later move of simply positing a low-entropy "Past Hypothesis" as a brute initial condition more congenial than either option, or Roger Penrose's insistence that the initial entropy is fixed by some deeper, still-unknown law rather than being a statistical accident at all. Please give me a specific, defended verdict on where he would have landed and why, rather than an even-handed tour of the possibilities.
