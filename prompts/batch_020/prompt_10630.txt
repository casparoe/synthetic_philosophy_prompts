I'm the undergraduate student representative on my university's Merit Scholarship Appeals Committee, and this term I'm also taking a seminar on vagueness that has spent the last few weeks on the sorites paradox -- epistemicism, supervaluationism, contextualism, and the rest. The two have now collided. Our scholarship requires a cumulative GPA of at least 3.50 to renew; a sophomore who is herself a philosophy minor just missed the cutoff with a 3.49 and has appealed on the grounds that the committee cannot draw a non-arbitrary line between a 3.50 student who keeps full funding and a 3.49 student who loses it completely: no single hundredth of a grade point, she argues, could plausibly carry that much moral weight. She adds that a forced march down the GPA scale, one hundredth of a point at a time, would show the same thing about any cutoff the committee might choose instead. Our bylaws let the committee waive the requirement for "extenuating circumstances," but that phrase is undefined, and half the committee wants to treat a near-miss like hers as exactly that; the other half worries that if we do, we will have no principled way to refuse the next student who falls short by a little more, and then the one after that. I mentioned the case to a couple of people in my seminar and got no consensus at all, which was not especially reassuring.

I have to write the short memo that goes to the full committee ahead of Friday's vote, and I would rather not fall back on "a line has to be drawn somewhere," since her appeal is precisely an argument that no particular line can be drawn without arbitrariness, and at least two committee members will recognize the move immediately. Could you draft that memo for me? It should recommend one specific outcome -- denying the appeal, granting it, or some intermediate option such as a one-semester probationary renewal -- and it should actually engage the sorites-style argument on its merits, the way someone who has thought carefully about vagueness would, rather than treating it as a debating trick to be waved away. It should also state plainly the strongest reason to doubt whatever recommendation you end up making.
