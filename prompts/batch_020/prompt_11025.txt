This term I'm taking a graduate seminar cross-listed between philosophy and computer science on the foundations of rational agency, and this week's set text was Abram Demski and Scott Garrabrant's "Embedded Agency" write-up for MIRI. The core move there is to contrast idealized "Cartesian" or "dualistic" agents — like Marcus Hutter's AIXI, which sits cleanly outside its environment and interacts with it only through a well-defined input/output channel — with "embedded" agents, which are themselves part of the environment they are modeling, are smaller than it, and therefore cannot hold a single complete model of the world that also includes a complete model of themselves. In discussion section, two people in my group ended up talking past each other on how much this distinction really matters philosophically, and I'd like to see the disagreement worked out properly rather than rely on my notes from a fast-moving argument.

Could you write a dialogue between two characters, Priya and Sam, who hold opposing views on this? Priya thinks the Cartesian picture is still the right regulative ideal for rationality, and that embeddedness only ever introduces practical, resource-bounded complications: an agent cannot run a perfect internal simulation of itself, granted, but that's just an extreme case of ordinary computational limitation, to be chipped away at with better approximations rather than a new theory of what rational agency even is. Sam thinks embeddedness marks a genuine conceptual break, and wants to press the point using the "robust delegation" problem: because an embedded agent must decide whether to trust a future or successor version of itself using only its own current reasoning, it runs into the Löbian obstacle — roughly, the fact that a system generally cannot consistently prove "whatever my future self proves to be good, is good" without that scheme collapsing into triviality. Sam's claim is that this is a fact about what can be derived from within a formal system, not a resource constraint, so no amount of extra compute fixes it, which is part of why some people reach for genuinely different tools, like logical induction, rather than a more powerful version of ordinary Bayesian updating.

Please have Priya and Sam actually engage with each other's strongest moves rather than talk past one another — I especially want Priya to have a substantive reply to the Löbian-obstacle point rather than simply conceding it, and Sam to have a substantive reply to the charge that all of this is "just" bounded rationality under a new name. Let the exchange run several rounds, and close with each of them stating, in a single sentence, what would actually change their mind.
