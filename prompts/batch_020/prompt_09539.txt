A friend and I spent most of dinner last night arguing about whether racial categories in medicine track anything biologically real or simply launder old prejudice into clinical numbers, and neither of us budged. It turns out the question isn't merely academic for me: I sit on my hospital's clinical algorithms committee, formed a few years ago after the controversy over race adjustments in kidney-function estimates, and it has just inherited a case nobody anticipated back then. Our pulmonary function lab still uses spirometry reference equations that assign a lower "normal" predicted lung capacity to Black patients than to white patients of identical height, age, and sex — a correction with roots in nineteenth-century claims about racial differences in chest size, which some pulmonologists on staff nonetheless defend as a statistically real predictor, whatever its causal story, warning that the "race-neutral" equations now recommended by several pulmonary societies will simply misclassify real patients to make a moral point. That was close to my friend's position too, and I never managed to answer it well. Our charter says we should favor whichever option is "most clinically valid and equitable," without saying what to do when those two criteria pull apart, which is exactly our situation. We need a recommendation before next month's quality meeting — adopt the race-neutral equations, keep the current ones, or report both. Please draft the memo I'll bring to that meeting: a concrete decision with an implementation timeline, one that engages the objection on its merits rather than dismissing it.
