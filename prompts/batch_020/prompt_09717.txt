I have been asked to give the commencement address for a physics department's graduating PhD cohort. The department's top dissertation prize is named for Émilie du Châtelet, and the chair has asked that the address engage with the actual argument of her "Discourse on Happiness" rather than the usual Newton-translator anecdote. Her claim, briefly: because love, ambition, and reputation all depend on luck and on other people, lasting happiness requires one passion that depends on nothing but oneself and does not fade with age, rejection, or bad luck — for her, that passion was study. Half the room will be physicists who have lived through the job-market grind and will wince at anything glib; the other half will be family who have never heard of her and just want a happy send-off. Make her argument do real work for graduates facing years of postdocs and grant rejections, without collapsing into a generic "find your passion" platitude or turning into a lecture on the eighteenth-century exclusion of women from the academy — even though that exclusion is exactly why she needed such an argument in the first place. Write the address (about seven minutes).
