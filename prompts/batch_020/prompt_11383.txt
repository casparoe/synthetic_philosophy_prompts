Question: Van Inwagen's Special Composition Question (SCQ) asks: under what conditions do some objects, the xs, compose a further object y? (a) Briefly state the universalist and the nihilist answers to the SCQ. (b) Explain van Inwagen's own answer, and how the "trout-turkey" case is supposed to motivate it. (c) Give one objection to van Inwagen's answer, and say whether you think it succeeds.

Student answer: The Special Composition Question asks, for any objects the xs, what has to be true of them for there to be some further object that they compose. Universalism answers that composition is totally unrestricted: literally any two or more objects, however scattered or dissimilar, add up to a further object. Nihilism gives the opposite answer: composition never occurs, so, strictly speaking, there are no tables or rocks, only simples arranged tablewise or rockwise.

Van Inwagen thinks both answers are too extreme, and he uses the trout-turkey case to make the point vivid: nihilism has to deny that gluing the front half of a trout to the back half of a turkey produces any new object at all, but that seems wrong, since we can point right at the hybrid thing sitting on the table. His own answer, defended in Material Beings, is that the xs compose something exactly when their activity constitutes a single life. That's why cats and people count as composite objects -- and since a beating heart or a growing leaf is itself alive in the relevant sense, organs and limbs count as further composite objects nested inside the organism, even though artifacts like chairs don't count as objects at all.

The obvious objection is that this is wildly revisionary, since it implies chairs, cars, and stars don't exist. But van Inwagen can just paraphrase ordinary talk about chairs into talk about simples arranged chairwise, so I don't think the objection really lands: he keeps everything we wanted to say, just without the extra ontological commitment.
