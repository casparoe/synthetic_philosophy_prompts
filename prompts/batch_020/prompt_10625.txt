So I'm teaching Hume's theory of motivation this week and got completely derailed noticing how much of Bostrom's orthogonality thesis is basically already sitting there in the Treatise. Hume says reason is "the slave of the passions," and even claims its no more contrary to reason to prefer the destruction of the whole world to the scratching of his finger than the other way round, since reason can only ever serve ends, never rank them -- which is pretty much the exact move AI safety people make when they argue a superintelligence could be flawlessly rational while single-mindedly maximizing paperclips. If you actually sat Hume down and explained what a paperclip maximizer was, do you think he'd just own that as a natural extension of his own view, or would he dig up principled resources in his own psychology -- sentiment, sympathy, the calm-versus-violent-passions stuff -- to resist calling such a thing "rational" at all? Don't just lay out both options for me, tell me which way you actually think he'd land, and why.
