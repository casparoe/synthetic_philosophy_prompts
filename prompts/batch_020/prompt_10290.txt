I'm several chapters into a historical novel set in the eastern Mediterranean during the naval wars that followed Alexander's death, and I've reached a scene I would like to anchor in a genuine philosophical dispute rather than in battle-eve atmosphere alone. My fleet commander keeps two advisers close to him, and on the night before an engagement the two of them fall into an argument that amounts to a dramatized version of the old dispute over future contingents -- fittingly, since Aristotle's own example in the Peri Hermeneias (On Interpretation) was itself a sea battle, a coincidence my narrator can't resist remarking on.

One adviser, trained by logicians in the circle of Diodorus Cronus, pushes toward a fatalist conclusion using something close to Diodorus's own Master Argument: every truth about the past is now necessary; nothing impossible follows from something possible; and these two claims cannot be squared with there being any real, unrealized possibility about tomorrow's fight. So, he argues, whatever is true right now about who wins is already as fixed as yesterday's wind, and the night's planning is theater dressed up as deliberation. The other adviser, trained in the Lyceum's tradition, is certain this is a trick, but I don't want her simply to insist that the future is "open" and leave it at that -- she needs to locate the actual joint she is prying apart, whether that means denying that tomorrow's claim about the battle is already true or false at all, or granting that it is while denying that this makes it necessary (I've come across the suggestion that the fatalist quietly slides from "necessarily, either the fleet wins or it doesn't" to "either it is necessary that the fleet wins, or it is necessary that it doesn't" -- which is not obviously the same claim).

Could you write their exchange as a dialogue -- a dozen or so turns -- that I could plausibly adapt for the novel? I want both advisers to be real equals: the fatalist should not be a caricature of grim resignation, and the Aristotelian should not win simply by gesturing at the importance of courage and deliberation before a fight. Each should answer the other's actual last point, and at least one exchange should turn on a genuine logical distinction rather than on morale or rhetoric. The scene need not end in a concession on either side -- that might even be truer to the material -- but by its last line it should be entirely clear exactly which premise of the fatalist's argument the two of them now understand themselves to disagree about.
