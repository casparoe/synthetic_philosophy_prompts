I coordinate the peer-review meetings for the quality and patient-safety committee at the hospital where I work -- I'm not a doctor, and I'm certainly not a philosopher, but part of my job is turning our messier discussions into memos the medical staff office can act on, and this particular one sent me down a rabbit hole of reading on my own for the better part of two weeks. The case: a chest CT read as clear eighteen months ago, on which the same faint shadow is now, in hindsight, an obvious two-centimeter mass. The radiologist involved isn't arguing that he glanced at it and dismissed it as nothing; he's arguing that with "rule out pneumonia" on the requisition and a full worklist that day, the mass genuinely didn't perceptually stand out from the surrounding tissue at the time he looked, in much the way expert radiologists in a well-known experiment were shown to walk their eyes right over a gorilla image spliced into a lung scan they were scanning for nodules. His claim, in effect, is that what you're told to look for changes what you're able to see, not just what you conclude afterward, and that this was a perceptual failure rather than a lapse of care or attention.

The committee split three ways, and no one's position struck me as silly. Two of our senior radiologists sided with him: they said the effect is real and well-documented, that penalizing him for it is like penalizing someone for falling for an optical illusion, and that any fault lies in a system that hands radiologists a diagnostic frame before they've looked at the images. Our risk manager pushed back hard: she said "my expectations shaped my perception" describes the mechanism of the failure, not an excuse for it, since the entire reason double reads and structured checklists exist is to catch exactly this kind of expectation-driven blindness -- he's credentialed precisely on the promise that he's trained to compensate for it. One of the senior radiologists answered that you can't train yourself out of a genuinely perceptual effect any more than you can stop seeing the Müller-Lyer illusion by learning the lines are equal, so "he should have compensated" quietly assumes the very thing in dispute. A younger attending tried a middle path: she agreed the perceptual story is probably true, but said that only tells us to fix the system going forward (for instance, always reading images once before the clinical history is revealed), not that this particular miss was blameless, since he still had some discretion over how carefully he looked even if not over what ultimately registered.

I have to draft the memo the committee is expecting before next month's meeting, and I'd rather not just split the difference to keep everyone comfortable. Could you draft it for me? It should recommend, with reasons, what happens to this specific case (treated as a reportable event requiring remediation, or not) and whether we change our reading protocol going forward, with a rough timeframe for any change -- and it should say plainly whether "his perception was shaped by what he was told to look for" is the kind of thing that should reduce a person's responsibility, engaging honestly with the strongest point on whichever side the memo doesn't fully adopt.
