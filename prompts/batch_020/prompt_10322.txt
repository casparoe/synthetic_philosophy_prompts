I've been teaching Popper's stuff on verisimilitude in my philosophy of science seminar this week, and I keep fumbling how to present the punchline to my students. So Popper wants a notion of "truthlikeness" that lets us say a false theory can still be closer to the truth then another false theory -- that's supposed to be his answer to the worry that if all our best theories eventually get refuted, then "progress" talk in science is empty. He cashes it out via truth-content and falsity-content: roughly, how much of what a theory logically implies is true versus false, and theory B beats theory A in verisimilitude if B has at least as much true content and no more false content than A (I'm paraphrasing from memory here, so don't hold me to the exact clauses).

Then, famously, Miller and Tichý independently showed in the mid-70s that the whole thing collapses: on Popper's own definition it turns out that no false theory can ever come out more verisimilar than any other false theory, full stop. Which is kind of disastrous for Popper, since on his own fallibilist view literally every theory science has ever produced is false in some respect, so the concept ends up doing zero work for the exact case it was invented for.

I've seen this result cited a dozen times but never gotten a clean feel for WHY it falls out of the definition -- like, why does tacking on extra true consequences to a false theory always seem to drag along at least as much extra false content, so the comparison never goes through? Could you walk me through the intuition in a way that would actually land with second-year undergrads, ideally with a small toy example instead of staying fully abstract? If you've also got a quick take on why the later "quantitative" fixes (Oddie, Niiniluoto, that whole literature) are supposed to dodge the same problem I'd be curious, but mainly I just want the gut-level reason Popper's original version breaks.
