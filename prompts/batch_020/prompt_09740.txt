While researching a magazine piece on overlooked corners of utilitarian thought, I learned that Jeremy Bentham spent decades drafting a sustained defense of what his era branded sexual "irregularities" — chiefly male homosexuality, but also masturbation and other non-procreative acts — arguing purely from his felicific calculus that when consenting parties derive pleasure and no third party is harmed, the suffering inflicted by criminalizing the act (imprisonment, blackmail, even the gallows, since sodomy was a capital offense in his day) vastly outweighs any harm the act itself could cause. Tellingly, Bentham seems to have sensed how incendiary this reasoning was: he left most of it in manuscript, his own editor suppressed it after his death, and it did not begin reaching print until 1978, when a single essay appeared in an academic journal; the fuller body of writing only reached a proper scholarly edition, "Of Sexual Irregularities," in 2014 — nearly two centuries after most of it was composed.

A philosopher friend and I got into a debate about this over dinner recently, and it's left me wanting a more rigorous outside opinion. The puzzle is how Bentham's reasoning would fare against a distinctly modern feature of sexual ethics: the rise of "affirmative" or "enthusiastic" consent as the operative test, in some campus codes and state laws (California's "yes means yes" statute among them), for whether a sexual encounter was ethically legitimate — where the load-bearing criterion is an ongoing, mutual, freely given "yes," rather than simply the absence of coercion or demonstrable harm. Bentham's own case for sexual liberty was built from the absence of harm and the presence of pleasure, not from treating consent as a freestanding moral category that matters independently of any pleasure-pain ledger. So: is affirmative consent basically a natural extension of Bentham's own reasoning, or does it quietly import a rights-based notion of bodily sovereignty that sits awkwardly with a purely aggregative calculus of pleasure and pain? And is there a consistent, no-exceptions version of Bentham's utilitarianism that could actually end up in tension with strict consent requirements — say, in edge cases where overriding someone's hesitation might be argued, on purely hedonic grounds, to raise net pleasure?

I'd like your reasoning in some depth rather than a quick verdict either way, since this is precisely the kind of nuance that gets lost when Bentham is invoked as a straightforward patron saint of sexual liberalism.
