I don't have any formal training in biology or philosophy - I fix industrial refrigeration units for a living - but I've spent most of my evenings this past year reading my way through popular genetics books after a stray Wikipedia click led me down a rabbit hole. The question I can't let go of is whether it actually makes sense to call DNA a "code," rather than just borrowing that word because it sounds impressive. I brought this up at a book club a few of us started, and after getting mostly blank stares I decided to write out my own answer properly instead of just half-arguing it out loud. I'd like your comments on the essay below before I bother anyone else with it.

Here is what I've written so far:

Everybody concedes that the genetic code is "arbitrary" in one specific sense: there is no chemical law forcing the triplet GCU to mean alanine rather than some other amino acid. As far as anyone can tell, the assignment is what Francis Crick called, in a phrase that stuck with me, a frozen accident - whichever pairing got fixed first stayed fixed, because by the time anything complicated had evolved on top of it, changing the dictionary would have scrambled every protein at once. People who scoff at the word "code" and insist DNA is "just chemistry" seem to be denying exactly this arbitrariness, and I think that is the wrong place to plant the flag, because the arbitrariness is real, and it was apparently suspected before anyone had even seen the double helix: Schrödinger was already reaching for something like a "code-script" for the chromosomes back in the 1940s, purely by reasoning about how stable, rearrangeable hereditary information would have to be stored.

Where I part ways with the "it's a real code" side is that I don't think arbitrariness is what makes something a code in the first place. An actual code - Morse, a cipher, a traffic light - is something that can be used wrongly: misread, sent incorrectly, or used to lie. There has to be room for the symbol to come apart from what it's supposed to mean. A ribosome doesn't misread a codon in that sense. It can attach the wrong amino acid because of a chemical error, but that is a different chemical event, not a mistranslation, and we only call it a "mistake" because we have already decided, from outside the whole system, what the "correct" outcome should be by looking at what past selection happened to favor. The rightness and wrongness a real code needs isn't sitting inside the molecule; we bring it in from evolutionary history and then talk as though we discovered it in there.

That has consequences for the other half of the popular picture, the idea of DNA as a "blueprint," and this is where the honeybee case bothers me. A queen and a worker start out with essentially the same genome; which one a given larva becomes seems to hinge on whether it was fed royal jelly, and from what I understand this works through chemical marking of the DNA rather than any change to the sequence itself. If one "blueprint" produces two different animals depending on what the builder happens to be fed, it is hard to see how the sequence is doing the job a blueprint is supposed to do. Whatever is deciding "which bee" looks like it lives in the feeding and the marking, not in the four-letter text everyone points to.

What I'd like from you: first, tell me if I have the history right, or if I'm garbling the Crick and Schrödinger bits into something that sounds good but isn't true. Second, and more importantly, is my normativity test for code-hood - the idea that a real code has to allow for misuse - actually doing philosophical work, or have I just smuggled in the conclusion by defining "code" so that DNA is guaranteed to fail? Third, if this is basically a well-worn argument that already has a name and a literature I've never encountered, I'd rather know that than keep reinventing it badly. And please don't be gentle with whichever paragraph is weakest - if the honeybee example actually cuts the other way, or proves less than I think it does, I want that said plainly rather than folded into a compliment.
