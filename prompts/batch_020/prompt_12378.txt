Here is the question (worth 20 points total; no notes or outside sources were allowed):

"Christopher Heath Wellman ('Immigration and Freedom of Association,' 2008) argues that legitimate states have a right to exclude potential immigrants. Michael Huemer ('Is There a Right to Immigrate?,' 2010) argues, via his 'Starving Marvin' thought experiment, that immigration restrictions are presumptively unjust. (a) Reconstruct the core argument of each paper, identifying the primary moral consideration each author relies on. [10 pts] (b) Sarah Fine ('Freedom of Association Is Not the Answer,' 2010) criticizes Wellman on the grounds that the analogy between states and voluntary associations like clubs or marriages breaks down in important ways. Give one specific respect in which she thinks the analogy fails. [5 pts] (c) Would Fine's objection, if successful, also threaten Huemer's argument? Why or why not? [5 pts]"

And here is the student's answer, transcribed exactly as submitted:

"(a) Wellman's argument rests on an analogy between individuals and states. He holds that legitimate states are entitled to political self-determination, that freedom of association is a core part of self-determination, and that freedom of association includes the freedom not to associate with people one doesn't want to associate with. Just as a person is free to turn down a marriage proposal for any reason (or no reason), a political community is free to turn away would-be members. This gives states a presumptive right to exclude, though Wellman is careful to say it isn't unlimited — for instance, it doesn't license just killing unwanted migrants, and he pairs it with an expanded duty of humanitarian assistance elsewhere. The moral consideration doing the work is the self-determination and associative interest of the excluding community.

Huemer's argument works differently. He asks us to consider Marvin, who is starving and on his way to a market to buy bread from a willing seller, when Sam coercively blocks him from getting there, so Marvin dies. Nearly everyone agrees Sam has wronged Marvin. Huemer thinks the same structure is present whenever a state turns away a migrant who has a willing employer or landlord on the other side: the state is coercively interfering with an otherwise consensual, mutually beneficial arrangement. Given how large the resulting harms are — economists have calculated that letting labor move to where it's most productive could nearly double gross world product — Huemer thinks the state needs an unusually strong justification for imposing this cost, and he goes on to argue that the standard justifications (job competition, fiscal burden, crime, cultural change) don't clear that bar. So where Wellman's argument privileges the associative interest of the excluding community, Huemer's privileges the sheer scale of the harm done to excluded migrants and, ultimately, to the world economy.

(b) Fine denies that the analogy between states and clubs or marriages holds up. Individuals and voluntary associations are entered and exited by consent, and someone turned down by one partner or club is free to seek out others; state exclusion isn't like this, since there's no equivalent alternative state waiting in the wings for a rejected migrant to join instead, and being excluded from a wealthy, stable state can be far more consequential for someone's basic interests than being excluded from a golf club. So the analogy quietly treats two very different kinds of 'refusal to associate' as morally equivalent.

(c) I think Fine's objection generalizes to Huemer as well. Both authors are ultimately working within the same picture, on which the central question is whether a bounded political community is entitled to act like a private association with discretion over who joins it — Wellman answers yes, and Huemer's Starving Marvin case is really just a vivid way of arguing no, at least not when the costs to outsiders are this large. But since Fine's whole point is that the state/association analogy doesn't hold up in the first place, her objection removes the shared premise that both Wellman's defense and Huemer's critique of exclusion depend on. If there's no good sense in which a state is relevantly like a club or a marriage, the entire debate about whether that kind of entity is entitled to exclude on associative grounds is confused from the start, on both sides."
