I'm on my department's undergraduate curriculum committee, and we've hit a genuinely fun/thorny snag while revising the syllabus for the utilitarianism unit in "Intro to Ethics." One colleague wants to pair the usual excerpts from the Principles of Morals and Legislation with a page or two of Bentham's own writing on sexual morality — the stuff he was too nervous to publish himself, like "Offences Against One's Self" (written around 1785, arguing for decriminalizing consensual sodomy at a time when it was still a hanging offense, and not printed in anything like full form until Louis Crompton's 1978 edition) and the mid-1810s material the Bentham Project finally released in 2014 as Of Sexual Irregularities. Her pitch is that nowhere else does Bentham run the felicific calculus so nakedly on a question everyone around him treated as settled by disgust rather than argument — he ranks harms (adultery worse than sodomy, because of illegitimate children), leans on Greek and Roman precedent, even argues non-procreative sex is a demographic upside given Malthus — and that keeping all this out of the syllabus just gives students a tame, textbook Bentham who never has to bite a real bullet. Two other colleagues are pushing back: one thinks it's simply not appropriate reading for a mixed 100-level lecture hall of mostly non-majors, the other thinks it'll derail into "look how edgy old Jeremy was" instead of teaching the actual logical structure of hedonic calculus. And I keep getting stuck on a side question nobodys quite resolved: Bentham spent his career arguing that publicity was the great check on abuses of power, then sat on his own most radical conclusion for decades — does teaching that irony properly require assigning the primary text, or can you make the point just as well at one remove?

Could you draft the one-page memo I'll circulate before next Tuesday's meeting? I need it to land on an actual recommendation — in, out, or some modified form (optional reading, moved to the upper-level history-of-ethics seminar instead, a specific excerpt paired with a framing question, whatever you think is defensible) — backed by reasons that would actually move my colleagues rather than a "well, it's complicated" shrug, and it needs to take the appropriateness worry seriously rather than just rolling its eyes at it.
