I'm about two-thirds into a novel that's got a subplot involving a restorative justice circle -- the kind of thing where instead of a straightforward trial-and-prison route, the victim, the offender, and a bunch of community members sit in a room together and try to work out repair and accountability face to face. To get a scene right where an older, cynical philosophy-professor character starts riffing on it, I went back and reread Nietzsche's Genealogy of Morals (the second essay especially, all the debtor/creditor stuff and bad conscience), and now I honestly can't decide what he'd say, which is a problem since the professor needs an actual position, not just vague crankiness.

Here's my issue: restorative justice explicitly tries to avoid stigmatizing shame in favor of what the theory calls "reintegrative" shaming -- disapproval that's meant to repair the bond with the community rather than brand someone permanently -- and it drops the idea that an offender owes some abstract debt that isolation or punishment "pays off." That sounds like it's almost undoing the exact move Nietzsche says Christian morality made, turning a literal, payable debt into a permanent, internalized guilt -- so maybe he'd read it as a return to something healthier. But it's also communal, consensus-driven, therapeutic in tone, deeply suspicious of hierarchy and of the older "festival of cruelty" logic he says punishment grew out of -- which sounds exactly like the slave-morality, herd-taming instinct he spends the whole book sneering at.

So could you actually reason this through instead of hedging -- using the specific machinery of the Genealogy (the debtor/creditor logic, bad conscience as internalized cruelty, the ascetic priest, the noble vs. slave-moral responses to being wronged) -- and tell me where Nietzsche would actually land on restorative justice as its practiced now, and why? I'd rather hand my professor character one real, defensible argument than the usual stereotype of "Nietzsche was basically edgy and would've hated anything nice."
