Ive been down a Thomas Hobbes rabbit hole lately, not the social contract stuff but the weirder side where he was obsessed with geometry and spent decades convinced he'd squared the circle, doubled the cube, all of it, and kept getting publicly wrecked over it by the mathematician John Wallis. From what I can tell, his whole reason for thinking geometry was the one truly certain science was that we construct the figures ourselves, so we can't be wrong about them the way we can with nature, and he also seemed to genuinely hate Wallis's algebra, like symbols on a page didn't count as real math unless there was an actual construction behind them.

So heres what I'm curious about: what would Hobbes have made of non-euclidean geometry, where you mess with Euclid's parallel postulate and still get a totally consistent geometry out the other end? And then what happens to his whole view once you tell him Einstein later showed that real physical space isn't even euclidean? Does his reason for why geometry is uniquely certain just collapse at that point, or is there a way he could've made it work?
