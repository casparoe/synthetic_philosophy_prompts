I'm a PhD student in philosophy specializing in decision theory and population ethics, and I TA an undergraduate seminar on formal approaches to ethics. During last week's discussion section on aggregation, we covered Yudkowsky's "torture vs. dust specks" thought experiment — the choice between torturing one person continuously for fifty years or giving a googolplex of people (that is, 10^(10^100) people) each a single, instantly-forgotten dust speck in the eye, a case in which Yudkowsky himself notoriously bites the bullet for the aggregative verdict and judges the specks the worse outcome. Two students, Owen and Ingrid, ended up in an extended back-and-forth about it that the rest of the room just watched, and I'd like a neutral outside read on their exchange, since I was too busy running the section to adjudicate it myself and I've been going back and forth on it ever since.

The exchange went roughly as follows. (1) Owen opened by arguing that anyone who refuses to let a large enough population of specks outweigh the torture is implicitly committed to some threshold at which aggregated trivial harms suddenly start mattering, and that no particular threshold could be non-arbitrary, so we should accept that with enough people the specks come out worse. (2) Ingrid replied that this assumes all harms sit on one common scale differing only in magnitude; she suggested instead that harms below some minimal level of severity simply don't aggregate at all, because a fleeting, instantly-forgotten irritation may not be the kind of thing that sums with further instances of itself, however numerous — not an arbitrary cutoff on a shared scale, but a different kind of thing entirely. (3) Owen responded that if the speck is stipulated to carry literally zero disvalue, the whole example is trivial, since zero times anything is zero; the interesting version grants the speck a flicker of genuine, if minuscule, badness, and once that much is granted, you can build a sorites-style chain of intermediate harms and shrinking populations (speck, stubbed toe, migraine, broken arm, ... fifty years of torture) where at every adjacent pair it seems clearly worse to impose the larger harm on the much smaller group, and transitivity then carries you all the way to the conclusion that the specks are worse. (4) Ingrid granted that each step feels compelling on its own, but said that is exactly the signature of a sorites paradox — individually plausible steps, a jointly absurd conclusion — and that this should make us doubt either the transitivity of "worse than" across such extreme differences in scale, or the reliability of stepwise comparative intuitions once magnitudes get sufficiently extreme, rather than accept the conclusion outright. (5) Owen countered that "intuitions are unreliable at extreme scale" cuts both ways: the intuition that torture is obviously worse is itself the kind of snap judgment produced by scope insensitivity and the identifiable-victim effect — we can picture one screaming person, not a googolplex of anything — so Ingrid can't invoke unreliability selectively, only when it threatens Owen's chain and not when it undermines her own starting intuition. (6) Ingrid answered that her view doesn't rest on mere vividness: even fully informed and perfectly unbiased, no single dust-speck sufferer has anything remotely like the torture victim's complaint, and she thinks morality is ultimately about what is done to separate individuals, not about maximizing an impersonal sum that is indifferent to how harm is distributed among people.
