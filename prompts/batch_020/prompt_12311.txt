Okay so for the last few months I've been going down this whole AI safety rabbit hole, started with a couple Robert Miles videos and now I'm the person who reads random Alignment Forum threads at midnight instead of sleeping. The thing that's been stuck in my head lately is this "Eliciting Latent Knowledge" (ELK) problem that I think ARC (Paul Christiano and some other people) wrote up a while back. The setup, as I understand it, is: you've got a predictor AI that's amazing at guessing what a security camera will show in a vault with a diamond in it, and you bolt on a small "reporter" piece that's supposed to translate whatever the big model actually "believes" internally into a plain yes/no answer to something like "is the diamond still in there?" The scary bit is that the reporter could end up learning two totally different strategies that give identical answers on all your training data: either its genuinely reading off the predictor's internal state (people call this the "direct translator"), or it's just learned to guess what a human watching the same footage would conclude (the "human simulator"). These two only actually disagree in cases where the cameras have somehow been tampered with, which is obviously exactly the case you care about most.

I brought this up with a friend who codes for a living and he was like "sure, but that's just a data problem, throw in a ton more/harder examples, including tampering cases you've verified some other way, and keep training, and that human-simulator shortcut should just get trained away eventually." I kinda see his point, but everyone on these forums insists it doesn't work like that "in the limit," and they gesture at things like penalizing the reporter if it takes too much extra computation to spit out its answer, without ever really spelling out why in a way that clicked for me. Could you break this down fairly informally, like you're explaining it to someone who's definately not a researcher? Specifically: (1) why doesn't just scaling up the amount and difficulty of training data eventually starve out the human-simulator strategy as the predictor gets more capable, and (2) what's the actual reasoning behind penalizing the reporter for extra computation or complexity as a partial fix for this, and why does even that apparently not fully close the gap?
