I'm writing a novel built around a found-document conceit: a previously unknown continuation of Berkeley's Querist, supposedly discovered among his papers, in which the Bishop turns his method of rhetorical questioning on a kind of money he never lived to see — one issued by no mint or bank, backed by no metal, and limited in supply by mathematics rather than decree. Before I draft the passage myself, I need the underlying argument right, not merely the voice. The historical Querist repeatedly asks, in its characteristic interrogative form rather than as a flat assertion, whether the true idea of money is nothing more than a "ticket or counter" for recording and transferring the power to command other people's industry, and in doing so denies gold and silver any value beyond that function. It builds on this claim to argue for a national bank issuing managed paper currency for Ireland. His 1721 essay on the collapse of the South Sea Company, meanwhile, treats speculative trading in stocks as a symptom of a nation that has abandoned "Religion, Industry, Frugality, and Public Spirit" for gambling on price movements. Bitcoin's advocates often justify its value in terms that sound superficially Berkeleyan, prizing resistance to arbitrary debasement by any single authority, while resting that value on a scarcity they treat as intrinsic — exactly the move Berkeley refuses to grant gold and silver. Working strictly from the logic of his own economic writing, tell me how he would have judged it: does his anti-commodity theory of money make him more sympathetic to a currency backed by nothing but a protocol than to gold, or does his insistence that currency be actively managed by a public institution to excite industry make him more hostile to it than any defender of the gold standard? Give me the reasoning itself, in plain prose — I'll write the pastiche myself once the argument is sound.
