I have spent close to fifteen years reading LessWrong, the old Overcoming Bias archives, and more recently the Alignment Forum, and one of the closer friendships that came out of that world was with a woman named Priya, an independent AI alignment researcher who spent a couple of years at MIRI in the early 2010s before going out on her own. She died ten days ago after a long illness, and her partner has asked me to deliver the eulogy at her memorial this coming weekend, mostly because I am the one friend from her research life who also knows her family well enough to talk to them properly.

Priya spent most of her career on the shutdown problem specifically — the technical version that goes back to the 2015 corrigibility paper by Soares, Fallenstein, Yudkowsky, and Armstrong — and had more recently come to think it was better handled by something like an incomplete-preferences approach, of the kind Elliott Thornley has written about, than by the older utility-indifference proposals. About a year before she died, half in jest, she wrote a short set of instructions for her own funeral, and there is one line everyone who was close to her keeps quoting back to me: she did not want a single person to say she had "made her peace" with dying, and she did not want anyone to say she had "fought to the very end" either. Her stated reasoning was that both descriptions quietly force a complete preference ordering onto a mind that had, by her own repeated insistence, stayed genuinely incomplete on the question right up to the end — which is exactly the property she had spent years arguing a properly corrigible system needs to be permitted to have about its own shutdown, rather than being made to either welcome it or resist it.

My difficulty is that I do not want to honor the letter of her instruction by simply lecturing on decision theory for five minutes instead of eulogizing her, and I do not want to betray the spirit of it by quietly resolving her incompleteness into whichever of the two forbidden stories is easier to tell. The room will be split down the middle: half her old colleagues from the alignment world, who will know at once if I reach for her research as a cute metaphor without actually respecting what it means, and half her parents, her brother, and old friends from before any of this, who have never heard of the shutdown problem and came to grieve, not to sit through a technical talk. Could you write the eulogy for me — about five minutes of spoken remarks, not an essay — in a way that gets the idea genuinely right for the people who will notice, stays warm and comprehensible for the people who will not, and never quietly answers, on her behalf, the question she asked us to leave open?
