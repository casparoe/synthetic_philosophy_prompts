For a research project in my philosophy class on the history of utilitarianism, I went down a bit of a rabbit hole on Henry Sidgwick — one of the classical utilitarians we covered — and found out that he also spent nearly two decades as the founding president of the Society for Psychical Research, applying remarkably strict evidentiary standards to claims about telepathy and life after death. He apparently became convinced that telepathy itself was genuine, yet on the question he cared about most — whether anything survives bodily death — he never felt able to declare the case proven and remained formally agnostic to the end, despite having already been personally disillusioned by fraudulent mediums in his own informal experiments back in the 1870s. Given that particular mix of hope, open-mindedness, and self-skepticism, what do you think Sidgwick would have made of Daryl Bem's 2011 "Feeling the Future" experiments, which used entirely mainstream psychology methods to report statistically significant evidence of precognition and ended up helping trigger psychology's later replication crisis? I'm especially curious whether his own investigative habits suggest he would side more with Bem's defenders or with the statisticians and replicators who eventually undermined the findings, and whether he would have seen anything genuinely new in that controversy at all, given what he had already confronted in his own research.
