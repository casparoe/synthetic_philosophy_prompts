I spent the last decade or so of my career in clinical neuroscience, part of a group that used perturbational complexity measures -- descendants of Giulio Tononi's Integrated Information Theory -- to try to detect covert awareness in patients with severe disorders of consciousness who could not otherwise communicate. Since retiring, I have had time to go back and read the philosophical literature behind the theory far more carefully than clinical work ever allowed, and it has pulled me, somewhat against my expectations, into the middle of the panpsychism debate.

What strikes me is that IIT's exclusion postulate and panpsychism's combination problem look like two attempts to handle the same underlying difficulty from opposite directions. Chalmers and others pose the combination problem as the trouble of explaining how a multitude of micro-level experiences, or merely proto-experiential properties, could add up to, or give rise to, the single unified macro-experience each of us seems to have; Goff's proposed answer is a primitive "phenomenal bonding" relation that binds the micro-subjects into one. IIT's exclusion postulate confronts a structurally similar situation -- given any physical system, there are countless overlapping ways to carve out candidate subsets at various spatial and temporal grains, each a candidate subject of experience -- but rather than explaining how these candidates might combine, it simply stipulates that only the one composition with maximal irreducible integrated information (what the theory calls Φ) is ever actually experienced, excluding all the rest, including the very micro-level constituents that IIT itself credits with some nonzero degree of experience when they are considered on their own.

I would like you to compare these two strategies for handling the relation between the many and the one in theories of consciousness. First, does exclusion genuinely dissolve the combination problem, or does it just relocate it -- for instance into the question of why maximal integrated information, rather than some other quantity, should be the criterion that decides which candidate gets to exist, or into the instability that arises because seemingly small changes to a system could in principle flip which grain wins and is therefore conscious? Second, given that IIT attributes at least a modicum of experience to almost any system with the right causal structure -- Tononi and his collaborators have run the calculation on systems as humble as small networks of interconnected logic gates -- do you think they are entitled to keep their distance from the panpsychist label, or does the theory commit them to a form of panpsychism whether or not they choose to embrace the word? I would like your own reasoned position on both questions, rather than a neutral survey of who has said what.
