So I went down a bit of a rabbit hole a while back reading about old school utilitarianism and ended up on Henry Sidgwick, the guy who wrote The Methods of Ethics, which is supposed to be one of the most rigorous ethics books ever written. Then I found out this same guy was the founding president of the Society for Psychical Research back in the 1880s and spent like two decades going to seances, testing mediums, and trying to get hard evidence for telepathy, ghosts, and life after death. That seemed like such a weird contradiction to me, but then I read that it actually ties back to something he talks about at the end of Methods of Ethics called the "dualism of practical reason" -- basically the worry that reason tells you both to maximize your own happiness and to maximize everyone's happiness impartially, weighting your own good as no more important than anyone else's, and he couldnt find a way to prove those two things always line up. Without some deeper fact about the universe, like god existing or there being an afterlife where being moral actually pays off for you personally, he thought the whole thing might collapse into something arbitrary. And apparently that's part of why he got so obsessed with trying to scientifically prove there's an afterlife or some kind of "moral order" to the universe -- if psychical research could actually show minds survive death, maybe thats the missing piece that saves morality from being irrational for the individual person.

What I can't figure out is whether that reasoning actually holds up, and that's what I want your take on. Is it a legit philosophical move to think that empirical evidence about ghosts or psychic phenomena or life after death could help resolve a problem about the rational structure of ethics, or is that just mixing up two totally different kinds of questions -- an empirical one about what exists and a normative one about what you ought to do -- in a way that wouldn't actually work even if the psychical research had succeeded? Can you write me something like an essay on this, laying out the strongest version of the case that Sidgwick's move makes sense, the strongest version of the case that it doesn't, and then actually land on a conclusion instead of just saying "it's complicated"? I want it to really dig into the structure of the dualism itself and not just stay at the level of him going to seances, if that makes sense.
