I'm a PhD student in political science with a focus on formal political theory, and a strange episode from my department's latest faculty search has been nagging at me for weeks. As the graduate student representative on the search committee for a position in comparative political institutions (an irony that has not been lost on me), I was appointed, along with two faculty members, to a three-person subcommittee tasked with giving a final yes-or-no recommendation on our leading candidate before the case went to the full faculty. Departmental procedure requires the subcommittee to certify a candidate as adequate on two independent dimensions, research promise and teaching potential, and to recommend the candidate only if both dimensions are certified as adequate by majority vote.

When we voted dimension by dimension, I judged the candidate adequate on both research and teaching; one colleague judged the research record adequate but the teaching record inadequate; the other judged the teaching record adequate but the research record inadequate. Research was thus certified adequate two votes to one (myself and the first colleague), and teaching was separately certified adequate two votes to one (myself and the second colleague), so under our procedure the candidate cleared both bars and was to be recommended. Yet when the chair, uneasy that this meant recommending someone whom two of the three of us privately opposed, called for a direct up-or-down vote on the recommendation itself, it failed two to one, since I turned out to be the only one of us who judged the candidate acceptable overall.

The chair's working assumption was that someone must have voted inconsistently, or that the tally had simply gone wrong, but as far as I can tell each of our individual judgments was perfectly coherent: each of us recommended the candidate just in case we personally regarded both dimensions as satisfied. I raised the episode at my department's formal theory reading group, where we have spent the semester on preference aggregation, and opinions split: one friend insisted this had to reduce to a Condorcet-style cycle, while another maintained that two procedurally sound votes on the very same question simply cannot come apart if everyone involved is minimally rational, and that we must be missing an error somewhere. Neither response has fully satisfied me, which is a little embarrassing given that puzzles about combining individual judgments into collective ones are supposed to be closer to my specialty than most. I would like to understand, first, whether there is a genuine and general phenomenon behind what we witnessed, or whether one of the two votes must indeed be misrepresenting someone's judgments. Second, if our subcommittee faces the same structure again with our next finalist, I would like to know whether there is a principled basis for trusting the dimension-by-dimension tally over the direct up-or-down vote, or vice versa, or whether the choice between the two is ultimately arbitrary.
