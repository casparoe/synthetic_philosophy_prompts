I'm about a third of the way into a novel that has this framing device where my narrator keeps turning up "lost" fragments of an old Scottish essayist's notebooks -- it's basically Hume, just thinly disguised -- and I want one of the fragments to be him holding forth on modern central banking. The problem is I don't want it to read like generic "dead economist would hate government spending" fan fiction. I've been rereading his actual essays on commerce for background and there's a real tension in there I want the fragment to wrestle with, not paper over.

So here's the actual question, which at this point is honestly more for me than for the book. In "Of Money," Hume argues that pumping more currency into a country can genuinely juice industry for a while, before wages and prices catch up -- money isn't wealth itself, but a temporary injection of it isn't neutral either. In "Of Public Credit" he's borderline apocalyptic about nations piling up public debt, like it's a slow-motion catastrophe waiting to happen. Given both of those, what do you think he'd actually say about quantitative easing -- central banks conjuring new money specifically to buy up huge quantities of their own governments' bonds, for years on end, partly to keep barely-sustainable debt loads serviceable? Does he read that as his own "stimulate industry" mechanism finally getting institutionalized, or does the fact that it's specifically propping up public debt set off every alarm from the public credit essay? And a second, more technical bit: his balance-of-trade story is all about specie physically flowing between countries and prices adjusting in response -- a country starts loosing gold, its prices fall, its exports get cheap, the gold flows back in. Does that mechanism even survive the move to pure fiat currency and floating exchange rates, or does it just quietly stop applying once there's no metal left to flow?

Don't give me the tidy "Hume was basically a free-marketeer so he'd be horrified" take -- I already tried that line on my writing group and it fell flat, because it doesn't actually engage with what he wrote. I want the real tension worked through, including the places where his framework starts to strain once you push it past a world of coins and clipped shillings and into ours.
