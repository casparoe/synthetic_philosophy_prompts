I'm a backend engineer at a mid-sized company that makes a personal finance app, and a debate came up during a routine experiment review that I haven't been able to shake. We shipped a redesigned onboarding flow to a randomized half of new signups, and the top-line number looked bad: 30-day retention came in about three percentage points lower for the treatment group. When one of our analysts sliced the same data by acquisition channel (organic, paid search, referral, and so on), though, retention was higher for the treatment group within every single channel. Someone on the data team dismissed this immediately as "just Simpson's paradox" and said the fix is obvious: ignore the aggregate, trust the sliced numbers, since the aggregate is the artifact and the breakdown by channel is the "real" effect.

That explanation has been bugging me. A friend of mine who did a statistics minor pushed back when I described it to her, arguing that whether the aggregate or the sliced numbers deserve our trust isn't something you can settle with a fixed rule of thumb — it depends on how the slicing variable is causally related to the treatment and the outcome, and that stratifying by the wrong kind of variable (say, one that the treatment itself has some influence over) can manufacture a misleading reversal just as easily as failing to stratify at all. That was new to me; I had more or less assumed "more granular" was basically a synonym for "closer to the truth," and I don't have the statistics or philosophy background to referee this on my own, or to know how worried I should be about it in the dashboards and experiment write-ups my team ships every week.

Could you describe the most important implications of Simpson's paradox for how a team like mine should design, slice, and interpret experiments and analytics more generally? I'm especially interested in what actually determines, in a given case, whether the aggregated or the segmented numbers are the ones we should act on; whether there's any reliable principle for telling these two situations apart before we make a decision based on the wrong one; and what, if anything, all of this implies about whether an experiment even has a single well-defined "true effect" independent of how we choose to slice the population.
