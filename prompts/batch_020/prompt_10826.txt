I'm one of the public members on my state's parole board (long story — a piece I wrote a few years back on actuarial risk tools apparently impressed the right person), and we've hit a snag this month I can't stop turning over. We're deciding on a file where the risk instrument our office uses was run by two different consultants who reached very different numbers for the exact same guy: one anchored his five-year reoffending estimate to "all men convicted of this offense category released in the past decade," which comes out to 44%, and the other used a narrower comparison group — same offense, same age band, completed the same in-prison program, no juvenile record — which comes out to 13%. Everyone agrees both groupings are legitimate and both rest on decent sample sizes, but nobody can tell me why one is "more correct" for this specific human being than the other. A board member who used to teach philosophy says this has an actual name, something like "the reference class problem" (hope I'm getting that right), and that it isn't just the usual algorithmic-bias story with extra math — it's that literally any person belongs to endless overlapping reference classes that would each hand you a different number, bias or no bias.

I need to send round a short memo before thursdays vote, and I'd like it to do double duty: what we should do with this particular file, and what our standing policy should be for weighing these actuarial numbers whenever this kind of split shows up again (apparently it's more common than I realized). Make the recommendation concrete enough that the board could actually vote on it line by line, and please don't let the memo off easy on the strongest pushback it'll get in the room — that walking away from the single "official" number just hands the decision back to individual members' gut instincts, which is exactly the unaccountable, possibly more biased discretion these tools were brought in to replace.
