I'm preparing a lecture for a graduate seminar on philosophical critiques of empire, and I want to include Adam Smith. In Book IV, chapter vii of The Wealth of Nations, Smith weighs the profits that the colonial trade monopoly generated for British merchants against the costs of defending and administering the American colonies, and concludes that Great Britain derived "nothing but loss" from the whole arrangement. This makes Smith one of the earliest anti-imperialist thinkers, since he shows that the colonial project was a bad bargain even by the lights of Britain's own self-interest. Which strand of his argument would you say most directly anticipates the moral case against colonialism developed by later, more explicitly anti-colonial writers?
