I teach an advanced seminar on the moral status of animals, and last week's session covered the handful of pages Nozick devotes to the topic in Anarchy, State, and Utopia -- a passage often skipped over in political philosophy courses that focus on the book's arguments about the minimal state. There he floats, without fully committing to it, the idea that a coherent overall position might combine a Kantian side-constraint framework for how persons may treat one another with a broadly utilitarian calculus for animals -- a view sometimes summarized as "Kantianism for people, utilitarianism for animals," on which animal suffering counts and can be outweighed by sufficient benefits to us, whereas no aggregate benefit ever licenses using a person merely as a means. Two of my strongest students, Maya and Devon, kept arguing about this after class; since I only caught the tail end, I asked them to write up the exchange afterward so I could think it through properly before we return to it next week.

(1) Maya opened by calling the two-tier view ad hoc: Nozick's side constraints are supposed to track something about persons -- their capacity to shape a meaningful life through their own choices -- and since he never explains why nothing short of full rational agency can generate even a weaker constraint, the line separating "may be aggregated against" from "may never be used merely as a means" looks drawn wherever it's convenient for permitting meat-eating and animal research, not wherever the moral facts actually fall. (2) Devon replied that a threshold isn't automatically arbitrary just because it's a threshold: every side-constraint view needs some cutoff, and tying it to reflective self-authorship of a life plan is a substantive, non-question-begging criterion that most animals plausibly fail and virtually all persons plausibly meet. (3) Maya answered that this concedes too much to earn the label "Kantianism for people": Kant's own reason for denying animals direct moral status -- their lack of rational autonomy under the moral law -- is stricter than Nozick's utilitarian-for-animals view, which still lets animal suffering count for something in its own right, whereas on Kant's actual view we owe animals nothing directly at all, only an indirect duty to ourselves not to blunt our own moral feelings; so Nozick's proposal is in fact more generous to animals than real Kantianism is, and dressing it up in Kant's name is misleading. (4) Devon's rejoinder was that this is a dispute about labels, not substance: Nozick is using "Kantian" loosely, to mean "side-constraint-based" rather than "identical to Kant's own view," and whatever we conclude about that label has no bearing on the real question, which is whether splitting aggregative from side-constrained treatment along that particular capacity is defensible.
