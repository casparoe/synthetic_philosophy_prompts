So I've been falling down a rabbit hole researching a piece on simulation theory, of all things, and it somehow looped back around to Al-Ghazali's "Incoherence of the Philosophers" -- specifically the 17th discussion, where he goes after Avicenna on causation. His example is fire and cotton: yes, we observe the fire touch the cotton and the cotton burns, every single time, but for Ghazali there's no necessary connection there at all -- fire has no actual power to burn anything. The real agent is God, recreating the world moment to moment, and what we call "causation" is just His habit or custom of pairing fire with burning, a pattern He could break whenever He likes (that's literally what a miracle is). I was explaining this to a friend and it clicked for both of us how close this gets, structurally, to what a tick-based simulation would look like from inside it, even though Ghazali's motive was theological rather then computational -- he's protecting miracles and divine omnipotence against the philosophers, not anticipating Hume's later, more secular argument that we never actually perceive necessity between cause and effect, only constant conjunction.

What I'd love is a short story that dramatizes this rather than explains it, something in the Borges or Ted Chiang register -- a character, or maybe a whole community, starts noticing cracks in the "custom" governing cause and effect and has to reckon with what that implies. Feel free to set it wherever, an 11th century Baghdad madrasa, a generation ship, a kid's backyard, doesn't matter to me, but I want the actual mechanics of occasionalism, not just a vague "maybe nothing is real" vibe, to be doing real work in the plot.

If you can work it in, there's also genuine scholarly disagreement over whether Ghazali intended full strict occasionalism (God is the only cause, period, secondary causes do literally nothing) or a weaker claim, that secondary causes might be real enough, he's just denying the connection is necessary. So I'd rather you not flatten it into a cartoon of "and then God zapped it" -- the more interesting story, I think, sits in that ambiguity, maybe with a character who can't actually tell from the inside which version is true, or two characters who disagree about it and can't settle the question empirically.
