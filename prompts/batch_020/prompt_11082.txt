So my philosophy meetup (a bunch of us who found each other through lesswrong and SSC years ago) finally did Bernard Williams' "Internal and External Reasons" this month, and it turned into a whole argument in the group chat afterward. Laying it out here bc I honestly can't tell who's winning and both of these two are usually pretty sharp.

Nadia took the internalist side: whether someone actually has a reason to do something depends on whether there's a sound deliberative route from stuff already in their actual motivational set -- not just surface desires, but projects, loyalties, self-image, whatever -- to doing it. So if her uncle, after all the reflection in the world, genuinely doesn't care about his estranged kid, then "he has a reason to reach out" is just false, however cold that sounds.

Theo said that's nuts: if someone feels literally nothing about hurting people, internalism says they have no reason to stop, and a theory that spits that out has a problem, not a feature.

Nadia's reply: internalism isn't about what you want on the surface right now, it's about everything sitting in that motivational set, and for almost anyone, even people who feel nothing in the moment, there's some scrap in there careful deliberation would activate. True zero-content monsters are way rarer than Theo thinks.

Theo shot back that this is a dodge either way -- if the deliberative route can always dig up some scrap to lean on, internalism is quietly doing externalism's work, and if it can't, you're stuck saying the actual monster has no reason to stop, which was the objection to begin with.

Nadia's last move: fine, but we're not silenced about people like that either way -- we can still call them selfish, cruel, ungrateful, and mean it, without any reasons-talk. Theo's comeback: those words are already loaded with the normativity she's trying to avoid -- if "selfish" doesn't imply he should've weighed other people's interests, it's not a criticism, just a label, and if it does, she's smuggled the external reason back in under a different name.

Neither budged, and I dont think either was just bullshitting -- they where both making real points. Can you go through it move by move, saying who's right and who's missing what at each step? End with a one-line verdict on its own line, in exactly this format: \verdict{Nadia}, \verdict{Theo}, or \verdict{draw}.
