Since retiring two years ago after thirty-six years running an experimental optics lab, I have finally had the leisure to read philosophy properly, and a few of us former faculty have started an informal reading group to keep our minds occupied. This term we took up Moritz Schlick's short 1927 essay "Vom Sinn des Lebens" ("On the Meaning of Life"), and I was pleased to learn along the way that Schlick's own doctoral dissertation, supervised by Max Planck, concerned the reflection of light in a non-uniform medium — not so far removed from the optics I spent my career on, before he left physics for philosophy of science and, eventually, for essays such as this one.

As I understand his argument, Schlick draws a sharp line between "work," which he defines as activity whose whole point lies outside itself, in some further goal, and "play," activity that carries its purpose within itself, and he denies that work of this kind can itself be the meaning of life. He thinks, however, that a certain kind of creative activity — he names the artist's, and the scientist's pursuit of a problem for its own sake — can fuse means and end so thoroughly that the work simply becomes play, a thought that rather flattered me, since research of the sort I did for thirty-six years was apparently exactly what he had in mind. He stops short of saying that meaning is simply play, though, and locates it instead in what he calls "youth": not literal age, but a recurring disposition, available at any stage of life, to act from joy in the doing rather than from calculation about outcomes. He is careful, too, to distinguish this "joy" from mere "pleasure," which he associates with being distracted or diverted from life's toils, whereas joy involves a kind of complete absorption in the activity itself.

What prompted this letter is something several of us noticed about our own retirements: having shed the "work" that structured our days for decades, most of us seem to have filled the resulting free time not with anything resembling Schlick's creative play but with precisely the sort of digital entertainment he could never have anticipated — news feeds, social media, and mobile games explicitly engineered by product teams to hold our attention for as long as possible. I would value your best reconstruction, reasoned strictly from Schlick's own stated distinctions rather than from anything about digital technology that he obviously never knew, of how he would likely have judged this sort of activity. Does compulsive scrolling or app-based gaming count, on his own terms, as the very "absorption" he prized, seeing as it is freely chosen and often does feel all-consuming while it lasts, or do his emphasis on creativity and his sense that joy differs from a pleasure that merely distracts us give him principled grounds to treat such activity instead as a disguised, alienated continuation of "work" — given that its real purpose, someone else's engagement metrics, lies entirely outside the experience of the person absorbed in it? And, pressing the same logic a little further, what would this imply about how Schlick might have assessed a future, now often discussed in connection with AI-driven automation, in which most instrumental work disappears and people are left mainly to decide how to fill their time?
