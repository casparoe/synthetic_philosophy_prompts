I'm a software engineer who has served for the last three years on the technical steering committee of a mid-sized open-source data-validation library -- nine voting members, a public RFC process, and a monthly call where the committee votes on anything contentious. Right now we're deciding whether to merge a from-scratch rewrite of the project's core validation engine in Rust, replacing the existing C implementation, proposed by one of our most respected long-time contributors. The whole discussion has played out on a single public RFC thread: his post was followed within a day by an enthusiastic reply from another founding member, and within the week five more of the nine had added supportive comments. Going into this Tuesday's call, where we're expected to ratify it, we look close to unanimous. What complicates this for me is that three of the seven people who posted something supportive have separately told me, in private, that they still have real doubts about the migration cost and about losing contributors who don't know Rust -- they just didn't want to be the lone objection against what already looked, from the thread, like a settled consensus.

That gap between the public thread and the private conversations reminded me of something I once read about casually, under names like 'information cascades' and 'herding': situations where people decide in sequence, each one able to see only the choices -- not the private signals -- of everyone who went before them, to the point where it becomes individually rational for each new person to just go along with the visible pattern rather than act on their own signal. I have no formal training in economics or philosophy, but the more I think about our RFC thread, the more it looks like that dynamic rather than genuine technical convergence, and I'm worried Tuesday's call will simply ratify a consensus that never really existed as independent judgment in the first place.

Could you draft the memo I'll send to the rest of the committee before that call? It should explain, in terms colleagues who've never heard the phrase 'information cascade' will actually follow, why a sequential public RFC thread is structurally capable of producing this kind of hollow consensus even though nobody on it has been lazy or dishonest; recommend a specific, concrete change to how we handle whatever is left of this decision (I'm inclined toward something like simultaneous private ballots, but I want the reasoning spelled out rather than just asserted); and say what we should actually do about the rewrite itself, given that the apparent seven-of-nine support may not mean what it looks like. I'd also like the memo to take seriously the strongest argument against my own diagnosis -- that public, sequential discussion lets people genuinely change their minds because of each other's reasons, not just their bare votes, and that a blunt switch to secret simultaneous balloting could destroy exactly the kind of open deliberation that makes a technical consensus worth having.
