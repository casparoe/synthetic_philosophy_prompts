I chair my philosophy department's promotion and tenure committee. The candidate's book manuscript went to six confidential external referees, matched for seniority and subfield expertise; the letters split three to three, with no disclosed conflicts of interest and no evident asymmetry in competence. Discussion has hardened into two camps. One, echoing Elga's equal-weight view, holds that an even split among apparent peers is itself higher-order evidence that none of us should trust our own reading, and that we owe the candidate another year rather than a vote now. The other, echoing Kelly's right-reasons view, holds that since each of us actually read the book and formed a first-order judgment, the bare fact that other qualified readers disagree doesn't defeat that judgment, so we should simply vote our honest read. A further complication: the positive letters all come from scholars in the candidate's own methodological tradition and the negative ones from a rival tradition, leading some members to doubt this is peer disagreement at all rather than a dispute traceable to differing methodological priors. Please draft the memo I will circulate before our final meeting. It should recommend exactly one of three concrete actions -- vote this cycle, extend the clock a year, or commission two tie-breaking letters, each with a stated timeline -- and it should engage honestly with the strongest reasons for the options it sets aside.
