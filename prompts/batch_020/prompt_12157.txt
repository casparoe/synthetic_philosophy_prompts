I cover AI safety and the philosophical questions it raises for a science-and-ideas magazine, and I also host the outlet's companion interview podcast. Next week's episode pairs me with an alignment researcher who has spent much of the last several years as one of the most articulate defenders of "debate" as a protocol for scalable oversight: the proposal that a much weaker judge -- a human, or a weaker model standing in for one -- can be kept in control of a far more capable system by having two copies of that system argue opposing sides of a question in front of the judge, rather than by having the judge evaluate the system's reasoning directly. It has become something like the default answer whenever a conversation about supervising superhuman models turns practical, and she's coming on partly to discuss a new paper in which she tries to answer the field's standard objections to the idea. I've sat in on enough panels on this topic to notice that its defenders can retreat into the same handful of comfortable formulations without ever really being pinned down, and I don't want that to happen here.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having her restate the proposal in its usual terms? I want at least one question on the analogy she and other proponents draw to results in complexity theory like IP = PSPACE -- the idea that a judge confined to polynomial-time reasoning can, purely through the back-and-forth of the exchange, be walked into correctly verifying claims drawn from a far larger complexity class than it could decide unassisted -- and on whether a theorem about idealized optimal play actually licenses any conclusion about what a distractible human judge does when faced with two confident, fluent models arguing past each other; one on the so-called obfuscated-argument problem, where a dishonest debater can win simply by making its case long and diffuse enough that the honest debater can't cheaply localize the flaw, and on whether her new paper actually resolves that problem or just relocates it; one on the gap between what the equilibrium analysis promises and what a policy trained by self-play under a realistic compute budget is actually likely to converge to; and one on results from recent debate experiments showing that making a debater more persuasive sometimes raises a judge's accuracy and sometimes just makes the judge more confidently wrong, and what that should do to her claim that the protocol optimizes for truth rather than mere persuasiveness. For each of these, add a short follow-up question keyed to the reassurance or deflection she's most likely to reach for first. Close with one question asking what result -- theoretical or experimental -- would actually change her mind about debate being a viable way to oversee systems smarter than their evaluators.
