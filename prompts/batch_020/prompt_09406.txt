I'm a PhD student in economics with a strong side interest in social epistemology, and a disagreement came up in our reading group this week that I haven't been able to settle to my own satisfaction. We were working through the classic Bikhchandani–Hirshleifer–Welch and Banerjee models of information cascades, in which fully Bayesian-rational agents, observing only the actions (not the underlying private signals) of everyone who has moved before them, eventually find it optimal to disregard their own private information entirely and simply imitate the crowd. One person in the group insisted that this phenomenon should be diagnosed as a genuine failure of collective rationality — a "breakdown of the wisdom of crowds" — since the resulting action can lock onto the wrong state of the world with strictly positive probability no matter how large the population eventually grows, in direct tension with the optimistic convergence promised by something like the Condorcet Jury Theorem. I pushed back, arguing that nothing about the model requires any irrationality, myopia, or conformist psychology on the part of individual agents, and that the entire phenomenon is better described as a public-goods problem in the provision of information: acting on one's private signal reveals a sliver of that signal to everyone downstream "for free," and once the accumulated public history is informative enough, a purely self-interested Bayesian will rationally stop paying that externality by suppressing her own signal.

Could you write the strongest possible version of my side of the argument? I'd like it to engage directly with, and try to defeat, the "collective irrationality" framing rather than just talking past it. In particular, I'd like it to explain why the well-known fragility of cascades — the fact that they can be overturned by an arbitrarily small piece of new public information, precisely because they may form on the basis of very little accumulated evidence — should be read as supporting the externality diagnosis rather than undermining it, and to explain why the purely informational mechanism at work here is not the same thing as the reputation-driven herding one gets in Scharfstein–Stein-style stories, where fund managers mimic each other to protect their career concerns rather than because of any Bayesian updating on payoff-relevant evidence. I'm not looking for an even-handed survey of both positions; I'd like a genuinely persuasive brief for the externality view, pitched at a level that assumes real familiarity with the underlying sequential-decision setup (bounded private-signal precision, the public likelihood ratio, up-cascades and down-cascades, and so on) rather than one that has to rebuild the model from scratch.
