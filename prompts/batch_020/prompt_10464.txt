And here's one student's answer, typed up exactly as submitted (I only fixed a stray autocorrect issue, nothing substantive):

"Molina distinguishes three logical moments in God's knowledge. God's natural knowledge covers all necessary truths and every metaphysically possible state of affairs, including every possible free creature in every possible circumstance; it is prevolitional (prior to any decision of God's will) and its content is necessary. God's free knowledge, by contrast, is God's knowledge of what actually will happen, and it is postvolitional — it depends on and follows God's free decision about which world to create — so its content is contingent. Middle knowledge sits between these two: like natural knowledge it is prevolitional, but like free knowledge its content is contingent rather than necessary. What God knows by middle knowledge is the set of true counterfactuals of creaturely freedom (CCFs): truths of the form 'if agent S were placed in circumstances C, S would freely do A.' These are not up to God — whichever way Peter would in fact freely choose is not fixed by God's decree, but it's nonetheless a determinate fact that outstrips what mere possibility (natural knowledge) can tell you.

Because God knows, prior to creating anything, exactly how every possible creature would freely act in every circumstance God might place them in, God can plan providence around free creatures rather than through them. Rather than causing Peter to deny Christ (which would be strong actualization and would destroy Peter's freedom), God merely sets up circumstances in which Peter is free either way, and Peter's own choice weakly actualizes the outcome. Since not every logically possible world is compatible with the true CCFs, Molinists like Flint say God is choosing among the feasible worlds, a proper subset of the possible worlds, rather than among all of them — there might be no feasible world, for instance, in which everyone always freely does right.

The most famous problem for this picture is the grounding objection, first pressed by Plantinga: since CCFs are contingent, they can't be true by logical necessity the way 'if S is free in C, S either does A or doesn't' is; but since they are prevolitional, they can't be grounded in God's decree either, and since S doesn't yet exist (and may never exist), they can't be grounded in S's actual choice. So there seems to be nothing for these truths to be true in virtue of — a truth without a truthmaker.

Hasker's complaint is really just this same point wearing different clothes: if there's no fact 'out there' fixing which CCFs are true independently of God, then God's 'providence' via middle knowledge amounts to no more than getting providentially lucky that the CCFs came out favorably, which is exactly the grounding problem restated as a worry about control rather than truth. So I don't think there are two objections here, just one objection described from two angles."
