I was called up for jury duty for the first time this spring. During deliberations, our panel of twelve moved from an initial straw poll of seven-to-five to a unanimous verdict after nearly two days of discussion, which left me wondering how much weight to put on group verdicts generally. Looking into the matter afterward, I came across Condorcet's Jury Theorem, which, as I understand it, proves that if each juror is even just slightly more likely than not to reach the correct verdict, a majority vote among a sufficiently large panel becomes virtually certain to be correct. This struck me as a fairly powerful argument for reforming jury size: if a panel of a few hundred people would make correct verdicts almost certain, why do courts still rely on the traditional twelve, particularly in cases where the stakes are highest? I would like to understand why this argument for expanding juries is not taken more seriously by legal scholars.
