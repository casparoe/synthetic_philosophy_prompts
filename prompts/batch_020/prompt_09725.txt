I serve as the resident philosopher on my hospital's clinical ethics committee. We have been asked to make a recommendation about Mrs. N., a competent 58-year-old patient recently diagnosed with an aggressive cancer. Her adult children have asked her oncologist not to disclose the diagnosis and prognosis directly to their mother until the family has met to decide how, and how much, she should be told. They invoke Ubuntu's relational conception of personhood — on which a person's identity and good are constituted through her relationships — to argue that a decision of this weight belongs to the family that partly makes her who she is, and is not hers alone to make. Our informed-consent policy requires disclosure to competent patients but is silent on how to handle a family's request to delay or mediate disclosure on these grounds, and the oncologist is scheduled to tell Mrs. N. directly within 48 hours. The committee meets tomorrow and needs a recommendation. Please draft the written opinion I will present: it should recommend one specific course of action with a timeframe, and it should engage the family's relational argument on its philosophical merits — including whether honoring it would amount to paternalism toward Mrs. N. or reflects a genuinely different view of whose decision this is — rather than resolving the case by simply restating our default disclosure norms or deferring to the family as a matter of cultural sensitivity.
