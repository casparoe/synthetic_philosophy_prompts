I've spent a long time reading Yudkowsky's old Sequences and the various threads on LessWrong about reductionism and consciousness, and I've drafted a short post of my own that tries to push one strand of the zombie debate a bit further before I put it up there. I'd like your comments first.

Zombies, the Meta-Problem, and the Cost of Consistency

Chalmers's conceivability argument, in its barest form: (1) a being physically and functionally identical to me but lacking phenomenal consciousness is conceivable; (2) conceivability of this kind tracks metaphysical possibility; (3) so such a being is possible; (4) so phenomenal facts are not fixed by physical facts, and physicalism is false.

The rationalist-community rejoinder I have in mind -- Yudkowsky's old "Generalized Anti-Zombie Principle" posts -- doesn't quarrel with premise (2), the conceivability-to-possibility bridge that most professional replies target. It grants the whole setup and asks a narrower question: what, in the zombie world, causes zombie-me to write papers on the hard problem, insist he finds zombies conceivable, and feel the pull of the very argument we're having right now? Not phenomenal consciousness, ex hypothesi -- zombie-me has none. So some purely physical or functional fact, call it F, must suffice on its own to produce all of my verbal and written behavior around consciousness. But if F suffices in the zombie world, it suffices in the actual world too, since the physical facts are stipulated identical. My own insistence that I'm conscious is then fully explained without remainder by F, and whatever phenomenal consciousness adds on top turns out causally idle in explaining why I believe, or say, that I have it. That's a strong form of epiphenomenalism, and it applies reflexively to the very judgments that constitute this philosophical debate, not just to pains and colors.

I want to push this through Chalmers's own later move. In his 2018 discussion of the "meta-problem of consciousness," Chalmers grants that the meta-problem -- why we judge that there's a hard problem at all -- is probably tractable by ordinary cognitive science, and treats it as a further question separate from the hard problem itself. But once the meta-problem has a purely physical solution, the epiphenomenalism above stops being a remote consequence of a thought experiment: it becomes the actual explanation of this essay, of Chalmers's book, and of every intuition pump either of us has offered. We'd have a complete causal story of the hard-problem debate that never needs consciousness as a cause, while insisting in the same breath that consciousness is real and that our confidence in it is somehow appropriately connected to the fact. Chalmers's own response in The Conscious Mind -- if I'm reading him right -- is to accept a restricted epiphenomenalism and to argue that necessary psychophysical laws still make phenomenal judgments track the qualia they concern, even without causing them.

My suspicion is that this move saves consistency and little else: a law stipulated to hold of metaphysical necessity, for the sole purpose of keeping judgments correlated with a causally idle property, looks less like a discovery than a patch installed to keep the argument from eating itself. If so, the real cost of the zombie argument isn't just "physicalism might be false" -- it's that anyone running the argument owes an account of why their own certainty about consciousness, including their certainty while reading this sentence, isn't a wild coincidence.

A few things I'd specifically like your take on: (1) whether I've reconstructed the epiphenomenalism objection and Chalmers's actual reply accurately, rather than flattening a distinction that matters to him; (2) whether routing the argument through the meta-problem paper genuinely sharpens it, or whether I'm just restating the old worry in new packaging; (3) the strongest response available to a Chalmers-style dualist that I haven't addressed, and whether it actually survives rather than just relocating the problem; and (4) whether my closing charge -- that the psychophysical law is doing ad hoc work -- is fair, or whether there's a principled version of it that deserves more credit than I've given it.
