I'm reporting on why Robert Nozick's entitlement theory keeps resurfacing in arguments over extreme tech wealth, and it's sent me back to the Locke passage his "proviso" is supposedly built on. This is §33 of the Second Treatise, in the chapter on property:

"Nor was this appropriation of any parcel of land, by improving it, any prejudice to any other man, since there was still enough, and as good left; and more than the yet unprovided could use. So that, in effect, there was never the less left for others because of his enclosure for himself: for he that leaves as much as another can make use of, does as good as take nothing at all. No body could think himself injured by the drinking of another man, though he took a good draught, who had a whole river of the same water left him to quench his thirst: and the case of land and water, where there is enough of both, is perfectly the same."

What I can't quite square is that Nozick's "Lockean proviso" drops the "enough and as good" condition entirely and replaces it with the much weaker requirement that appropriation merely not leave others worse off than some baseline. Could you explain, for readers with no philosophy background, what work Locke's actual condition is doing here, why Nozick couldn't keep it as stated and still reach his conclusions about the legitimacy of unequal historical holdings, and whether his weakened version is really strong enough to rule out his own example of someone who ends up owning every water hole in a desert?
