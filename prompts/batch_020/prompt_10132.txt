Discussions of Hume on causation are a staple of rationalist blogs: we never perceive a necessary connection between events, only that one kind of event is constantly conjoined with another, so that causation reduces to reliable succession plus our own habit of expecting the second thing once we've seen the first. Al-Ghazālī reached a related but stranger conclusion centuries earlier. In the seventeenth discussion of the Tahafut al-Falasifa (The Incoherence of the Philosophers), he argues that when fire touches cotton, the fire itself burns nothing: nothing in either object necessitates burning, since God could just as easily create unburnt cotton beside the fire, or burnt cotton beside none. What we call a law of nature is just God's habitual choice to keep re-creating the world a certain way; objects have no causal powers of their own, and whatever efficacy they seem to exercise is really his, repeated by custom rather than necessity.

Write a short story set in a world where this is not a doctrine anyone argues for but a literal fact its inhabitants could, with enough attention, work out about their own world — something closer to discovering that the universe is recomputed from scratch each moment than to settling a theological dispute. Follow a single character to the point where they've pieced together enough to be sure of it, and let the plot and concrete detail carry that realization rather than having anyone recite the theory. I'm especially interested in what becomes of planning, promising, blame, and love once nothing anyone does can truly be said to make anything else happen.
