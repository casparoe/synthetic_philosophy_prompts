I got called for jury duty last month (burglary case) and it kinda broke my brain. None of the witnesses gave us anything we could take as fact — it was all "I'm like 80% sure that was his jacket" or "it was dark, so I can't swear to the car color, but it looked more grey than blue." So as a juror your confidence in stuff like "the defendant was there" is just shifting around based on how convincing each hedgy bit of testimony is, not conditioning on some solid fact. I got curious afterward and found out theres an actual name for updating beliefs this way instead of normal Bayes — Jeffrey conditionalization. But then I read that unlike regular conditionalizing, if you get two uncertain bits of testimony, the order you hear them in can literally change what you end up believing, even though its the "same" evidence either way. That seems really bad for a trial, where the order witnesses get called is basically arbitrary. Is that order-dependence actually a serious strike against Jeffrey conditionalization as a rational updating rule, or is there a good reason it's fine even though ordinary conditionalization doesn't have this problem? Write me an essay that actually weighs this properly (bring in whatever justifies conditionalizing in the first place, like the dutch book argument, and whether it still holds up here) and land on a real answer — not a "both sides have a point" cop-out.
