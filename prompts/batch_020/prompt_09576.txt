I've been following the scalable oversight literature for a while now, mostly through the AI Alignment Forum and occasional LessWrong cross-posts, and there's one piece of the "AI safety via debate" proposal I've never managed to get a solid gut feeling for, even though I think I follow the basic setup. As I understand it, the pitch is that you pit two copies of a highly capable model against each other in a zero-sum game over some question, have a much weaker judge — a human, or a weaker model standing in for one — award the win to whichever debater seems more convincing, and hope that the resulting training pressure makes honesty the equilibrium strategy, on the theory that it's cheaper to expose a flaw in an argument than to construct one that survives determined scrutiny.

What trips me up is the "obfuscated arguments" problem that later discussions raised against this picture: the worry that a dishonest debater can smear a single load-bearing error across a large branching tree of individually plausible sub-claims, so that no single claim is cheap enough for the judge to check on its own, and the honest debater can't afford to locate and expose the one rotten node before the judge's patience (or compute budget) runs out. My hazy recollection is that the proposed fix was to let debaters cross-examine fixed transcripts or earlier snapshots of one another, and that there's a complexity-theoretic story lurking in the background — something in the spirit of interactive proof systems, where letting a bounded verifier interrogate one or more far more powerful provers lets it decide statements well outside what it could check unaided — meant to justify why this should work even for arbitrarily capable debaters. I want to say I've also come across more recent work, possibly out of DeepMind, that tries to patch the picture using ideas borrowed from doubly-efficient proof systems, though I've never actually worked through whether that closes the gap or just relocates it.

Could you walk me through, at a fairly intuitive level: (1) why obfuscation is supposed to be a genuine problem for single-turn debate even when both debaters are treated as unbounded optimizers rather than merely clever humans or current-day models; (2) why cross-examination is supposed to help against it; and (3) what the remaining soft spots look like once you swap the idealized, computationally unbounded verifier back out for an actual bounded human judge? A small constructed toy example — something where I can actually see the obfuscation happening and see why interrogating past copies would or wouldn't catch it — would be more useful to me than another restatement of the general argument.
