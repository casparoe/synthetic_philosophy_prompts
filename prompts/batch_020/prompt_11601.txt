My grandmother turns ninety in three weeks, and I've been asked to give the toast at her birthday dinner. She taught Latin and Roman history at a small college for thirty-six years, and Cicero's De Senectute -- the dialogue in which the elderly Cato methodically talks his way out of every standard complaint about old age -- has functioned as something like her personal rulebook for aging ever since she first assigned it as a young professor. She still runs an informal reading group with three former students, now in their sixties, who come to her house once a month to argue about translation choices, and all three will be at the party. The complication is that she is also a lifelong skeptic about anything resembling an afterlife, and Cato, for all his bracing good sense about the actual business of getting old, closes the dialogue with a long, speculative passage about the immortality of the soul and the joy of one day meeting his dead son and the great men of history. My grandmother loves the rest of the book precisely because it doesn't need that ending, and she has told my mother, in so many words, that if anyone at this party gets sentimental and starts implying she'll be reunited with my grandfather or watching over us, she will consider it a betrayal of everything she has spent her career teaching that book to mean.

So I'd like help writing a toast that takes Cato's real arguments seriously rather than name-dropping Cicero the way people quote Marcus Aurelius on inspirational posters. I'm thinking especially of the material on authority and judgment replacing physical strength as the currency of old age (she can't kneel in her garden anymore, but she still out-argues everyone at Thanksgiving), the long stretch on farming and cultivation as the ideal occupation for later life (she now has opinions about tomato varieties that rival her opinions about the subjunctive), and above all the image, near the end, of fruit that is torn unripe from the branch versus fruit that simply falls once it's ready -- which seems to be the one piece of the ending she would actually endorse, since it makes death a natural fact without promising anything about what follows it. I want the toast to be honest about the fact that she is quite old and will not be here forever, in a way she would respect, without ever reaching for the comfort she has explicitly ruled out.

I'm a backend engineer, not a classicist, and my instinct when I don't understand a subject is to over-research it and produce something that reads like documentation rather than a toast, so I'd appreciate your help getting the tone right. Besides the three former students, who will recognize a real reference when they hear one, there will be about twenty-five other relatives who have never opened the book and shouldn't need to in order to enjoy it. Could you write the toast? I'm picturing something in the four-to-five-minute range: warm enough to work as a birthday toast first, and only secondarily as a Cicero seminar.
