I am a PhD student in social psychology working on trust and reciprocity in one-off interactions between strangers. Part of my dissertation involves coding people's judgments about how "rational" a described response is, and one vignette I have been piloting has started to bother me in a way that feels more philosophical than empirical. It is loosely based on something that happened to a colleague of mine during fieldwork abroad. Her rental car broke down on an isolated stretch of road, and a local farmer she had never met agreed to drive her several hours to the nearest town with a bank; she was quite certain she would never see him again. He did this on nothing more than her word that she would wire him money for his fuel and his afternoon once she got there, telling her afterward that he had only agreed because he considered himself a good judge of who would actually follow through. She did pay him.

When I recounted this to a friend in the economics department, he insisted that her paying was not, strictly speaking, rational. By the time she was standing in the bank, the favor had already been fully and irreversibly rendered; she would never see the man again, had no mutual acquaintances with him, and faced no legal exposure either way, so handing over the money at that point secured her nothing she did not already have. On his view, the only thing a rational agent could do at that final moment was keep the money, and the fact that most people would still pay anyway is simply evidence of a limit on human rationality, not a point in its favor. This struck me as too quick, but I am an empiricist rather than a philosopher, and I could not locate the flaw in it, if there is one. Was my friend right that, strictly speaking, paying was irrational — and if he was, does that verdict actually undercut the sense in which paying still seems like exactly the right, even rational, thing for her to have done?
