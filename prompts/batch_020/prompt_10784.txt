I've recently been working through Robert Aumann's 1976 paper on "agreeing to disagree," along with some of the literature it provoked (Harsanyi's doctrine, the 1998 Gul–Aumann exchange in Econometrica, and the associated no-trade theorems), after a running argument with a friend about whether two fully rational, fully honest people can ever end up in a genuine, persistent disagreement about a factual matter once each knows exactly what the other believes. The bare theorem is easy enough to state: two Bayesian agents who share a common prior and have common knowledge of each other's posterior probabilities for some event must hold identical posteriors. What I keep getting stuck on is why the common prior assumption specifically is the piece of machinery doing the real work here, rather than the Bayesian updating condition or the common-knowledge condition.

Could you give me a fairly intuitive, non-formal explanation of why relaxing only the common prior assumption is enough to reopen the possibility of rational, mutually transparent disagreement? I can follow the state-space-and-partitions formalism well enough, but I want more of a feel for what is actually doing the work underneath it, in the way you'd explain it to someone who already has the mechanics but not the intuition. I'd also be curious to hear whether you think there is a principled epistemic reason two rational agents should be expected to share a prior in the first place, or whether building that requirement into the model already smuggles in something like the Uniqueness Thesis about rational credence -- so that the theorem ends up presupposing roughly what it is often advertised as proving.
