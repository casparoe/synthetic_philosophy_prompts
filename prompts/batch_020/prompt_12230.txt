Over the holidays I watched two of my cousins get into an argument that I still haven't been able to resolve in my own head, mainly because I went in assuming one of them was obviously right and came out much less sure. My cousin Mark has spent years calling out his brother Dan for being unreliable with family things — skipping recitals, cancelling plans at the last minute — and this time he brought it up because Dan hadn't shown up to our grandmother's eightieth birthday, an event the rest of us had rearranged flights and work schedules around. Mark's point was that this showed Dan simply doesn't treat the family as a priority the way everyone else does. Dan shot back that Mark, of all people, had no right to say that, since Mark had missed at least as many family occasions himself over the past year. That set off a longer exchange that I want to lay out in full, because both of them kept making points that struck me as genuinely reasonable, and I've never worked out exactly where either one goes wrong.

The exchange went roughly like this:

1. Dan: "You missed Thanksgiving and two other things yourself last year. You don't get to lecture me about this."
2. Mark: "Whether I've missed things or not has nothing to do with whether what I said about you is true. I could be the worst offender in the whole family and it wouldn't change the fact that you skipped Grandma's eightieth for no real reason."
3. Dan: "I'm not saying you're wrong that I should have gone. I'm saying you don't get to be the one telling me that, like you're above it. There's a difference between me being wrong and you having any standing to point it out."
4. Mark: "Why would that matter, though? If it's true, and I actually care about this family, why shouldn't I be the one who says it?"
5. Dan: "Because you always have an excuse ready for your own absences — work, travel, whatever it is — and you've never once admitted that yours look just as bad from the outside as mine do. That's not the same as just being imperfect. That's holding me to a rule you quietly let yourself off of."
6. Mark: "By that logic nobody who's ever messed up could criticize anyone else for anything, since none of us are perfect. That can't be right — it would mean almost no one is ever allowed to say anything to anyone."

Nobody at the table settled it, and I'd genuinely like an outside opinion, since this sort of thing seems to come up constantly — in families, at work, in politics — without anyone stepping back to work out the underlying logic of it.
