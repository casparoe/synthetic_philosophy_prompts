I'm drafting a column on "coherence arguments" — the claim, standard in decision theory and increasingly invoked in AI-safety writing, that any agent whose preferences depart from expected-utility maximization can be turned into a "money pump" and drained of everything it values, which is taken to show that sufficiently capable agents must eventually behave like expected-utility maximizers. Below is the passage where I lay out the argument and then push on what I think is its weakest link. Before I go further, I'd like your comments: Is this a fair and accurate rendering of the argument and its standard motivation? Does the objection in the closing paragraph actually succeed, or is there a stronger reply available to a defender of coherence arguments that I'm missing? And does the pivot to AI shutdown research at the end feel like a genuine illustration of the stakes, or a stretch grafted on for relevance?

Draft passage:

Here is the argument in its oldest form. Suppose you rank apple over banana, banana over cherry, and, completing the circle, cherry over apple. A trader holding all three fruits can now empty your pockets without ever offering you a bad deal: starting from your cherry, sell you the banana for your cherry plus a penny (you prefer banana), then the apple for your banana plus a penny (you prefer apple), then the cherry back for your apple plus a penny (you prefer cherry) — and repeat indefinitely. Every single trade looks like an improvement, yet three trades later you own exactly what you started with, minus three pennies. Since no rational agent should pay to run in circles, the argument concludes that rationality forbids cyclical preferences. A structurally similar pump, built from options "sweetened" by small amounts, has been aimed at preferences that are merely incomplete — cases where you neither prefer one option to another nor rate them as equally good. String these results together with a couple of further axioms and you arrive at expected-utility maximization: not a convenient modeling assumption, but something closer to a precondition for not being a sucker. It is this chain of inference — exploitable, therefore irrational, therefore bound to act like an expected-utility maximizer — that underwrites arguments, Eliezer Yudkowsky's among them, for why sufficiently capable AI systems should be expected to behave as goal-directed optimizers.

What nags at me is that every pump above requires its victim to walk into each trade without noticing the pattern. If the trader had to announce the whole three-trade cycle up front, you would simply decline the first offer and keep your cyclical ranking forever, free of charge. Decision theorists have a name for this kind of forward-looking refusal to be walked into a bad sequence — "resolute" choice — and its availability suggests the pump shows only that shortsighted incoherence is exploitable, not that incoherence is irrational for any agent capable of planning one step ahead. That gap is not merely academic: one recent proposal for keeping advanced AI systems safely shut-downable, due to the philosopher Elliott Thornley, deliberately trains in a preference gap of exactly this kind — the system is meant neither to prefer, nor to rate as equally good, being shut down sooner rather than later — wagering that an agent sophisticated enough to plan need not be logically forced to close that gap merely to avoid getting pumped.
