So in my formal epistemology seminar we just moved from ordinary Bayesian conditionalization to Jeffrey conditionalization, and I think I've basically got the main idea: instead of learning some proposition E with certainty and updating via P(H|E), sometimes your evidence just shifts your confidence across a partition without making you certain of any one cell (the classic example is glancing at a cloth by candlelight and coming away, say, 70% confident its green and 30% blue, without ever being fully sure either way), and then Jeffrey's rule pushes everything else through using the old conditional probabilities on that partition, held fixed -- that's the "rigidity" assumption.

What's throwing me off is something the professor mentioned almost in passing: that unlike plain conditionalization, chaining two Jeffrey updates back to back is order-dependent. Shifting your probabilities on partition A and then partition B can leave you somewhere different than doing B first and then A, whereas with ordinary conditionalization, learning E and then F always gets you to the same place as learning F and then E, no matter the order you do it in. She mentioned there's apparently some special condition on how the two partitions have to relate to each other for commutativity to be restored, but class ended before she really got into it. A couple of us in my study group tried building a toy example that night just to double check we weren't misremembering, and sure enough, we got genuinely different final numbers depending on the order we did the updates in. Could you explain what's structurally going on here -- why chaining rigidity-based updates breaks order-independence in a way ordinary conditionalization doesn't -- and give me a sense of how big a deal philosophers of probability actually take this to be? Like, is it considered a real mark against Jeffrey conditionalization as a norm of rational updating, or more of a curiosity that doesn't really threaten it?
