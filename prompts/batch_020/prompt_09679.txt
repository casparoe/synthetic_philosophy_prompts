This summer I got certified as a lifeguard, and I've been picking up shifts at my town's public pool. During certification training, our instructor walked us through a series of hypothetical emergencies to get us thinking on our feet, and one of them has stayed with me even though the course ended weeks ago. The scenario was this: you're the only guard on duty at a pool with two deep sections on opposite ends, too far apart to keep both in view at once. During a crowded Saturday session, three young children start struggling in the deep end at the exact moment a teenager swimming alone on the other side of the pool gets a cramp and goes under. You can only reach one side before it's too late for the people on the other. Almost everyone in my training group said the answer was obvious: swim toward the three children, since that saves more people. But one of the other trainees pushed back, arguing that the lone teenager's life matters just as completely to him as any one child's life matters to that child, so the mere fact that he happens to be by himself shouldn't automatically make his claim on the guard weaker than theirs. Our instructor's response was basically "you go with the greater number, that's how triage works," which struck me as more of a slogan than an actual answer to her point.

I haven't been able to stop thinking about who was actually right. It feels different from the ethics material I've come across before -- we read Peter Singer's essay about the child drowning in a shallow pond in my school's intro philosophy elective, but that piece is about whether you're obligated to help a stranger at some cost to yourself, not about choosing between two different groups you could equally well help. In my lifeguard scenario, nobody is being sacrificed or used to save anyone else, and the guard didn't cause either emergency -- it's purely a question of which direction to swim first. So is "save the greater number" simply the obviously correct principle once lives are on the line, and the other trainee's objection just a confused scruple, or is she onto something real? I'd like to actually understand the strongest version of both positions rather than just get a quick verdict.
