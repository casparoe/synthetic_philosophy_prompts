In philosophy of religion, the kalam and Leibnizian contingency arguments are usually presented as complementary routes to the same theistic conclusion, but their most careful defenders don't always extend each other the same courtesy. Write a dialogue between a philosopher who champions the kalam argument but regards the contingency argument as more liability than asset, and one who champions the contingency argument and returns the compliment about kalam. Have the kalam defender press the objection that any Principle of Sufficient Reason (PSR) strong enough to ground a necessary being invites the modal collapse problem, since a fully sufficient reason for that being's choosing to actualize this contingent world rather than another threatens to make the choice—and so the world—necessary after all. Have the contingency defender press back that kalam's two standard arguments against an infinite past—that no actual infinite can be concretely instantiated, and that an infinite temporal series could never be traversed to reach the present—rest on substantive, contestable commitments in the metaphysics of mathematics and of time respectively, commitments a PSR-based argument never needs to make, since it can stay agnostic about whether the past is finite. Neither interlocutor should concede more than is dialectically forced, and each should engage the specific point the other has just made rather than simply re-running the standard case for their own view.
