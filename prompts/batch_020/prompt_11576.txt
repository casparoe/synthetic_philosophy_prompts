Among the AI-safety-adjacent rationalist blogs I follow, there's a long-running debate about whether any sufficiently coherent agent -- human or artificial -- must ultimately have complete preferences, since money-pump arguments purport to show that any gap in an agent's preference ordering can be exploited by a sufficiently clever counterparty. The stakes are not merely academic: several proposals for keeping advanced AI systems safely corrigible rely on deliberately leaving parts of their preferences incomplete. It strikes me that Zorn's Lemma already settles the purely mathematical version of the underlying question, since it guarantees that any partial preference ordering over a set of outcomes can be extended to some total ordering that agrees with it wherever the original ordering was defined. Please describe the most important implications of this fact for the completeness debate just described. In particular, does the guaranteed existence of such a completion actually strengthen the money-pump case for completeness, or does the non-constructive and highly non-unique character of the extensions Zorn's Lemma provides undercut its relevance to real, boundedly rational agents?
