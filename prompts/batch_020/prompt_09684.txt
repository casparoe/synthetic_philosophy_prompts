I'm part of a graduate reading group working through Quine this term, and for our next meeting I've drafted some notes that try to isolate one specific technical move in "Ontological Relativity," rather than just rehashing the "gavagai" story most people already know from Word and Object. I'd like feedback before I circulate this, ideally on whether the central distinction and the analogy I draw actually hold up, not just on the writing.

My starting point is that popular expositions of Quine tend to run together two theses he himself treats as distinct. Indeterminacy of translation, strictly speaking, is a holophrastic claim: two translation manuals for a language can agree on the truth-value assigned to every whole sentence, given all possible speech dispositions, and yet diverge systematically once you try to match up their sub-sentential parts. Inscrutability of reference is a further, more radical claim: even within a single fixed language — your own, at home, with no bilingual translation in sight — there is no fact of the matter about what your terms refer to, so long as whatever you substitute for them preserves the truth-value of every sentence you're disposed to assert. The famous "rabbit" versus "undetached rabbit parts" case is usually told as though it were one intuitive puzzle about field linguistics, but I think it's best read as a compressed illustration of the second, more radical thesis, smuggled in via the first.

The argument for inscrutability of reference I find most interesting is the proxy-function maneuver: take some one-to-one mapping of the objects in your domain onto some other set of objects — one of Quine's own examples maps every object onto its complement, literally everything else in the universe — and then systematically reinterpret every predicate so that it holds of a proxy object just when the original predicate held of whatever it's a proxy for. Every sentence you're disposed to assert keeps exactly the same truth-value under this global reshuffling, so nothing in your total theory, however complete, could ever adjudicate between the original interpretation and its proxy rival. What I find striking is that none of this trades on Quine's more contentious behaviorist assumptions about meaning; it looks like a purely formal point about what fixing the truth-values of sentences can and cannot fix.

That formal character is what makes me suspect the argument is a philosophy-of-language cousin of the Newman objection to structural realism in philosophy of science: the point that saying a domain merely "has such-and-such structure" is nearly empty, since any sufficiently large set of objects admits some relation realizing that structure. If Quine's proxy functions are doing essentially the same work as Newman's counting argument, then inscrutability of reference isn't a special discovery about language or translation after all — it's an instance of a fully general fact about the gap between structure and content that would afflict any theory whatsoever, linguistic or physical. That would make the thesis considerably less startling than it's usually presented as being: "reference is inscrutable" would just be a special case of "no theory can, from its internal resources alone, pin down its own subject matter," which ought to worry structural realists and radical translators for exactly the same reason, rather than being a distinctive insight about meaning as such.

I'd appreciate comments on three things in particular: whether I've drawn the line between indeterminacy of translation and inscrutability of reference in a way careful Quine scholars would recognize, rather than flattening a distinction that matters; whether the Newman parallel is actually apt, or whether there's some disanalogy between the two arguments I'm overlooking that would block the comparison; and whether my closing suggestion — that this deflates the philosophical interest of inscrutability of reference — is fair, or whether it misses something that makes Quine's version distinctively troubling in a way Newman's isn't.
