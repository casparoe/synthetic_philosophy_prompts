Last night a friend and I got into a long, rather heated argument about Isaac Newton's theology, prompted by an offhand comment in a history-of-science seminar about the General Scholium he appended to the second edition of the Principia. My friend maintained that Newton's God is best understood as something like a working celestial mechanic rather than a distant architect: Newton seems to have believed that the mutual gravitational perturbations among the planets and comets would, left to themselves, gradually destabilize the solar system, and that part of what he meant by God's "dominion" over creation was this ongoing, hands-on correction of the system's irregularities — the very thing Leibniz mocked in his correspondence with Clarke as making God into an incompetent watchmaker who has to keep mending his own machine. I objected that this ties Newton's theology far too tightly to a specific, falsifiable physical claim, and that once Lagrange and Laplace later showed, via perturbation theory, that these same irregularities are actually self-correcting over long timescales, something in Newton's argument from design should have been quietly undercut, whether or not he lived to see it.

Neither of us could agree on how Newton himself, given his particular cast of mind, would actually have reacted to that development, so I'd like your best-reasoned reconstruction, not just a rehearsal of the standard history. Suppose Newton could somehow be shown both the Laplacian resolution of the stability problem and, further along, the contemporary physicist's habit of explaining the apparent fine-tuning of fundamental constants by appeal to a multiverse plus anthropic selection rather than to design. Would he treat this as a minor tactical retreat that leaves his deeper argument from nature to God untouched, or as something that cuts into the whole structure of that argument? I am especially curious whether you think his own explicit methodological scruple against inventing unobserved mechanisms not grounded in observed phenomena — his "hypotheses non fingo" — would end up telling against a multiverse invoked to explain away fine-tuning just as strongly as it once told in favor of reading design directly off the observed order of the heavens. Please reason from what you actually know of his idiosyncratic theological commitments rather than treating him as a generic "religious scientist" placeholder.
