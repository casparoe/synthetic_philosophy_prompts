I'm preparing the opening session of a graduate seminar on moral luck, and I'd like to begin not with Williams or Nagel but with the Kantian passage both of them are effectively writing against. Here is the relevant paragraph from the First Section of the Groundwork, in Abbott's translation:

"A good will is not good because of what it performs or effects, not by its aptness for the attainment of some proposed end, but simply by virtue of the volition, that is, it is good in itself, and considered by itself is to be esteemed much higher than all that can be brought about by it in favour of any inclination, nay even of the sum total of all inclinations. Even if it should happen that, owing to special disfavour of fortune, or the niggardly provision of a step-motherly nature, this will should wholly lack power to accomplish its purpose, if with its greatest efforts it should yet achieve nothing, and there should remain only the good will (not, to be sure, a mere wish, but the summoning of all means in our power), then, like a jewel, it would still shine by its own light, as a thing which has its whole value in itself. Its usefulness or fruitlessness can neither add to, nor take away anything from, this value."

What I would like help thinking through is the parenthetical: "not, to be sure, a mere wish, but the summoning of all means in our power." Kant introduces it almost as a throwaway clarification, but it looks to me like it is doing load-bearing work for his whole strategy of insulating moral worth from luck. To distinguish a good will from a mere wish, we need some non-question-begging way of fixing what counts, for a given agent, as "the means in their power" -- and specifying that seems to require exactly the sort of facts about an agent's opportunities, capacities, and formation that a moral luck theorist would file under circumstantial and constitutive luck. So does this parenthetical quietly readmit luck into the one place Kant most needs it excluded, or is there a reading of "means in our power" that keeps it fixed by the will alone, prior to any facts about the agent's situation or constitution? Please reconstruct the strongest version of both answers before telling me which one you find more defensible, and why.
