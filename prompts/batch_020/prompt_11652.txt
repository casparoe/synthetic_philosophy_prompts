I've spent the past month reporting on the effective-altruist "longtermism" debate, and in trying to understand how its proponents justify weighing the far future so heavily, I fell into the "infinite ethics" literature: the question of what happens to consequentialist theories once you take seriously the possibility that value, or population, is genuinely infinite. Most popular treatments of this stop at Pascal's-mugging-style worries about infinite payoffs, but the result that has actually stuck with me is older and narrower, and comes from social choice theory rather than decision theory. As I understand it (and I may be garbling some of the details, so please correct me if so), Peter Diamond showed back in 1965 that no way of ranking infinite streams of well-being — one utility number per future generation, which philosophers of infinite ethics treat as essentially the same problem as ranking welfare across an infinite population, just indexed by time instead of by person — can simultaneously satisfy three conditions that each sound close to non-negotiable on their own: giving priority to any change that helps someone without hurting anyone else, treating every generation impartially, so that merely relabeling who receives which utility level can never flip the verdict, and not behaving erratically under small perturbations to the stream. I also gather that later economists showed something stranger still: if you give up that last condition, you can construct rankings that do satisfy the first two together, but only by using explicitly non-constructive tools, so that the ordering's very existence depends on the Axiom of Choice and the rule itself can never actually be written down, applied to a specific case, or even checked for agreement between two people who both claim to be using it.

What I'd like from you is the intuitive version of why this happens, not the formal construction, which I've tried and failed to hold in my head. Concretely: why is it trivial to rank finite populations both impartially and efficiently — you can just add up the numbers — and what specifically breaks the moment the population becomes infinite? Is it fundamentally a problem about pairs of futures that disagree with each other infinitely often in both directions, so that no rule short of an arbitrary, unappealable tie-breaker could ever rank them? And I'd be interested in your own view on what, if anything, this should unsettle about the longtermist project specifically: if the weakest, most innocuous-looking fairness conditions on how we treat an infinite sequence of future generations turn out to be jointly satisfiable only by objects that are provably impossible to construct or apply, does that make "impartial concern for the whole, potentially infinite, future" an incoherent demand, a coherent but practically empty one, or does the theorem leave the actual policy conclusions longtermists draw untouched?
