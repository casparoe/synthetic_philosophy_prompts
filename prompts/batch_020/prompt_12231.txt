I'm putting together next week's session for my grad seminar on free will and moral responsibility, and I want a gut check on something before I spring it on my students. We just went through Galen Strawson's "Basic Argument" against ultimate moral responsibility — the one where he argues that to really be responsible for what you do, you'd have to be responsible for the way you are (the values, motives, etc. that produced the act), which means being responsible for whatever produced *that* way of being, and so on back in a regress that has to stop somewhere you didn't choose. So genuine, buck-stopping responsibility turns out to be impossible for any agent, deterministic or not (he even runs the argument on God, of all beings). Funnily enough his dad, P.F. Strawson, was one of the great compatibilist defenders of ordinary responsibility-talk — but that's a story for another class.

Anyway, I was re-reading Nietzsche's Beyond Good and Evil over break and got stuck on section 21, the "causa sui" passage, where he goes after the idea of a will that's the ultimate, self-caused source of its own acts, basically calling it a logical self-contradiction that people only crave because it lets them feel fully, cosmically guilty (or praiseworthy) for what they do. It struck me that Nietzche might basically be running Strawson's regress a century early, just in more polemical, psychological dress instead of careful analytic prose.

So here's what I'd like from you: compare the two arguments in some real depth, not just a surface plot-summary of each. Are they actually making the same underlying point, or is Nietzsche's target narrower — say, a specific theological or folk-psychological notion of guilt-grounding freedom — such that Strawson's version, for all its surface similarity, is doing something genuinely more general and rigorous? I don't want a "both raise interesting points" fence-sit on this: tell me whether Strawson's argument is a real philosophical advance over Nietzsche's, or just Nietzsche restated in tidier analytic form, and defend the call.
