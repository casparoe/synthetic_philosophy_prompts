My grandmother and her two sisters bought a small lake cabin together in the early 1960s, and when they drew up the family trust that would let it pass down through the generations, they included a clause I have never quite been able to stop thinking about since I first read it as a teenager: the cabin can never be sold, rented out long-term, or mortgaged, and this particular clause cannot be changed except by the unanimous agreement of every living beneficiary, however many there eventually turn out to be. My mother tells me my grandmother insisted on that wording after watching two neighboring families lose their own places within a few years of each other, in both cases because one restless heir talked the rest into selling "just this once." She apparently wanted a rule strong enough to hold "even when everyone still around was completely convinced their reasons for breaking it were different."

That was over sixty years ago. There are now nineteen of us with a stake in the cabin, scattered across three countries, and most of us can no longer really justify our share of the taxes and upkeep, since almost nobody has visited more than once or twice in the last decade. Eighteen of us would vote to sell tomorrow if we could. The nineteenth, a second cousin I have met exactly once, refuses, and points out, not unreasonably, that the whole purpose of the clause was to stop a tired, cash-strapped majority like us from doing exactly what we are now trying to do. Half the family wants to find a lawyer who can get the clause set aside, since our grandmother could never have anticipated our specific situation; the other half insists that assuming we are the exception is precisely the mistake she was guarding against, and that her wish still deserves to be honored now that she is no longer here to renegotiate it with us.

I have no background in law or philosophy, but I have turned this over often enough, usually on the long drive back from the lake, that it no longer feels like an ordinary family squabble to me. When I raised it with a few friends recently, all I really got back was "just sell the cabin already," which does not really engage with what is bothering me. What I would like to understand is whether there is a principled way to tell apart the cases where a rule like this deserves to keep binding the people currently subject to it from the cases where it is really just one long-dead woman's preferences holding a group of changed descendants hostage -- and, assuming that distinction can be drawn at all, which side of it my own family actually falls on.
