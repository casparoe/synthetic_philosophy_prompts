I've spent enough time around LessWrong-style decision theory to have seen Parfit's Hitchhiker invoked as the go-to test case for a theory of rational choice, but I've never seen a clean verdict on what the case actually establishes about rationality itself. The setup, briefly: you're dying of thirst in the desert, and the only reason a driver picks you up is that she can reliably tell you'll pay the fare once you're safe in town; once you're actually in town, though, an act-by-act rational calculation says keep your money, since paying now buys nothing further. Parfit was willing to accept a striking conclusion here: a fully rational agent must sometimes be disposed to act irrationally when judged at the moment of action, a pattern he and later commentators have labeled "rational irrationality." Gauthier's constrained maximization and McClennen's resolute choice reach the same practical verdict -- pay up -- while denying that anything irrational is actually going on, and updateless or functional decision theorists try to dissolve the puzzle entirely by treating policies or dispositions, not isolated acts, as the correct unit of rational evaluation. Write an essay that weighs these positions and defends a specific verdict: is "rational irrationality" a genuine, irreducible feature of rationality, or does every serious response to the case eliminate it by changing what gets evaluated? Say what your answer implies for the ordinary cases with this same structure, like keeping a promise once its benefit to you is already secured, which lack a dramatic predictor to make the stakes vivid.
