I've been asked to give the pre-concert talk before our orchestra's performance of the Tristan prelude next season, and since Wagner's own aesthetic turn is usually traced to his discovery of Schopenhauer in the 1850s, I've finally sat down with Book 3 of The World as Will and Representation. One claim there has me stuck. Schopenhauer holds that music is metaphysically unlike the other arts: painting, poetry, and sculpture only ever present the Platonic Ideas, whereas music bypasses the Ideas entirely and is a direct copy of the Will itself — so directly that he suggests the world might as well be called embodied music as embodied will. But his whole account of aesthetic experience elsewhere turns on the perceiving subject becoming will-less, since for him contemplation across every art form is supposed to work by suspending the very willing he treats as the root of all suffering. If listening to music means confronting the Will in its most immediate, unmediated form — more directly than any other art achieves — why doesn't that pull us deeper into willing rather than release us from it, the way he thinks the Ideas do? Could you explain how Schopenhauer's own text tries to hold these two claims together, and whether the reconciliation actually works?
