Okay so this is a bit of a tangent from my day job but I've been re-reading Alan Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" because a coworker started an informal reading group after all the "does our chatbot pass the Turing test" chatter going around the office. I'd only ever seen the famous bits everyone quotes -- the imitation game setup, the bit where he predicts machines will be pretty convincing by around the year 2000 -- and never actually read section 6, where he goes through nine objections to the idea that machines can think. Objection 9, "The Argument from Extra-Sensory Perception," is where I got stuck.

Paraphrasing loosely: Turing says he assumes the reader is already familiar with telepathy, clairvoyance, precognition and psychokinesis, and then just states that "the statistical evidence, at least for telepathy, is overwhelming" and that this is a genuinely strong objection to his whole setup, not a throwaway one. He then walks through a scenario where the interrogator asks a telepathic human witness to name the suit of a card he's holding, and the human scores way above chance. Worse, he points out that if the machine's answers come from an internal random number generator, that generator could get psychically influenced by the interrogator without anyone being able to detect it. His actual proposed fix, and I'm not making this up, is to put both competitors in what he calls a "telepathy-proof room."

I have zero philosophy training (I write backend services for a living, so this is not exactly my wheelhouse), so I can't tell how much context I'm missing that would make this less bizzare. Was Turing just reflecting a moment where card-guessing ESP experiments were seen as legit unexplained lab data by serious scientists, and he's applying the same "don't dismiss uncomfortable evidence" instinct he uses against the other objections? Or is there some dry humor here that I'm just not picking up on? Seperately, even taking the passage fully at face value, I can't tell if the logic actually holds up: if you grant that telepathy is real, why does that specifically break the imitation game rather than just being one more channel of information a human witness has and a machine doesn't -- which, elsewhere in the paper, Turing treats as totally fine (he doesn't care that the machine can't see, has no hands, etc.)? Could you walk me through what's actually going on in this section, and whether the "telepathy-proof room" patch is a coherent fix to the test or if it quietly undermines the whole framework he spent the rest of the paper building up?
