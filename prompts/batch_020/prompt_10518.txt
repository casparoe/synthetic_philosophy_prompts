I'm finishing grading for a graduate seminar on Plato's late political philosophy, and before I enter final marks I'd like an independent second opinion on one student's answer. I set this particular question because, in my experience, students tend either to wave away the Athenian Stranger's discussion of wine-drinking in Laws Book I as an eccentric aside or to flatten it into a generic "Plato distrusted pleasure," when in fact he is mounting a quite specific and carefully constructed argument for why a properly run symposium benefits a city precisely because, not in spite of, its power to strip away inhibition.

The question I set was: "In Book I of the Laws, the Athenian Stranger persuades Megillus and Kleinias that a well-organized wine-party, unlike the kind of drinking Sparta and Crete simply forbid, has genuine value for a city's education and government. Reconstruct this argument in your own words, making sure to address: (i) the analogy the Athenian draws between training courage and training self-mastery; (ii) why he thinks a citizen's age should govern how freely he is permitted to drink; and (iii) what role whoever presides over the gathering must play if the whole practice is to remain a controlled test rather than collapse into ordinary license."

Here is the student's answer in full: "The Athenian's argument rests on an analogy with courage. Just as a city trains soldiers for courage by deliberately exposing them, under controlled conditions, to frightening situations, it can train citizens in self-mastery by deliberately exposing them, under controlled conditions, to wine, which strips away shame and inhibition much as danger provokes fear; a person's real character shows itself once shame is removed, so the symposium becomes a kind of safe laboratory in which self-command can be tested and strengthened, rather than being left to reveal itself catastrophically for the first time out in the world. This is also why age has to regulate the practice: the young are already hot-tempered and impulsive, so giving them wine on top of that is like adding fire to fire, whereas older citizens, whose characters have grown rigid, can be safely loosened by wine and even benefit from it, since it helps them shed the stiffness of age and take part more freely in the songs and dances through which the city hands on its values. Plato dramatizes exactly this danger later on, in the Symposium, where a drunken Alcibiades bursts in at the end of the party and, all his usual composure gone, blurts out his private feelings for Socrates in front of everyone present -- a vivid illustration of the very failure of self-command that the Laws is designed to guard against. Finally, since the point of the whole exercise is to keep it a controlled test rather than a free-for-all, the Athenian puts a designated president in charge of each gathering, choosing whoever is most seasoned at this kind of occasion, on the reasoning that practice at drinking is exactly what teaches a person to sense, from the inside, the moment when the evening is about to tip over into real excess."
