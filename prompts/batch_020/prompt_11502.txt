I joined my hospital's ethics committee as a family representative shortly after my sister, who has bipolar I disorder, was hospitalized on our psychiatric unit during a manic episode. Two years earlier, while she was stable, she had worked with her psychiatrist to sign an advance directive instructing staff to disregard any refusal of medication or hospitalization she made while manic, and to proceed with treatment anyway. When the crisis came, she technically passed a bedside capacity evaluation, despite being, as our family later agreed, grandiose and barely sleeping for days, and she coherently and insistently demanded release. The on-call psychiatrist chose to honor the advance directive over her objections, and the committee is now split down the middle: some members call this straightforward paternalism, since an articulate, present refusal that survives a capacity evaluation should never be overridden by an earlier document, while others argue that honoring the refusal would have betrayed the very autonomy the directive was written to protect. Our written policy says only that such directives are to be given "significant weight," with no guidance on what to do when they conflict with an apparently competent contemporaneous refusal. Please draft the short memo I will bring to next week's meeting, recommending concrete criteria and a decision procedure (including who adjudicates and on what timeline), and engaging seriously with the strongest objection: that deferring to the directive treats her past self as more authoritative than the person who was actually in the room.
