I am putting together the moral responsibility unit of my ethics seminar, and I would like a livelier way into the paradox at the center of Nagel's and Williams's companion 1976 papers on moral luck than a straight lecture affords. Please write a dialogue between two philosophers, Elena and Tomas, who happen to grab coffee together after a department colloquium on the subject. Elena defends what the literature sometimes calls the Control Principle, in a broadly Kantian spirit: an agent's moral standing should depend only on her will, so that any assessment that varies with results, circumstances, or even temperament is thereby a mistake, however natural it feels to make it. Tomas, arguing in the spirit of Nagel and Williams, insists the Control Principle cannot be honored consistently: once we grant that an agent's temperament and capacity for self-control are themselves matters of what Nagel calls constitutive luck, and that the very circumstances that test or spare her character are matters of circumstantial luck, there seems to be nothing left of "the will" for the principle to protect, so that taking it seriously threatens to dissolve responsibility rather than purify it.

I would like the two of them to actually engage with each other rather than trade prepared position statements. Tomas should press Elena with at least one of Nagel's own examples, such as the pair of equally reckless drivers, only one of whom happens to hit a pedestrian who steps into the road, together with either Williams's notion of agent-regret or his case of Gauguin, the painter who forsakes his family for an artistic vocation whose justification, on Williams's telling, could only be settled retrospectively, by a success Gauguin could never guarantee in advance. Elena should do more than simply restate the Control Principle in response: she might distinguish the psychological pull of differential blame from its normative justification, or try to locate some principled stopping point for the regress Tomas presses on her, but she should take his strongest objection seriously rather than deflect it. Let the exchange end with both having sharpened or slightly revised their opening position under real pressure from the other, without either simply conceding the point.
