And here's the student's answer, copied exactly as she turned it in:

"Molyneux's question, posed to Locke in the 1690s, asks whether a person born blind who has learned to distinguish a cube from a sphere by touch would, immediately upon gaining sight, be able to tell which is which by vision alone, without touching them first. Locke agreed with Molyneux's own answer: no. The man has learned how a cube and a sphere each feel, but hasn't yet learned which visual appearance goes with which felt shape, so there's no basis yet for him to connect the two.

Leibniz, responding to Locke in the New Essays, took the opposite side and said yes: because we can work out spatial relations like the ones defining a cube and a sphere through reason and geometry rather than needing bare sense experience, the formerly blind man would be able to identify the shapes correctly the moment he opened his eyes, with no adjustment period needed and no help from touch.

Modern neuroscience has actually tested this. In Held et al.'s 2011 Project Prakash study, congenitally blind patients in India had their sight surgically restored and were tested on whether they could match a shape they saw to one they'd only ever felt. Right after surgery the patients performed barely above chance, which lines up with Locke's prediction and basically settles a 300-year-old debate."
