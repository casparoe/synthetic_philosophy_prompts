Ok, this is a bit of a rabbit hole but here goes. I was reading up on proper scoring rules for probabilistic forecasts (Brier score, log loss, the same stuff we use to eval calibration of ML models) and somehow ended up on the philosophy side of it -- this "epistemic utility theory" / "accuracy-first epistemology" thing where philosophers score credences (degrees of belief) for accuracy against how the world turns out, then argue certain rationality rules are mandatory because breaking them means some other set of credences "dominates" yours in accuracy no matter what happens -- basically a dominated-strategy argument like you'd see in game theory.

The classic move (Joyce's argument) apparently uses this to justify probabilism, i.e. why credences should obey the probability axioms. But I gather people have tried the same trick to justify a bunch of other Bayesian norms: updating by conditionalization, the Principal Principle (credence should match known objective chances), the Reflection Principle (defering to your own future credences), and even the Principle of Indifference (spread credence equally when you have literally no evidence either way). Some of these accuracy-dominance arguments are apparently on a lot shakier ground than others, but I can't tell which from the pop-sci-level stuff I've read.
