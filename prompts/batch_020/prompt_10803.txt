A friend and I spent much of last night arguing about Max Black's classic two-sphere universe and never reached agreement. She holds that the thought experiment is decisive against the identity of indiscernibles: because the spheres are perfect duplicates, any relational property one possesses — such as being three feet from a sphere of a given size — is possessed by the other as well, so relations cannot break the symmetry any more than intrinsic properties can. I argued two lines in response: that the spheres might nonetheless be distinguished by a primitive, unanalyzable thisness, or that they are at least weakly discernible, since a relation like "is three feet from" is irreflexive and so can hold between the spheres without ever holding of either sphere and itself. She maintains that both moves are either question-begging or fail to vindicate anything worth calling the identity of indiscernibles. Write a dialogue between two philosophers, imagined as standing together inside Black's symmetric universe alongside the spheres, in which one defends some version of the principle against Black's case and the other insists the case refutes it. Each should engage directly with the other's strongest argument rather than restate their own position, and neither side should be written as the weaker one.
