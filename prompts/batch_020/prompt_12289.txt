In the centipede game—where two players alternately choose to "take" a growing pot of money or "pass" it to the other, and mutual passing for more rounds leaves both players better off than stopping early—backward induction implies that the first player should take the pot on the very first move. Some philosophers and game theorists have argued that this argument is not merely counterintuitive but self-undermining: the reasoning that licenses stopping at the first node presupposes that both players would continue to reason and act "rationally" at every later node, yet those later nodes, on the argument's own terms, should never be reached. Explain, at an intuitive level, why this tension is supposed to be a genuine problem for the backward-induction solution rather than just a muddle about reasoning along paths that never get played. Then explain how a defender of backward induction working in the common-knowledge-of-rationality tradition associated with Aumann can try to resist this charge, and identify exactly where the disagreement between the two sides ultimately lies.
