I have been asked to give the toast at the wedding of two close friends from my PhD cohort in philosophy of physics, and I would like some help getting one piece of technical content exactly right rather than reaching for generic wedding-toast material. Priya's dissertation defends a sophisticated, anti-haecceitist version of manifold substantivalism against the Earman-Norton hole argument -- spacetime points are real, she argues, but have no identity apart from the roles they occupy, so the indeterminism the argument threatens never actually gets off the ground. Her now-husband Teo's dissertation takes the very same argument the other way, as evidence that spacetime points were never good candidates for being real, mind-independent individuals in the first place. They met in a seminar on general covariance, and the disagreement has run through their entire relationship ever since.

At their engagement dinner, Teo's toast to Priya ended with a line about loving her "regardless of which diffeomorphism she turns out to be," which was met with a huge laugh from about a third of the room and a polite, uncomprehending smile from everyone else. That same split will show up at the wedding: some guests will catch a reference to Leibniz equivalence or to the underdetermination the hole argument generates between diffeomorphic models, while the rest -- parents, cousins, old friends from before graduate school -- have only ever absorbed "the hole argument" as a running family joke, without anyone explaining what it actually refers to.

There is one further constraint, though, that matters more than getting the physics jokes right. Both of them have asked, half in earnest, that nobody at the wedding describe the marriage as "meant to be" or "written in the stars," since each of them thinks that kind of talk smuggles in exactly the sort of primitive identity-across-possibilities they have separately spent years arguing against, for opposed reasons: Teo because he does not think there is any independent fabric of fact for a destiny like that to be written into, and Priya because she thinks "soulmate" talk is haecceitism about persons dressed up as romance, and she gave up haecceitism about far less contentious entities than people long ago. Could you write me a toast of about five minutes, built around one joke that actually turns on the content of the hole argument rather than merely name-dropping it, that treats the fate-talk prohibition as an opening rather than an obstacle to work around, and that ultimately sounds like a friend who is happy for them rather than a discussant at their dissertation defenses?
