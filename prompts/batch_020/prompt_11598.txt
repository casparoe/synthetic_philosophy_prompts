I'm a novelist writing a book set inside a fictional AI interpretability lab, and while researching it I've been shadowing a real one. At a dinner after a workshop, the lab's interpretability lead and a philosopher of mind visiting for the summer got into an argument I haven't stopped turning over: whether the "features" these tools find inside a language model are real internal representations, or a story imposed on the network from outside.

(1) The researcher raised a well-known case: a single direction, recovered by a sparse autoencoder, that fires on mentions of the Golden Gate Bridge across dozens of languages and across both text and images, and that, when artificially amplified, makes the model bring up the bridge in almost every reply, including when asked to describe itself. A state that generalizes that far past its training triggers, and reliably drives behavior under intervention, simply is what "representation" means, she said; denying it is just moving the goalposts.

(2) The philosopher answered that reliable leverage isn't enough, invoking what he called the "job description challenge": a thermostat dial reliably changes the temperature, but that doesn't make it a representation of the temperature. A state earns that title only if the system actually uses it as a stand-in — recruited by further processing because of what it stands for, able to misfire, detachable from whatever switched it on. Treating any reliable lever as a representation, he said, confuses the switch for the message.

(3) She responded that neuroscientists hold real brains to exactly this weaker standard: a visual-cortex cell is called an "edge detector" on the strength of selective firing plus the fact that stimulating it biases perception, and nobody calls that goalpost-moving — so refusing the network the same courtesy looks like special pleading against artificial minds.

(4) He said the two cases only look alike from a distance. With cortex, the firing-plus-stimulation evidence is one strand among several independent ones — developmental trajectory, anatomical wiring, effects of damage — that converge on a single story; every strand of evidence for the network's features, by contrast, comes from the same family of procedure: train a dictionary, then check whether it predicts or steers behavior. He added that changing the sparsity penalty on the very same network changes which "features" turn up, which is a strange thing to be true of joints the network had supposedly already carved before anyone went looking.

Neither of them gave ground. Could you work through the four moves and say, at each step, who has the stronger position — not just who wins overall — and flag anything you think both of them are missing? Close with a one-line verdict, \overall{researcher} or \overall{philosopher}, for whichever is closer to right in the end.
