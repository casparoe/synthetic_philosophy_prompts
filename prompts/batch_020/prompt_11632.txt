"Semantic anti-realists deny that truth can outrun our capacities to find things out, and many of them accept what is usually called the knowability principle: the claim that every truth is at least capable of being known by someone, even if it never actually is. Using a knowledge operator K and a possibility operator ◊, I'd formalize this as: for every proposition p, ◊(p → Kp) — it is possible that, whenever p is true, someone knows it.

Fitch's proof, first published in 1963 (though the underlying argument was actually worked out eighteen years earlier, in 1945, by Alonzo Church, in an anonymous referee report on one of Fitch's own papers — a fact that only came to light decades later, through archival work by Joe Salerno), shows that this apparently modest principle has a very immodest consequence. Suppose, for reductio, that there is at least one truth nobody will ever know, i.e. some p such that p ∧ ¬Kp is true. By the knowability principle, this conjunction, since it's true, must itself be capable of being known: it must be possible for someone to know that (p ∧ ¬Kp).

That, however, cannot be possible. Suppose per impossibile that someone does know p ∧ ¬Kp. Since knowledge distributes over conjunction — if you know A-and-B, you know A and you know B separately — this person knows p, and also knows ¬Kp. But knowledge is factive: you can only know what is actually true. So from their knowing ¬Kp, it follows that ¬Kp is true. We now have both Kp (built into the original supposition) and ¬Kp (just derived): an outright contradiction. So knowing p ∧ ¬Kp is impossible, for any p whatsoever.

Putting the two halves together: the knowability principle forces p ∧ ¬Kp to be knowable, while the second argument shows it can never be known. Since the only assumption that generated this clash was that some truth goes forever unknown, that assumption must be rejected: there is no unknown truth, i.e. every truth is actually known. That conclusion is absurd on its face, so the argument is normally run in reverse, as a reductio of the knowability principle itself. It is aimed above all at the logical positivists, whose verifiability criterion of meaning held that a statement is meaningful only if it can be verified; Fitch's result shows that pushing this kind of verificationism to its natural conclusion collapses it into the claim that everything true is already known, which is a large part of why the positivists' program is now considered untenable."
