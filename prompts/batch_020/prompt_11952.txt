I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, but I've recently gotten hooked on a few metaethics podcasts for my commute, and one topic has stuck with me: the so-called "wrong kind of reasons" problem for fitting-attitude (or "buck-passing") accounts of value. As I understood it, the core idea of these accounts, associated with people like Scanlon, is that for something to be good just is for there to be reason to have a favorable attitude toward it — to desire it, admire it, and so on — rather than the attitude being fitting because the thing is independently good. The wrinkle is a thought experiment I gather is due to Rabinowicz and Rønnow-Rasmussen, involving a demon who threatens to do something terrible to you unless you come to genuinely admire something utterly unremarkable (a heap of wet gravel, say). The threat clearly gives you a reason to admire the gravel, but nobody wants to conclude that the gravel has thereby become admirable, which means the theory needs some principled way of throwing out reasons like "a demon is coercing me" while keeping reasons like "this sunset is genuinely beautiful."

Could you write me a joke that turns on this distinction between the "right kind" of reasons (grounded in the object's own value-making features) and the "wrong kind" (grounded in incentives, threats, or other considerations that leave those features untouched)? I don't want a joke that just name-drops demons and philosophers for an easy laugh — I want the punchline itself to depend on genuinely understanding the right-kind/wrong-kind distinction, so that it would actually land with people who work on this stuff, rather than merely sounding clever to someone who doesn't. Think of it as a joke I could tell at a party full of metaethicists without a philosopher friend later taking me aside to explain, gently, that I've gotten the whole thing backwards.
