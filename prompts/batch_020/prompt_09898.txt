I spent thirty-five years running an experimental low-temperature physics lab, and although I was on perfectly comfortable terms with the Bell inequalities as a working experimentalist -- one did not get through that period without following the Aspect-type tests -- I never had occasion to work through the interpretive literature seriously until a reading group I joined after retiring took it up. What is troubling me at the moment is the relationship between two families of response to Bell's theorem that both try to preserve locality by denying the same premise, measurement independence, rather than by denying locality itself: superdeterminism, in the versions associated with 't Hooft and, more recently, Hossenfelder, and the retrocausal or time-symmetric approaches associated with Price and Wharton.

Superdeterminism is routinely dismissed as "conspiratorial," on the grounds that it requires an implausibly exact correlation, fixed since some common causal past, between the experimenters' choice of apparatus settings and the particles' hidden states, holding across every Bell-type experiment anyone might ever perform. Retrocausal accounts are usually presented, including by their own defenders, as escaping this charge, since the dependence there is supposed to run through an ordinary-looking, if time-reversed, causal law rather than an inexplicable coincidence. I would like a careful, mechanistic account of why that asymmetry is supposed to hold -- what a retrocausal model is actually doing differently from a superdeterministic one in generating the same correlations -- followed by an honest assessment of whether the asymmetry survives scrutiny. I recall, perhaps imperfectly, an argument to the effect that any retrocausal model wanting to avoid superluminal signalling has to build in a fine-tuning condition of its own, structurally rather similar to superdeterminism's; if that is right, I am not sure what is actually purchased by relocating the dependence from the past to the future, and I would welcome your assessment of whether the two proposals are, at bottom, the same maneuver wearing different clothes.
