The following is a question from a graduate seminar take-home exam on David Lewis's theory of convention, together with one student's answer to it.

**Question.** In *Convention* (1969), David Lewis analyzes convention as a solution to a recurring coordination problem.
(a) State Lewis's definition of a convention, explaining the roles that coordination equilibria and common knowledge play in it.
(b) Lewis holds that a convention need not be reached by explicit agreement, and need not be the best of the coordination equilibria available to a population. Explain why his account has these two features.
(c) Some theorists have argued that Lewis's apparatus, designed for pure coordination problems, cannot by itself explain norms that resolve interactions of a different structure — for instance, Prisoner's-Dilemma-type interactions, or problems of dividing some good fairly. Explain this worry, and assess whether Lewis's account of convention extends to cover norm-governed behavior of this kind.

**Student's answer.**

(a) Lewis defines a convention as a regularity R in the behavior of members of a population P, in a recurring situation S, such that it is common knowledge in the population that: everyone conforms to R; everyone expects everyone else to conform to R; and everyone prefers to conform to R on condition that the others do, because R is a coordination equilibrium — a combination of actions such that no one could have been better off had they, alone, acted differently, given what everyone else does. Common knowledge is what makes this self-perpetuating: each agent must not only conform and expect conformity, but know that everyone else expects it too, know that this is known, and so on, since it is this shared, self-referential awareness — not any external authority — that gives each agent standing reason to keep conforming. The regularity is also arbitrary, in that some alternative regularity R' could have equally well served the same coordinating function; this is what separates a convention from a rule that happens to be the only physically or rationally available option. Driving on the right is Lewis's standard illustration: given that everyone else drives on the right, and that this is common knowledge, I have every reason to do so too, even though keeping left would have worked equally well.

(b) Neither feature is surprising once the coordination-equilibrium picture is in view. No agreement is needed because the mutual expectations that sustain a convention can arise tacitly, through precedent or salience — what later writers such as Schelling would call a "focal point" — rather than through any exchange of promises; Lewis's central ambition is to show that a Humean convention can originate and perpetuate itself without contract, without design, and without sanctions, since conforming is already each agent's best response once the others' conformity is common knowledge. And no convention need be optimal because the coordination-equilibrium condition is purely local: it only requires that no single agent could have done better by unilaterally deviating, holding the others' behavior fixed. It says nothing about how R compares to some other coordination equilibrium R' the population might have hit upon instead. Keeping right and keeping left are both coordination equilibria of the same driving problem, and nothing in Lewis's definition ranks one above the other; whichever a population actually settles into counts as its convention, regardless of whether a better one was available.

(c) The worry is that not every norm-governed regularity is a coordination equilibrium in Lewis's sense. In a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, mutual cooperation is not an equilibrium at all: each party does strictly better by defecting no matter what the other does, so a norm of cooperation cannot be held in place by the mechanism Lewis describes, where common knowledge of what others will do is enough, by itself, to make conforming each agent's rational choice. Much the same seems true of norms governing the fair division of some good: whoever gets less than a fair share would do better, unilaterally, by grabbing more, so fairness is not self-enforcing the way a coordination convention is. But the interactions that give rise to such norms are, like coordination problems, recurring interactions among a population of rational agents who can accumulate precedent and settle on salient regularities over time; there is no reason the same tacit, expectation-driven process that lets a population drift into driving on the right could not equally let it drift into a stable norm of cooperation or fair division, common knowledge of which would then keep it self-perpetuating in just the way a coordination convention is. So although Lewis restricts his own discussion to pure coordination problems, the apparatus of common knowledge and rational conformity on which it rests does not appear to depend on that restriction, and should extend to these other cases without much modification.
