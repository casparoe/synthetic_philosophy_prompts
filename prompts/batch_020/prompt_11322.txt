Over the winter break I went down a rabbit hole on Thomas Hobbes that started, a little embarrassingly, with a Wikipedia link about the history of pi. I already knew him the way most non-philosophers do, as the state-of-nature, Leviathan philosopher, but I hadn't registered that he had a whole theory of why geometry, specifically, is the one certain science: because we construct its objects ourselves. In "Six Lessons to the Professors of Mathematics" he argues that a subject is demonstrable only "where the causes are known," and that geometry qualifies because "the lines and figures from which we reason are drawn and described by ourselves" — and he uses exactly this reasoning to argue that his own political theory could be just as certain as geometry, since a commonwealth, too, is something we build rather than discover. What really struck me is that "Six Lessons" is itself Hobbes's rebuttal in a public dispute with the mathematician John Wallis, who had just shown, in humiliating detail, that Hobbes's published proof for squaring the circle, in "De Corpore," was simply wrong. Hobbes never accepted this. He kept producing new "proofs" for the rest of his life, over two decades, while Wallis and others kept taking them apart in print.

I'm a software engineer, and I keep hearing (and making) a version of Hobbes's argument about formally verified code and systems I designed myself: the idea that building something, or having a proof assistant check a construction I wrote, gives me a specially secure kind of knowledge that it's correct — categorically different from trusting somebody else's black box. Hobbes looks like the perfect stress test for that idea, since he held the "maker's knowledge" theory in its strongest form and was still spectacularly, permanently wrong about the one subject where his own epistemology said he should have been safest. Could you describe the most important implications of this — the argument itself, and Hobbes's failure to live up to it — for how much epistemic weight engineers should actually place on having built or formally verified something ourselves? I'm less interested in the "even brilliant people get attached to their own ideas" moral than in whether there is a specific, identifiable gap in the maker's-knowledge picture that would predict a failure of exactly this shape, and whether that same gap is still sitting underneath the confidence we place in our own systems today.
