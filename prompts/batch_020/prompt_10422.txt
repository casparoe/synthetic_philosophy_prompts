I recently joined the resident cast of an immersive theatre production—the kind where audience members roam freely through the set and are periodically drawn one by one into private scenes with performers, with no stage, fixed seating, or fourth wall in sight. At a company talk, our director cited Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert in defense of the form, arguing that Rousseau's real target was never drama as such but the passivity of spectators seated at a fixed distance, and that his preferred alternative—the open-air civic festival, where citizens are at once performers and audience for one another—is essentially what we stage nightly. This reassured everyone else in the room rather more than it reassured me, since my actual job still consists of counterfeiting passions I do not feel in front of people who have paid to be drawn into the scene with me. Please argue that immersive theatre of this kind does not actually escape Rousseau's condemnation of the stage, and that his deeper objection was never really about where the audience sits at all—engaging directly with his separate charge that the actor's profession of counterfeiting passions not his own corrupts the performer regardless of how intimately or symmetrically the performance is staged.
