I'm drafting a novel whose central figure, a physicist, becomes quietly convinced that the past and future are exactly as real as the present, and that her own sense of "now" sliding forward is some kind of cognitive illusion. Tracing the philosophical lineage of that idea for background research, I kept being pointed to the same 1908 source as the ancestor of the whole presentism/eternalism dispute, and now that I have the actual text in front of me I've gotten stuck on the crucial paragraph. It's from J. M. E. McTaggart's "The Unreality of Time" (Mind, vol. 17):

"Past, present, and future are incompatible determinations. Every event must be one or the other, but no event can be more than one. This is essential to the meaning of the terms. [...] Yet every event has them all. If M is past, it has been present and future. If it is future, it will be present and past. If it is present, it has been future and will be past. Thus all the three incompatible terms are predicable of each event, which is obviously inconsistent with their being incompatible, unless it can in some way be explained."

I can follow the contradiction he's setting up, but not why he thinks the obvious escape -- just say each incompatible term holds of the event at a different time rather than all at once -- fails; I gather this move is supposed to trigger a vicious infinite regress, but I can't reconstruct where the viciousness actually enters rather than mere repetition. Could you spell out that step? And, separately, so that my physicist's reasoning is honest rather than a fudge: if the regress does succeed against real tensed "becoming," does it count equally against a straightforward presentist and against a "moving spotlight" eternalist who accepts a block universe but insists one moment is objectively picked out as present, or does one of them have a resource for blocking the regress that the other lacks?
