(1) Jake started it off by saying there's this classic argument, Hume's I think, that you should basically never believe a miracle report because it'll always be more probable that the witnesses are mistaken or lying than that an actual law of nature got suspended. (3) Jake pushed back that this isn't quite fair to the argument: the claim isn't that testimony could never in principle be enough, just that for it to be enough, the testimony would have to be so strong that its being false would itself be a bigger miracle than the thing it's reporting -- so it's a very high bar, not a locked door. (4) Mina granted that but came back with two points: first, you always get to decide after the fact whether the bar was cleared, which is awfully convenient in practice; second, independent testimony from people who don't know eachother should multiply, not just stack -- a dozen doctors who each have only a small independent chance of being wrong is way stronger evidence than Jake's framing gives credit for. (5) Jake's answer was that Hume has a backup argument for basically every real case anyway: the people reporting miracles are almost never neutral, there's usually a rival explanation or a competing religious claim floating around that cancels the story out, and people just love a good wonder-story enough to stop checking -- so you don't even need the probability math to be suspicious of most reports. (6) Mina's last word was that the backup argument might work fine on a 2,000-year-old story with three anonymous sources, but it doesn't obviously touch a case with a dozen named, non-religious doctors on the record, and if that backup is really doing the work, then the big "in principle" argument Jake led with was never the actual reason to disbelieve the story -- it's just cover for a much narrower, case-by-case point about who's testifying.
