For my IB Extended Essay in Philosophy, I'm examining whether Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles survives Max Black's thought experiment about two qualitatively identical iron spheres, alike in every respect, sitting some fixed distance apart in an otherwise completely empty universe. I keep getting stuck on the passage below, from Leibniz's fourth letter to Samuel Clarke (1716), which is usually treated as his main defense of the principle, but I can't work out how its parts are supposed to fit together:

"3. 'Tis a thing indifferent, to place three Bodies, equal and perfectly alike, in any order whatsoever; and consequently they will never be placed in any order, by Him who does nothing without Wisdom. But then, He being the Author of things, no such things will be produced by him at all; and consequently there are no such things in Nature.

4. There is no such thing as Two Individuals indiscernible from each other. An Ingenious Gentleman of my Acquaintance, discoursing with me, in the presence of Her Electoral Highness the Princess Sophia, in the Garden of Herrenhausen, thought he could find two Leaves perfectly alike. The Princess defied him to do it, and he ran all over the Garden a long time to look for some; but it was to no purpose. Two Drops of Water, or Milk, viewed with a Microscope, will appear distinguishable from each other. This is an Argument against Atoms; which are confuted, as well as a Vacuum, by the Principles of true Metaphysicks.

5. Those great Principles of a sufficient Reason, and of the Identity of Indiscernibles, change the State of Metaphysicks. That Science becomes real and demonstrative by means of these Principles; whereas before, it did generally consist in empty Words.

6. To suppose two things indiscernible, is to suppose the same thing under two Names. And therefore to suppose that the Universe could have had at first another position of Time and Place, than that which it actually had; and yet that all the Parts of the Universe should have had the same Situation among themselves, as that which they actually had; such a Supposition, I say, is an impossible Fiction."

What I can't pin down is whether Leibniz is really offering one argument here or several different ones he's treating as interchangeable. Section 4 reads like an empirical claim resting on the fact that no one has ever actually managed to find two perfectly alike leaves or drops of liquid. Section 6 reads like something stronger and non-empirical: that supposing two things indiscernible is really just supposing one thing under two names, which would make indiscernible duplicates impossible by definition rather than by failed observation. Section 3 brings in yet another consideration about God having no reason to prefer one arrangement of identical bodies over a permutation of it. Could you help me sort out how many distinct arguments are actually at work here, whether and how Leibniz means them to support one another, and which version Black's spheres would actually have to overcome, given that Black just stipulates his universe rather than needing to search it or explain why anyone arranged it that way?
