I'm a software engineer who was recently elected to my condominium association's board, and I've somehow ended up being the one asked to write up a recommendation on a dispute the rest of the board can't settle on its own — possibly because I was the only one willing to reread our entire declaration of covenants line by line. That declaration, drafted back in 1988, says units may be used 'only for single-family residential purposes' and that 'no trade, business, or commercial activity of any kind shall be conducted on or from any unit.' One owner, who lives out of state and has never actually stayed in her unit, has been renting it out through Airbnb for stretches as short as two nights, several times a month. About half the board wants to send a cease-and-desist letter and start the fining process under our enforcement policy; the other half thinks the declaration doesn't clearly cover this, since the guests are still just sleeping and living there the way any resident would, and the 'business' language was obviously aimed at things like a storefront or a home office with clients coming and going, not a unit filled through an app. We paid for an hour of a real-estate attorney's time, and she told us both readings were 'legally defensible' and that it was up to the board — which was not the answer anyone wanted.

The timing makes this more than academic: a unit two doors down is under contract to sell, and the buyer's lender has sent the board a standard questionnaire asking whether the declaration restricts short-term or transient rentals and whether any such restriction has been consistently enforced, since an unclear or inconsistent answer can reportedly complicate financing for the whole building, not just that one sale. Amending the declaration to add an explicit minimum-lease term would require a two-thirds vote of all owners by mailed ballot, which realistically takes months we don't have before the closing date. A couple of board members keep appealing to 'what the original drafters intended,' but none of them are still owners here, and the only surviving 1988 meeting minutes we could dig up don't mention rentals at all. The whole argument reminds me, oddly, of fights I've had at work over whether code should do exactly what a spec says or whatever the spec was obviously trying to accomplish when it didn't anticipate some case — except here there's no author left to go ask.

Could you draft the short memo I'll circulate to the rest of the board before our meeting next Tuesday? It should recommend one concrete path forward — enforcement, non-enforcement, or something like a conditional or grandfathering arrangement — along with a realistic timeline for acting given the pending sale, and it should take the strongest version of whichever side it doesn't recommend seriously, rather than treating that reading of the declaration as obviously careless or unreasonable.
