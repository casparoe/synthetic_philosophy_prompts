My son got diagnosed with dyslexia about six weeks ago, and reading through his evaluation sent me down a rabbit hole thats now bleeding straight into my actual job. I'm a school psychologist, and our elementary school's eligibility team meets this Thursday on a case that's turning out to be more contentious than usual: a third grader, we'll call him Mateo, whose reading is way below grade level but who doesn't cleanly fall into any of the usual exclusion buckets (vision or hearing problems, intellectual disability, lack of instruction) that would let us dodge the question entirely. Our state's SLD criteria require something like "a disorder in one or more basic psychological processes," and it turns out my team is quietly split on what "disorder" even means there, without anyone quite naming it that way.

Half the team is reasoning backward from design history: writing is maybe 5,000 years old, nowhere near enough time for natural selection to have built (or broken) any circuit dedicated to it, so whatever Mateo's brain is doing with the visual and phonological systems it borrowed for reading, it was never "supposed to" do this in the first place -- meaning there's nothing there to malfunction, just an unlucky point on a normal curve. The other half thinks that's a trap dressed up as rigor: you don't need an ancestral just-so story to say a system is failing relative to a capacity we actually care about right now, and if "no selection pressure for it" is enough to disqualify a disorder, that logic disqualifies dyscalculia too, and eventually anything tied to a capacity modernity invented -- which seems like the wrong result, not a discovery.

I need to actually send something to my team before Thursday, not just a shrug. Can you draft the memo? I want it to take a real stance on which notion of "disorder" our criteria should be read as invoking (or how the two should be combined), apply it to Mateo's case with a concrete eligibility recommendation, and include a genuine paragraph engaging the "there's nothing to malfunction, reading is too new" camp on their strongest ground rather than brushing them off -- I don't think they should win here, but I don't think they're just confused either. A sentence of general guidance we could reuse next time this argument comes up (it will) would help too.
