One member sent in the answer below. I don't feel confident judging it myself, so I'd like your assessment before I pass on any feedback to the group.

"Whether a feature counts as real, rather than a useful fiction, turns on whether it plays a genuine causal role in the network's computation, and not merely on whether we can find some pattern that correlates with it. This is where the superposition hypothesis matters: because a network typically has to represent far more features than it has neurons, it compresses many nearly-orthogonal feature directions into the same set of neurons. This is what produces polysemanticity, and it is also why individual neurons are not a reliable guide to what a network represents: real features have to be recovered as directions in activation space rather than read off single units. This is close to Dennett's argument, in his account of real patterns, that a pattern counts as real whenever treating it as such lets you compress and predict a system's behavior better than tracking every element individually, which is usually taken to mean that Dennett sides with deflationists who treat talk of a network's 'features' as a convenient fiction rather than a discovery about its actual ontology. On the methodological side, the standard way researchers confirm that a feature is genuinely represented is to train a linear probe on the network's internal activations and check whether it can decode the property of interest with high accuracy; when a simple linear classifier can recover a feature from a layer's activations, that shows the network is relying on that feature to produce its output, which is why probing has become the workhorse method for validating proposed features."
