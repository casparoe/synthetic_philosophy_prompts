I'm a political theory PhD student and TA the undergrad political philosophy survey course, and every year when we hit Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain argument section basically erupts. This year two students, Marcus and Sofia, went at it for a solid twenty minutes after class and then both turned to me to declare a winner. I ducked it, but its been bugging me since -- I think they're each landing real points.

Quick recap in case it's useful: Nozick has you imagine any patterned distribution D1 you like (say, one satisfying Rawls's difference principle), then a million fans each pay Wilt Chamberlain an extra 25 cents on their ticket just to watch him play. He ends up $250,000 richer, the resulting D2 no longer fits the pattern, even though every step from D1 to D2 was a voluntary exchange. Nozick's slogan: "liberty upsets patterns" -- keeping any pattern intact means either constant interference or refusing to let people do what they want with what's theirs.

Marcus: this kills patterned theories of justice: to keep the pattern you either block the transfers or keep clawing money back from Wilt forever, and either way you're using people as means to a social end instead of agents living their own lives.

Sofia: only if you've already smuggled in the idea that people at D1 have unrestricted rights to transfer their holdings to anyone, in any amount. a patterned theorist can just deny that -- you get to use and enjoy your share, justice never promised an unlimited right to give big chunks of it away. so the argument assumes exactly what's in dispute.

Marcus: but telling someone they can't hand a quarter to watch a guy play basketball, with money that's already legitimately theirs, is precisely what treats them as an instrument for propping up a pattern instead of a separate person with their own ends.

Sofia: there's a difference between "no liberty to use your share" -- nobody's proposing that -- and "unlimited rights to transfer it to anyone, forever." Rawls's own scheme allows plenty of everyday liberty while restricting big transfers and bequests. so "liberty" is doing two jobs in Nozick's argument, and only the narrow one, unlimited transfer, is what actually gets you from D1 to D2.

Marcus: then draw me the line. paying a quarter to watch Wilt play is a purchase, same as buying a coffee -- that looks like "using your share" to me. if every purchase counts as a restrictable "transfer" you've banned commerce; if it doesnt, you've got no basis for stopping the Chamberlain case specifically.

Can you go through this move by move, say who's got the better of each specific point (not just an overall winner), and flag anything important neither of them notices? End with your overall call on its own line, formatted exactly as \verdict{Marcus} or \verdict{Sofia}.
