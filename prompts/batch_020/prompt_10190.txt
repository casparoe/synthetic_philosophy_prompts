And here's the answer one student gave, word for word:

"Harsanyi's Social Aggregation Theorem (1955) considers a society of n individuals together with a 'social preference' relation over the same set of lotteries (probability distributions over social outcomes) that the individuals have preferences over. Both each individual's preferences and society's preferences are assumed to satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, so each individual i has an expected-utility function U_i and society has an expected-utility function W. The key extra assumption is a Pareto condition: if every individual weakly prefers one lottery to another then so does society, and if at least one individual strictly prefers it (with none opposed) then society strictly prefers it too. Given these assumptions, Harsanyi proves that W must be an affine function of the individual utilities: W = a + Σ b_i U_i, for constants a and b_i ≥ 0.

Harsanyi took this to be a vindication of utilitarianism because it shows that, once we grant that individuals and society are expected-utility maximizers, the only social welfare functions compatible with the Pareto principle are weighted sums of individual utility — exactly the utilitarian form. The intuitive picture behind the theorem is the veil of ignorance: we're asked to imagine an impartial spectator who doesn't know which member of society she is going to be and assigns herself an equal chance of ending up as any one of them; maximizing her own expected utility from this impartial standpoint is what forces the social ranking into the additive, utilitarian shape, since from behind the veil the 'social good' just is the average of what each person gets.

One influential objection to reading the theorem this way comes from Sen, who points out that the b_i weights aren't fixed by anything in the theorem itself. Because each U_i is only cardinal up to its own separate positive affine transformation, choosing a different (but equally admissible) scale for, say, U_3 changes what b_3 has to be for the representation to still hold, without changing anyone's actual preferences — so the theorem is consistent with wildly different weightings of different people's interests, and doesn't by itself deliver a determinate utilitarian social welfare function, only a family of them. Rawls raises essentially this same worry in A Theory of Justice as part of his broader rejection of Harsanyi's utilitarian use of the original position, preferring instead a maximin rule that doesn't require this kind of interpersonal weighting at all."
