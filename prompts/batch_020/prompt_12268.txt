Okay, this is a weirdly specific one, but it's been rattling around in my head for weeks and I think it's an actual philosophical mess, not just a logistics problem. I cover conservation and animal-welfare science for a living, and seperately from that I'm one of five trustees of a small charitable trust that puts modest grants toward seabird conservation projects. We've just been asked to help fund a second attempt at eradicating the mice on Gough Island — if you don't know the story, house mice got dropped there by sailors a couple centuries ago, and with no native predators around they've grown to a genuinely freakish size and started eating albatross and petrel chicks alive in the nest, which is a big part of why several of the island's seabirds are sliding toward extinction. There was a huge aerial poison-baiting operation in 2021 that hammered the mouse population but, if I remember the coverage right, fell short of a full eradication, and apparently also ended up killing some of the island's own endemic little songbirds in the process. Now the partners running the project want more money for a redo, and our little board has to decide by our next call, which is in about three weeks, whether to chip in.

Here's what's actually bugging me, and why I'm asking you instead of just calling a seabird biologist: underneath the "will this work" logistics, this is a wild-animal-suffering question, and I don't think our trust has ever had to think one through this explicitly before. We'd be paying to kill an enormous number of sentient mice with a poison that doesn't exactly offer a quick death, specifically so that a smaller number of chicks are spared an even worse one, on the theory that this nets out to less total suffering — except the chicks we save then go on to live ordinary wild-albatross lives, which are not exactly cushy either, and the last attempt already showed the intervention can accidentally kill the very native animals it's meant to protect. I brought this up with a couple of philosopher friends over dinner and got completely opposite reactions: one said obviously you intervene, the other started talking about "compassionate conservation" and said we might just be laundering a preference for cute, endangered animals over "pest" ones through the language of welfare.

Could you draft the memo I'd actually bring to my fellow trustees? I need a real recommendation — fund it, decline, fund it with specific conditions, or put the money toward something else entirely — not an even-handed survey of considerations. But I do want it to take the strongest version of the case against whatever you recommend seriously, rather than mentioning it once and moving on.
