I run a tiny philosophy podcast on the side (something like 200 subscribers, half of whom are probably my own students padding out their podcast app), and for the next episode I'm finally straying outside my usual epistemology beat. My guest has a new book resurrecting Moritz Schlick's 1927 essay "On the Meaning of Life" -- title's something like "Back to Play," I'd have to check -- and her pitch is that the whole contemporary meaning-of-life industry (Wolf, Metz, all the hybrid and objective-list theories) basically reinvented a worse wheel, since Schlick already had the answer: meaning isn't found in chasing purposes, which just spawns the next purpose forever, but in play, activity that carries its point inside itself (what he calls Selbstzweck) rather than in some further payoff. I read the essay once, years ago, in grad school, and came away charmed and a little suspicious of it in about equal measure, so I don't want to just lob her softballs for twenty minutes.

Could you draft me eight to ten interview questions that actually push on the argument rather than let her restate it? I want one on the odd move partway through the essay where Schlick backs off calling play itself the meaning of life and says instead that meaning is youth, since youth is just the period when play predominates -- which has the strange upshot that a life cut short in infancy comes out fully meaningful, maybe more so than most adult lives. I want a separate question on how "youth" seems to be doing two jobs for him at once, literal and biological (needed to get that infant-mortality result) and recoverable and metaphorical (needed for his other line that an old man who's turned his work into joyful play counts as young in the sense that actually matters) -- can one word really carry both without equivocating? I want one on the joy-versus-mere-pleasure distinction he leans on so the view doesn't collapse into "just do whatever feels good in the moment," and whether that distinction is doing real philosophical work or is just a label pasted over a hedonism he doesn't want to own. And I want one tying the essay back to his day job as founder of the Vienna Circle: if value-talk isn't strictly the kind of thing that's true or false on his own verificationist picture, what's he actually doing when he tells us, in the tone of a real argument, that play -- rather than duty, or contemplation, or achievement -- is where meaning lives? Give each of those four a sharp follow-up keyed to whatever answer she's most likely give, so there's no room to just restate the position, and round it out to eight or ten with other questions that hit just as hard.
