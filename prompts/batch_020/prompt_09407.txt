I'm a retired condensed-matter physicist who's fallen hard into philosophy podcasts since I stopped working (Peter Adamson's history-of-philosophy-without-gaps show is my current obsession), and I just got through the stretch on al-Ghazali and kalam occasionalism. I know, I know — the guy who spent forty years measuring things in a lab now has opinions about 11th-century Persian theology, but bear with me. You probably know the famous move: fire touching cotton doesn't *cause* burning in any necessary sense, its God who creates the burning each time, as a matter of habit ("custom," or 'āda) rather than logical necessity — and since there's no contradiction in imagining the fire not burning the cotton on some occasion (cf. Abraham in the furnace), the "causal" connection was never more than God's consistent habit to begin with. I'd always sort of mentally filed this under "Hume's problem of induction, but with a theological twist," but the more I sit with it the more I think that's the wrong comparison: Hume's point is epistemic (we never perceive or infer a necessary connection), while Ghazali's is a much stronger modal claim — there simply isn't one, full stop, regardless of what anyone could ever observe or predict.

So here's the question I can't shake, which a friend from my old lab and I went back and forth on over coffee without resolving anything: given that Ghazali's argument is explicitly about the *absence of logical necessity* rather than about predictability, what would he make of the move you see in some popular writing today that quantum indeterminacy somehow vindicates occasionalism? My gut says he'd find that a category error — he'd have denied necessary secondary causation just as confidently in a fully deterministic Newtonian universe as in an indeterministic quantum one, so indeterminacy is neither here nor there for his actual argument — but I want you to actually reason it through rather than just take my word for it.

And a bonus round, since it's been rattling around in my head too: occasionalism (at least in the Ash'ari form Ghazali inherited) also denies that we're the real cause of our own actions — we merely "acquire" (kasb) acts that God creates in us. Do you think Ghazali would treat Libet-style findings (that measurable brain activity precedes our conscious sense of deciding) as support for that picture, or would he say that's answering a completely different question than the one he was asking? Please don't just hand me a textbook summary of occasionalism, I've had plenty of that from the podcast already — I want your actual reasoning about what does and doesn't follow from his specific argument.
