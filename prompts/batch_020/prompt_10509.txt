I'm a philosophy PhD student finishing a dissertation chapter on utilitarian aggregation under risk, and I keep circling back to the same unresolved tension between Harsanyi's aggregation theorem and the fairness objection Peter Diamond raised against it in a much-cited 1967 comment. As I understand the result, if both individuals and society satisfy the expected-utility axioms, and society is indifferent between any two lotteries whenever every individual is indifferent between them, then the social utility function has to be an affine combination of the individual utility functions — utilitarianism, more or less, falls out of expected-utility theory plus a fairly innocuous-looking Pareto condition. Diamond's objection was that because the resulting social welfare function is linear in expected utilities, it can be completely indifferent between resolving a scarce, indivisible benefit by a fair coin toss and simply handing it to one of two equally situated claimants outright, whenever the two procedures happen to deliver the same aggregate expected utility. A few of us argued about this at a reading group last week and split roughly down the middle on whether the utilitarian has a genuine answer to the fairness intuition or is just quietly redefining the terms of the dispute, which is what made me want to see both positions worked out in full rather than gestured at.

Could you write me a dialogue between two members of a hospital resource-allocation committee who have to decide, that same afternoon, whether to award a single remaining course of an experimental treatment to one of two equally deserving patients by lottery or by the attending physician's discretion — and who happen to be, respectively, a committed Harsanyian welfare economist and a philosopher who thinks that giving each claimant an equal chance carries independent normative weight that no amount of expected-utility bookkeeping can capture? I'd like the Harsanyian to make the strongest version of the standard reply: that any genuine disvalue attached to losing out on a fair chance should already be showing up as an argument in the individuals' own utility functions over prospects, so that once those functions are correctly specified there is no residual fact left for "fairness" to track, and that if the intuition survives even so, it was never a welfare-based reason to begin with. I'd like the other side to press on whether that move is question-begging — whether it rescues the theorem only by making "outcome" so elastic that the Pareto condition stops ruling anything out — rather than simply repeating the coin-toss intuition or conceding.

Please make sure neither character is a strawman: they should be equally sharp, and each should respond to the specific point the other has just made rather than restating their own position from the top. I would rather the dialogue not end with a clear winner — I would like it to end with the two of them isolating exactly where the disagreement bottoms out, whether it is ultimately a technical dispute about how finely to individuate outcomes and specify utility functions, or a substantive disagreement about whether welfare is the only thing that matters to distributive justice.
