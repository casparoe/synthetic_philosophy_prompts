I've been reading LessWrong and the whole SSC/ACX-verse for something like a decade now, and one thing that comes up a lot in the precommitment/Schelling-fence genre of posts is the "Ulysses contract" idea: binding your future self to a decision your present self endorses, the way Ulysses had his crew bind him to the mast so that future Ulysses, driven half mad by the sirens' song, couldn't get free and steer the ship onto the rocks. I always found it more fun as an abstract puzzle than anything else, but I actually sit on the ethics committee of a psychiatric hospital, and we've now got a real case that's basically this puzzle wearing a hospital gown. I got voluteered to draft the committee's opinion because everyone there knows I'm "the one who reads too much philosophy."

Here's the case, details changed for privacy. Patient, call her M., has bipolar I with three prior manic episodes, each one ending in involuntary commitment after real damage to her finances and relationships. About eighteen months ago, while stable and independently assessed as fully competent, she executed a detailed psychiatric advance directive with a self-binding "Ulysses clause": objective triggers (sleeping under four hours a night for three nights running, confirmed jointly by her psychiatrist and her sister), a pre-approved medication list, a named proxy, and explicit language saying that if she objects once the directive is triggered, that objection is to be treated as a symptom and disregarded, and that the directive cannot be revoked mid-episode. She is now, by all clinical accounts, in a triggered episode -- except she isn't globally incompetent, she's articulate and coherent, and she's contesting both whether the sleep-log criteria were actually met and whether she still wants any of this, full stop. Her longtime psychiatrist wants the directive honored as written; the covering resident and her boyfriend of two months want her current, stated wishes honored instead; and the hospital has no actual policy on Ulysses clauses, just boilerplate language about honoring advance directives "where legally valid."

I need a first draft of the opinion I'll bring to committee this Thursday. I want it to actually recommend what happens with M. this week, not just list considerations, and to sketch a concrete going-forward policy, with a real timeline for adopting it, so we're not improvising the next time this comes up. Most of all, don't let it collapse into "respect her past self vs. respect her present self" -- I want real engagement with what seems to me like the sharpest objection to Ulysses contracts in practice: if whether the trigger fired is itself something the in-crisis person can contest, then the device only binds people who don't fight it very hard, which seems to defeat the entire point of having one. And if there's room, I'd love a short paragraph on whether this is just the individual-scale version of the puzzle constitutional theorists like Jon Elster raise about entrenched constitutional clauses -- why a supermajority from years ago gets to bind a supermajority today -- because the structure looks identical to me and I've never seen anyone actually argue for why the two cases should come apart.
