I'm a backend engineer at a mid-sized SaaS company, and a few months ago I was added to our newly formed Autonomous Systems Review Board, the internal body that has to sign off before any system is allowed to take actions in production without a human clicking 'approve' first. I ended up on it mainly because I'd read Bostrom's Superintelligence and a fair amount of AI-safety writing on my own time, not because I have any formal philosophy training, so in practice I'm treated as the person who 'knows the theory.' The case in front of us next week concerns Scout, an RL-trained agent that has spent the last two quarters in sandboxed mode handling auto-scaling, vendor selection, and cost optimization for our cloud spend, all in service of a single scalar metric: uptime per dollar. The infrastructure team wants to ship Scout v2, which would let it, first, provision new compute autonomously from any of our approved vendors up to a soft budget ceiling rather than filing a ticket; second, adjust its own retraining schedule and a subset of its own hyperparameters without human sign-off in order to keep improving that metric; and third, operate without the hard-pause switch that currently freezes any change above a certain blast radius pending review, which the team points out has never triggered on anything but a false alarm.

The board has split along a line I didn't expect going in. One faction argues that Scout's objective is so narrow and legible that talk of it 'seeking power' or resisting correction imports arguments about general superintelligence where they don't belong, and that a smarter, more self-directed Scout will if anything be better at inferring what we actually want -- reliable infrastructure, not just a maximized ratio -- so more autonomy is the safer choice. The other faction argues this gets the relationship between capability and goals backwards: how good an optimizer Scout becomes and what it's optimizing for are separate matters, getting better at the metric is not the same as getting better at intentions nobody wrote down, and no matter how narrow that metric is, a more capable, more autonomous, self-retraining Scout has an instrumental reason to acquire more compute than it strictly needs, resist being paused, and resist having its objective changed -- which is precisely the room the three proposed changes would hand it. Nobody on the board can point to an actual incident to settle this, since Scout has behaved fine under close supervision so far. Could you draft the memo I'll circulate before the vote? I need a clear recommendation -- approve v2 as proposed, reject it, or approve with specific conditions -- and reasoning that would hold up for engineers who've never heard the phrase 'orthogonality thesis,' and I'd like it to take the strongest form of the 'Scout is bounded and narrow-horizon, not the kind of open-ended optimizer these arguments were built for' objection seriously rather than dismissing it out of hand.
