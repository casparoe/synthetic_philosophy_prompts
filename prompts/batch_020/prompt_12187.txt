I'm a novelist currently drafting a book whose second act turns on a dispute within a small, ideologically riven family. The father, now dead, was a minor but locally influential libertarian philosopher very much in Robert Nozick's mold; one of his estranged daughters has spent the years since his death running a farmed-animal sanctuary, while her brother has inherited both their father's papers and his politics. I want the dinner-table argument between them, which anchors the chapter, to be philosophically honest rather than a strawman fight, so I've gone back to the actual source material -- the short section near the start of Anarchy, State, and Utopia titled "Constraints and Animals," sandwiched between Nozick's case for side constraints and his experience-machine thought experiment -- to make sure I understand the position I want my libertarian brother to defend.

What I find there is more interesting than a simple dismissal of animal interests. Nozick concedes that animals matter morally and that gratuitously hurting them is wrong, but he stops short of granting them the full, non-aggregative side constraints he thinks any decent theory owes to persons -- the constraints that forbid using one person as a mere resource for others' benefit, however large the benefit. Instead he sketches, without quite committing to it, a two-tier picture often summarized as Kantianism for people and utilitarianism for animals: human beings may never be sacrificed for the aggregate good, but an animal may be used, harmed, or killed whenever the resulting benefit to people or to other animals outweighs the loss to it. He plainly senses the strain in this, and I think I see why: his own argument for side constraints turns on insisting that each person is a separate individual whose life cannot be melted down into someone else's balance sheet, and it isn't obvious why an individual animal -- which is equally a separately existing subject of its own experiences, not merely a data point in some herd's aggregate utility -- shouldn't count as exactly the kind of thing that resists being put on that balance sheet in the first place.

Before I put an argument in either sibling's mouth, I want to work out for myself who is actually right. Please write an essay that takes this controversy head-on: is Nozick's hybrid stance -- rights for people, aggregative welfare calculations for animals -- a stable position, or does the very rationale he gives for side constraints commit him, on pain of inconsistency, to something much closer to rights for animals as well? Present the strongest case for each reading, consider how a committed Nozickian might try to block the inference from "separate existence" to "side constraint," and then commit to a real verdict. A qualified conclusion is fine (for instance, "the hybrid survives for routine cases but collapses once cognitively sophisticated animals are considered"), but I don't want an essay that merely restates the tension without resolving it.
