So I host a smallish fiction workshop (we meet monthly, mostly literary character-driven stuff), and after last week's session two of the regulars ended up in a genuine argument at the bar about a chapter someone had shared. I was just sitting there with my drink so I didnt weigh in, but its been bugging me since.

The chapter's from a novel where the protagonist, Iris, has spent like 300 pages being established as the loyal one -- she rats out her boss to protect a coworker, takes a beating rather than give up a friend's location, that kind of thing. Then in this new chapter, at the climax, her best friend is about to get taken by some very bad people, and Iris has one real shot to intervene at serious risk to herself -- and she just doesn't. She runs and saves herself. The room was split on whether the twist earns its keep, and two of them, Marisol and Gabe, kept going at it after:

Marisol: if Iris is really loyal, that's exactly the moment it should show up, since loyalty that costs nothing isn't actually being tested. She folded the one time it cost her something real, so all those earlier "loyal" moments were cheap -- she was never loyal, just risk-averse in a way that looked like loyalty until the price got too steep.

Gabe: that's an unfair bar. Genuinely loyal people can freeze under real terror without the whole trait being retroactively fake -- it's like a fragile glass that doesn't break one time because it happened to land on a cushion. The glass is still fragile, the disposition was there, it just didn't get to show itself that one time.

Marisol: a cushion is some outside thing though. What "protected" Iris was her own fear -- the very same self-preservation that was supposedly getting overridden every other time. If the thing blocking the loyalty is just more of the exact variable it's competing against, that's not a cushion, that's finding out there was no separate loyalty-trait at all, just self-preservation that hadn't hit its price ceiling yet.

Gabe: sure, but then nobody's ever really loyal, or brave, or honest, since you can always crank the stakes high enough to make literally anyone fold eventually. That can't be the right conclusion, we obviously still want to say some people are more loyal than others, even granting everyone's got a breaking point somewhere.

Neither of them budged and it kind of derailed the rest of the night. Could you go through the four moves and say who's got the better of it at each step (not just an overall winner), and flag anything you think both of them are missing? Then separately, tell me straight: is Iris's arc a coherent piece of characterization or a cheat on the reader?
