Your answer should (a) lay out the technical construction of the argument, including the role of active diffeomorphisms and the thesis of Leibniz Equivalence; (b) explain precisely why Earman and Norton took the argument to show that generally covariant theories such as general relativity are indeterministic, given substantivalism; and (c) explain one substantivalist strategy for resisting this conclusion, and evaluate whether you find it successful."

Here is the student's answer in full: "The hole argument is directed against manifold substantivalism, the view that the points of spacetime are individuals that exist in their own right, independently of the matter and metric fields defined on them. It exploits the general covariance of general relativity: if (M, g) is a solution of Einstein's field equations, and d is a diffeomorphism of M that is the identity everywhere except inside some bounded region H (the 'hole') where the stress-energy tensor is zero, then (M, d*g) is also a solution. Here d is what is called an active diffeomorphism: it genuinely redistributes the metric over the same fixed set of manifold points, rather than merely relabeling those points with new coordinates, which would be a passive transformation and would generate no interesting puzzle. Crucially, d*g agrees with g everywhere outside H but can disagree with it inside H.

For the substantivalist, who holds that the points of M retain their identities no matter which properties are draped over them, (M, g) and (M, d*g) must represent two distinct physical possibilities, because the same points end up with different metrical properties across the two models — a galaxy's worldline might pass through a given point in one model and miss it in the other. The substantivalist is therefore committed to denying Leibniz Equivalence, the thesis that diffeomorphic models always represent one and the same physical situation.

This generates the indeterminism Earman and Norton found so troubling. Since (M, g) and (M, d*g) agree completely outside H, where all the matter distribution and boundary data are fixed, nothing in the equations of the theory can select one of the two developments inside H over the other — both are equally good continuations of exactly the same data, yet they disagree over which point is which. This is not garden-variety indeterminism of the sort familiar from quantum mechanics: the two developments agree on absolutely every invariant, observable pattern of properties, and differ only over which spacetime point plays which role, which is precisely why Earman and Norton thought a theory should not tolerate it.

Faced with the force of this argument, Earman and Norton eventually retreated to a weaker, 'sophisticated' version of substantivalism, on which spacetime points exist as individuals within a given world but do not carry their identities across possible worlds. On this view, points essentially have the metrical properties they do, so d*g and g never really assign different properties to 'the same' point in the first place — the alleged rival possibilities were never rivals at all, and the indeterminism evaporates before it starts. I find this response fairly persuasive: it lets substantivalists keep spacetime points as genuine individuals without paying the price of Earman and Norton's indeterminism, and giving up primitive cross-world identity for spacetime points seems like a small metaphysical price next to giving up substantivalism altogether."
