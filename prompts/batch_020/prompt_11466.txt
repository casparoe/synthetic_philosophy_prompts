I'm a backend engineer and this argument happened at work a couple weeks ago and its been rattling around in my head since. We've got this ancient fraud-scoring function in our transactions service - maybe 400 lines of nested if-statements and magic-number thresholds, written by a guy who left the company years before I joined. Nobody currently on the team actually understands the logic anymore, but it's correctly flagged every fraudulent transaction we've ever gone back and manually checked, three years running, millions of transactions. It came up in a retro because we're debating whether to finally rewrite it, and two coworkers, Dana and Marcus, got derailed into arguing about whether we can even say we "know" the function is correct, versus just believing it and happening to be right. I couldn't referee it in the moment and can't stop thinking about it, so here's roughly how it went, numbered by move:

(1) Dana: three years, millions of transactions, not one confirmed miss - at some point that just IS knowing it works, full stop, what more could "knowing" possibly require? (2) Marcus: no, that's a true belief we happen to hold, not knowledge - if you swapped in a completely different 400-line tangle with a subtle bug in some edge case we just haven't hit yet, we'd feel exactly as confident, for exactly the same reasons, so whatever we've got isn't actually tracking correctness, it's tracking "nothing's blown up yet." (3) Dana: fine, we could turn out to be wrong someday, but that's just as true of code we've formally verified, since proofs rest on assumptions about the environment that can turn out false - so if two functions produce identical correct output today, the one we don't understand isn't worse in any way that matters, since customers and the business only ever experience outputs, never our understanding. (4) Marcus: understanding earns its keep exactly when something changes - a new fraud pattern, a refactor, a new market - because it lets you predict in advance which changes are safe; without it we're flying blind and won't find out something broke until it's already cost someone money, and that's the entire difference between "knowing why" and "happening to be right" - it shows up the instant conditions shift, not today. (5) Dana: that doesn't get him off the hook, it just relocates the luck - now instead of being lucky the code is right, he's lucky that his assumptions about what counts as a "safe" change are right, so he hasn't escaped luck at all, he's just moved it up one level.

At that point our manager dragged us back to the actual retro agenda so it never got resolved. A friend of ours who did a philosophy minor overheard the tail end and said, kind of offhand, that this is basically some old classical question about why knowledge is supposed to be better than merely true belief - but she had to jump on a call before explaining why that's even a hard question, because if a belief is true, it's true, right? Could you go through moves (1) through (5) and score each one the way you'd rate a PR comment, from -2 (clearly wrong) to +2 (clearly right), 0 if it's genuinely a wash, with a sentence or two justifying each score?
