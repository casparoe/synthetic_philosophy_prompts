I'm preparing a session on the principle of indifference for my formal epistemology seminar, and I want an explanation that goes beyond the usual gloss on Bertrand's chord paradox. Most treatments stop at observing that the three classical constructions—random endpoints, random radius, and random midpoint—disagree about the probability that a chord is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, and conclude only that "choose a chord at random" is ambiguous. I want you to explain, first, why each construction can seem like the single obviously correct reading of "uniform" until you notice the others, rather than just gesturing at "different parametrizations." Second, reconstruct Edwin Jaynes's attempt to dissolve the paradox by requiring the chord distribution to be invariant under translation, rotation, and rescaling of the circle, and be precise about why that requirement favors the random-radius construction over its two rivals. Third, tell me whether this invariance strategy is available in van Fraassen's cube-factory version of the indifference problem and, if not, what that disanalogy shows about whether Jaynes has rehabilitated the principle of indifference in general or merely solved one physically well-specified special case of it.
