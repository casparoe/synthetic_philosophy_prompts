I'm a junior in high school, and for an independent project in my philosophy elective I've been working through Pascal's Pensées in W. F. Trotter's translation. I've gotten stuck on fragment 294 (Brunschvicg numbering), from the section his editors titled "Justice and the Reason of Effects." Here is the part I can't quite work out:

"The result of this confusion is that one affirms the essence of justice to be the authority of the legislator; another, the interest of the sovereign; another, present custom, and this is the most sure. Nothing, according to reason alone, is just in itself; all changes with time. Custom creates the whole of equity, for the simple reason that it is accepted. It is the mystical foundation of its authority; whoever carries it back to first principles destroys it. Nothing is so faulty as those laws which correct faults. He who obeys them because they are just, obeys a justice which is imaginary, and not the essence of law; it is quite self-contained, it is law and nothing more. ... We must not see the fact of usurpation; law was once introduced without reason, and has become reasonable. We must make it regarded as authoritative, eternal, and conceal its origin, if we do not wish that it should soon come to an end."

Could you help me work through three things here? First, what does Pascal mean by calling custom the "mystical foundation" of authority, and why does he think tracing a law back to its "first principles" would destroy that authority rather than confirm it? Second, is he presenting the concealment of a law's origins as a regrettable trick that societies end up playing on themselves, or as sound advice for anyone who genuinely wants to govern well? Third, how does this fragment connect to his more famous remark elsewhere in the Pensées that justice without force is powerless while force without justice is tyrannical — is custom functioning here as a kind of disguised force, or is Pascal making a somewhat different point?
