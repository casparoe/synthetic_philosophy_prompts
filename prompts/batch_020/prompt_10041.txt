As a political science PhD student working on deterrence theory, I've been reading Kavka's toxin puzzle alongside David Gauthier's claim that a rational agent can dispose themselves to carry out a costly retaliatory action even once doing so would serve no further purpose -- the background case for both of them is a government's threat of nuclear retaliation, where following through after an attack has already happened no longer deters anything. But this looks like exactly the kind of problem the game-theoretic literature on credible commitment already handles: reputational stakes, repeated interaction, and delegation to actors with different incentives are all standard ways that self-interested parties bind themselves to costly follow-through, and they're routinely invoked to explain why real deterrence threats are credible in the first place. Given that these mechanisms are well understood, why do philosophers still treat the toxin puzzle as posing a live, unresolved problem, rather than as just a needlessly exotic restatement of a commitment problem that game theory already knows how to solve?
