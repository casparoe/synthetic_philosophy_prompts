Here's roughly how it went (reconstructing from memory so the wording's off but the moves are right):

(1) Mara opened with the classic "chicken claw and shovel" case -- the patient's left hemisphere sees a chicken claw and picks a chicken with the right hand, while his right hemisphere sees a snow scene and picks a shovel with the left hand, and then the talking half of his brain, which has no idea about the snow, confidently invents a reason for the shovel pick anyway. She said this proves there are literally two conscious minds in there, because the "speaking self" doesn't know why its own body is doing something and yet acts like it does -- which only makes sense if there's a seperate agent in there with its own reasons the first one can't access.

(4) Colin then brought up something I didn't know about: apparently there's a 2017 study (I think out of Amsterdam?) that retested split-brain patients and found they could actually respond correctly to stuff shown on either side of their visual field, using either hand or out loud, which isn't supposed to be possible on the classic story. He said this suggests way more integration survives than the old experiments implied, and that the "two totally separate perceivers" picture might just be wrong.

(5) Mara said she'd read about that one too, and that other researchers hit back at it by arguing the result could just be "cross-cueing" -- the hemispheres tipping each other off through outside signals like posture or facial expression rather than any genuinely shared awareness -- so it doesn't show unified consciousness at all, just two still-separate minds getting good at signaling each other indirectly.

(6) Colin's comeback was that if cross-cueing is a live enough worry to explain away the new evidence, it should cast just as much doubt on the classic disunity experiments, since those also depend on inferring what's happening inside the patient from behavior alone -- so wheeling it out to rescue the two-minds view, but not to question the confabulation studies, looks like picking your skepticism to fit your conclusion.
