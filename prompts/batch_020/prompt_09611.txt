Over the past few months, I have been working through some free game theory materials online, partly to see whether I could find a genuinely optimal way to play a simplified card game that a few of us have turned into a standing Thursday night ritual, rather than just relying on instinct. The main technique I picked up is iterated elimination of dominated strategies: you look at the payoff table, discard any option that is always at least as bad as some other option regardless of what your opponent does, and keep repeating the process until nothing further can be discarded. I built a payoff table for a stripped-down, three-option version of our game and confirmed, working through it by hand, that neither of my two more aggressive options ever strictly beats my cautious middle option: each aggressive option does great against one of my opponent's two possible replies and poorly against the other, while the cautious option is steady, doing respectably against either reply without ever being the best or the worst response available. Since neither aggressive option beats the cautious one in both columns, and the two aggressive options plainly do not dominate each other either, it seemed clear that nothing in my three-strategy table could be eliminated, so I planned on treating all three as live candidates.

When I showed the table to a friend who took a couple of economics electives in college, he told me this was not quite right, and that my cautious middle option can still be discarded, because it is dominated by a mixed strategy built out of my two aggressive options, even though neither of those options dominates it on its own. I am having trouble seeing how that is supposed to work in a game we only ever play once per sitting: since I am not actually permitted to split my move between two options on a single turn, it seems odd to say I am "beaten" by some blend I never literally play, much as it would be strange to say a boxer lost a fight to the average of two other boxers he never once stepped into the ring with. Is my friend simply invoking a mathematical technicality that carries no real weight in a genuine one-shot game, or is he pointing at something real that should actually change which of my three strategies I keep on the table?
