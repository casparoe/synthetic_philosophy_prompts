One of my longest-serving colleagues, Diane, is retiring this spring after thirty-six years in the department, and I have been asked to give the toast at her farewell dinner. Diane has spent her entire career as our resident neopragmatist: her work builds directly on Rorty's antirepresentationalism, and she has never once, in my hearing, allowed a colleague or student to get away with saying that some theory "gets it right" about how the mind or the world really is. There is an added wrinkle: I am, and have been for those same thirty-six years, the correspondence theorist down the hall, and we have argued about exactly this point at every faculty colloquium since we were hired in the same year. The philosophers in the room will therefore be listening closely to see whether my toast quietly smuggles in the very "mirroring" picture of knowledge she has spent her career dismantling. Roughly half the guests are colleagues and former students who will catch every such slip; the other half are her children, her book club, and retired friends from outside the university who have never encountered the word "antirepresentationalist" and do not need to.

The trouble is that the ordinary grammar of a toast is deeply representationalist: one wants to say she "saw into the true nature" of the mind, that her papers "uncovered" or "revealed" what was there all along, that she always got things right. Using any of that sincerely, in front of her, would undercut the whole point of the evening. So I would like your help writing a toast of about five or six minutes that praises her career in terms she could actually endorse -- redescription rather than discovery, usefulness rather than correspondence, solidarity with a community of inquirers rather than objectivity about how things stand independent of us, keeping a conversation going rather than settling it once and for all -- while still working as a toast: warm, a little funny, and legible to people who have never opened Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature.

If there is room for it, I would love one joke built on the double sense of "ironist": Rorty's technical term for someone who never stops doubting her own final vocabulary, and the ordinary English word for someone who is drily funny, which Diane also happens to be. And please do not let me close with anything like "here's to always seeing things as they truly are" -- after thirty-six years of this argument, she would notice, and she would be right to call me on it.
