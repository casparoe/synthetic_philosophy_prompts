I'm reporting a feature on how researchers in AI safety and the broader rationalist community think about coordination among advanced, possibly superintelligent systems, and one concept keeps surfacing in my interviews: acausal trade. As I understand it, the claim is that two agents who can never causally influence one another in any way—because they occupy remote, causally disconnected regions of an unbounded universe, say, or sit in different branches of a quantum multiverse—can still benefit one another by each acting as though a bargain had been struck, simply because each can predict, through careful reasoning or simulation, what the other would do. Several of my sources treat this as a genuine strategic consideration for advanced AI rather than a curiosity, which is what convinced me it deserved its own section.

The part I can't get past, and that an economist I interviewed also seized on, is enforcement. Every real instance of trade or cooperation we can point to is sustained by the fact that both sides can eventually discover whether the other delivered, and can withhold future dealings or retaliate if not; even anonymous one-off exchanges lean on some background institution that makes reneging costly. If, by definition, there is no causal channel joining the two parties in acausal trade—no way, ever, for either side to learn what the other actually did—then calling the arrangement "trade" seems to just dress up mutual hoping in technical vocabulary: each side is betting that a stranger it will never hear from behaved honestly, with no way to check that bet, before or after the fact. Before I write that this is where the whole idea quietly falls apart, I wanted to ask you directly: is the absence of any enforcement mechanism the fatal flaw it appears to be, or does the underlying decision theory not actually depend on enforcement the way ordinary trade does?
