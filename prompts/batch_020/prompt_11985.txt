I'm a backend engineer at a mid-size tech company, and a few months back I got talked into joining a small employee-elected panel that has to sign off before HR and Legal can actually finalize any for-cause termination -- basically a peer sanity check so a manager cant just fire someone they dont like. I figured this would mostly be rubber-stamping obvious stuff, but my first real case is way messier than the onboarding deck made it sound, and I don't have any philosophy background to fall back on here, just a nagging feeling that both sides of our panel are being too quick to call this an easy case.

Here's the situation: one of our trust-and-safety engineers, Dana, came across internal contract docs (a shared-drive permission was broader than it should've been) showing that a feature pitched to her team as "spam and bot detection" for a foreign-government client was actually meant to de-anonymize accounts flagged for political content. She raised it with her manager, then the ethics hotline, then a skip-level, over about two months, and kept getting told the deal was already signed and there was nothing left to discuss. So she posted a long message in our engineering-wide Slack channel laying out exactly which lines of our published values statement the project crossed, said she personally wouldn't write the integration, offered to hand her piece off to anyone willing to take it, and said flat out that she knew this could get her fired and wasn't hiding what she'd done. A few people screenshotted it and passed it into channels way outside engineering, including one the deal team was in, so HR's termination now cites both insubordination and a confidentiality violation, since the deal was technically still pre-signature. In her written appeal, Dana doesn't contest a single fact -- she just calls the whole thing "civil disobedience" and says she's only appealing the punishment, not the story. I looked that phrase up and got a bunch of stuff about Thoreau and MLK that doesn't obviously map onto "declined to write one integration and posted about it on Slack," so now I trust my own read on this even less.

The panel's split down the middle and I have to bring a recommendation memo to Tuesday's meeting. Half of us think this is ordinary rule-breaking with a flattering label stuck on it -- the company isn't a government, nobody signed up for any civic duty toward it, and unlike a citizen stuck with their state, Dana could've just quit and said whatever she wanted on her way out. The other half thinks she hit basically every mark you'd want from someone trying to dissent responsibly -- exhausted the internal channels first, was completely open about what she was doing and why, hurt no one, and isn't even contesting the consequences -- and that firing her anyway makes the "we welcome employees who raise concerns" line in our handbook read like a joke. Can you draft the memo I should actually bring? I need an actual recommendation -- uphold the termination, overturn it, or downgrade it to something like a formal warning -- not a "both sides have a point" shrug, and whichever way it comes down, it has to seriously engage with the strongest version of the argument on the other side, not just wave at it. If you think there's a general rule worth writing down for the next person who tries something like this, throw that in too.
