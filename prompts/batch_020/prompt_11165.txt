This semester I've been taking two graduate seminars that, quite unexpectedly, keep bleeding into each other. One is on early modern rationalism, where we just spent several weeks on Part II of Spinoza's Ethics: the claim that mind and body are strictly one and the same thing, conceived now under the attribute of Extension and now under the attribute of Thought; that no mode of one attribute can ever cause a mode of the other; and, in Spinoza's own formulation, that "the order and connection of ideas is the same as the order and connection of things." The other is a philosophy of mind seminar currently working through Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument — the claim that if the physical domain is causally closed and mental properties are merely supervenient on, rather than identical to, their physical bases, then whenever a mental event appears to cause a physical effect, the actual causal work is already being done by the physical realizer, leaving the mental property nothing left to contribute; hence non-reductive physicalism collapses into either reduction or epiphenomenalism. My professor mentioned, almost as an aside, that Spinoza "saw a version of this whole mess coming three centuries early," and I haven't been able to let go of the comparison since.

Before I commit to it as a term-paper thesis, I'd like to use you as a sounding board. Taking Spinoza's actual commitments seriously — substance monism, the attributes as distinct but causally and conceptually sealed-off ways of expressing the one substance, and the parallelism doctrine that follows from this — how do you think he would respond if he were somehow briefed on Kim's argument and the specific dilemma it poses to non-reductive physicalists? Would his parallelism amount, in Kim's own terms, to denying the causal closure of the physical, denying the exclusion principle itself, denying that mental and physical properties are ever the kind of thing that could be distinct causal competitors in the first place, or would it simply fail to engage the modern debate because it isn't a physicalist view at all? Please commit to one specific reconstruction of his likely response rather than cataloguing all the possibilities, and be explicit about which of his own doctrines is doing the real argumentative work.
