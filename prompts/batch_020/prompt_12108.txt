I'm prepping my grad seminar on the metaphysics of causation for next week, and we've finally reached causation by omission, which always turns into a minor shouting match by the end of the session. The issue that gets everyone worked up is what's sometimes called the "profligacy" (or "promiscuity") problem: once you let a Lewis-style counterfactual analysis say that my not watering your ficus while you're on vacation is a cause of its death, you seem stuck saying that some total stranger on the other side of the world, who's never heard of you or your ficus, is equally a cause of its death, since if they had watered it, it wouldn't have died either. But if you rule omissions out as causes entirely, you lose a bunch of judgments people really don't want to give up, like the on-duty lifeguard who doesn't jump in, or the ship's officer who forgets to close a bulkhead door before a collision.

I've written up seven stock-ish cases along these lines that I want to throw at my students cold next week, and before I do that I'd like your gut reactions as another data point, because me and my TA already disagree about two of these and its bugging me. For each case, judge how strong the case is that the described omission is a genuine cause of the outcome, as opposed to something we only loosely call a "cause" for pragmatic, moral, or normative reasons. Sort all seven into tiers S/A/B/C/D, where S = "counts as a cause on basically any serious theory of causation" and D = "clearly not a cause, this is just the profligacy problem wearing a trenchcoat."

1. You agree to water a friend's ficus while she's out of town for a week; you forget, and it dies.
2. Someone on the other side of the world, who's never heard of your friend or her ficus, also doesn't water it that week.
3. A lifeguard on duty is looking the other way and doesn't notice a swimmer drowning nearby.
4. An off-duty stranger sitting on the same beach also doesn't notice, and also doesn't swim out to help.
5. A ship's officer forgets to close a watertight door before a collision, and the ship sinks as a result.
6. A bodyguard whose entire job is to watch the crowd for threats is checking his phone instead, doesn't spot the gunman stepping out of the crowd, and doesn't tackle him before he gets a shot off.
7. A farmer's crop fails after a growing season with much less rain than usual; nobody "failed" to make it rain.

Please give me the answer as a JSON object with keys "S" through "D", each mapping to a list of the relevant case numbers, and then add two or three sentences on whatever general principle seems to be doing the most work in your sorting -- normative or role-based expectations, statistical normalcy, physical connectedness, or something else entirely.
