For the past couple of weeks I've been in a stubborn but perfectly friendly disagreement with a colleague about whether we should migrate our job queue to a new event-driven design before the holiday code freeze. We built the original service together three years ago, we're both looking at the same design document and the same load-test results, and over several long conversations we've each walked the other through our complete reasoning more than once, including the specific probability each of us would assign to the claim that the migration would have prevented the outage we had back in October. I keep arriving at something like 80 percent, he keeps arriving at something like 30 percent, and neither number has moved despite all the back-and-forth.

I mentioned this standoff to a friend outside of work, and she made a claim that has been bothering me ever since: that if two people are both genuinely honest and rational, and have truly exchanged all their evidence and reasoning with one another, they should not be able to end up holding different numbers like this at all. On her view, persistent disagreement of this kind is really a sign that someone is deceiving themselves, or not being fully candid with the other person, rather than a normal feature of reasonable disagreement between smart people. That struck me as a strong claim, since in ten years of writing software I have watched plenty of sharp, good-faith engineers pore over identical data and never fully converge, without it ever seeming like proof that one of them was being irrational or dishonest. Is there something to my friend's claim? If there's a rigorous version of it, what exactly would it require of two people for it to actually apply, and if it holds under those conditions, what does that say about why ordinary disagreements between competent, honest colleagues seem to survive so much shared evidence in practice?
