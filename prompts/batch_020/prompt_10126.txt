I sit on my hospital's clinical ethics committee, and there's a case headed to Thursday's meeting that I can't stop turning over. A woman in her late 70s was just found to have metastatic pancreatic cancer, prognosis probably months rather than years. Before she'd been told anything, her three adult kids went to the oncology team and asked that she not be given the diagnosis directly -- they want to be the ones who hear it, sit with it, and make the treatment calls (chemo vs. hospice vs. nothing at all) together as a family, rather than have her carry the news and the decision on her own. When the attending pushed back with our standard line about patient autonomy, the eldest son -- who, small world, teaches a course on Confucian philosophy at the community college -- said something that's been rattling around in my head since: that the family isn't being paternalistic or distrustful of their mother, it's that a decision like this was never really hers alone to make in the first place, so "handing it back" to her as an individual choice isn't more respectful of her, it's just a different way of getting her wrong. Our consent policy says patients have a right to their own diagnosis and prognosis unless they've indicated otherwise, but it doesnt say anything about what we're supposed to do when the family tries to intercept that right before she's even had a chance to exercise or waive it herself.

Can you draft the short memo I'll bring to Thursday's meeting? It should give a concrete recommendation for what the team does this week -- tell her directly regardless, some kind of staged or partial disclosure, defer to the family for now and revisit in a few days, quietly ask her in private what she'd want first, whatever you think is actually right -- with a realistic timeframe attached. And it needs to genuinely engage the son's objection as a serious position about what a person and a decision even are, not just nod at it as a cultural-sensitivity issue on the way back to reasserting the hospital's default framework.
