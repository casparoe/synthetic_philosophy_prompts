For the past several months I've been working nights at a regional fulfillment warehouse to bridge the gap between finishing my degree and finding something in my field, and I've been surprised by how often my mind drifts back to the Marx I read in a single undergraduate seminar nearly a decade ago. A handheld scanner routes me between shelves, logs every "idle" second, and times my bathroom breaks against a productivity target I never get to see in full; meanwhile, I'm well aware that the gap between what my labor generates for the company and what shows up in my paycheck is, by design, considerable. On my days off I've been rereading Capital and the 1844 Manuscripts, half-expecting to simply feel vindicated in my old undergraduate outrage, but instead I've fallen down a rabbit hole of secondary literature that complicates the picture. I learned, for instance, that the labor theory of value behind the classical exploitation charge has been considered technically shaky since at least Böhm-Bawerk's 1896 critique and the resulting "transformation problem" — shaky enough that analytical Marxists like G.A. Cohen and John Roemer tried to rebuild the case for exploitation on entirely different foundations. Separately, I ran into a long-running scholarly dispute, associated especially with Allen Wood on one side and Ziyad Husami on the other, over whether Marx himself even regarded capitalism as unjust, as opposed to merely alienating, dehumanizing, and historically doomed.

I'd like an essay that helps me actually think this through, rather than one that surveys the debate evenhandedly and shrugs. Specifically: does the charge that capitalist wage labor is exploitative survive the well-known technical and philosophical troubles with the labor theory of value, or does the more defensible core of Marx's critique of capitalism turn out to be the alienation diagnosis instead, with "exploitation" doing more rhetorical than argumentative work — or do the two ultimately stand or fall together? Please reconstruct the strongest version of each side of the case; engage seriously with how Cohen's and Roemer's non-value-theoretic reformulations of exploitation bear on the question, and with how the Wood–Husami dispute over whether Marx's own framework supports calling capitalism unjust bears on whether "exploitation" can do any real normative work for him; and then commit to and defend one conclusion rather than settling for a non-committal split verdict.
