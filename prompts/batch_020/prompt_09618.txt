For a feature on the ongoing reckoning in experimental psychology and biomedicine, I've spent the last few weeks interviewing statisticians about what actually caused the replication crisis, and I keep bumping into an argument that seems more interesting than the headline version of the story. The popular narrative pins the blame squarely on null-hypothesis significance testing: a bright-line p < .05 threshold, combined with publish-or-perish incentives, all but guarantees p-hacking, and the fix is to migrate toward Bayesian methods, Bayes factors, or estimation-based reporting. Yet several Bayesian statisticians I've spoken with are oddly reluctant to claim their own toolkit as the cure. They point to something like Andrew Gelman and Eric Loken's "garden of forking paths" argument: because researchers face countless defensible choices -- how to code variables, which outliers to exclude, which interactions to model, when to stop collecting data -- a study can produce an impressive result without anyone ever explicitly fishing for significance. That same flexibility, they argue, can just as easily generate an unreplicable Bayesian posterior, via priors, models, or comparisons chosen only after peeking at the data.

I'd like an essay that takes this narrower dispute seriously, rather than rehashing the generic Bayesian-versus-frequentist debate. First, lay out the strongest case that the replication crisis is at least partly a problem of statistical paradigm: that something specific about how p-values are computed, taught, and routinely misread -- as evidence against the null, or even as the probability that the null is true -- deserves real blame, and that a shift to Bayesian reporting would meaningfully reduce it. Then lay out the strongest "paradigm-neutral" case: that researcher degrees of freedom, publication bias, underpowered studies, and weak theory would produce a crisis under any inferential framework, Bayesian included, so that swapping test statistics without reforming preregistration, incentives, and study design accomplishes little. Don't split the difference: tell me which side has the better of the argument, and say concretely what, if anything, about statistical practice itself -- as distinct from the incentives surrounding it -- ought to change.
