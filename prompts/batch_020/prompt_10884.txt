For my IB Philosophy Extended Essay, I have been researching the debate over what philosophers call moral testimony: whether it is ever fully appropriate to adopt a moral belief, and act on it, simply because someone you trust asserts it, without grasping the reasons behind it yourself. Most people sense an asymmetry here — deferring to a doctor's testimony about a diagnosis seems perfectly fine, but deferring to someone else's say-so about whether an act is right or wrong feels deficient, even when the testifier is entirely reliable. Some philosophers, such as Alison Hills, argue this is because virtuous action requires genuine moral understanding rather than merely a true belief, while others deny there is any principled asymmetry at all. I would like to know what Aristotle's own ethical theory actually commits him to on this question. Given his claim that virtuous action requires acting knowingly, choosing the act for its own sake, and acting from a settled character, would he side with the skeptics about moral testimony? Or does his account of moral habituation, where the young learn virtue partly by trusting and imitating those who are already virtuous, leave room for testimony to play a legitimate role after all? Please reconstruct and defend the verdict that his own theory most plausibly supports.
