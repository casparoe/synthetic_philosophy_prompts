A friend and I spent the better part of an evening arguing about whether Tolstoy would have accepted twentieth-century abstract painting as art at all, judged strictly by the standards he lays out in What Is Art? At issue, specifically, was whether Kandinsky's early abstractions, or the later work of Rothko and Pollock, count as genuine art or as one more instance of the elitist "counterfeit" art he spent much of that book attacking. My friend argued that because such paintings can still transmit a single, sharply individuated feeling with total sincerity, they would satisfy Tolstoy's "infection" criterion even without any representational content. I argued that his insistence on clarity of expression, and on art's power to unite viewers across class and education around one shared, easily communicable feeling, would place most of this work alongside the Wagnerian opera and French Symbolist poetry he already scorned as sincere-seeming yet exclusionary. Neither of us persuaded the other. Working strictly from the criteria Tolstoy actually lays out — the individuality, clarity, and sincerity of the feeling conveyed, plus his further moral test of whether a work tends to unite people or set them apart — how do you think he would have judged abstraction as a movement, and whose reading of his theory seems closer to the mark, mine or my friend's?
