Four months ago I received a pacemaker after an arrhythmia caused me to faint twice in one week, and the surgery has left me stuck on a metaphysical worry that feels almost embarrassing beside the medical one. On Peter van Inwagen's organicist answer to the special composition question, composition occurs only among things caught up in a single life, so that, strictly speaking, there are no chairs and no pacemakers, only particles arranged chair-wise or pacemaker-wise, and the only genuine composites are organisms. Organicists typically illustrate this with digestion, where food becomes part of you only gradually, as it is metabolized, not the instant you swallow it; my pacemaker's leads, by contrast, are threaded directly into my heart muscle, and its circuitry now performs timing work my sinoatrial node used to do unassisted. Argue for a specific, organicist-consistent verdict: are the particles arranged pacemaker-wise inside me caught up in my life, making the device a genuine part of the composite object that is me, or does van Inwagen's criterion instead imply that I am an organism merely attached to a permanent tool? Whichever verdict you defend, engage directly with the worry, central to David Lewis's argument from vagueness against restricted composition, that any degree-based notion of being "caught up in a life" smuggles back in exactly the vagueness that organicism's rejection of universalism and nihilism was meant to avoid.
