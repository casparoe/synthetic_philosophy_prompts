Three weeks ago my closest friend from graduate school, Nora, died of a sudden brain aneurysm at twenty-six — an age she would have been the first to point out was also Frank Ramsey's when he died in 1930, a few years into a fellowship at King's College and already, by most accounts, one of the most brilliant minds in Cambridge. Nora was finishing her own dissertation on the foundations of subjective probability, but the piece of Ramsey she actually loved, the one she'd bring up unprompted over dinner, was a passage from near the end of his life in which he says his picture of the world is drawn in perspective rather than to scale, so that the stars shrink down to something about the size of a coin, and in which he insists he feels no humility at all before the vastness of the universe, in space or in time, because mere size and duration aren't the kind of thing that could outweigh a mind capable of thinking and loving. She used to say it was the only piece of philosophy that had ever actually changed how a bad day felt to her, rather than just being fun to argue about.

I've been asked to speak for four or five minutes at her memorial this Saturday. The first two rows will be her parents and younger brother, who have never read a word of Ramsey and are in no state to sit through a gloss on subjective probability; further back will be her adviser and most of her reading group, who will notice immediately if I get the argument wrong or use it as decoration. Nora was also, for years, completely explicit — to her parents directly, more than once — that she thought stories about souls watching over people, or everything happening for a reason, were simply false, that grief would not make them retroactively true, and that she did not want them said over her now.

Here is what I can't resolve on my own. Ramsey's passage is doing a very specific piece of philosophical work: defending the significance of ordinary, human-scale goods against the charge that a sufficiently large, old universe makes them trivial. It was never built to answer the question this particular room actually has, which is not whether the thinking and loving Nora did counted for anything, but what to make of her losing forty or fifty more years in which to do more of it. Please write the eulogy so that it sits honestly inside that gap: recognizable as Ramsey to the people who will check, plain and genuinely comforting to the people who won't, and careful not to smuggle in a bigger conclusion than his actual argument can support.
