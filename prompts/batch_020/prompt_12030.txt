Okay so I'm about a third of the way into a novel where the protagonist is a metaphysics postdoc quietly falling out of love with her subfield, and to get the department politics (and the actual arguing) right, I've been tagging along to dinners with my friend Mara, who's still in the field, and her old officemate Devon, who's adjuncting somewhere now and seems permanently annoyed about it. Last week the two of them got into a real argument over dessert about whether "grounding" is even a real thing philosophers need to posit, and I just sat there half-transcribing on my phone because I knew it was going in the book — except now I actually want to know who was right, not just how it sounded on the page.

Here's the exchange, as close as I can reconstruct it. (1) Devon opened by saying the whole grounding literature is a waste of everyone's time: when a metaphysician says the mental is "grounded in" the physical, that's just a fancy way of saying the mental facts hold because of the physical ones, and we already had that covered — physical facts necessitate the mental ones (supervenience), plus ordinary explanatory "because" — so theres no need to invent some further hyperintensional relation called Grounding on top. (2) Mara said supervenience can't be the whole story because it can't fix direction: Socrates and his singleton {Socrates} necessitate each other, neither can exist without the other, but obviously the singleton depends on him and not the reverse, and that asymmetric "in virtue of" fact is exactly what grounding, and only grounding, is suppose to capture. (3) Devon conceded the direction point but said fine, keep "because" — just don't treat it as a relation between facts, treat it as a plain connective like "and" or "if," so the asymmetry comes for free without inventing new entities to be its relata. (4) Mara replied that a bare connective can't be quantified into, so you lose the ability to state the big general theses that started this whole research program — you can't even write down physicalism (every mental fact is grounded in some physical fact) without something relation-shaped to quantify over, so dropping the relation drops the very thesis grounding was invented to express. (5) Devon's retreat was that fine, you need something to quantify over, but that doesn't get you one unified, capital-G Grounding relation — you could just have a pile of unrelated small-g dependencies (causal, mereological, functional, whatever) each doing its own local job, and then define "fundamental" the way Sider does, as whatever shows up in the complete roster of perfectly structural, joint-carving predicates, no grounding relation required. (6) Mara's closing line was that this just relocates the bump in the rug: now "structural" is quietly doing all the work "ungrounded" used to do, and Devon still owes an account of why physical predicates get to be the structural ones and mental predicates don't that isn't just restating that the physical is fundamental.

Can you referee this move by move rather than just declaring an overall winner?
