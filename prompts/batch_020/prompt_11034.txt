Three weeks ago, a colleague I've worked beside for eleven years, with a comparable case volume and no track record of outperforming me on our practice's peer-review audits, looked at a mammogram I had just called BI-RADS 3 ("probably benign, short-interval follow-up") and, from the identical films, called it a BI-RADS 4 ("suspicious, biopsy recommended"). Neither of us could point to anything the other had overlooked; we simply weighted the same architectural distortion differently. The case sent me back to the peer-disagreement literature I hadn't touched since a graduate seminar, and one worry from it won't leave me alone. Adam Elga's Equal Weight View says that on learning of my colleague's read, I should move my credence roughly to the midpoint between "probably benign" and "suspicious." Please argue that the view is self-undermining rather than merely impractical: working epistemologists are themselves in exactly the kind of persistent, symmetric dispute over the Equal Weight View that the view says should trigger conciliation, so its own defenders seem obligated, by their own principle, to hold it with substantially reduced confidence. In making the argument, address whether a more moderate proposal like Kelly's, on which a peer's disagreement counts as further evidence to be weighed rather than an automatic trigger for equal-weighting, actually escapes this bind or merely relocates it one level up.
