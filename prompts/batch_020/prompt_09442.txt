I'm assembling a small collection of short fiction in which each story takes its premise from some specific, genuine puzzle in the philosophy of mind, rather than from the broader thought experiments that have already been dramatized dozens of times over. The entry currently giving me trouble concerns the octopus. I watched one in a public aquarium spend several minutes working open a childproof jar, and what struck me was that its arms didn't look coordinated by some single watcher lodged inside the animal so much as they looked like four or five separate problem-solvers, each trying a slightly different approach and occasionally getting in one another's way.

That impression turns out to have real neurological backing. Roughly two-thirds of an octopus's half-billion neurons sit in its arms rather than in its central brain, and a severed arm can go on reaching, gripping, and curling its suckers around objects for hours with no input from the brain at all. What interests me isn't quite the familiar Nagel puzzle of what it is like to be a creature very unlike us -- that's the bat essay, and I'd rather not retread it. It's closer to a question Nagel raised a few years earlier, in his much less-discussed paper on split-brain patients: whether the very idea of a single subject "having" an experience is even well-defined in a case like this, or whether whatever experience is present might be smeared across several loosely coordinated centers that never quite add up to one "I."

Could you write me a short story, a few pages long, that takes this specific problem as its engine rather than its garnish? I'd like the actual biology -- the semi-autonomous arms, the live possibility of fragmentary or non-unified experience -- to do real work in the plot or in the telling, not just supply exotic atmosphere for a story that could just as easily have been about a dolphin or an AI. You might even let the form carry some of the argument: interleaved perspectives, a narrator unsure how many of "her" there are, that sort of thing -- though a more conventional structure that still takes the biology seriously would satisfy me just as well. Setting, characters, and ending are otherwise entirely yours; I'd rather be surprised by where you take it than receive back something I could have written myself.
