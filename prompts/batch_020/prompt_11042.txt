I clerk for a justice on my state's supreme court, and we've drawn a case of first impression: a child born with a severe, degenerative genetic disorder is suing, through a guardian ad litem, the clinician whose negligent prenatal testing cost her parents the chance to terminate the pregnancy. Unlike an ordinary "wrongful birth" suit for the parents' own damages, this "wrongful life" claim is brought on the child's own behalf and alleges that she herself was harmed by being brought into existence in this condition. The court below rejected the claim on the traditional ground that damages require comparing her actual life to some baseline, and no baseline is available, since non-existence has no one for whom it could be better or worse. Her brief answers this with Benatar's asymmetry: the presence of suffering is bad for the sufferer, while the absence of well-being in someone who never exists is bad for no one, so her life can be a genuine harm without any coherent comparison class. I've only ever seen this kind of argument dissected on rationalist blogs, never relied on as a controlling theory of harm, and my justice now wants a bench memo recommending a disposition before oral argument next month. Please draft it, with a clear recommendation, the theory of harm it would commit the court to, and a fair account of why a court might still decline to recognize the claim.
