I've spent years reading the EA Forum and LessWrong as a lurker, mostly during the recurring cause-prioritization debates, and I recently ended up on the other side of one of those debates for real. I sit on the three-person grants committee of a small donor-advised fund set up by alumni of my university's effective altruism group; a bequest left over after the group's main fundraising campaign wound down gave us $180,000 in undesignated money, and the founding memo just says it should go to "whatever does the most good." We are down to two finalists. The first is the Against Malaria Foundation, backed by GiveWell's well-documented cost-per-life-saved figures. The second is a two-year fellowship that trains early-career researchers in pathogen surveillance and biosecurity policy; the proposal leans explicitly on Greaves and MacAskill's case for strong longtermism, arguing that because the far future contains vastly more potential people than the present, our options should be ranked mainly by their effects on long-run outcomes like existential risk, even though no particular beneficiary of the fellowship can be named in advance.

The committee is deadlocked in a way I don't think a vote will resolve honestly. One member finds the expected-value case for the fellowship close to decisive and thinks any hesitation is just scope insensitivity dressed up as prudence. A second has been reading about the cluelessness objection and argues we have no non-arbitrary way to tell whether the fellowship's causal chain to reduced extinction risk is positive, negative, or a wash, and suspects the whole case is a Pascal's-mugging in respectable clothing -- a tiny, unverifiable probability multiplied by an astronomical payoff, arranged so that it swamps any comparison with a well-evidenced alternative. A third wants to just split the money, and is being told that splitting is only a principled response if the two options are genuinely incommensurable rather than merely disputed under a shared standard. None of us has training in moral philosophy beyond what we've picked up from years of online reading, and I'd rather bring the committee a memo that has actually been stress-tested than sit through another round of the same three of us restating our priors. Could you draft the short written recommendation I'll circulate before we vote next week? I'd like it to recommend a single allocation rather than a split (unless you can defend a split as something more than conflict-avoidance), give reasoning the cluelessness-minded member could actually engage with point by point, and honestly acknowledge the strongest thing to be said for whichever option you don't recommend.
