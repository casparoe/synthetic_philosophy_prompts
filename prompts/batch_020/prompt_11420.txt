I am several chapters into a novel set in London and Hanover across 1715 and 1716, structured around the Leibniz-Clarke correspondence: Samuel Clarke writing as Newton's closely supervised mouthpiece, a fictional secretary of Leibniz's carrying drafts across the Channel, and the two principals never once addressing one another directly. I want the philosophical spine of these letters to be right, not just the costumes, so I have been working back from Westfall's biography into the primary material -- the General Scholium that Newton appended to the Principia's second edition in 1713, with its insistence that a being lacking dominion over the world is not God but mere Fate and Nature, and then the correspondence itself, where Leibniz ridicules the notion that God's cosmic machine needs occasional winding and repair, and Clarke answers that a universe requiring no further attention from its maker would be a universe that had, for all purposes, dispensed with Him. The longer I sit with these letters, the less certain I am whether this theology was actually load-bearing inside Newton's physics, or whether it was an independent commitment -- rooted in Newton's private rejection of the Trinity and in Clarke's need to distance Newton's God from the necessitarian tendencies he was busy attributing to Leibniz's (and, more provocatively, Spinoza's) rationalism -- that simply rode alongside the Principia without the Principia's mathematics ever actually requiring it.

What I would like from you is an essay that resolves this rather than sitting on the fence. Set out the strongest case that Newton's celestial mechanics -- his account of absolute space, his treatment of gravity as real action at a distance, his admission that the planetary system's long-term stability was not something his equations alone could guarantee -- genuinely depended on an actively governing deity of the specific kind the General Scholium describes; then set out the strongest case that this theology was a devotional and polemical overlay, separable in principle from a physical theory already complete without it. Having weighed both, commit to one and defend it as your considered view. I would rather be given a wrong answer I can argue with in the book than an even-handed summary I can't put in anyone's mouth.
