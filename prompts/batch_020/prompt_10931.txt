Here is Theo's answer in full:

"Gödel calls a property 'positive' if it is the sort of attribute that makes whatever has it greater or more perfect, independent of the particular way the world happens to be arranged. He does not mean 'morally good' in a narrow sense so much as something like a pure perfection in Leibniz's sense. Using this idea, he lays down axioms saying, among other things, that for any property exactly one of it and its negation is positive, and that any property necessarily implied by a positive property is also positive. 'God-like' is then defined as the property of having every positive property. Since positive properties can never contradict one another (this follows from the axioms above), and since Gödel also takes being God-like itself to be a positive property, being God-like turns out to be a consistent property — that is, it's possible for something to have it.

From this it already follows that a God-like being is possible: there is no barrier to something having all and only the positive properties. To get to actual existence, Gödel introduces the idea of an 'essence': a property φ is an essence of x if x has φ and φ necessitates every other property x has. He argues that if x is God-like, then being God-like is x's essence, since a God-like being has every positive property necessarily (positive properties are positive as a matter of necessity, not accident), and every property it lacks is ruled out by the same token. He then defines 'necessary existence' as the property of having one's essence necessarily instantiated, and takes it as a further axiom that necessary existence is itself positive. Since a God-like being has every positive property, it has necessary existence, which means that in every possible world, something exemplifies God-likeness.

Strictly, what the previous paragraph gives us is that it's possible that a God-like being necessarily exists, not yet that a God-like being necessarily exists, full stop — there's a 'possibly' sitting outside a 'necessarily.' To peel off that outer 'possibly,' the argument needs the modal system S5. S5 is distinguished from weaker systems by including the axiom that whatever is necessary is also actually true (□p → p), and it's exactly this axiom that licenses us to infer, from 'possibly, necessarily a God-like being exists,' that a God-like being necessarily exists. In a logic that lacked this axiom the inference would be blocked, which is why the proof is often said to depend essentially on adopting S5 rather than some more modest modal logic.

The best-known problem for the argument is the 'modal collapse' objection, first raised by Alvin Plantinga: if positive properties are always necessarily positive, the same reasoning that gets God's properties to hold necessarily can be run on ordinary true propositions, with the result that every truth turns out to be a necessary truth and nothing is really contingent. That's an alarming conclusion, since it seems to erase any distinction between what is and what merely could be, and would mean not even God had any real choice in what to create. Some defenders of the argument, notably Anderson, have tried to rewrite the axioms defining essence and necessary existence so that necessary existence still follows but the objectionable collapse no longer does."
