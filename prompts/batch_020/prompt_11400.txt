Here's the question:

"The Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA) says you should reason as if you were a random sample from the observers in your 'reference class.' The Self-Indication Assumption (SIA) adds that hypotheses implying more observers should get a higher prior, in proportion to how many observers they predict, since there are more 'slots' for you to occupy.
(a) Using Bostrom's presumptuous philosopher scenario, explain which of the two principles runs into trouble, and why.
(b) Briefly explain how the Doomsday Argument is generated, and say which principle is doing the work.
(c) In 2-3 sentences, say which of the two principles you find more palatable, and why."

And here's the student's answer:

"SSA and SIA are both ways of spelling out how to reason from self-locating evidence. SSA says to treat yourself as though randomly picked from everyone in your reference class – so on its own, if I don't know whether I'm in a trillion-observer universe or a trillion-trillion-observer one, SSA doesn't push me either way; it only kicks in once I compare my own rank or properties against others in the class. SIA adds an extra move: since hypotheses with more observers give you more 'chances' to exist, SIA reweights the prior probability of the hypotheses themselves, in proportion to population, before any sampling even happens.

(a) The presumptuous philosopher case is generally taken to show that SSA proves too much. Physicists are about to run an expensive experiment to pick between two theories, one implying a far bigger universe (and many more observers) than the other. The presumptuous philosopher barges in and says don't bother: SSA already tells us to overwhelmingly favor the big-population theory, since we're far more likely to have been sampled into existence at all if there are vastly more observers on offer. Most people think this is an absurd way to settle a physics question, so something's gone wrong with SSA reasoning here.

(b) The Doomsday Argument comes from the same family, and it's a pure SSA move: if you treat your birth rank as a random sample from everyone who will ever live, a much bigger total population makes your own early birth rank a bigger coincidence, so your birth rank counts as evidence for 'doom relatively soon' over 'humanity lasts a long time.' No population-weighted priors involved, just sampling within a single hypothesis.

(c) I find SIA more palatable overall – it avoids the doomsday-style shift, and it doesn't run into the presumptuous philosopher either, since that one's really an artifact of self-sampling, not self-indication, reasoning. SSA also saddles you with the separate headache of picking a reference class, which never has a principled answer."
