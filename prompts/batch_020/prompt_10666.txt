Question: Bell's theorem is often summarized in popular accounts as showing that "nature is nonlocal." Explain (a) the locality condition invoked in the Clauser–Horne–Shimony–Holt (CHSH) derivation of the Bell inequality, (b) why this derivation does not depend on treating the hidden variable as deterministic, and (c) why the experimentally confirmed violation of the inequality is nonetheless compatible with relativity's prohibition on superluminal signaling. Support your answer with reference to the relevant experimental history.

Student's answer: The argument begins with the Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen thought experiment: two particles are prepared in a singlet state and separated by a spacelike interval. Because measuring one particle's spin along a given axis lets us predict with certainty the outcome of the same measurement on the other particle, EPR argued that, on pain of "spooky action at a distance," each particle must already possess a definite value for every measurement that could be performed on it — a hidden variable λ — and that quantum mechanics, which assigns no such value prior to measurement, is therefore incomplete.

Bell turned this into a quantitative claim. The locality condition used in the CHSH derivation, often called factorizability, states that the joint probability of the two outcomes, conditional on λ and on the two local settings, factors as P(A,B|a,b,λ) = P(A|a,λ)·P(B|b,λ): whatever λ is, Alice's outcome cannot depend on Bob's distant setting b, or vice versa. Bell's own 1964 proof first used the perfect anti-correlations of the singlet state to show that, given locality, λ must fix each outcome deterministically, and then derived the inequality for such deterministic instruction sets. Because that route to the inequality leans on determinism, a strictly statistical hidden-variable theory — one where λ only weights the probabilities of the two outcomes rather than dictating them — is not obviously covered by the 1964 argument, and preserving locality by giving up determinism instead of realism remains one of the more attractive ways of reading the theorem's moral.

Finally, no violation of the inequality permits signaling, because Bob's marginal statistics — his distribution of outcomes averaged over Alice's results — are exactly the same whether Alice measures along one axis or another; the correlation is only visible once the two lists of outcomes are compared side by side, which requires an ordinary classical channel. This was already demonstrated experimentally in 1982, when Alain Aspect and collaborators switched the polarizer settings with acousto-optic modulators while the photons were in flight, closing the locality loophole and producing the first loophole-free violation of the inequality; subsequent experiments have refined the precision of this result rather than its substance.
