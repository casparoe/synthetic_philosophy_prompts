During office hours after a seminar on the philosophy of probability, two of my graduate students, Elena and Marcus, kept arguing about Bertrand's paradox after everyone else had left; a teaching assistant who stayed behind wrote down the gist for me. Background: Bertrand's chord problem asks for the probability that a 'randomly drawn' chord of a circle is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle; the classic ways of making 'randomly drawn' precise -- uniform over the two endpoints, uniform over the chord's distance from the center, uniform over the position of its midpoint in the disc -- give three different answers: 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4, respectively.

(1) Marcus argued that Jaynes settled the matter: require the answer to be invariant under the translations, rotations, and rescalings that a physically generated chord shouldn't be sensitive to, and only the 'uniform distance from center' method survives, so 1/2 is uniquely correct. The moral, he said, is that Bertrand's statement of the problem was incomplete, not that the principle of indifference is unsound.

(2) Elena replied that this relocates the difficulty instead of removing it. Nothing in 'choose a chord at random' forces that particular invariance requirement over some other one, and in structurally similar cases there is no invariance group available to break the tie at all: in van Fraassen's cube factory, where a machine's output has side length distributed uniformly, indifference over length puts the chance the side comes out under half the maximum at one-half, while indifference over volume puts it at one-eighth, with nothing physical to adjudicate between the two. Even where Jaynes's fix works, she said, it shows you got lucky, not that indifference was well-defined to begin with.

(3) Marcus responded that the cube factory is beside the point, because ordinary talk of picking a chord 'at random' already presupposes some physical generating procedure, and once that is granted, requiring invariance under translation, rotation, and scale is not an arbitrary add-on -- it is just what it means for such a procedure not to depend on where you happened to draw the circle or what units you measured it in.

(4) Elena answered that this only moves the dispute into semantics: a spun needle, a point dropped uniformly in the disc, and two points picked uniformly on the circumference are all equally faithful physical readings of 'at random,' and Marcus still owes a non-circular reason for privileging invariance of the chord's location over, say, invariance of the joint distribution of its two endpoints. Absent that, Jaynes's invariance requirement is an extra assumption doing the real work, not a discovery that the problem was well-posed all along.
