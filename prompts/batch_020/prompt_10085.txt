And here, in full, is what my classmate submitted: "Leibniz defines justice as 'the charity of the wise' (justitia est caritas sapientis). Charity, for him, is universal benevolence: a settled disposition to love everyone, where loving someone just means taking pleasure in their happiness. Wisdom is the science of felicity -- it tells the benevolent person how to direct that love effectively, rather than just feeling it, so that justice ends up being benevolence disciplined by knowledge of what actually benefits people. This is explicitly opposed to Hobbes and Pufendorf, who make right depend on the command of a superior rather than on any independent standard of goodness; Leibniz's complaint is that this reduces 'God is just' to an empty tautology, since whatever God commanded would count as just by definition. He cashes this out in three degrees of right, each keyed to one of the Roman-law precepts. Strict right (jus strictum) tracks 'harm no one' and is the one grade courts can actually enforce. Equity (aequitas) tracks 'give each his due' and covers duties like gratitude that matter morally but aren't legally compellable. Piety (pietas) tracks 'live honorably' and brings in our relationship to God. Because strict right is the only grade with real teeth -- the only one backed by coercive enforcement -- it is the one that has to be secured before the more optional duties of equity and piety even become relevant, which is why Leibniz treats it as the most basic, load-bearing layer of the whole scheme. This structure also has to survive the charge that 'best of all possible worlds' collapses into Spinoza's necessitarianism. Leibniz's response is that God, being perfectly wise and good, could not have chosen otherwise than the best; since that choice tracks a truth in the divine understanding in just the way a geometric theorem does, the necessity involved is absolute rather than merely hypothetical -- which is exactly what secures the objectivity of moral standards against the voluntarists."
