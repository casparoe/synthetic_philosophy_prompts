A friend of mine and I spent most of a long car ride arguing about whether it is ever rational to deliberately avoid finding something out. She is the kind of person who skips all coverage of a football match before watching her recording of it that evening, who declined a free genetic test for a hereditary condition that runs in her family even though there is no treatment either way, and who avoids logging into her retirement account whenever the market drops sharply. I argued that all three habits are indefensible, because I remembered from an intro decision theory course that the statistician I. J. Good proved a result, now usually called Good's theorem, showing that the expected value of a cost-free piece of evidence relevant to an upcoming decision is never negative for a rational Bayesian agent: in expectation, you can never do better by deciding blind than by looking first. That struck me as a fairly devastating and quite general mathematical argument against her whole policy of studied ignorance, almost a formal vindication of the "principle of total evidence" I had vaguely heard of elsewhere.

She wasn't persuaded, though neither of us could say exactly where the argument was supposed to break down, if indeed it does. So, given that Good's theorem seems to establish, in full generality, that acquiring free evidence before a decision can never lower one's expected utility, I would like a rigorous, decision-theoretic explanation of exactly why her habit of avoiding bad news is a mistake.
