For the past few weeks I've been sitting in on my department's "agent foundations" reading group, mainly because my advisor thinks the MIRI-adjacent literature on self-modifying agents offers a useful formal testbed for the questions about diachronic rationality that my dissertation is actually about -- roughly, under what conditions an agent is justified in trusting the reasoning of its own future self. I've just been asked to lead next week's session on what everyone in the group calls "the Löbian obstacle," and I have to admit that although I can recite the statement of Löb's theorem on request, I don't yet have a gut-level sense of why it actually blocks self-trust, as opposed to being a curiosity about provability predicates that happens to come up in these proofs.

Here's the specific thing I'm stuck on. It seems intuitively harmless for a proof-based agent to adopt a policy along the lines of: "if a future version of me, reasoning from the same axioms and rules of inference I have now, proves that some action is safe, I will trust that verdict and let the action proceed without re-deriving the safety proof myself." Every source I've read insists that trying to formalize just this one conditional is exactly what invites Löb's theorem to bite, but most of them state the theorem symbolically and move straight to the conclusion, or gesture at Gödel's second incompleteness theorem as though that alone makes the connection obvious. Could you talk me through, without resting on a symbolic derivation, why licensing that inference forces the system either to already possess a direct proof that the action is safe -- which would make the appeal to the future self's proof pointless -- or to become inconsistent? I'd also like to understand why the standard patches, such as only ever trusting proof systems strictly weaker than one's own, or replacing proof-based certainty with calibrated probability, avoid the trap rather than merely relocating it.

If it isn't too much of a detour, I'd be grateful for your sense of whether this formal obstacle tells us anything about the more everyday problem of trusting one's own future judgment, or whether the analogy between a mind trusting its future self and a proof system trusting a successor's derivations actually breaks down somewhere important. Someone in the reading group is bound to ask, and I'd like to have a real answer rather than a hand-wave.
