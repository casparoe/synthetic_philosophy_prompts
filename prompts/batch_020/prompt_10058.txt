"The Special Composition Question asks: under what conditions do some objects (the 'xs') compose a further object? This is different from the general composition question, which asks what composition actually is, rather than when it happens. Van Inwagen surveys common-sense answers like contact, fastening, and cohesion and rejects all of them, then gives his own answer, Organicism: the xs compose something iff their activity constitutes a life. So the only composite objects that really exist are living organisms — ordinary artifacts like chairs and ships don't exist, there are just simples 'arranged chairwise' or 'arranged shipwise.' Non-living natural things like rocks, mountains, and stars are unaffected by this and still straightforwardly exist, since Organicism is really only targeting the artificial unity we project onto arrangements of matter, not natural physical wholes. His main argument against moderate views like contact or fastening is the argument from vagueness: because being 'in contact' or 'fastened' is a matter of degree, any such view has to draw a line somewhere in what's really a smooth continuum of cases (like slowly tightening a bolt), and any such line would be completely arbitrary, since there's no principled reason composition should kick in at exactly one degree of fastening rather than one more or less. Since there's no way to non-arbitrarily locate such a cutoff, moderate answers must be rejected."
