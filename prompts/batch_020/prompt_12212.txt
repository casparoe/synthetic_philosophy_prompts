I spent thirty-five years as an experimental condensed-matter physicist before retiring, and have only taken up philosophy seriously in the last few years, reading on my own without much system to it, so please forgive any imprecision in how I've set this up. One habit from my working life that I've kept is a volunteer seat on the grants committee of a small foundation that funds early-career researchers in the physical sciences. Under our charter, each proposal is assigned to a three-person subpanel and is funded just in case the subpanel judges it, first, methodologically feasible given the applicant's preliminary data, and second, genuinely novel relative to the applicant's own prior published work; both conditions must hold. By long-standing practice, rather than voting on funding directly, the subpanel votes on the two conditions separately and lets the charter's rule determine the outcome mechanically, and whichever way a proposal goes, we send the applicant a short written rationale keyed to whichever condition or conditions were decisive.

At our last cycle this practice produced a result that has since become a running quarrel between two of my fellow panelists, Renata and Sam. On one proposal, Renata judged it feasible but not novel, Sam judged it novel but not feasible, and our third colleague judged it both feasible and novel; applying the charter's own rule to their personal judgments, Renata and Sam therefore each believed, for opposite reasons, that the proposal should not be funded, while our third colleague believed it should. Voted condition by condition, however, feasibility passed two to one (Sam dissenting) and novelty passed two to one (Renata dissenting), so the charter's rule funded the proposal anyway — a result that only one of the three people who had actually reviewed it endorsed. Sam's complaint afterward was that this is simply indefensible: the point of asking three scientists to review a proposal is to obtain their considered judgment on whether to fund it, and a majority of the people who reviewed this one did not want it funded, full stop; that the procedure turned two "no" votes into a "yes" is a defect to be fixed, not a nuance to be admired. Renata answered that Sam's "majority" is a mirage: a real, substantive majority (Sam and the third panelist) agreed the science was feasible, and a different real majority (Renata and the third panelist) agreed it was original, so the funded outcome was backed, condition by condition, by reasons panelists actually hold in common — whereas the two "no" votes share no reason at all, since Sam thinks the work derivative and she thinks it shaky, and neither could sign the other's rejection letter. Sam replied that voting condition by condition is a practice the panel adopted, not a requirement of logic; had the subpanel simply voted "fund, yes or no" directly, this proposal would have failed cleanly, two to one, and the fact that decomposing the question into sub-questions reverses the answer shows that the decomposition, not anyone's judgment, is doing the deciding — which should worry anyone who wants funding decisions to track merit rather than the form of the ballot. Renata's rejoinder was that a direct vote has the mirror-image defect: it would let a panelist who privately believes a proposal both feasible and novel nonetheless vote "no" for any unstated reason at all — a rivalry, a favorite competing proposal, plain inattention — and nothing about an up-or-down tally could ever distinguish that vote from an honest one, whereas voting condition by condition at least forces every panelist's reasons onto the record and answerable to the very standards the charter cares about.

Neither has given an inch, and I've been asked, as the least invested party, to referee, which is why I'm turning to you. Could you go through the exchange move by move — Sam's opening complaint, Renata's reply about the mirage majority, Sam's point about decomposition being a policy rather than a logical necessity, and Renata's closing point about accountability — and say who has the better of it at each step, rather than just awarding an overall winner, and flag anything you think both of them are missing? As a minor, secondary matter: since retiring I've also read a little about Arrow's impossibility theorem for elections, and I can't shake the feeling that our subpanel's mess is a relative of that problem rather than a defect peculiar to our charter; if that hunch is right, I would be curious how the two connect, though sorting out Renata and Sam is the main thing I'm after.
