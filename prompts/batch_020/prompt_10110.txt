I spent almost forty years doing experimental condensed matter physics before I retired, and now I've fallen down the philosophy rabbit hole (my wife says I've traded superconductors for a midlife crisis). A few of us from the old department started an informal reading group, and lately we've been chewing on the presentism vs eternalism debate - partly because relativity always made me a bit uneasy about the idea of a single "now" sweeping across the whole universe, and it turns out philosophers were worrying about this stuff long before Einstein gave them new ammunition.

Anyway, I got my hands on McTaggart's old 1908 journal article "The Unreality of Time" (he's the guy the A-series/B-series business originally comes from - he apparently expanded it all into a whole book later, The Nature of Existence, but I'm just working off the original Mind article for now), and I hit a wall on the part where he tries to show the A-series is flat out contradictory. The line that stopped me cold was "Past, present, and future are incompatible determinations." Fine, I get that much - each event can only be one at a time. But then he turns around and says every event actually is all three (it's been future, it is present, it will be past), just not simultaneously, and that the second you try to explain away the apparent contradiction by appealing to those "has been" and "will be" bits, you're smuggling in a second time series to locate them in - and that move just repeats itself forever, which is apparently what people now call a vicious infinite regress.

I've read that this exact question - whether that regress is genuinely vicious, or just a harmless, run of the mill infinite regress like you get all over math and logic without anybody panicking - has been argued over for decades (Dummett apparently defended McTaggart on this, others have pushed back). Could you walk me through, in plain terms, what's actually supposed to make THIS particular regress the vicious kind, so I don't loose track of where the contradiction is supposed to bite? And seperately - if the argument does work, does it actually do eternalism any favors over presentism, or does it just as easily undercut the block-universe/B-series picture too, given that McTaggart's own conclusion was that time itself, not just the "flowing now" version of it, is unreal? That last bit trips me up because half the eternalism-leaning stuff I've read cites McTaggart like he's on their team.
