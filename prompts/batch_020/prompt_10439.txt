I'm a software engineer, and a philosopher friend recently had me read David Lewis's "Convention" after I complained about how arbitrary some of our team's coding standards feel (tabs versus spaces, camelCase versus snake_case, that sort of thing). Lewis's picture, as I understood it, is that a convention is an arbitrary regularity that solves a recurring coordination problem, sustained purely by everyone's mutual expectation that everyone else will keep conforming, and that the initial equilibrium can be fixed either by tacit precedent or by an explicit agreement or salient suggestion, provided the result still meets his conditions of arbitrariness and self-perpetuation. That framework mapped so cleanly onto disputes we have at work that I started wondering about a case that seems messier: the switch from "master" to "main" as the default git branch name.

For context, "master" was for a long time the near-universal name for a repository's primary branch. Around 2020, following the George Floyd protests and an industry-wide push to remove "master/slave" terminology from technical vocabulary, GitHub, GitLab, and other major hosting platforms changed the default for newly created repositories to "main," and many companies separately mandated the switch for their existing repos. Years later, the result is a genuinely fragmented equilibrium: a large share of older or externally maintained repositories still use "master," new projects overwhelmingly default to "main," and engineers routinely have to check which convention a given codebase follows before opening a pull request.

Lewis died in 2001, four years before Git was even written, so he never had anything like this case in mind. But since he built his account to be general enough to cover conventions seeded by agreement or authoritative suggestion rather than only spontaneous precedent, I'd be curious how you think he would have analyzed this particular shift using his own technical apparatus rather than a generic gloss about tech and social justice. Specifically: would he treat the master-to-main switch as a genuine change of convention in his strict sense, or as something categorically different, such as a directive that merely rides on top of a pre-existing convention rather than replacing it; how would he characterize a state in which two incompatible regularities persist side by side for years without either fully collapsing; and what, if anything, would his account predict about whether "main" eventually displaces "master" entirely?
