Over dinner last week, a friend of mine who trades derivatives for a living and I ended up in a long argument about Robert Aumann's famous "agreeing to disagree" result and what, if anything, it shows about real disagreement. Her position was a fairly hard-nosed subjectivism: Bayesian coherence governs how you update on evidence, but it says nothing about where your prior comes from, so two equally rational people can simply start out, before any evidence arrives, with different priors over the very same state space, and no amount of talking or evidence-sharing need ever bring those priors into line. As evidence, she pointed to the sheer amount of trading that happens in markets like hers, which she thinks is best explained by traders honestly holding different priors rather than different information. I pushed back with what's sometimes called the Harsanyi doctrine: that any lasting disagreement between two parties who are mutually convinced of each other's rationality, and whose opinions have become common knowledge between them in Aumann's technical sense, can only be rationally sustained if it traces back to some genuine difference in the evidence each side has seen — a "difference in priors" that cannot be pinned to any possible informational difference just looks like one side being arbitrary. Neither of us budged, and I'd like a more rigorous version of my case than I managed at the table.

Write an argumentative essay defending the common prior assumption as a genuine requirement of Bayesian rationality, rather than as a mere technical convenience that agents are free to dispense with. Explain clearly what Aumann's agreement theorem and the associated no-trade results actually establish, and then take on the two strongest objections a committed subjectivist would raise: first, that demanding a common prior either smuggles in some further rationality norm that Bayesian decision theory was never meant to supply, or collapses into the implausible demand that everyone adopt one uniquely privileged prior; and second, that the persistence of speculative-looking trade in real financial markets is direct empirical evidence that rational people do not, in fact, share priors. Close by stating plainly what you think a rational agent is obligated to do upon discovering that her posterior differs from that of a peer she has no reason to consider less rational or less informed than herself.
