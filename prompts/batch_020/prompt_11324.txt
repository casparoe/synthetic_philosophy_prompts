I'm working on a piece about how intelligent-design folks love to quote-mine old Popper lines about evolution — specifically the bit where he calls natural selection "almost a tautology," not really a testable theory but more of a "metaphysical research programme." Going down the rabbit hole for the article, I found out Popper actually recanted this, sort of, in a Darwin lecture he gave at Cambridge (late 70s, I want to say 1977 or 78), where he says outright that he's changed his mind about whether the theory is testable. My philosopher friend Dave thinks that basically settles it and the ID crowd is just quote-mining a position Popper himself abandoned. But the more of the philosophy-of-biology stuff I skim, the less convinced I am that the recantation itself actually solves the circularity problem he raised in the first place (you know, "the fittest survive" boiling down to "those who survive, survive"). It looks to me like the real technical fix — defining fitness as a propensity or disposition rather than an actual survival outcome — came from other people (Mills and Beatty, I think, around '79) working the concept over from scratch, and isn't really contained in anything Popper says in his own recantation.

So what I'd love from you is the strongest possible argument for this position: that Popper's original tautology worry was basically sound, that his recantation doesn't actually answer it on its own terms, and that it took that seperate propensity move on fitness to do the real work of resolving the objection. Don't give me the "well, Popper changed his mind, so the objection is dead" story you'd get in an intro philosophy of science class — assume I've got the basic background already, and just make the actual case for why the recantation was inadequate as a response to the circularity charge.
