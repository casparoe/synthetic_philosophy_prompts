I'm a software engineer who works on the ranking logic behind a social media feed, and a recent detour into reading Kierkegaard has left me with a question I can't quite work out on my own. In "The Present Age," Kierkegaard argued that "the public" is a dispersed, faceless abstraction produced by the press and by idle chatter, answerable to no one — a diagnosis he arrived at partly through his own bruising experience of being caricatured and mocked in the streets after provoking a feud with the satirical newspaper The Corsair. Yet Kierkegaard himself published most of his major works under an elaborate roster of invented pseudonyms, which he defended as a deliberate technique of indirect communication rather than as a way of dodging responsibility for his views. Suppose he were shown a present-day platform on which anonymous or pseudonymous accounts pile onto a single target, with the pile-on itself amplified by a ranking algorithm optimized purely for engagement. Does his own body of thought give him principled grounds for distinguishing that kind of anonymity from his own pseudonymous authorship, or is this a case where a sharp critic could fairly accuse him of special pleading? Work through this using his actual concepts — leveling, the public, the crowd as untruth, indirect communication — rather than defaulting to a generic verdict that he'd simply have disapproved of social media, and note which parts of your answer are well-grounded in his texts versus speculative extrapolation to a world he never saw.
