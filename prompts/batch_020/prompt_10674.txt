In axiomatic bargaining theory, the Nash bargaining solution and the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution are two of the best-known ways of picking a single "fair" point on the Pareto frontier of a two-party bargaining problem, and I've been trying to get a sharper feel for exactly where and why they part ways. Both solutions are pinned down by adding one further axiom on top of Pareto-optimality, symmetry, and invariance to affine rescalings of utility: Nash adds independence of irrelevant alternatives, while Kalai and Smorodinsky add a monotonicity condition instead (roughly: if the set of feasible agreements expands in a way that only ever increases what is available to one party, without reducing anything available to the other, that party should never end up worse off than before). These two additional axioms turn out to be genuinely incompatible given the rest of the framework, which is part of why the two solutions can prescribe different splits of the very same bargaining problem, and not merely offer different justifications for the same split.

I'd like a dialogue that makes this concrete rather than staying at the level of axioms. Suppose Priya and Tomas, both of whom know this literature well, unexpectedly win $100 together -- a shared scratch-off ticket, say -- and have to agree on how to divide it. Priya is risk-neutral over sums of money in this range; Tomas is currently quite risk-averse, to the point that his utility for money here is well approximated by the square root of the dollar amount (he is short on cash at the moment, so the first several dollars matter far more to him than the last). Priya proposes settling the split via the Nash bargaining solution; Tomas insists on the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution instead. Write their conversation as they work out, in real time, what each solution actually recommends given their different utility curves, and as each tries to convince the other that their own favored axiom -- independence of irrelevant alternatives or monotonicity -- is the one that really deserves to be called a requirement of fairness, while directly answering the standard objection to their own view (for Priya, that the Nash solution can respond in a seemingly perverse way to an expansion of the bargaining set that helps only one side; for Tomas, that the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution's recommendation can be shifted by alternatives that neither of them would ever actually choose). Neither character should concede a point too easily or slightly misstate what the other has just said; each should respond to the specific thing the other said a moment earlier, and each position should come across as being defended about as well as it can be.
