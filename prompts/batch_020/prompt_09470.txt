I'm about two-thirds into a novel set in London in the 1780s, and there's a subplot where my narrator's oldest friend gets hauled in on a sodomy charge — still a hanging offense back then, a fact that genuinely stops me cold every time I sit down to write that chapter. I want to give my narrator (he trained briefly as a barrister before giving it up) one big scene where he actually reasons his way to being appalled by the law, rather than just handing him 21st-century opinions in a powdered wig. Digging around for source material, I learned Bentham himself wrote a private essay around that same decade defending what he called "paederasty" (his term for male homosexual acts generally — broader than how we'd use the word now) on purely utilitarian grounds, and then sat on it for the rest of his life, too nervous to publish. I definately want to steal the real argument rather than invent one, if I can actually get my head around it.

Here's where my grasp gets shaky. In the Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation, Bentham goes after both "the principle of asceticism" and what he calls "the principle of sympathy and antipathy" as bogus rivals to the utility principle — the second one basically being "I feel like this is wrong, so it's wrong," just wearing a costume like moral sense or the law of nature. Is his unpublished case against the sodomy laws essentially that same antipathy-argument pointed at one specific target, i.e. society punishing an act that causes no actual pain to anybody, purely because the act disgusts people?

And is it true (this is a half-remembered thing from a footnote somewhere, so correct me if its wrong) that Bentham confesses to feeling some of that very disgust himself, even while insisting it should carry zero legal weight? If he really does say something like that, does he go on to offer any kind of psychological explanation for where the antipathy comes from, given that his own framework says it's normatively worthless? And if you happen to know the later Hart-Devlin debate about legislating morality, I'd love to know whether it's fair to call that basically a rerun of this same argument — I have a feeling it is, but I couldn't defend the claim if pressed.
