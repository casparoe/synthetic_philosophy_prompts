I spent thirty-five years as a mineralogist before retiring, and over the past couple of years I have become quite absorbed in philosophy, mainly through a small reading group that meets monthly at our local library. An episode from an older hobby of mine recently came up in conversation there, and it left both the group and me without a clear answer.

I keep a ledger of my mineral acquisitions and trades, a practice left over from decades of recording laboratory data, and reviewing it turned up the following sequence. Last spring, at a regional show, I traded a well-terminated smoky quartz point I had owned for a decade, plus forty dollars, for a pyrite specimen with an unusually crisp cubic habit, having just attended a talk on crystal symmetry that left me newly captivated by the cube's geometry. By late summer I had traded that same pyrite specimen, plus thirty dollars, for a dense cluster of azurite crystals, whose depth of color and relative rarity struck me at the time as simply more interesting than a cube's tidy geometry. This past winter, back at the show where the sequence had begun, I came across another smoky quartz point much like my original one, and traded the azurite plus twenty-five dollars to get it, having by then tired of vivid color and rediscovered an appreciation for the quartz's restraint. It was only in updating the ledger that I noticed I had paid ninety-five dollars, across three trades I would still defend individually, to arrive back at essentially the specimen I began with.

When I described this at the reading group, one member said it reminded her of some sort of problem that philosophers discuss, though neither she nor anyone else could reconstruct what it was or why it mattered. What I would like help with is this: was there a genuine failure of rationality somewhere in that sequence of trades, given that each one, judged on its own terms and with the tastes and information I had at the time, seemed perfectly sound? If there was a failure, I doubt it lies in any single one of the three preferences taken by itself; it may belong only to the pattern the trades form together, in which case it is not obvious that any one of my past selves who actually made these trades did anything wrong. I would also be curious whether there was anything I could reasonably have done differently at the time of any single trade, since at no individual step did I seem, to myself, to be doing anything amiss.
