I'm finishing a long-form piece on whether there are principled limits to what artificial reasoning systems can ever achieve, and I keep circling back to the Lucas–Penrose argument, which I clearly didn't absorb as well as I thought the first time around. I have the skeleton down: Gödel's first incompleteness theorem guarantees that any consistent formal system powerful enough to encode arithmetic contains a true sentence it cannot prove; Lucas, and later Penrose in "Shadows of the Mind," argue that a human mathematician can nonetheless see that this Gödel sentence is true, and conclude that the mind cannot simply be an instantiation of that system, or of any Turing machine at all. What I keep losing hold of is why the standard rebuttal — something to do with the system not being entitled to assume its own consistency — is supposed to actually defeat the argument, rather than being a technicality Penrose had already anticipated and answered.

Could you walk me through, at a gut level, exactly where the regress bites? What does it mean, concretely, for a mind to "know" that a formal system is consistent; why can't that knowledge simply be added to the system as a further axiom; and why doesn't that fix just reproduce the identical predicament one level up, with a new Gödel sentence and a new consistency question? I'd also like help weighing how much of the ongoing disagreement between defenders and critics of the argument really turns on this consistency point, as opposed to the prior and murkier issue of whether human mathematical competence corresponds to any single, well-defined formal system in the first place. One of the mathematicians I interviewed for the piece waved the second issue away as "the objection nobody arguing for mechanism actually has an answer to," and I'd like to know whether that's a fair characterization or an overstatement.
