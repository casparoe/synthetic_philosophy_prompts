At work we just got greenlit to let our internal coding agent open PRs against its own scaffolding/prompt chain overnight (nothing crazy, just refactors and prompt tweaks, a human still has to click merge), but it made me nervous enough that I went down a rabbit hole reading about "self-trust" in AI systems and landed on this thing from the old MIRI papers called the Löbian obstacle. I even tried explaining it to my roommate, who has a math PhD, and she just said I was overthinking it, which did not actually resolve anything for me.

From what I've pieced together: Löb's theorem says a formal system can't prove "if I can prove P, then P is true" for some arbitrary P unless it can already prove P outright. And apparently that's exactly the kind of trust an agent A0 needs to have in a successor A1 (or in itself a minute from now) before it modifies its own code -- it needs something like "if my future self proves this action is safe, then it's actually safe," and Löb's theorem seems to say that's off the table in general. Isn't this basically the same move as Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, just with an extra step, showing that no system can ever verify a successor system is trustworthy? Because if so, I don't get why there's a whole subfield working on corrigibility, tiling agents, and scalable oversight instead of everyone just agreeing that provably-safe self-modifying AI is a dead end for reasons this basic and moving on. Is there something wrong with how I'm reading the theorem, or is this definately as damning as it sounds?
