I'm working on a novel that follows a philosophy department through the year its longest-serving member retires, and I've reached the scene I most need help with. Ruth Adler has spent thirty years as one of the most exacting defenders of Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility: the claim that because a person cannot be causa sui, the self-caused source of their own character, nobody is ever ultimately responsible -- praiseworthy or blameworthy in the deep, buck-stops-here sense -- for what they do, and that this holds whether or not determinism turns out to be true. She has always insisted the argument leaves ordinary, everyday responsibility-talk untouched, but in thirty years of print she has never once allowed herself a personal exception to the rest of it. In her final semester she is asked to speak at her department's own small commencement exercises, held the morning before the university-wide ceremony, to a hall holding a handful of colleagues who have spent decades arguing with her about exactly this claim, and, mostly, parents who came to hear their children praised for what they have earned. Ruth won't give them the usual rhetoric of pride and desert, which she thinks false even said of herself, but she also refuses to let the occasion curdle into a seminar on the illusion of achievement. Please write the address she actually delivers -- eight minutes or so, one her sharpest colleague in that hall couldn't catch quietly contradicting a single premise she's published, and one that still sends the families out feeling what families are supposed to feel on that particular morning. If she can find room for one dry joke at her own argument's expense, so much the better.
