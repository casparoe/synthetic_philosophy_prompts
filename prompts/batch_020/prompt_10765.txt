I teach fiction writing in my university's MFA program, and — in a plot twist I certainly wouldn't have assigned myself — I've spent this year as the contingent-faculty representative on the university's Teaching Effectiveness Committee. The dispute in front of us right now concerns a colleague in the History department, an adjunct whose contract comes up for renewal this spring and whose overall student-evaluation average sits at 3.4 out of 5, just under the 3.5 line our policy uses to trigger a mandatory teaching improvement plan. Across the university, adjuncts average 3.4 and tenure-line faculty average 3.9, which looks damning until you break the numbers down by course level: adjuncts score as well as or better than tenure-line faculty within every category of course, but they teach a much larger share of the large, mandatory intro sections that students tend to rate lower no matter who's teaching them, while tenure-line faculty teach a disproportionate share of the small upper-level electives students chose for themselves. Someone on the committee called this "Simpson's paradox" and seemed to think that naming it settled the matter; I'd like the analysis, not just the label.

Half the committee now wants to replace the university-wide average with a course-level-adjusted score, which would clear my colleague and several others like her. The other half isn't convinced: they point out that chairs, not a coin flip, decide who teaches what, and it's entirely possible that weaker instructors get steered toward "easier," lower-stakes intro sections precisely because they're weaker — in which case adjusting for course level wouldn't be correcting a statistical illusion so much as laundering a real difference in quality out of the record. A few members also worry that once you slice the data this finely, some cells have so few instructors in them that the "adjusted" numbers are barely more trustworthy than the raw ones. I have to bring a short written recommendation to the full committee next month, when the five-year policy is up for renewal, suspension, or amendment, and I'd rather not hand the dean something he can dismantle in the first two minutes — I have a novel to get back to. Could you draft that memo for me? I need a clear recommendation for how evaluation scores should factor into renewal decisions, on what timeline, and I need it to actually reckon with the selection-effect worry rather than wave it away. My own statistics education ended with one forgettable undergraduate course, so plain reasoning my humanist colleagues can follow without a refresher would help too.
