I'm a second-year in a Hispanic Studies PhD program and I'm writing my seminar paper this term on Sor Juana's "Respuesta a Sor Filotea de la Cruz" — her long 1691 self-defense letter to the bishop who published her theological essay without asking and then told her, gently but pointedly, to stick to safer subjects. My paper's argument is that her actual case for women's education is more rigorous (and weirder) than the "protofeminist icon" version everyone gets in survey courses. Problem is, everyone always quotes the same three bits (cutting her hair as a kid if she didn't learn fast enough, "philosophies of the kitchen," "yo no estudio para escribir, ni menos para enseñar") and my advisor pushed me toward the denser scholastic section instead, where she actually works through St. Paul and the church fathers on whether women can study at all, and that part is kicking my butt a little. My Spanish is fine for modern prose, but something about the syntax and the constant Latin interpolations keeps making me loose the thread of what she's actually arguing.

Here's the passage, straight from the letter:

"El venerable Doctor Arce (digno profesor de Escritura por su virtud y letras), en su Studioso Bibliorum excita esta cuestión: An liceat foeminis sacrorum Bibliorum studio incumbere? eaque interpretari? Y trae por la parte contraria muchas sentencias de santos, en especial aquello del Apóstol: Mulieres in Ecclesiis taceant, non enim permittitur eis loqui, etc. Trae después otras sentencias, y del mismo Apóstol aquel lugar ad Titum: Anus similiter in habitu sancto, bene docentes, con interpretaciones de los Santos Padres; y al fin resuelve, con su prudencia, que el leer públicamente en las cátedras y predicar en los púlpitos, no es lícito a las mujeres; pero que el estudiar, escribir y enseñar privadamente, no sólo les es lícito, pero muy provechoso y útil; claro está que esto no se debe entender con todas, sino con aquellas a quienes hubiere Dios dotado de especial virtud y prudencia y que fueren muy provectas y eruditas y tuvieren el talento y requisitos necesarios para tan sagrado empleo."

What I can't untangle is what kind of move this actually is, logically. She just concedes, no pushback at all, that women can't teach from a lectern or preach from a pulpit, and then pivots to defend private study, writing, and even private teaching as not merely tolerated but genuinely valuable — and she makes that pivot by hiding behind some minor theologian's scholastic Latin question rather than arguing it plainly in her own voice. Is that concession-then-pivot doing real philosophical work, actually carving out legitimate intellectual territory for women, or is it basically making peace with the cage and calling the inside of it a garden? And that last clause, restricting all of this to women whom God has specially "dotado de especial virtud y prudencia" (endowed with special virtue and prudence) — is that a genuine, load-bearing condition in her argument, or a hedge she needs so the whole thing doesn't read as too radical to the bishop shes writing to? I keep going back and forth on how strong a claim about women's education this passage is actually making once you factor in all the hedging and borrowed authority, so I'd love a close, almost sentence-by-sentence read of the logic here if you're up for it.
