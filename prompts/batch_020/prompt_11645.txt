I don't have any background in philosophy -- I'm a software developer by trade -- but a couple of years ago a friend pointed me toward Kripke's short book on Wittgenstein and rule-following, and the central puzzle in it has been rattling around in my head ever since. As I understood it, the argument is that no finite stretch of a person's past behavior with a symbol like "+" can settle whether they meant addition or some deviant function -- Kripke calls it "quus" -- that agrees with addition on every sum they have actually performed but diverges from it afterward, and that the natural fallback of appealing to a disposition to keep answering correctly doesn't rescue the situation either, since any real disposition is finite and can misfire, whereas what a person meant is supposed to set a standard that their actual responses might fail to meet. Now that I spend a large part of my working day prompting large language models, I keep noticing how neatly this maps onto them: a model is shaped by a finite training set and has to extend some pattern to inputs it has never seen, and it is well known that these systems get noticeably shakier at arithmetic as the numbers grow larger -- which looks almost like a real-world case of a "quus"-style function taking over past some threshold nobody ever specified.

What I would like from you is a genuinely careful attempt to work out what Wittgenstein would have said about whether a language model can be correctly described as following the rule for addition, or a grammatical pattern, at all -- a question that obviously could not have arisen in his lifetime. I know that Kripke's reading of Wittgenstein is contested enough that people half-jokingly call the resulting position "Kripkenstein" rather than attribute it to Wittgenstein himself, so feel free to draw on whichever of the two positions strikes you as more defensible, so long as you say clearly which one you mean at each point. Does the same skeptical argument bite the model just as hard as it is meant to bite a human calculator, or is there a genuine disanalogy -- for instance, that a model's weights are shaped by a vast community's actual usage, and that its outputs go on being checked and corrected by users and by fine-tuning -- that changes the diagnosis in one direction or the other? And separately, does the "skeptical solution" built around a community's shared practice of endorsing and correcting applications give us any real purchase on something like human-feedback fine-tuning, or is that just a comforting analogy that falls apart once you look closely? I would rather you work through the actual argument, premise by premise if need be, than settle for the observation that Wittgenstein would probably have found the question interesting.
