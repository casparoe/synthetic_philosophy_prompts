I've become a bit obsessed lately with a puzzle that I later learned the philosopher Peter van Inwagen dubbed "the special composition question": under what conditions do some things add up to make a further thing? Do two Lego bricks pressed together create a new object over and above the two bricks? Does a heap of loose bricks? What about the same bricks once they've been mortared into a wall? I have no background in philosophy at all -- I fell into this after a couple of late-night YouTube videos on "mereological nihilism," the view that, strictly speaking, there are no tables or chairs, only particles arranged table-wise or chair-wise, and that rabbit hole eventually led me to the opposite extreme, "universalism," the view that absolutely any two things whatsoever, however scattered or unrelated, compose a further object -- so that, apparently, there's a single object made of my left shoe and the Moon.

What I can't get past is the main argument I found for universalism, which I gather traces back to David Lewis and has been defended by others since. It goes roughly like this: if composition happened only sometimes -- say, two bricks compose something once cemented, but not while merely touching -- then, as you move by tiny degrees from a clear non-composition case to a clear composition case (adding the cement molecule by molecule, or pressing the bricks together fraction of a millimeter by fraction of a millimeter), there would have to be some exact point where composition switches from "no" to "yes." Any such precise cutoff seems absurdly arbitrary, so it looks like the transition has to be vague. But, the argument continues, that's impossible: vagueness in how many objects exist would be vagueness in existence itself, and existence isn't the sort of thing that can come in degrees or be a fuzzy, borderline matter the way "baldness" or "being a heap" can -- something either exists or it doesn't. So, to avoid this supposedly incoherent "vague existence," composition has to be all-or-nothing across the board: either it always happens (universalism) or it never does (nihilism), with nothing stable in between.

I find this weirdly persuasive on a first pass, but I also can't shake the feeling that there's a sleight of hand somewhere in the move from "it's a vague, borderline matter whether these things compose something" to "therefore the world itself contains a vague, borderline number of objects." I don't have the philosophical tools to say what the sleight of hand is, if there is one. Could you construct the strongest case you can that this particular inference fails -- that reality can perfectly well contain vague or borderline cases of composition without that committing anyone to a fuzzy head-count of objects or to existence coming in degrees? I'd like the actual argument, not just a gesture at "well, some philosophers reject this premise" -- and if, having built the strongest version you can, you conclude the inference really does go through after all, please say so plainly rather than manufacturing a case you don't believe.
