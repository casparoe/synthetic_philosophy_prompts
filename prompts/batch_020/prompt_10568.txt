So in my ethics seminar we just finished a two week unit on "why do promises bind" — we read Hume's stuff on promises as an artificial virtue and then Scanlon's Fidelity principle from What We Owe to Each Other — and it bled into real life this week because two people in my study group, Marcus and Sana, got into it over a story Marcus told at lunch. On his deathbed, his grandpa made Marcus promise, privately, with literally no one else in the room, that he'd secretly redirect a chunk of the inheritance to a cousin the rest of the family can't stand, instead of splitting everything evenly like the will technically says. Grandpa died about an hour later. No one alive knows the promise was ever made, so Marcus could just split things "fairly" and there'd be zero social fallout, ever.

Here's roughly how the argument went (paraphrasing, but I think this is fair to both of them):
(1) Sana said Marcus is obviously still bound, and that this actually confirms the class's "expectation" reading of Scanlon — promises bind because you deliberately get someone to rely on you, and breaking that reliance is a kind of exploitation of trust, so what matters is the expectation you created in your grandfathers mind before he died.
(2) Marcus said that can't be right, since his grandfather is now dead and literally cannot be disappointed, relieved, or affected by anything that happens afterward — there is no expectation left anywhere in the universe to violate — yet it obviously still feels wrong to break the promise, so the wrongness can't be located in an expectation.
(3) Sana said the expectation existed at the moment of promising and that's sufficient, the same way we still think you can wrong someone by defaming them after they've died even though they can't feel betrayed anymore.
(4) Marcus said that move quietly changes the theory from "don't violate expectations" into "don't violate whatever expectations you once created, forever, even once its metaphysically impossible to violate them" — which just sounds like a complicated way of saying promises bind because you willed an obligation into existence, period, closer to the "normative power" view (the sort of thing Raz and Owens gesture at) he'd been defending from the start.
(5) Sana said that's a false choice, because a pure will/power view can't explain why promises extracted through obvious manipulation, or made as a joke both people understand isn't serious, fail to bind — while the expectation view explains this easily, since no reasonable reliance gets created in those cases.
(6) Marcus didn't really have a comeback for that one, but held that whatever the full story turns out to be, it can't be fundamentally about expectations, since his case shows the obligation surviving the total, permanent destruction of any expectation there is left to violate.

Neither of them budged and I genuinely think they're each landing real punches on the other. Could you go through the exchange move by move, (1) through (6), and say who has the better of it at each step and why? Then tell me whether any single theory of promissory obligation we discussed actually survives the grandfather case as it stands, or whether it needs some third ingredient neither of them mentioned — and if so what that would be. End with a one-line verdict in the form \verdict{Marcus}, \verdict{Sana}, or \verdict{Neither}, for whoever comes closest to being right overall about why the promise still binds.
