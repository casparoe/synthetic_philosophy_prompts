So I went down a rabbit hole this week reading about the Kelly criterion -- the rule that says you should size a bet (or a portfolio) to maximize the expected logarithm of your wealth, i.e. the long-run geometric growth rate, instead of maximizing expected value. Turns out theres a real decades-long fight over whether this is actually THE rational way to bet or invest, and not just a neat trick out of information theory. Ed Thorp used it to beat blackjack and then ran a hedge fund basically treating "maximize long-run growth rate" as the obviously correct target. Paul Samuelson spent years attacking that -- he even wrote a whole journal paper using nothing but one-syllable words just to mock the reasoning -- arguing that maximizing E[log W] is just what falls out of picking log utility specifically, one option among infinitely many possible utility functions, so it has no special claim to rationality, and that "you'll almost surely end up ahead of any other strategy in the long run" is true but useless for someone who only gets to make a handful of big bets in one lifetime. Then more recently there's this whole "ergodicity economics" move from physicists like Ole Peters, claiming standard expected-utility theory secretly averages over parallel possible worlds (an ensemble average) when what an actual person lives through is a time average, and that once you correct for that, growth-rate maximization stops being a preference and starts looking like the objectively correct answer -- no utility function needed at all.

Can you write me an essay that actually wrestles with this instead of landing on something bland like "diversify, don't bet the farm"? I want the strongest version of the Samuelson-style objection and the strongest version of the Thorp/Peters-style defense (ergodicity reframing included) weighed against each other, and then an actual verdict -- not a wishy-washy "both sides raise good points." If the honest answer turns out to be conditional, like geometric-growth maximization is uniquely rational only under such-and-such conditions and just collapses into "one utility function among many" outside them, that's fine, but spell out exactly what those conditions are and defend the line you're drawing.
