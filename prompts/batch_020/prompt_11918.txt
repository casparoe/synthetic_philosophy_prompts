For the past few weeks I've been stuck on a disagreement that started over something fairly trivial: whether it's reasonable to call a film "brilliant" on the strength of a critic's review alone, having never actually watched it myself. A friend of mine argues this is no different from believing any other fact on good authority — I've never been to Peru, but I don't doubt it exists just because I haven't seen it with my own eyes, so why should a verdict about a film's quality be held to a stricter evidential standard than a verdict about geography? And yet something about it nags at me. If all I have is someone else's say-so, it feels like I'm merely reporting their opinion that the film is brilliant rather than actually holding that opinion myself, in a way that doesn't seem to happen when I take someone's word for an ordinary fact.

Thinking about it more, and reading around online, I discovered that this exact tension has a name: the problem of "aesthetic testimony." There's a real split between people called "pessimists," who think testimony can't hand you a genuine aesthetic judgment the way it hands you an ordinary factual or historical one, and "optimists," who think this supposed double standard doesn't survive scrutiny. I also stumbled on a near-identical debate about moral testimony — whether you can count as genuinely virtuous for doing the right thing purely because someone else told you it was right — which made me wonder whether the aesthetic case is just one instance of something more general, or whether art really does demand something from us that morality and plain facts don't. Apparently the suspicion that taste can't simply be borrowed from other people goes back at least to Kant, who thought that even unanimous agreement among every critic alive shouldn't settle the matter for you, since a judgment of beauty has to be grounded in your own response to the object rather than adopted secondhand.

What I'd like is for you to write me an essay that works through this properly instead of just laying out both sides and shrugging. Please engage with the strongest arguments for pessimism — for instance, whatever exactly is supposed to be special about needing first-hand "acquaintance" with an artwork before you can justifiably judge it — as well as the strongest pushback from optimists, including the fact that people rely on reviews and recommendations to make everyday choices about what to watch, read, and listen to, seemingly without disaster. Then I'd like you to commit to an actual verdict and defend it on the merits. A qualified answer is completely fine — for example, distinguishing what testimony can secure from what it can't — but please don't let the essay end on a noncommittal "reasonable people disagree."
