In my school's critical thinking elective, we spent a whole unit on informal fallacies, and the one that stuck with me most was tu quoque — the mistake of responding to an accusation of wrongdoing by pointing out that the accuser does the same thing. Our teacher was very firm that this is a genuine fallacy: whether an action is wrong cannot depend on the moral track record of whoever happens to be pointing it out, so "you do it too" never actually refutes the original charge. That made a lot of sense to me at the time.

Recently, though, while researching a debate club topic on political accountability, I came across some philosophical work on what's called the "standing to blame," including an essay by G.A. Cohen about whether anyone really has the right to condemn terrorism, and I believe R. Jay Wallace has written something similar about an "equal standing" condition for blame. From what I can tell, these philosophers argue that being a hypocrite really can do something important: it can strip you of your standing to blame someone else, even though it doesn't make the original accusation false. This seems to directly contradict what I learned about tu quoque being a fallacy. Here's a concrete version of the problem that I keep coming back to: suppose our student council president gives a big speech publicly punishing a classmate for cheating on a test, and it later comes out that the president cheated on an almost identical test the year before and was never caught. My instinct is that the classmate should be able to shoot back, "you have no right to lecture me," and that there is something to this reply beyond mere rhetorical deflection — but if hypocrisy can never make the original accusation false, how could pointing it out ever legitimately accomplish anything in an argument?

So I feel stuck between two things I've apparently been taught: that the truth of a claim never depends on who is making it, and that hypocrisy can nonetheless undermine someone's moral standing. Since these seem to be in real tension, should I just conclude that all this academic talk of "standing to blame" is essentially the tu quoque fallacy dressed up in more sophisticated language, and that the classmate's comeback, however emotionally satisfying, doesn't actually hold up logically once you think it through carefully?
