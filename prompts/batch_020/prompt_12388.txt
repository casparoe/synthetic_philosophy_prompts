For a seminar on the history of twentieth-century economic and political thought, we have spent this week on Keynes's 1930 essay "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren," which our professor is using as an entry point into debates about what people are supposed to do with their lives once material scarcity stops being a binding constraint. I follow the essay's overall argument, but I am stuck on the logic of one particular passage, where Keynes introduces and defines what he calls "purposiveness":

Of course there will still be many people with intense, unsatisfied purposiveness who will blindly pursue wealth – unless they can find some plausible substitute. But the rest of us will no longer be under any obligation to applaud and encourage them. For we shall inquire more curiously than is safe to-day into the true character of this "purposiveness" with which in varying degrees Nature has endowed almost all of us. For purposiveness means that we are more concerned with the remote future results of our actions than with their own quality or their immediate effects on our own environment. The "purposive" man is always trying to secure a spurious and delusive immortality for his acts by pushing his interest in them forward into time. He does not love his cat, but his cat's kittens; nor, in truth, the kittens, but only the kittens' kittens, and so on forward forever to the end of cat-dom. For him jam is not jam unless it is a case of jam to-morrow and never jam to-day. Thus by pushing his jam always forward into the future, he strives to secure for his act of boiling it an immortality.

Let me remind you of the Professor in Sylvie and Bruno: "Only the tailor, sir, with your little bill," said a meek voice outside the door. "Ah, well, I can soon settle his business," the Professor said to the children, "if you'll just wait a minute. How much is it, this year, my man?" The tailor had come in while he was speaking. "It's been a-doubling so many years, you see," the tailor replied, a little gruffly, "and I think I'd like the money now. It's two thousand pound, it is!" "Oh, that's nothing!" the Professor carelessly remarked, feeling in his pocket, as if he always carried at least that amount about with him. "But wouldn't you like to wait just another year and make it four thousand? Just think how rich you'd be! Why, you might be a king, if you liked!" "I don't know as I'd care about being a king," the man said thoughtfully. "But it dew sound a powerful sight o' money! Well, I think I'll wait—" "Of course you will!" said the Professor. "There's good sense in you, I see. Good-day to you, my man!" "Will you ever have to pay him that four thousand pounds?" Sylvie asked as the door closed on the departing creditor. "Never, my child!" the Professor replied emphatically. "He'll go on doubling it till he dies. You see, it's always worth while waiting another year to get twice as much money!"

What I cannot work out is what this anecdote is doing to Keynes's own argument. He has just defined the "purposive" man as someone who continually defers present enjoyment in pursuit of what Keynes himself calls a "spurious and delusive immortality" for his actions, and this same deferral is apparently the disposition that, elsewhere in the essay, he credits with all the saving and accumulation that will eventually deliver humanity into abundance. Yet the anecdote plays the tailor's decision to keep deferring payment on an ever-doubling debt purely as a joke, and the joke depends entirely on the fact that the money is never actually collected. Is Keynes using the story to satirize purposiveness as such, in which case his own account of how humanity worked and saved its way into abundance seems to undercut itself? Or is there some distinction between the tailor's particular error and the "purposive" habit of mind more generally that I am not seeing? Since the essay's larger claim is that we will eventually have to unlearn this habit in order to "live wisely and agreeably and well," I would like to understand whether this passage is quietly working against Keynes's own optimism about how we got here, or whether I am missing something that keeps it consistent.
