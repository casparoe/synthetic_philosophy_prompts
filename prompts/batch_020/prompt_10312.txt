I don't have any formal training in philosophy, but for the last year or so I've spent a lot of my free time reading rationalist and effective-altruism-adjacent blogs, and one idea from that world has stuck with me: the notion of a "transformative experience." As I understand it (I believe the term comes from a philosopher named L.A. Paul, though I could be misremembering the attribution), the idea is that some big life choices can't be handled by the ordinary business of weighing pros and cons, because going through the choice would change the very values and preferences you'd use to judge whether it was worth it, and there's no way to access those future values from where you stand now. The idea came up a couple of weeks ago in an argument between two friends of mine, Naomi and Callum, about a friend of ours named Yusuf, who already has a one-year-old daughter and is torn about whether to have a second child. Yusuf keeps saying that the first year of parenthood was nothing like what anyone described to him beforehand, in ways both good and bad, and that he no longer trusts his own judgment about what a second, entirely different kid would be like.

Here's roughly how the argument between Naomi and Callum went. (1) Naomi said Yusuf is right to feel stuck, and that this is exactly the kind of case where he should stop trying to predict outcomes, since he can't know in advance what it is like to raise this specific, not-yet-existing child; instead, he should ask himself whether he wants to be the kind of person who says yes to that sort of unknown. (2) Callum objected that Yusuf isn't actually in the dark the way a first-time parent is, because he already has the relevant experience — the sleeplessness, the lost freedom, the particular kind of love — so whatever a second child brings is just ordinary uncertainty about the specifics, not the deep, in-principle-unknowable kind that is supposed to make normal reasoning break down. (3) Naomi replied that this misses the point, since children are different enough from one another that knowing what parenting is like in general doesn't tell you what parenting this particular child will be like, so the uncertainty involved is still the deep kind. (4) Callum shot back that if any amount of novelty counts as "transformative" in that strong sense, the idea stops doing any real work, since every choice anyone makes involves particulars they haven't personally lived through before; at that point, "transformative experience" is just a fancy label for ordinary uncertainty, and Yusuf doesn't get to use it as a reason to stop weighing what he already knows.

I went back and forth on this and couldn't settle it myself. Could you go through the exchange move by move — (1) through (4) — and say which specific claims hold up and which don't, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'd also like to know whether either of them is overlooking some important consideration, and whether, in your view, there's a way of rescuing the "transformative experience" idea from Callum's final objection, or whether he's basically put his finger on a fatal flaw in it.
