So this is going to sound very niche but bear with me. I started as an RA for a decision theory prof this semester (total 180 from what I was doing before, which was mostly just grading calc homework), and I've been sitting in on the lab's reading group to catch up on the literature. Two weeks ago we covered the "absent-minded driver" problem -- Piccione and Rubinstein's puzzle about a driver who needs to get home by exiting at the second of two intersections on a road, where both intersections look completely identical from the driver's seat. If he exits at the first one by mistake he gets a payoff of 0, if he sails through both without exiting he gets 1, and if he exits at exactly the right (second) one he gets 4. The catch is he's genuinly "absent-minded": standing at either intersection, he cannot tell which one it is, so whatever rule he uses (like "continue with probability p") has to be literally the same rule both times. If you just maximize his expected payoff before he even sets off -- i.e. (1-p)*0 + p*(1-p)*4 + p^2*1 -- the answer comes out to p = 2/3.

Afterwards two of the senior PhD students in the group, Mara and Theo, got into it over beers, and I haven't been able to let it go since. (1) Mara said the ex-ante 2/3 answer is a bit of a cheat, because once the driver is actually standing at an intersection not knowing which one it is, he should update: merely being at an intersection at all is weak evidence that he already continued once, so he should think it's a little more likely he's at the second one -- and once you plug that revised belief back into an expected-utility calc for "continue vs. exit right now," you get a different, better number than 2/3, meaning the planned policy isn't actually something he'd want to stick to once he's in the moment. (2) Theo said she wasn't updating correctly -- if you work out the real odds of being at the first vs. second intersection given that he's following probability p, and then recompute the payoff of continuing vs. exiting under exactly that belief, it comes out exactly indifferent at p = 2/3, so there's no temptation to deviate at all; the plan is self-enforcing. (3) Mara replied that this is circular: of course it looks stable, since the only reason you get indifference is that you fed p = 2/3 into the belief calculation to begin with. She wants some way of pinning down the belief that doesn't already presuppose the answer. (4) Theo said that's just what a fixed point / equilibrium is -- you check that a candidate strategy generates beliefs under which that very strategy is a best response, exactly like solving for a Nash equilibrium, and nobody calls that viciously circular. (5) Mara said fine, mathematically, but theres still something philosophically off: "the probability that I'm currently at the first intersection" presupposes there's a fact about which visit "I" am having right now, and with genuine absent-mindedness (as opposed to mere uncertainty) she isn't convinced that's even coherent -- she said it's basically the Sleeping Beauty problem from her epistemology seminar wearing a trench coat. (6) Theo answered that the decision problem doesn't need that metaphysical question settled at all: since both "visits" are stipulated to act identically anyway, you can derive the optimal shared rule straight from the tree and its payoffs without ever resolving the self-location puzzle. Mara's last word was that the two numbers matching (2/3 both ways) might just be a fact about this particular example, and that in general -- different payoffs, more intersections -- planning-optimal and "in the moment" optimal can genuinely disagree, so the real lesson is about when in-the-moment updating can be trusted at all, not this one case. Theo agreed they can diverge in other versions but didn't think that shows anything is actually confused about probability here specifically.

I can't tell who's ahead at any given point, honestly.
