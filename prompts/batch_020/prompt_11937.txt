This term I'm taking a cross-listed philosophy and computer science seminar called "Minds, Models, and Machines," and for the last two weeks we've been using mechanistic interpretability as a case study in realism about representation. The readings paired Anthropic's papers on toy models of superposition and on using sparse autoencoders to recover "monosemantic" features -- including the Scaling Monosemanticity paper, whose Golden Gate Bridge feature could be turned up until the model fixated on the bridge in almost every reply -- with older philosophy of mind material: Dennett's "Real Patterns" and Ramsey's discussion, in Representation Reconsidered, of what he calls the job description challenge for any posited representation. Our discussion section split roughly in two over how to reconcile these. One camp argued that a direction an autoencoder recovers counts as a genuine representation in Ramsey's demanding sense as long as causal interventions like activation patching show it's actually doing representational work downstream, rather than merely being something a probe can decode after the fact. The other camp thought this concedes too much, on the grounds that superposition, feature splitting as dictionaries get larger, and the general non-uniqueness of what counts as "a feature" suggest there may be no privileged fact of the matter to be a realist about in the first place -- so that the most we're entitled to is a Dennett-style instrumentalism, on which features are "real" only in the thin sense of being a useful, compressible pattern for us.
