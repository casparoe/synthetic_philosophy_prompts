My cousin Mireille writes pop songs for hire on the professional "writing camp" circuit, flown in for a few days at a time to co-write a single, credited alongside four or five others. Last month a song built around a bridge she wrote about a breakup she was still raw from became a real phenomenon, complete with a viral clip of a stadium of strangers weeping through that exact line in unison. At the dinner celebrating this, I mentioned that I'd just read Tolstoy's *What Is Art?* for an evening course, and my uncle Faisal and my cousin's partner Beatriz spent the rest of the meal arguing over whether the song counts as art at all on Tolstoy's own terms.

(1) Faisal: whatever you think of how it was made, the song passes Tolstoy's own test. Thousands of strangers with nothing else in common caught the same specific feeling at the same instant, no training required. That is exactly the "infection" he makes the hallmark of real art, and he ranked that kind of universally transmissible feeling above anything that needs cultivation to enjoy.

(2) Beatriz: that's form, not substance. Infection is only possible because of the artist's sincerity, and Tolstoy treats art made to order for money as the paradigm case of counterfeit art, whatever effect it happens to produce. Mireille and four co-writers sat fitting lines to a beat the label had already chosen because it tested well — nobody in that room needed to say anything; they needed a streamable single.

(3) Faisal: payment and committees don't rule out sincerity by themselves. Plenty of what Tolstoy calls real art was reworked by many hands over generations, like folk song, with no single author and no purity of motive required. What matters is whether real feeling got into it, not who's on the credits — and that bridge is about a breakup she actually went through.

(4) Beatriz: one true line doesn't launder a song assembled around it for chart performance. What counts is the inner need behind making the whole work, not a real detail deployed because the room already knew it would land.

Neither of them budged, and I've been drafted as referee every time the argument resurfaces since. Could you go through their four moves and say, for each one, who has the better of it and why? Please close with your own verdict on who comes out ahead overall, and on what, if anything, both of them are missing.
