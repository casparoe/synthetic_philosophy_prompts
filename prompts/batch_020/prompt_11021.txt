I'm a software engineer, and a few colleagues and I started an informal reading group on philosophy of language, mostly because the topic keeps coming up tangentially in our work on machine translation and NLP pipelines. We recently worked through the "radical translation" material in Quine's *Word and Object*, including the famous gavagai example: a field linguist hears a native informant exclaim "Gavagai!" every time a rabbit runs past, and can never be quite sure whether the word really means "rabbit," as opposed to "undetached rabbit part," "rabbit-stage," or some other object that would prompt exactly the same assent given exactly the same stimulus.

Before I present my summary to the group, I want a sanity check on how I've framed the upshot. My reading is that this is fundamentally a data-scarcity problem, not unlike the situation where a small, unrepresentative test suite leaves several very different implementations equally consistent with the observed input-output behavior: you just haven't sampled enough cases yet to rule out the "wrong" ones. Quine's linguist only has a handful of pointing episodes to go on, so naturally several translation manuals fit equally well. But it seems to follow that, given a large enough parallel corpus of situated utterances, plus modern tools like statistical word-alignment or the distributional embeddings behind today's large language models, you could keep narrowing the space of candidate manuals until only one (or a negligible equivalence class) survived, resolving the indeterminacy for all practical purposes. On that reading, gavagai isn't really a thesis about the nature of meaning so much as an artifact of 1960s field linguists not having enough observational bandwidth, and it ends up saying something close to the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis: translation is hard because communities carve up the world differently, but with enough cross-referenced data you can always triangulate the right carving eventually.

Is that a fair characterization of what Quine was actually arguing, or am I missing something more fundamental about the claim? I'd like to get this right before I say it out loud in front of people in the group who've clearly read more of the primary material than I have.
