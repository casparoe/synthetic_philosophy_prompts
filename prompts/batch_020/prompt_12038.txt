Over the winter holidays, a disagreement with my brother turned into something I haven't been able to shake. Our father, from whom my brother has been estranged for over a decade, is in declining health, and I have been pressing my brother to visit him. His answer was blunt: he says he feels nothing toward our father anymore — no love, no anger, nothing at all — and that no one can be under an obligation to act for the sake of someone they are simply indifferent to, so whatever reason I think he has to go, he doesn't actually have it. A friend finishing a philosophy doctorate overheard me recounting this and told me I had wandered into a genuine dispute in the philosophy of action, over "internal" and "external" reasons, and pointed me toward Bernard Williams's essay on the subject. I've since read enough to see the outline of the disagreement, but I keep losing the thread once the argument gets technical, and I think I would follow it much better if I could watch two people who actually hold these positions fight it out over a case shaped like my brother's.

Could you write me a dialogue between an internalist about reasons, in Williams's sense, and an externalist, arguing specifically over whether the estranged son has a reason to visit his dying father even though he can locate no element of his own motivational set that going would serve? The internalist should do more than assert her conclusion: she should reconstruct something close to Williams's actual case that external reason statements, however we try to cash them out, either turn out to be disguised internal reasons or fail to be genuine reason-statements at all, collapsing instead into claims about what would be good or what morality demands. The externalist needs a real answer to that challenge, not just a restatement that the son "objectively ought" to go — something that engages with how much the internalist can already accommodate through deliberation, new information, or vividly imagining the father's position before we reach for anything external, and that explains how the son could simply be wrong about his reasons in the way he might be wrong about a fact. Neither character should be easy to beat, and each should be pressing on the other's actual weak points rather than talking past them. Let it run long enough for real back-and-forth, and end with the externalist raising the amoralist worry against the internalist — that if the brother truly has nothing in him for her view to work with, internalism seems to leave him with no reason at all, however monstrous that sounds — and give the internalist a real reply rather than a shrug. They needn't reach agreement; I would rather see the disagreement sharpened than resolved.
