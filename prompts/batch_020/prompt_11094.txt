A friend and I spent most of a long dinner arguing about the backward-induction paradox in finite games of perfect information, using the Centipede game as our test case. She treated the matter as settled by Aumann's 1995 theorem that common knowledge of rationality (CKR) implies the backward-induction outcome, and concluded that anyone who thinks a rational player might reasonably pass a few times is simply confused about what rationality requires. I wasn't satisfied, but the best I could manage on the spot was that the theorem is stated in terms of the outcome reached, not in terms of what a player must believe or do at nodes that, if CKR truly holds, are never reached -- and that this gap might be doing more work than she gave it credit for. She wasn't moved, and Binmore's name came up without either of us managing to reconstruct his actual argument against her. Please argue for the position that Aumann's theorem, properly understood, does not settle the paradox the way my friend thinks it does: that CKR entailing the backward-induction outcome is compatible with there being no well-defined answer to whether deviating from that outcome at any node would be irrational, which undermines the theorem's use as a vindication of backward induction as a requirement of rationality. Engage with the strongest form of Aumann's own defense of his result, not a simplified target.
