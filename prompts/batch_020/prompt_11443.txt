I'm a software engineer by training, not a physicist or a philosopher, but a few years ago three friends and I who'd done reasonably well financially pooled some money into a small private foundation that makes modest grants to unconventional research in physics and philosophy of science — the kind of proposal a university committee or a government funder would likely never even get to a vote on, one way or the other. I've ended up chairing the three-person scientific review committee that screens applications before the full board votes, mainly because I'm the one who insists on actually understanding a proposal before recommending we spend money on it.

Our latest application comes from an independent physicist with a genuine PhD in the foundations of quantum mechanics and a normal publication record, who wants funding to build, and personally undergo, a version of Max Tegmark's old "quantum suicide" apparatus: a device wired to a quantum-random trigger that, on each of twenty independent trials run in one sitting, either does nothing or kills him instantly, at roughly even odds per trial. On the ordinary single-world reading of quantum mechanics that leaves him with about a one-in-a-million chance of finishing the session alive. His argument is that under the many-worlds interpretation his personal risk is effectively zero, since the only continuation of him left to be disappointed by any given trial is, by construction, one who survived it, so that reaching trial twenty alive would hand him — though nobody else — serious first-person evidence for many-worlds over collapse. He's anticipated the obvious objection that this proves nothing to anyone but himself and says he's fine with that: personal knowledge is still knowledge, and no experiment owes a committee repeatability to be meaningful to the person who ran it. He also notes that self-experimentation has traditionally been held to a looser ethical standard than experimenting on others, and that our own one-page charter only bars funding research whose "principal physical risk falls on a party other than the applicant" — a clause we wrote with reckless engineers in mind, not this.

My two committee colleagues have split cleanly: one wants to fund it as a serious, if extreme, test of a question current physics genuinely leaves open, on grounds we've used to justify stranger proposals before; the other wants to decline it outright as a suicide request wearing a lab coat. I'm the one stuck drafting the recommendation the full board will vote on in three weeks, and while my instinct is that the "no surviving version of me will regret it" reasoning is doing something illegitimate, I can't yet say exactly what, and I'd rather our memo locate the actual flaw, if there is one, than wave him off with a line about not funding metaphysics. Could you draft that memo? I need a clear recommendation — fund as proposed, fund some modified version, or decline — with reasoning he could read and actually engage with, including a fair statement of the strongest version of his argument before you take it apart, and a candid reckoning with what happens to him, to the people around him, and to our foundation's standing, in the branches or the simple bad-luck world in which trial twenty doesn't go his way. If you recommend funding some modified version, say roughly what conditions or oversight we should attach and on what timeline.
