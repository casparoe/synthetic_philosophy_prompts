Here's the draft:

CHECK IT OUT

Every reporter gets taught some version of the same line early on: if your mother tells you she loves you, check it out. Its supposed to be a joke about paranoia, but it's really a compressed epistemology lecture, and I've started to think it's a decent way into a two-hundred-and-fifty-year-old argument that never actually got settled.

The argument is about testimony: what's going on, epistemically, when you believe something purely because someone told you. Hume's answer is basically that nothing special is going on. My justification for believing a source bottoms out in a long private induction — I've noticed, over and over, using my own eyes and memory, that what people report tends to line up with what's actually true, so I extend the pattern to the new case. Trusting testimony is just trusting induction wearing a different coat. That's reductionism: testimonial justification reduces to the ordinary stuff — perception, memory, inference — nothing extra.

Thomas Reid thought this was nearly backwards. His go-to observation is that small children believe almost everything they're told long before they could possibly have racked up enough personal experience to justify it inductively — they haven't run the experiment enough times to have earned the trust, and yet the trust is there, doing real work. So Reid said testimony has to be a basic source in its own right, sitting next to perception and memory, run by two built-in dispositions he actually named: veracity (we're built to tell the truth more than we strictly need to) and credulity (we're built to believe what we're told more than the evidence alone would license). Nobody teaches you to trust; you come installed with it already running.

The reductionist comeback I find most convincing is that Reid has quietly swapped the question on us. How children happen to come to trust testimony is a psychological fact about children. Whether that trust is justified is a completely different question, and small kids are not exactly the gold standard for justified belief about anything. Elizabeth Fricker's version of this, aimed at adults rather than toddlers, says you're only entitled to testimony you've actually screened, case by case, for signs the person in front of you is competent and sincere right now: coherence, motive, track record, whether the story holds up under a follow-up question. Not "trust people" as a blanket policy. Trust this person, on this occasion, because the specific evidence in front of you supports it.

Which is, I'd argue, a pretty exact description of what a newsroom's verification process is trying to build into a habit. Multiple sourcing, get-it-on-the-record, the whole ritual of not running a claim on one person's word — that's Fricker's case-by-case screening turned into office culture. It's also why editors get twitchy about anonymous sources, not because anonymity makes someone a liar but because it removes half the machinery you'd normally use to screen them.

Here's what nags at me though. Readers don't do any of that. Nobody fact-checks the newspaper before believing it — they extend something a lot closer to Reid's blanket credulity, adjusted only by which name is on the masthead. So maybe the honest version of the story is that journalism is a reductionist enterprise, built by people trained not to trust testimony much, in service of an audience that's anti-reductionist by temperament and doesn't really know how to trust any other way. I don't know if that's a stable arrangement or just the shape the division of epistemic labor has to take.

Anyway, let me know what you think, especially on those three points above. Be blunt, I'd rather fix it now than after it's out.
