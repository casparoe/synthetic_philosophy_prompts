So me and my friend Dana got into it this weekend over the classic "you're a hypocrite so you don't get to say that" move — it started because she called out our other friend Marcus for bailing on plans last minute (again), and he came back with "didn't you flake on me literally last month though." Dana's position was that this is just a deflection, basically a fallacy, and shouldn't move anyone at all; mine was that you can actually loose your standing to blame someone even while being completely right that what they did was wrong. We argued about it for like two hours and didn't really land anywhere, which is annoying timing because I'm recording an episode of my little philosophy podcast next week with a guest whose whole research program is basically this question, and I don't want to just lob her the same stuff Dana and I already chewed through.

She defends what's apparently the leading account in the literature: that hypocrites lose their standing to blame specifically because, in blaming, they're implicitly denying the equal moral standing of persons — they've carved out an exception for themselves, and it's that self-exemption (not merely sharing the flaw) that does the standing-cancelling work. Her go-to move whenever someone raises a hard case is some version of "I'm not telling you the act wasn't wrong, I'm only telling you who doesn't get to be the one to say so," which sounds tidy but I suspect lets her sidestep exactly the cases that would actually threaten the theory.

Can you draft me 9 or 10 interview questions that push past that move rather than just inviting her to restate it? I'd like: one question built around a "hypercrite" (someone who blames themselves way harder than they blame others for the same kind of thing), to test whether her theory is really tracking self-exemption or just tracking inequality generally; one on whether losing standing requires the hypocrite to actually believe/know they share the fault, or whether merely sharing it unknowingly is enough — since those sound like two pretty different theories wearing the same name; one pressing the worry that basically everyone has some inconsistent moral weighting (in-group bias, etc.), so her account might end up implying almost nobody has standing to blame anybody for anything serious; and one on whether standing, once lost, can be earned back later through renewed self-scrutiny or confession, and what that would say about whether this is really about equality at all versus something more like sincerity or track record. For each, add a follow-up keyed to whatever answer she's most likely to give. End on a question asking what kind of case would actually make her abandon the equality account for a rival theory, rather than just patch it again.
