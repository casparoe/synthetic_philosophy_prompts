A few months ago I started using one of those habit-tracking apps that lets you put real money on the line: I've told it that if I skip a scheduled run, it should take twenty dollars from my account and send it to a political cause I strongly oppose. Around the same time, my brother set up something similar the old-fashioned way — he told his regular poker group that if he ever loses more than a fixed amount in one night, they have his permission to hold onto his car keys until he's calmed down, whether he agrees in the moment or not. Wondering whether arrangements like these are actually rational, or just clever tricks we play on ourselves, sent me down a rabbit hole that ended at something called Kavka's toxin puzzle, which I first heard described on a podcast.

As I understand it, the setup is this: an eccentric billionaire shows you a vial of toxin that would make you violently ill for a day but wouldn't kill you or leave any lasting effects. He tells you that if, by midnight tonight, you genuinely intend to drink it tomorrow afternoon, he will have a million dollars sitting in your account by morning — hours before you would ever need to actually drink anything. He doesn't care what you do after that: only the intention itself, formed by midnight, decides the payout. The puzzle is that once you're actually lying there at 11:59 working through the implications, you can see that tomorrow afternoon, with the money already banked, you will have no reason whatsoever to make yourself sick — it would accomplish nothing at that point. And if you can already see now that you'll have no reason to go through with it then, it's not clear you can really manage to intend it tonight, as opposed to merely hoping you will or predicting that you might. Oddly enough, I read that Kavka developed this puzzle out of his work on nuclear deterrence and mutual assured destruction rather than anything to do with everyday willpower, which makes me suspect it's less about this one strange scenario than about threats and promises generally. (Somewhere in the same reading I also ran into Newcomb's problem, which sounded related, though I couldn't say for certain whether it's really the same issue in different clothing or something distinct.)

What I'd like from you is your sense of the most important implications of this puzzle for arrangements like mine and my brother's, where the entire point is to bind your future self to something that, once the moment actually arrives, will serve no further purpose and will only cost you. Does the puzzle imply that my running app and my brother's poker rule only work because they hand the enforcement over to something outside our own heads — the app's code, the friends holding the keys — rather than either of us genuinely succeeding at intending anything on our own? Is there a real, substantive difference between sincerely intending to follow through on a costly commitment and merely predicting or hoping that you will, or is that distinction less solid than it sounds? And does any of this reach further, into ordinary promise-keeping, where the reason you had for making a promise may simply no longer apply by the time you're called on to keep it? I'm less interested in a plain restatement of the puzzle, which I feel I already grasp, than in what you think actually follows from it.
