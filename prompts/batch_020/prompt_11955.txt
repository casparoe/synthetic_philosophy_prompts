The question was:

In Principia Ethica, G. E. Moore argues that the two greatest goods we can conceive of are personal affection between people and the appreciation of what is beautiful. Explain how Moore's "method of isolation" is meant to establish that beauty has intrinsic value, using his comparison between a beautiful world and a hideous one, neither of which contains any being capable of experiencing them. Then explain how his doctrine of organic unities accounts for the special value of actually appreciating something beautiful, and why this combination of views commits Moore to rejecting hedonism.

The answer I have been asked to grade reads as follows:

Moore's method of isolation asks us to picture something existing in absolute isolation, cut off from every other thing in the universe, and then to ask whether its bare existence would be good. He uses this method to challenge the view that only states of consciousness, such as pleasure, can possess intrinsic value. To make the challenge vivid, he asks us to compare two worlds: one as beautiful as we can imagine, full of everything lovely, and the other a chaos of ugliness, containing everything foul, on the stipulation that neither world is ever seen or experienced by any being whatsoever. Moore claims that we must still judge the beautiful world to be the better of the two, which he takes to show not only that beauty has a kind of value that does not depend on anyone actually enjoying it, but also that "good" itself must be a simple, unanalyzable, non-natural property: since the two worlds are alike in every natural respect except for their beauty, no natural property could account for the difference in value between them, so this case is really just his open-question argument applied to a particular instance.

Organic unities become important once Moore turns from this abstract case to the ordinary case of a person actually appreciating something beautiful. His principle of organic unities holds that the value of a whole need not equal the sum of the values of its parts, so a state made up of two ingredients that are each only slightly valuable on their own can, taken together, be enormously valuable. Applied here, a correct cognition of an object's beautiful qualities is, by itself, worth very little, and a feeling of admiration is worth just as little by itself, especially if it is directed at something not actually beautiful; but the union of true cognition with a fitting emotion of admiration is, Moore says, one of the most valuable things we can conceive, on a par with the mutual affection between friends.

This combination of views is clearly meant to embarrass hedonists like Sidgwick, who held that pleasure is the only thing with intrinsic value. But once we notice that Moore's own ethics is still a form of utilitarianism, on which the right act is the one that produces the greatest amount of whatever is good, his disagreement with Sidgwick looks smaller than it first appears. Moore is best read as refining hedonism rather than rejecting it outright, since he still identifies the good with a kind of pleasant experience — just one narrowed to the pleasure we take in truly beautiful objects and worthy people, rather than pleasure of any kind whatsoever.
