So I'm TAing an intro philosophy of mind seminar this term and the prof's off at a conference, so grading the take-home short-answer set landed on me (first time marking anything more substantial than multiple choice, so bare with me). One of the questions was: "Explain Keith Frankish's illusionism about phenomenal consciousness. Be sure to distinguish the 'illusion problem' from the 'hard problem,' explain how illusionism is supposed to differ from straightforward eliminativism about consciousness, and reconstruct and assess the objection that illusionism is self-undermining because it has to presuppose the very phenomenal seemings it denies."

Here's the answer one of my students handed in:

"Frankish's illusionism is the claim that phenomenal consciousness — the way experience seems to involve special, intrinsic, ineffable qualia — is an introspective illusion. When I look at a ripe tomato, it seems like there's some extra qualitative 'feel' to seeing that particular red, over and above whatever the underlying physical or functional processing is doing. Illusionism says that seeming is misleading: introspection represents our experiences as having these rich phenomenal properties, but they don't actually have them. Frankish calls the project of explaining why and how experience seems this way, given that there's no real phenomenal property to explain, the 'illusion problem,' and thinks it should replace the 'hard problem' (Chalmers' term for explaining how physical processes give rise to actual qualia) — if illusionism's right there's no hard problem left, just the (still hard, but at least tractable) problem of explaining the illusion.

This makes illusionism different from flat-out eliminativism about consciousness, since Frankish isn't saying there's no experience or introspection going on at all — he's only an eliminativist about the specific phenomenal/qualia properties experience seems to have, not about experience or introspection wholesale. Really this is the same move Dennett already made with heterophenomenology in Consciousness Explained: heterophenomenology is the thesis that phenomenal properties are illusions produced by the brain's own representational/reporting systems, so Frankish's illusionism is basically just a relabeling of Dennett's older position.

The standard objection is that illusionism eats itself: if it's an illusion that experience seems to have qualia, there has to be a real seeming for it to be an illusion of, and a 'seeming,' surely, just is a phenomenally conscious state — so the theory smuggles in the very phenomenal consciousness it denies. Frankish's reply distinguishes the representation from what it represents: there really are introspective representations (states that are characterizable functionally) that represent experience as having phenomenal properties, and those representations really exist and really are produced by ordinary cognitive processing. What's illusory is only that experience actually has the properties represented — so 'seeming' doesn't have to mean phenomenal seeming, just false representational content. Frankish, who actually defends what he calls the weak version of illusionism (on which some genuine, if far less exotic than assumed, phenomenal residue survives) rather than a harder-line strong illusionism, thinks this is enough to block the objection."
