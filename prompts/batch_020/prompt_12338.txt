So this is going to sound like a weird rabbit hole but bear with me. A while back I got kind of obsessed with prediction markets and why some people are consistently better forecasters than others, and that eventually dragged me into this thing from game theory/economics called the "common prior assumption." As far as I understand it: if two rational people start out with literally the same prior beliefs about the world, and each one just updates fairly on whatever private info they happen to get, then once they both know each others opinions (and know that the other knows, and so on forever) they straight up can't end up disagreeing anymore. I think this is Aumann's theorem or close to it. It kind of blew my mind, because obviously in real life smart, honest people disagree about stuff constantly even after hashing it out for hours, so somewhere that whole setup has to be breaking down once you point it at actual humans.

I tried writing up my own guesses about where exactly it breaks down, mostly just to get my thoughts straight, and I'd genuinely like someone who actually knows this stuff to poke holes in it. Roughly what I've got:

1. Maybe nobody ever really shares a common prior to begin with. Priors come from your temperament, upbringing, whatever, not handed down from nowhere, so there's no reason two perfectly honest people would start in the same spot, and that's not really irrational of either of them.
2. Maybe the priors are close enough, but nobody actually updates like an idealized Bayesian anyway, we're all running on biased, tired brains, so the theorem's conclusion never really applied to us in the first place.
3. Maybe the real problem is the "common knowledge" part specifically. The theorem needs each person to fully know the other's opinion, know that they know it, know that they know that they know it, basically forever, and that never actually happens in a normal argument, even a really good faith one.

My gut says its mostly number 3 doing the heavy lifting, with number 2 close behind, but I honestly can't tell if that's a sensible way to carve up the problem or if there's some standard explanation people who actually study this go with instead. Could you go through it like you're grading it: are these even the right three buckets, is there a view most people in this area actually lean toward, and am I misstating what Aumann's result actually says anywhere in there?
