ok so this happened at a decision theory reading group two friends of mine run -- they'd just gone through a bunch of posts on Newcomblike problems and moved on to backward induction and the centipede game, and then spent something like 45 minutes going back and forth on it without resolving anything. I mostly lurk in that group so I didn't weigh in, but it's been stuck in my head since.

(1) Dana opened with the standard backward-induction case: at the very last node the mover strictly prefers taking the bigger pile, so the second-to-last mover should anticipate that and take too, and so on all the way back up the tree -- so the "rational" outcome, given common knowledge of rationality, is player 1 defecting on move 1 and torching the whole pot, no matter how many rounds are on the board.

(2) Jae said thats basically a reductio: if two "irrational" trusting players walk away with way more than two "rational" ones, for literally any length of the game, somethings broken in calling that outcome uniquely rational. He also pushed on the chain itself: it only goes through if you assume common knowledge of rationality all the way down, but the moment your opponent passes at a node where backward induction said they'd defect, that's direct evidence against the very premise the rest of your backward reasoning depends on -- so what licenses you to keep leaning on that premise for the rest of the tree?

(3) Dana's response was two-pronged: rationality isn't about which outcome has more total surplus, it's about whether each move is a best response given what you can infer about the other player, full stop -- and the "self-undermining" worry is basically what trembling-hand reasoning is for, you treat an out-of-line pass as noise on top of the rational benchmark rather than evidence that overturns it.

(4) Jae's comeback: appealing to noise quietly gives up the thing backward induction was supposed to deliver, a clean prediction of what a rational player does, because once you grant that mistakes are possible and both players know it, a rational player should be conditioning on that from move one, and doing so can easily make cooperating for a while the actual best response -- which, he pointed out, is close to how people really play in actual centipede game experiments.

They had to bail before it went anywhere. Can you referee this move by move -- who's got the better of it at (1), (2), (3), and (4), not just an overall winner -- and say what, if anything, they're both missing about the backward induction paradox?
