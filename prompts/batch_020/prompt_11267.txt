I'm a software engineer in my early forties, and over the past couple of years I've watched several senior colleagues get managed out during "reorgs," with an unspoken subtext that never quite makes it into the performance review but is clear enough from Slack threads and hallway conversations: that they're too slow to pick up new frameworks, too expensive compared with a twenty-five-year-old willing to work eighty-hour weeks for a fraction of the salary, or simply "not as hungry" anymore. Trying to work out whether any of that holds up, or is just prejudice dressed up as pragmatism, I ended up reading Cicero's "De Senectute" ("On Old Age") on a friend's recommendation, since it was apparently written as a point-by-point rebuttal of exactly this kind of case against the elderly. I have no formal background in philosophy or classics, so correct me if I'm oversimplifying, but as I understand it, Cicero puts the argument in the mouth of Cato the Elder, who organizes the whole dialogue around four standard complaints against old age — that it withdraws men from active affairs, weakens the body, strips away almost all pleasure, and is a short step from death — and answers each in turn, leaning on examples like Fabius Maximus's patient generalship against Hannibal, the blind and ancient Appius Claudius Caecus still swaying the Senate, Cato's own late-life study of Greek literature, and Sophocles supposedly rebutting a charge of senile incompetence by reading his latest tragedy aloud to a jury.

What I'd like from you is a reconstruction, worked out inside Cato's own framework rather than in generic modern terms, of how he would respond specifically to ageism in the software industry. Please take his four complaints one at a time, propose the closest tech-industry analogue for each (for instance, "withdrawal from active affairs" might correspond to being quietly rotated off customer-facing or high-visibility projects rather than formally let go), and then reconstruct, in something like Cato's own rhetorical style and with the same kind of exemplar he favors, the argument he would actually make against that specific analogue. I'm especially curious about where you think his case genuinely holds up against the industry's particular obsession with constantly relearning tools and frameworks under threat of obsolescence, versus where it would need real modification, or might simply fail, once it leaves the world of Roman oratory, warfare, and farming. Please flag those weak points explicitly rather than smoothing them over for the sake of a tidy argument.
