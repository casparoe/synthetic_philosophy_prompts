My colleague Marion died two weeks ago, completely unexpectedly, and the department's holding a memorial for her this Friday that's going to be a genuinely strange room: half of it is metaphysicians who've spent thirty years arguing with her at the APA about temporal parts, and the other half is her sister, her nieces, and neighbors who think "four-dimensionalism" is a type of yoga. I've been asked to give the main remarks, six or seven minutes, and I've been rereading her old seminar handouts trying to get the tone right, which is honestly just making it worse.

Marion built her whole career on perdurantism -- the view that persisting things aren't wholly present at every moment the way the endurantist says, but are more like four-dimensional "worms" made of temporal parts, with the problem of temporary intrinsics (how one thing can be a straight-backed young woman and a bent old one) as basically her signature puzzle, the one I still put on my grad seminar syllabus every year because of her. She was also famous in the department for being allergic to sloppy consolation-talk -- I watched her interrupt someone at a funeral once, very gently, for saying the deceased was "watching over us now." So here's my actual problem: on her own view there's no three-dimensional Marion who was wholly here and is now wholly gone, there's a Marion-worm that just reached its far edge two weeks ago, and I don't want to say one sentence at that podium she'd have footnoted a correction onto. I'm also definately avoiding the easy move, which is just reaching for the Einstein-to-Besso letter about past and future being "a stubbornly persistent illusion" -- she used to get genuinely annoyed when people mashed eternalism about time together with her actual thesis about parts and identity, like they were the same claim, when to her they were totally seperate.

So what I actually need is the eulogy itself. Something that uses her real metaphysics honestly to say something true, and if at all possible comforting, about what her death is and isn't on her own terms; that gives her four-dimensionalist friends in the second row one moment where they know exactly what she'd have said back (a joke about Heraclitus and the river would be very on brand if you can make it land); and that never once requires her sister to have taken a metaphysics class to be allowed to grieve properly.
