While drafting the preface to my next novel, I noticed that I hold two beliefs which sit uneasily together: I believe of each factual claim in the manuscript that it is true, yet I also believe, from ordinary experience of how such books fare, that at least one claim in it is false. A friend trained in philosophy tells me this is an instance of the "preface paradox," and that it is, at bottom, the same difficulty as the "lottery paradox": the holder of a single ticket in a large, fair lottery is confident that her own ticket will lose, yet equally confident that some ticket will win. Before I borrow this comparison for the novel itself, I should like to know whether it survives scrutiny. Compare the two paradoxes closely enough to say whether they truly share one underlying structure, or whether the purely statistical grounds for doubting a lottery ticket differ in some principled way from the grounds on which I trust my own sentences — and say, with reasons, whether the gambler and I are equally entitled to hold on to both of our beliefs, or whether one of us ought to give way.
