My grandfather died nine days ago, and I've been asked to give his eulogy this Saturday at the small Lutheran church where he was baptized as a baby; there will be a reception afterward at the VFW post he belonged to for almost fifty years. He was drafted into the Army in 1968 at nineteen, served a tour as an infantryman in the Central Highlands, came home to a country in no mood to thank him for it, and spent the rest of his life as a machinist, a church deacon, and, by his own cheerful admission, the worst fisherman in three counties. Everyone but my grandmother called him Ski, short for a surname no one outside the family could spell. I loved him without reservation and have far more stories than I'll have time to tell.

The complication is that I'm halfway through a university seminar on the ethics of war, and we just spent two weeks on what's called the moral equality of combatants. The traditionalist position, which goes back to Michael Walzer, holds that soldiers on either side of a war are equally entitled to fight and equally liable to be killed so long as they observe the rules of engagement, regardless of which side's cause is actually just. The revisionist position, associated with Jeff McMahan, denies this: combatants fighting in a war that lacks a just cause typically have no right to kill at all, even though most of them -- being non-culpably ignorant of the relevant facts, or having been coerced into service to begin with -- are not really to blame for fighting anyway. I no longer have the luxury of pretending I haven't thought about which description fits my grandfather. About six months before he died, apropos of nothing, he said almost exactly that himself: "I still don't know if we should've been there. I know I'd do the same thing again for the men next to me."

Here is my actual problem. Roughly half of Saturday's congregation will be his old squadmates and their families, for whom "a soldier's job is to fight, and the cause is somebody else's department" is not a claim up for debate so much as a settled article of loyalty, and I have no wish to unsettle it at his funeral. But two or three people from my seminar are coming to support me, and if I quietly help myself to the traditionalist line as though it simply weren't contestable, or act as though the question of the war's justice had never crossed my mind, they'll know exactly what I'm doing, and so will I. I don't want to deliver a lecture on jus ad bellum from the lectern, and I don't want to say anything I would have to walk back if one of them asked me afterward whether I really meant it. Could you write the eulogy for me? It should run five to seven minutes, work in his own words above somewhere near the close, and find a way to honor both his courage and his devotion to his men without committing me, in either direction, to a claim about whether the war itself was just.
