I cover science and ideas for a regional newspaper and also sit on our newsroom's editorial standards committee, which vets ethically contested stories before they run. We're deciding what to do with a source: an epidemiologist at the state health department who broke its confidentiality rules on unpublished findings by leaking internal emails showing that political appointees had ordered a report on groundwater contamination near a local town watered down before release. She has since resigned and insisted on being named in our story rather than treated as an anonymous source, telling us that staying hidden would defeat the point -- she wants to be seen answering for breaking the rules, because that, to her, is what makes this civil disobedience rather than a firing offense dressed up as principle. Half the committee thinks her self-description is beside the point and that we should decide purely on newsworthiness, verification, and our own legal exposure; the other half thinks that if she is right about what she is doing, it should shape how we name her, whether we help fund her legal defense, and how the story itself characterizes her actions. Draft the committee's decision memo: it should give a concrete recommendation, with a timeline, on whether to publish and how to handle her identity, and it should engage seriously with the strongest case for the side it doesn't take, rather than simply dismissing it.
