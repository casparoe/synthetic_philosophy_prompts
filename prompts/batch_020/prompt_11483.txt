My grandmother turns ninety next month, and I am giving the toast at her birthday dinner. She taught Latin and Roman history for forty years, and although she has grown physically frail and no longer travels or lectures, she still spends every morning tending her garden and has never, in my hearing, complained about old age in the way the very old are expected to. For decades she has instead quoted Cicero's De Senectute at anyone who will listen, treating it as something closer to scripture than to consolation literature. The guest list is a curious mix: three or four of her former doctoral students, now classicists in their own right, are flying in and will notice immediately if I mangle Cato's argument, while most of the table will be grandchildren, cousins, and neighbors who have never read a word of Cicero and simply want to feel something.

I would like the toast organized around the four charges that Cicero has Cato answer in turn — that old age removes us from active affairs, weakens the body, strips away most of our pleasures, and brings us near to death — using that structure to say something true about her present life rather than something merely nice. She has always insisted that the cheap comfort people offer the old ("you're only as old as you feel," "age is just a number") is precisely what Cato refuses to say, since his whole argument depends on old age being genuinely different from youth and still worth having on its own terms; the toast should not lapse into that cliché even in passing. The fourth charge, concerning the nearness of death, cannot be avoided at a ninetieth birthday, but my grandmother does not share Cato's hope of one day rejoining the company of great souls, so that closing movement will need real delicacy: it should neither put a belief in her mouth that is not hers nor flatten the argument into mere stoic resignation. The toast should reward the former students who will recognize every move from the text while still landing, unassisted, for relatives who have never heard of Cato. Please write it, aiming for about four or five minutes at the lectern.
