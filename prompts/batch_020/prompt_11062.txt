Zahavi's handicap principle is often glossed as the claim that a signal can be a reliable indicator of quality only if it is costly to produce, since cost is precisely what deters low-quality individuals from bluffing. Yet Grafen's game-theoretic vindication of the principle actually rests on a condition of differential marginal cost (or cost-benefit ratio) across signalers, not on the signal being costly for its bearer at the resulting equilibrium — a gap that later critics, most notably Számadó, have pressed in diagnosing a "fallacy" in the received view that cost as such guarantees honesty. Argue that this revisionist reading is correct: that cost, in the sense Zahavi originally emphasized, is neither necessary nor sufficient for honest signaling to be stable, and that the real stabilizing work is done by the incentive structure facing signalers of differing quality. Your argument should explain how this claim is compatible with honest signals being cheap, or even free, to their senders at equilibrium, and should confront the objection that once cost is demoted in this way, "handicap" becomes a misnomer for whatever the principle is actually describing.
