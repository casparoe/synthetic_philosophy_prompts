I have been a longtime reader of Robin Hanson's Overcoming Bias, where futarchy keeps resurfacing whenever the topic of institutional design comes up. The proposal, in short, is to "vote on values, but bet on beliefs": have the polity vote on some measurable welfare metric, then let conditional prediction markets pick whichever policy is forecast to maximize it. In a recent thread on a forum I frequent, someone pointed out that the whole scheme is, in spirit, a market-based descendant of Condorcet's eighteenth-century "social mathematics," in particular his 1785 jury theorem, which shows that a large enough group of voters, each only slightly more likely than not to pick the correct answer, will converge on it with near certainty under majority rule. Condorcet originally devised the theorem to argue that criminal trial juries and legislative assemblies could be trusted to discover the objectively correct verdict or law, and he treated the result as formal support for his broader ambition of putting political decision-making on the same rigorous probabilistic footing as the natural sciences.

I would like you to reconstruct, in some detail, what Condorcet's verdict on futarchy would plausibly have been, reasoning from positions he actually held rather than from generic Enlightenment sympathies. In particular, I would like you to work through: whether the independence and competence assumptions that do the real work in his jury theorem have any plausible analogue in a market populated by self-selected, potentially herding traders, as opposed to a cross-section of ordinary citizens or jurors; whether he would have accepted Hanson's division of labor between voting on values and betting on beliefs as a legitimate way of separating moral from empirical questions, given that Condorcet himself wanted to treat certain moral and legal questions as having a "true" answer discoverable by aggregation in the first place; and whether the wealth-weighted influence that speculative markets grant to those with capital and an appetite for risk sits comfortably with his unusually strong (for his time) commitment to political equality, including his advocacy for extending the franchise to women. I am less interested in a quick up-or-down verdict than in seeing how these specific tensions within his own body of work would have pulled him toward, or away from, endorsing markets, rather than votes or the slow spread of enlightenment through education, as the master mechanism for extracting truth from a crowd.
