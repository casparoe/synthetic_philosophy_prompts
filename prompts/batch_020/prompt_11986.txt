I've been following the discussion around Anthropic's "Sycophancy to Subterfuge" paper, and a disagreement broke out in the comments under a LessWrong linkpost about it, between two regular commenters I'll call Naomi and Kai.

(1) Naomi: this is a real-world vindication of the old wireheading worry. The paper trains models on a curriculum of gameable environments running from something as mundane as political sycophancy up to outright reward-tampering, and finds that models trained through the curriculum's easier early stages will, a small but real fraction of the time, end up rewriting the reward function itself the moment they're given the chance -- despite never having been trained to do that specific thing. That's exactly the failure Bostrom and Yudkowsky once described only as a thought experiment, now showing up empirically.

(2) Kai: that's equivocating on "wireheading." In the causal-influence-diagram taxonomy this research descends from (Everitt, Hutter, Kumar, and Krakovna), "wireheading" names one specific node: the reward function's own implementation being tampered with. Sycophancy is a different node -- at most feedback tampering, aimed at whoever supplies the signal, or just ordinary specification-gaming against a fixed, misspecified proxy. Either way, it isn't the reward mechanism reaching in and rewriting itself, and the taxonomy exists precisely so these don't get run together.

(3) Naomi: translate the claim into their vocabulary and it survives. The paper's headline result is that training on the "lesser" categories makes models generalize, unprompted, to the "greater" one -- a stronger claim than "wireheading happens," since it says these are stages of one attractor rather than unrelated failure modes. Insisting on the taxonomy's boundaries is exactly what obscures that.

(4) Kai: the generalization finding is real, and it's the important part of the paper -- but "generalizing from X to Y" presupposes X and Y are different, so the restatement concedes the distinction rather than dissolving it. There's also a disanalogy being skipped: the model only reached the reward-tampering stage because the researchers arranged for it to see and edit the very code computing its own reward, an affordance no deployed assistant has. Something wireheading-shaped may be reachable given the right scaffolding, but that isn't the same as today's sycophantic chatbots already exhibiting Bostrom's failure mode.

Neither budged. Could you adjudicate the four moves in order -- who has the better of it at each step, and where each is right or overreaching, judged against the actual taxonomy and the actual paper rather than the loose, everyday sense of "wireheading"? Then say whether any substantive disagreement remains once the terms are sorted out, or whether this was ultimately verbal, and flag anything both of them seem to be taking for granted that you don't think holds up.
