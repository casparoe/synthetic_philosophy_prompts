"H.L.A. Hart argued that many disputes between retributivists and utilitarians about punishment arise from a failure to distinguish different questions that a theory of punishment must answer. (a) Explain Hart's distinction between the 'General Justifying Aim' of punishment and questions of 'Distribution.' And here is her answer:

"Hart's central move in the Prolegomenon is to point out that 'Why do we have the practice of punishment at all?' and 'Who may be punished, and how much?' are two separate questions, and that theorists on both sides had been arguing past one another by assuming that a single theory had to answer both at once.

(a) For the first question -- the General Justifying Aim -- Hart is a utilitarian: the practice of punishment as a whole is justified by its beneficial consequences, chiefly the prevention of crime through deterrence, incapacitation, and reform. He is explicit that he does not think the mere infliction of suffering on a wrongdoer is good in itself, and he has little patience for the idea that pain simply owed to the guilty could justify the practice on its own. (He develops all of this at greater length in The Concept of Law.)

For the second question -- Distribution -- Hart says that liability to punishment must be restricted to actual offenders, and that the amount of punishment must be proportioned to the offence. These look like retributive constraints, but Hart insists we don't need to ground them in the idea that guilty people deserve to suffer; they can instead be defended by appeal to the value of a system in which individuals can know the rules in advance and choose whether to break them, which protects individual liberty and fairness.

(b) This is what lets Hart answer the classic utilitarian-punishes-the-innocent objection: because Distribution is governed by its own principles rather than being read straight off the General Justifying Aim, a utilitarian legislator isn't committed to punishing whoever it would happen to be useful to punish in a given case. This is really the same solution Rawls reaches in 'Two Concepts of Rules': once you see that punishment is a practice defined by rules rather than a series of one-off, utility-maximizing acts, the fact that framing an innocent man might maximize utility in one case is beside the point, because it's the practice as a whole -- including the rule that only the guilty may be sentenced -- that has to be justified on utilitarian grounds. So Hart's Distribution requirement just is Rawls's practice conception applied to punishment, and the two of them are offering the same solution, from within utilitarianism, to the same problem.

(c) The obvious objection is that this is more of a practical fix than a proof that the conflict can never arise: since the General Justifying Aim is still utilitarian at bottom, nothing rules out the possibility that in some extreme case, sticking to the Distribution principles would be so costly in prevented crime that the utilitarian aim would recommend overriding them anyway. So Hart's theory is a genuine hybrid, but it doesn't show that retributive-style constraints could never be swamped by a sufficiently large utilitarian payoff, which is exactly the guarantee a committed retributivist wants and thinks utilitarianism can never honestly give."
