Aristotle's discussion, in De Interpretatione 9, of whether it is already true that there will be a sea battle tomorrow gave rise to two enduring and opposed responses within the tense-logic tradition descending from Arthur Prior, usually labeled the "Ockhamist" and "Peircean" solutions to the problem of future contingents. I'd like to see this disagreement dramatized rather than merely summarized.

Please write a dialogue set on the night before a naval engagement, between two philosophers attached to the admiral's staff who get drawn into the dispute after the admiral wonders aloud whether tomorrow's outcome is already settled and his deliberations tonight are therefore pointless. One philosopher should argue the Ockhamist line: the sentence "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" is already either true or false tonight, fixed by the one course of history that will actually unfold, but the admiral needn't worry, since truth about a contingent matter is not the same thing as necessity, so his deliberations still matter. The other should argue the Peircean line: the sentence is not true yet, and indeed is simply false tonight, because a future-tensed claim only becomes true once the present state of things already settles the matter one way or the other, and it is precisely the admiral's deliberations, among other things, that will help settle it.

Make sure the two genuinely engage each other rather than talk past one another: the Ockhamist should be pressed to say what could possibly make one among all the still-open courses of events count as "the" actual future before anything has happened to distinguish it (the so-called thin-red-line problem), while the Peircean should be pressed to explain why, once the battle actually takes place, it feels wrong to say that the night before it was simply false that a battle would occur. Write both philosophers as equally sharp, each capable of anticipating and blocking the other's objections, so that neither position comes off as the obvious loser.
