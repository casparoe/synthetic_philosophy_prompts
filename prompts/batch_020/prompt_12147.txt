Last month, at the start of somatic-focused therapy for anxiety, I did an interoception exercise that involved silently counting my own heartbeats over a set interval without checking my pulse; my confidence in having counted correctly turned out to bear almost no relation to how close I actually came, once we checked my count against a pulse oximeter. Looking into why that kind of gap might be structural rather than just a correctable bias led me to Timothy Williamson's anti-luminosity argument in Knowledge and Its Limits, and its further use there against the KK principle — but every exposition I can find leans on one of his own examples, such as the overnight drift from feeling freezing cold at dawn to feeling hot by noon, or short-sighted Mr. Magoo misjudging a tree's height. I'd like a genuinely different example: choose a proposition p and a subject who, at one point along a series of closely spaced cases, knows that p, and work the margin-for-error reasoning through explicitly enough to derive — rather than merely assert — that the subject is nonetheless not in a position to know that they know p. Lay out the series of cases and the margin-for-error premise itself, and say where a defender of KK could most plausibly resist a step in your version.
