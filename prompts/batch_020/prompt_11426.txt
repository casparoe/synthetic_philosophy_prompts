I am taking an upper-level seminar this term on realism and anti-realism about truth, and this week's unit on Dummett-style semantic anti-realism led us into Fitch's paradox of knowability. The professor worked through the derivation on the board, and a few of us went over it again afterward in office hours: every inference checks out when followed step by step, but none of us could really say why the argument works, in the sense of having any intuitive feel for where the paradox actually bites.

As I understand the setup, the argument rests on two premises that each sound individually quite modest. The "knowability thesis" holds that every truth is at least possible to know, formally ∀p (p → ◊Kp); this is meant to be close to a minimal commitment for anyone who denies that truth can radically outrun our capacity for evidence. "Non-omniscience" holds that at least one truth is not, in fact, presently known by anyone: ∃p (p ∧ ¬Kp). Fitch's proof shows that these two premises, together with only two very weak-looking assumptions about knowledge — that knowing a conjunction requires knowing each conjunct, and that knowledge is factive — are jointly inconsistent, so that the knowability thesis by itself effectively commits you to the claim that every truth is actually known, which is exactly the conclusion that distinguishing "knowable" from "known" was supposed to let you avoid. The crucial move, as far as I can tell, is substituting the sentence "p and nobody knows that p" into the knowability thesis as an instance of its own bound variable. One classmate suggested this had to be some cousin of Moore's paradox (the oddity of sincerely asserting "p, but I do not believe that p"), but could not really spell out the connection, and it has been bothering me since.

Could you explain, in an intuitive rather than purely symbolic way, what is really going on with that substitution — why it is specifically self-undermining in a manner that makes an ordinary unknown truth suddenly impossible even to hypothetically know — and say something about whether the Moore's-paradox comparison is apt? I would also be curious why this result is treated as a distinctive problem for verificationists and anti-realists about truth specifically, rather than as a general embarrassment for anyone willing to grant that some truths are merely possible to know.
