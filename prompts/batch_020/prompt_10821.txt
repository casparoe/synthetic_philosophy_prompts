The question was: "Explain why a counterfactual analysis of causation that allows absences/omissions to count as causal relata runs into the so-called problem of profligate omissions. Then explain ONE strategy philosophers have used to restrict which omissions count as genuine causes, and assess whether that strategy manages to avoid the problem without simply turning causation into a normative matter." Here's what the student wrote, in full:

"Under a bare counterfactual analysis, C is a cause of E just in case E counterfactually depends on C — roughly, E wouldn't have occurred if C hadn't (holding the laws and other background conditions fixed). This licenses omissions as causal relata: if the gardener had watered the plant, it wouldn't have died, so we seem forced to say his not-watering it caused its death. The trouble is the very same test is satisfied by all sorts of other omissions — my not watering the plant, or the Queen of England's not flying over to water it, since in each case it's equally true that if that person had watered it, it would have lived. So the analysis can't distinguish the gardener's omission, which we want to count as a genuine cause, from all the other 'idle' non-doings that trivially satisfy the same counterfactual. This is the problem of profligate omissions: unless the test can somehow be restricted, literally everyone who didn't water the plant ends up a cause of its death, which is absurd.

The most natural first move is to notice that Lewis already had a distinction built in for ordinary cases: he separates 'a cause' from 'THE cause,' allowing that lots of events are, strictly speaking, causes of E even though conversationally we only ever cite the salient one. On this view the profligacy problem isn't a new problem at all, it's just the omission-flavored version of the familiar fact that any effect has many strict causes. So Lewis can say the Queen's not-watering the plant really is, in the strict counterfactual sense, just as much a cause of its death as the gardener's — it's simply never salient enough to mention, since she has no special relation to the plant. Because this pragmatic machinery was already sitting in the theory for ordinary redundant causation, nothing extra is needed to cover the omissive case.

A different strategy, due to McGrath, restricts the causal omissions to just those that depart from a normative or otherwise expected default: the gardener was supposed to water the plant, so his omission is a deviation and counts as a cause, while the Queen's inaction is the expected default and so doesn't. This gets the intuitively correct verdict without the profligacy. The cost, which is fairly obvious, is that facts about causation — which we'd like to be purely natural, non-normative facts — end up depending on facts about obligation or proper function, so whether an omission caused the plant's death depends on something like the terms of the gardener's employment, which seems like the wrong sort of fact to be doing metaphysical work."
