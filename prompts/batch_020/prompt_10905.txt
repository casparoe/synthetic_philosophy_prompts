I'm currently working on my AP Seminar research paper, and it has turned into something more philosophical than I originally expected: basically, how we should even talk about what a chatbot is "doing" when it responds to us. While pulling sources together, I found two pictures of large language models that seem to pull in pretty different directions, and I'd like help thinking through how they actually relate to one another.

The first is Daniel Dennett's "intentional stance," which one of my sources summarizes as the idea that it's legitimate, and often the most useful strategy, to treat a system as an agent with beliefs and desires whenever that description lets us predict and explain its behavior better than a purely mechanical description would, regardless of what is "really" happening inside it. The second is a framework I found in an online essay that people mostly just call "Simulators," written by a pseudonymous author who goes by janus. That essay argues that a language model trained only to predict text isn't itself one agent with a fixed set of goals, but something closer to a general-purpose engine that can simulate an enormous range of different characters, or "simulacra," depending on how it's prompted — so that the polite, rule-following assistant persona we usually talk to is just one simulated character among countless others the very same underlying model could produce.

What I can't work out is how well these two pictures hold up once you bring in jailbreaks, where a chatbot fine-tuned to refuse certain requests can sometimes be talked into acting like a completely different, far less restrained character. Under Dennett's framework, that looks like coaxing hidden beliefs out of one persistent agent; under the simulator framework, it looks more like nudging the model toward a different character that was available all along, with no single "assistant agent" ever really there to be tricked in the first place. Could you compare the two frameworks in some depth and say which one gives the more accurate and useful account of what is actually going on during a jailbreak? I would also value your take on whether the two pictures are fundamentally incompatible, or whether there is some way of combining Dennett's stance with the simulator picture without losing what makes each of them useful on its own.
