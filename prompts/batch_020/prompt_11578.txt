Growing up I was in a pretty evangelical church, and the one doctrine that always bugged me, even as a kid, was penal substitution — the idea that Jesus got punished so the rest of us wouldn't have to. It never sat right with me. Like if my brother breaks a window and I take the spanking instead, nothing about the actual wrong gets fixed, the guy whose window it was doesn't feel like justice happened, he just feels like some random kid got hit. Years later I fell down an internet rabbit hole reading about possible worlds and multiverse stuff and found out David Lewis, the modal realism guy, who as far as I know wasn't even religious, wrote an actual paper called "Do We Believe in Penal Substitution?" where he tries dead seriously to work out whether the doctrine can even be made logically coherent as a theory of punishment. I read through it (some of it went over my head not gonna lie) and the thing that stuck with me is he doesnt really commit either way, he just sort of leaves it hanging at the end.

Me and two friends do a casual recorded call every couple weeks where we argue about stuff like this, basically a homemade podcast nobody but us listens to, and next time our guest is a guy who's taken Lewis's careful, hedgy sketch and turned it into a full-on defense, way more confident than Lewis himself ever was. His go-to move whenever anyone pushes on how the punishment actually gets moved off the guilty person and onto Christ is to say "that's not a bug, that's what makes it grace and not just a legal trick" and then change the subject. I don't want him to get away with that this time. Can you write me 8 to 10 interview questions that actually dig into the mechanics, with at least one question each on: the difference between someone paying off your debt versus someone else serving your prison sentence for you, whether the whole theory secretly needs you to already be a retributivist (believing people deserve to suffer, not just that punishment deters crime) and falls apart otherwise, why Christ's suffering counts as suffering OUR specific punishment instead of just being some unrelated bad thing that happened to him, and whether leaning on "convention" to bridge that gap is a real answer or just quietly borrowing credibility from Lewis's totally separate famous work on conventions. Throw in the whipping-boy type precedents he likes to cite too if you can fit it in. For each question add a quick note on the answer he'll probably give and a sharper follow-up, and maybe end on something about what it would even take for him to admit the theory doesn't work, since so far every objection just gets relabeled as "part of the mystery of grace."
