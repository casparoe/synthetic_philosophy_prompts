The setup: you're dying of thirst out in the desert, a driver pulls up and says she'll only take you into town if you promise to pay her $1000 once you arrive, and she's supposedly almost impossible to fool, so she wont pick you up unless she's actually convinced you mean it. Here's the kicker though - once you're safely in town, there's no gun to your head anymore, and handing over the money doesn't get you anything back, so it looks like the "rational" move is to just walk away and keep your cash. But if that's what a truly rational person would do, the driver, who's no idiot, would never have picked you up to begin with, so you'd have just died out there. So weirdly, it's the people who'd go ahead and pay even with nothing forcing them who are the ones who actually make it to town alive.

The part that really got me was realizing this connects to something Parfit apparently calls "rational irrationality" - basically the idea that it can be perfectly rational to turn yourself into the kind of person who will do something that looks irrational in the moment, because having that disposition is exactly what saves you, even though the act of paying, judged purely on its own once you're already safe, still looks like a bad deal by ordinary self-interested lights. That gap between "rational to become" and "rational to do, once you're there" feels like the whole crux of it, and I honestly can't tell on my own which of the "official" theories of rational choice out there actually resolve that tension versus just quietly papering over it.
