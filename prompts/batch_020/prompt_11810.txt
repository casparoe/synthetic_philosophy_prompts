ok so I fell down a Hobbes rabbit hole this week after a comment thread compared him to Semmelweis as one of those "they laughed at him but he was right all along" cases — specifically about the whole squaring-the-circle saga with John Wallis. Quick version for anyone who hasn't run into it: sometime in the 1650s Hobbes claimed in De Corpore that he'd squared the circle, Wallis (the actual Oxford geometry professor) tore his proof apart, and the two of them spent something like two decades publishing increasingly vicious pamphlets at each other that basically didn't stop til Hobbes died. The thing that jumped out at me is that Wallis pretty much admitted his motives weren't purely mathematical — he wanted to discredit Hobbes's materialist philosophy generally, which he saw as atheism wearing a lab coat. Given that, plus the fact that nobody actually *proved* squaring the circle was impossible until Lindemann's transcendence-of-pi result in 1882, doesn't that mean 17th century mathematicians were in no real position to be so certain Hobbes's specific construction was wrong, as opposed to it just being an open problem that people had strong priors about? It kinda reads to me like the standard "Wallis obviously won" story is retroactive vindication of what was really more of a political/status hit job that happened to land on the correct side of history two centuries early — basically a mirror image of the Galileo gambit.

I'm also curious how this whole fight connects to Hobbes's bigger philosophical commitments about geometry — was his 20-year refusal to back down just personal stubbornness/ego, or does it actually trace back to something load-bearing in his view that all knowledge, including civil philosophy, needed to be built the same deductive way as geometry? Would love for someone who actually knows this history to help me seperate the real math/philosophy from the pattern-matching I'm doing here, because right now I honestly can't tell if I'm onto something or just forcing Hobbes into a narrative that doesn't fit him.
