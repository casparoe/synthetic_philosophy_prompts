Reconstruct his argument for why lending at interest is unnatural, and explain how it connects to his discussion of money as a measure of value in Nicomachean Ethics V.5.

Here's the student's answer, copied exactly:

Aristotle splits the art of acquiring goods into two kinds. The first, which he treats as a natural part of managing a household (oikonomia), just aims at getting the goods a family actually needs to live well, and since real needs are limited, this kind of acquisition is naturally self-limiting. The second kind, chrematistike, is the art of making money as an end in itself, and once currency exists this can go on forever, since there's no natural cap on how many coins someone can want. Aristotle thinks this second kind is a corruption of the first: people start out trying to get money so they can buy what they need and end up treating the money itself as the goal.

Within chrematistike, Aristotle singles out lending at interest as the worst offender, calling it the most unnatural way of making money. His reasoning is that currency was only ever meant to be a stand-in for the goods being traded – in the Ethics he says money is a common measure that lets us compare a pair of shoes to a house, standing in for the labour that went into producing each one, so exchanges can be fair. Interest breaks this because it makes money generate more money directly, with no goods or labour changing hands at all – money "breeding," as Aristotle puts it, since tokos literally means offspring, which is why he thinks its the most contrary to nature of all the ways people make a living. This idea fed pretty directly into medieval canon law's total ban on usury, and you can still hear an echo of it whenever people distinguish the "real economy" from "making money out of money."
