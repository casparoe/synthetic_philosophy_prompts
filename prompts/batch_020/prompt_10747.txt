Question (20 points). The Sorites paradox for "bald" arises from the following three claims: (1) a man with 0 hairs on his head is bald; (2) for any n, if a man with n hairs is bald, then a man with n+1 hairs is bald; (3) a man with 100,000 hairs is not bald. Since (1) and (2) jointly entail, by repeated applications of modus ponens, the negation of (3), at least one of these claims must be rejected, and the natural candidate is (2). (a) Explain how epistemicism and supervaluationism each justify rejecting (2), and how each assigns a truth-value (or lack thereof) to a borderline instance such as "a man with 5,000 hairs is bald." [7 points] (b) Explain whether each view preserves bivalence and whether each preserves the law of excluded middle. Your answer should make clear how these two issues can diverge. [7 points] (c) Which of the two views offers a more satisfactory treatment of higher-order vagueness — that is, the apparent vagueness of expressions like "borderline bald" and "definitely bald" themselves — and why? [6 points]

Student's answer: The Sorites paradox trades on the fact that predicates like "bald" seem to admit of no sharp cutoff, yet each premise looks true and the argument form is valid, so we are forced toward an absurd conclusion. Epistemicism and supervaluationism agree that the paradox should be blocked by denying the inductive premise, but they disagree about why we are entitled to deny it.

Epistemicists such as Williamson hold that "bald" does have a perfectly sharp boundary — some exact number of hairs is the last one for which "bald" is true — but that we are constitutionally unable to know where this boundary lies. This is not a brute stipulation: our use of the word does not track hair-counts finely enough to satisfy the margin-for-error condition that knowledge requires, so no belief about the exact cutoff could count as knowledge even though the cutoff is perfectly determinate. Because the semantics is entirely classical, epistemicism preserves bivalence — every sentence, including borderline ones, is true or false — and the full validity of classical logic.

Supervaluationists such as Fine instead say that "bald" is semantically indecisive between many equally good ways of making it precise (precisifications), each consistent with our actual usage. A sentence is true if it comes out true on every admissible precisification, false if it comes out false on all of them, and neither true nor false if precisifications disagree, which is exactly what happens with a borderline case like "a man with 5,000 hairs is bald." Since some sentences end up neither true nor false, supervaluationism must reject bivalence, and because it rejects bivalence it also has to give up the law of excluded middle for borderline sentences, since "a man with 5,000 hairs is bald or he is not bald" will itself be neither true nor false, just like its disjuncts.

The two theories also diverge on higher-order vagueness — the fact that expressions like "borderline bald" are themselves vague, with no sharp line between the bald cases, the borderline cases, and the not-bald cases. Here supervaluationism has the advantage: because it already operates with a whole space of precisifications rather than a single cutoff, it can simply relativize higher-order predicates like "definitely bald" to that same space, whereas epistemicism, having committed itself to one sharp fact about where "bald" stops applying, has no analogous resource for explaining why "definitely bald" or "borderline bald" should themselves lack sharp boundaries. So supervaluationism gives the more unified and principled treatment of higher-order vagueness.
