I recently joined the board of a small land trust, and I've been asked to help justify our practice of spending most of our budget protecting remote wilderness that almost no one will ever see. Looking for something sturdier than an appeal to intuition, I went back to G. E. Moore's thought experiment in Principia Ethica, which asks us to compare a beautiful world with a hideous one, stipulating that neither is ever perceived by anyone, as a test of what has value in isolation from everything else. Moore takes this to show that beauty retains intrinsic value even when wholly unappreciated. But I can't reconcile that with his principle of organic unities, which he also uses to explain why aesthetic appreciation and personal affection are the greatest goods: on that principle, a beautiful object joined with a fitting emotional response is worth far more than either alone. Does unperceived beauty keep most of its value on Moore's view, or only a small residue? And can he say anything parallel about a person's admirable qualities going entirely unloved, or does personal affection resist being isolated the same way? I'd like to understand how these pieces of his view actually fit together.
