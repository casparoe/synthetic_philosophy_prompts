I spent almost forty years doing optics and electromagnetism research before I retired, and only in the last couple of years have I gotten properly into philosophy -- there's a little discussion group that meets at my local library and we've worked our way from Kuhn into the scientific realism literature. A few weeks back we did Larry Laudan's "confutation" paper, the one with that whole graveyard list of theories that were empirically successful in their day and are now considered flat-out wrong -- phlogiston, caloric, the various ethers, crystalline spheres, and so on -- and it kind of got under my skin more than I expected, especially the ether bit, since I used Fresnel's equations constantly in my own work without ever thinking hard about the fact that they were derived from a mechanical ether theory nobody believes in anymore. Forty years of feeling like I was finding out how the world actually works, and now I keep wondering if the stuff I was most confident about will look like caloric theory to someone in 2150.

So here's what I'd like from you: a dialogue between a scientific realist and an anti-realist who both clearly know this literature cold, actually going at each other rather than trading slogans. Don't make the realist a strawman who just says "but science obviously works" -- have them already know the history and retreat to something like Worrall's structural realism (the argument that Fresnel's equations survive, reinterpreted, inside Maxwell's electromagnetic theory even after the mechanical ether itself got junked), or Psillos's move of separating out the "idle" parts of an old theory from the "working posits" that actually did the predictive labor. And don't let the anti-realist just recite the list of dead theories either -- have them push on whether we can ever tell which parts of a theory are the working posits except in hindsight, and maybe bring in something like Kyle Stanford's argument about unconceived alternatives. Feel free to set it wherever seems natural, two old colleagues arguing at a conference bar is fine, or no frame at all if thats easier -- what matters to me is real back and forth where every move gets an actual counter-move, anchored in at least one concrete historical case rather than staying purely abstract, and please don't wrap it up with a tidy resolution. I'd rather see both sides pushed as hard as they'll go.
