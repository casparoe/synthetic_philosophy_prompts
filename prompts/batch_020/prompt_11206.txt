Question (20 pts): Arrow's theorem is often summarized in the slogan "no voting system is perfect." (a) State the four conditions in the standard modern formulation of Arrow's General Possibility Theorem. (b) Give a worked numerical example — a specific profile of voters and candidates — showing that the Borda count violates Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives. (c) Advocates of cardinal methods like score/range voting sometimes claim their systems "escape" Arrow's theorem. Is this claim defensible, and why or why not? (d) Does Arrow's theorem itself tell us anything about whether voting systems can be strategically manipulated? Explain.

Student's answer:

(a) A social welfare function takes every voter's ranking of three or more candidates and returns one social ranking. Arrow's four conditions are: Unrestricted Domain (the function has to work for any logically possible set of individual rankings, not just "nice" ones), Weak Pareto (if literally everyone ranks x above y, the social ranking must too), Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives (the social ranking of x vs y can only depend on how individuals rank x against y, not on where some third option z sits), and Non-Dictatorship (no single voter's preference automatically becomes the social preference no matter what everyone else thinks). Arrow's proof shows that for 3+ candidates you cannot satisfy all four together.

(b) Take 7 voters and 3 candidates, A, B and C. 3 voters rank A > C > B, 2 voters rank C > B > A, and 2 voters rank B > A > C. Give 2 pts for first, 1 for second, 0 for third. A gets 3(2)+2(0)+2(1) = 8. B gets 3(0)+2(1)+2(2) = 6. C gets 3(1)+2(2)+2(0) = 7. So the full Borda ranking is A > C > B, with A the winner. But look just at A vs B: the 3 "A>C>B" voters prefer A, while the 2 "C>B>A" voters and the 2 "B>A>C" voters — 4 voters total — prefer B. So a majority actually prefers B to A. If C drops out of the race, the Borda count (now just a count of first-place votes between two candidates) makes B the winner over A, even though no one changed their mind about A vs B. That's the IIA violation — the standing of A relative to B flipped purely because of a candidate who wasn't even the winner.

(c) I'd push back on that claim. Score voting still has to output some ranking of the candidates from the ballots it collects, and any procedure that maps individual input into a social ranking is exactly the kind of object Arrow's proof is about. So once you look at what score voting actually does — aggregate individual input into a social ordering — it's subject to the same impossibility, cardinal ballots or not. The "escape" is just a rebranding of the inputs, not a real way around the theorem.

(d) Yes — this is really the same result under a different name. Arrow already showed that once you rule out a dictator, someone will always have an incentive to misrepresent their true ranking to change the outcome in their favour. That's why every reasonable voting rule is manipulable: it's a direct corollary of the 1951 impossibility theorem, just stated in terms of incentives rather than IIA.
