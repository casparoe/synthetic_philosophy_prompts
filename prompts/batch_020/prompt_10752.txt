Ok this is gonna sound like a weird lead-in but bear with me. At my company we have a design-review process for big changes where three senior engineers each vote independently on an RFC, and the rule is "approved only if the design is technically sound AND the rollout plan is safe" — two separate yes/no calls that are supposed to feed into one final approve/reject. On a doc last month, reviewer A said sound=yes, safe=no (so their vote was reject), reviewer B said sound=no, safe=yes (also reject), and reviewer C said sound=yes, safe=yes (approve). Tally the final votes and it's 1-2, rejected. But someone in the Slack thread pointed out that if you instead tally each sub-question seperately, "sound" gets 2 yes's (A and C) and "safe" also gets 2 yes's (B and C) — so by the letter of the rule it actually should've passed. We argued about this for an embarrassingly long time and never fully resolved it. Eventually a friend who minored in philosophy told me this is an honest-to-god known problem, something like the "discursive dilemma" (or "doctrinal paradox" for the original legal version — I think that one's from Kornhauser and Sager, about appeals judges), and that List and Pettit (not 100% sure I've got the names right) later turned it into a whole formal "judgment aggregation" theory, with an impossibility result that's apparently a bit like Arrow's theorem but for judgments instead of preferences.

I went down the wiki-hole on this for a couple of nights, and my honest takeaway is that nobody actually commits to an answer — every source just lays out both options and shrugs. That's not good enough for me. What I want is an actual verdict: please make the strongest case you can that whenever a group has to reach one binary decision that logically depends on several separately-contestable sub-judgments (my RFC case, or the classic multi-issue court case), the group should default to voting on each sub-judgment separately and deriving the final call from those votes (the "premise-based" procedure), rather than voting on the bottom-line outcome directly (the "conclusion-based" procedure). And don't just say "it respects the majority on every issue" and call it a day — actually grapple with the real objections, like the fact that whoever decides which sub-questions get put on the agenda in the first place can steer the outcome, and the fact that (from what I understood of my reading) issue-by-issue majority voting isn't even guaranteed to stay logically consistent once you've got more going on than my simple two-condition example.
