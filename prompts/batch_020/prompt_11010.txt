Here's the question I set: "Explain the difference between Wright's etiological theory of function and Cummins's causal-role theory of function, using the heart as your example throughout. Then explain (i) why Cummins thought his account was an improvement over etiological accounts of function, and (ii) which of the two accounts gives a more natural treatment of cases of malfunction (e.g. a heart with a defect that pumps blood only weakly), and why."

And here's the student's answer, verbatim:

"Wright's etiological account says the function of a trait is whatever effect explains why that trait is there: X's function is Z iff X is there because X does Z. So on this view, the heart's function is to pump blood because ancestral hearts that pumped blood well were selected for, and that's why present-day organisms still have hearts. This ties function essentially to natural selection and the trait's history.

Cummins rejected this and proposed instead that a trait's function should be assigned relative to an analysis of some capacity of a larger containing system. On his view, the heart's function is to pump blood because pumping blood is the causal contribution the heart makes to the circulatory system's capacity to distribute nutrients and oxygen around the body, which in turn keeps the organism alive and improves its fitness. Unlike Wright, Cummins doesn't care about the trait's causal history -- the heart would have the function of pumping blood even if, per impossibile, it had popped into existence yesterday by sheer chance, since the causal contribution is all that matters and not how the trait came to be there.

I think Cummins's account is actually the one that handles malfunction better. Take a heart with a defect that pumps blood only weakly: the causal-role account can just say the heart is the kind of thing that's disposed to make that causal contribution when everything is working normally, so it still counts as having the function even though this particular token is under-performing. Wright's account has a harder time here, because the whole point of the etiological story is to explain the trait's current existence by pointing to what it did for its ancestors, and a token that's currently failing to pump blood well isn't doing the thing that explains why it's there, so it's not obvious the etiological account can say it still has that function at all. So on balance I'd give the edge to Cummins."
