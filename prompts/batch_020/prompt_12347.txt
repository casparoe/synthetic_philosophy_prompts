A friend and I spent an evening arguing about Jeremy Bentham's private writings on sexual morality — manuscripts defending consensual same-sex acts that he drafted but, wary of public backlash, never published. In them he argued that English law's harsh treatment of such acts was really just an instance of what he elsewhere called the "principle of sympathy and antipathy" — condemning conduct merely because it provokes disgust rather than because it causes real harm — and that hanging someone for it was grossly disproportionate to any pain the act itself could cause. My friend insisted that this same reasoning, combined with Bentham's famous remark about animals — "The question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" — commits Bentham to treating romantic and sexual relationships between humans and AI companions as straightforwardly unproblematic. I'm not convinced. Reconstruct, using Bentham's own arguments and his felicific calculus, how he would actually have reasoned through this case, and identify any grounds on which he might have qualified or resisted my friend's confident conclusion.
