I'm putting together a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of chance, and I keep getting tangled up on one specific wrinkle in Lewis's Principal Principle that I know I had straight at some point in grad school but have clearly let atrophy since. The basic Principal Principle is easy enough to state to students — credence in A, conditional on admissible evidence and the information that the objective chance of A is x, should be x — but the catch is that Lewis is also committed to a thoroughly Humean account of what chance even is: chances are whatever the best system (balancing simplicity, strength, and fit) says they are, and that "best system" gets picked out by looking at the whole history of the world, past AND future. That seems to generate this weird self-undermining structure, where there can be "undermining" futures which, if they occurred, would themselves be evidence that some rival system was the best one all along, even though the very law we're using to fix our credences assigned a perfectly respectable chance to exactly that future occuring. Lewis apparently thought this was a big enough problem for his whole program that he spent real effort trying to patch it (I have a vague memory he even had a jokey name for how bad the problem was, but don't quote me on that).

What I'd love from you is a from-the-ground-up explanation of exactly where the inconsistency bites — ideally illustrated with a toy example (a long run of coin tosses would probably do it) so I can see concretely how an undermining future produces a clash with just naively applying the Principal Principle — and then a walkthrough of how the standard fix (redefining what the chance-proposition "the chance of A at t is x" actually refers to, so that its already sensitive to whatever happens later) is supposed to restore consistency. I'd also genuinely like your take on whether that fix feels like a principled way of vindicating the spirit of the original principle, or more like a technical patch that quietly gives up on part of what made the principle intuitive in the first place. My students are sharp and will absolutely press on this, so I want a version of the story that's a bit more satisfying than "trust me, it works out."
