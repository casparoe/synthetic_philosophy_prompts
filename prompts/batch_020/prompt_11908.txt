In my seminar on the epistemic foundations of game theory this term, we've spent the last couple of weeks on formal treatments of common knowledge, and two of the standard examples we discussed -- the muddy children puzzle and Rubinstein's electronic mail game -- seem to point in almost opposite directions, which has left me a bit confused about what the "real" lesson about common knowledge is supposed to be.

In the muddy children puzzle, each child can see every other child's forehead but not their own, and once two or more children are actually muddy, every child already privately knows the fact "at least one of us is muddy" before anyone says a word. Yet when the father publicly announces exactly that fact, it sets off a cascade of rounds of silence and inference after which every muddy child comes to know their own status with certainty -- even though, on the face of it, the announcement told nobody anything they didn't already know. In Rubinstein's email game, by contrast, two players' computers exchange a long chain of automatic confirmations -- as many rounds as you like -- establishing that some rare, payoff-relevant state has occurred, so that each player knows the state, knows that the other knows it, knows that the other knows that they know it, and so on up to a very high but still finite order. And yet the essentially unique equilibrium has both players act exactly as if no message had ever been exchanged at all, no matter how many rounds of confirmation occurred.

Could you compare these two examples and explain what's actually driving the divergence between them? I'm especially interested in whether the contrast comes down to something structural -- e.g., public versus private/sequential communication, or the all-or-nothing character of the common-knowledge operator itself as opposed to the continuum of finite mutual-knowledge orders -- or whether it's really about the difference between a pure inference/deduction problem and a genuine coordination game with off-path incentives to deviate. I'd also value your judgment on which of the two puzzles gives the more instructive picture of how, or whether, common knowledge actually gets established in real institutions, such as financial markets or diplomatic signaling, where fragile chains of confirmation seem much closer to what actually happens than a single clean public announcement does.
