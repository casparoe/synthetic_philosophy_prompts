Novelists spend a lot of time watching how people talk to each other, so I've found myself unable to stop replaying something that happened at my writers' workshop a few months ago, partly because I suspect a version of it will end up in the book I'm currently drafting, which is itself about a fractious literary community. One of our most senior members, a writer I'll call Ruth, has for years played the group's self-appointed conscience on questions of representation: she's reliably the one who speaks up when a manuscript reduces a character from a background unlike the author's own to a stereotype, and on the whole her instincts are good. At our last meeting she gave a newer member a fairly severe dressing-down along these lines, and rereading the pages afterward, I have to admit her literary judgment was basically sound. What complicates it for me is that Ruth's own breakout novel, published well over a decade ago, does something quite similar with a minor character, a fact everyone in the room that evening was perfectly aware of, and one Ruth has never, to my knowledge, revisited or even acknowledged. A few of us left feeling uneasy on the newer writer's behalf, though we argued afterward about whether that unease was tracking something real or whether we were just letting what we know about Ruth's history unfairly color our sense of a critique that was, on its own terms, entirely fair.

I'd like to work out my own view before I decide how honestly to render the equivalent scene in the novel. Is there a principled way of saying that Ruth's history weakens her position, or even disqualifies her, from being the one to level that particular criticism, as distinct from saying anything about whether the criticism itself is true? And does that same reasoning extend to the rest of us, who merely watched and said nothing, or is it really only available to the newer writer himself, if he even wants to use it, given how easily "you did it too" can become a way of dodging a fair point rather than answering it?
