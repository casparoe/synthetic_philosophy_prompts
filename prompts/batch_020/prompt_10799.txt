I keep running into a version of the following argument in discussions of algorithmic fairness, and I haven't been able to convince myself it's mistaken. The Kleinberg-Mullainathan-Raghavan and Chouldechova impossibility results seem to settle the ProPublica/Northpointe dispute over the COMPAS recidivism tool: since calibration within risk groups and equal false-positive/false-negative rates across racial groups can't both hold when the two groups have different base rates of reoffending, and Black and white defendants did have different base rates in the ProPublica data, Northpointe's defense of COMPAS as fair, because well-calibrated, turns out to be the mathematically rigorous position, while ProPublica's disparate-error-rate critique amounts to objecting to a disparity that was statistically guaranteed. I'd normally be suspicious of this sort of move, the way Arrow's theorem gets over-interpreted as proving that democratic voting is incoherent, but here the base-rate asymmetry seems to genuinely break the tie in calibration's favor rather than merely reveal a trade-off. Where, if anywhere, does the argument break down?
