So my friend Jordan and I had this long, kind of pointless argument last night that I can't stop thinking about. It started because our other friend Priya just signed up for one of those kidney donor chain programs -- she's donating to a total stranger, no family connection, nothing. I said that's a textbook case of supererogation: its good that shes doing it, obviously, but she's not required to, its above and beyond the call of duty. Jordan agreed it's not required, but said the more interesting question is why it's not required, and he brought up something from an ethics seminar he's taking called an "agent-centered prerogative" -- this idea (I think it's from Samuel Scheffler?) that morality gives you permission to NOT always maximize the good, to weigh your own life and projects more heavily than a fully impartial calculation would allow. He said supererogation and prerogatives are basically mirror images of each other: one is permission to do more than the impartial minimum, the other is permission to do less than the impartial maximum, and any theory with room for one should have room for the other. I wasn't convinced -- it feels like a permission not to donate a kidney is doing something totally different from praise for someone who does, and mashing them together seemed sloppy to me. We went around on this for like an hour and neither of us actually knows the literature well enough to settle it.

Can you properly compare supererogation and agent-centered prerogatives for me? I want to know exactly how they're related and where the disanalogies are, whether Jordan's "mirror image" framing actually holds up under pressure, and whether you think a moral theory could coherently accommodate one of these notions without the other -- e.g. is it weird that prerogatives were basically invented to make consequentialism less demanding, while supererogation is the thing maximizing consequentialism has always struggled the most to make room for at all?
