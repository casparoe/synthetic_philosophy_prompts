Question: Aristotle's sea-battle passage (De Interpretatione 9) worries that if statements about the future are already true or false today, fatalism follows.
(a) [5 pts] Reconstruct the argument for why bivalence about future contingents seems to lead to fatalism.
(b) [5 pts] Explain Aristotle's own way of blocking the argument.
(c) [5 pts] Diodorus Cronus's "Master Argument" is often brought up alongside this puzzle. Reconstruct it and say which of its three premises he actually rejects, and why.
(d) [5 pts] Briefly explain how Thomason's supervaluationist semantics treats future contingent sentences, and say whether it vindicates Aristotle's solution.

Student's answer:
(a) Aristotle's puzzle assumes bivalence -- every statement, including ones about the future, is true or false. Suppose it's true right now that there'll be a sea battle tomorrow. Since truth doesn't change over time, if it's true now it was equally true yesterday and every day before that, so the battle's occurrence was already fixed at every earlier point, and since the past can't be changed, whatever was true of it is now necessary. So the battle is unavoidable, and by the same reasoning so is literally everything that will ever happen -- fatalism follows and deliberation becomes pointless.

(b) Aristotle's way out is to deny the law of excluded middle (LEM) for singular statements about future contingents. He thinks "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" and "there will not be" are neither true nor false right now -- only once tomorrow comes and the matter's settled does one of them become true. That blocks (a), because if neither disjunct is true today, neither one was "always true," so nothing about the past forces the battle to happen.

(c) The Master Argument, reported by Epictetus, rests on three claims that can't all be true together: (1) every true statement about the past is necessary, (2) an impossible thing never follows from a possible one, (3) there's something possible that neither is nor ever will be true. Diodorus took (2) and (3) to be obviously correct, so the one he gave up was (1) -- he denied that truths about the past are all necessary, since some "past truths" (like its having been true a year ago that some coin toss would land heads) really just depend on how things later turn out. Giving up (1) is exactly what let him conclude that possibility collapses into actuality: the only things that are ever really possible are the things that either happen or will happen.

(d) Thomason's supervaluationism keeps classical logic intact: a future contingent sentence is true if it's true on every open history consistent with how things are now, false if false on all of them, and neither true nor false if it varies across histories. Crucially, "there will be a sea battle or there will not be" still comes out true no matter what, since on every single history one of the two disjuncts holds, even though it's a different one from history to history. So supervaluationism arguably vindicates Aristotle's instinct that we shouldn't have had to give up LEM just because individual future contingents aren't yet true or false.
