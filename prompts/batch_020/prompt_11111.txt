Had this whole argument with my friend Priya last night about Adam Smith and colonialism. She was saying that since hes THE free-market guy, he basically must've been fine with the British Empire, and I was trying to convince her that the "Of Colonies" chapter in Book IV of Wealth of Nations (part of his big critique of the mercantile system) is actually pretty savage about how colonial trade worked in practice - the East India Company especially - and that he even floats the idea of Britain just cutting the American colonies loose because the empire cost more to run than it brought in. She definately wasn't buying it and we basically argued in circles until 1am.

Anyway it got me wondering how he'd size up stuff happening right now. Specifically I mean the whole China-in-Africa thing over the last decade or two - state-linked companies building ports, railways, mines etc in exchange for resource concessions and big loans, plus all the "debt-trap diplomacy" accusations that get thrown around by Western commentators. Smith spends a lot of time on why it's uniquely bad to combine sovereignty with trading privilege in one body (that's basically his whole case against "exclusive companies" like the East India Co), and on how colonial schemes tend to mainly benefit a narrow merchant lobby back home rather than the nation as a whole, even while getting sold publicly as being good for everyone. So, genuine question: do you think he'd look at Belt and Road-type arrangements and see basically the same playbook in a new outfit, or would the differences - no formal claim on territory, contracts instead of royal charters, no East-India-Company-style private army - actually matter to his analysis? I don't want the "Smith just liked free trade, the end" answer, I want you to actually engage with the specifics of his colonies arguments and see where they do or don't map onto the modern case.
