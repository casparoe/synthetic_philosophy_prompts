I'm reporting a piece on the "is it socially constructed?" debates -- race, gender, money, all of it lumped together in the popular conversation even though I suspect they're not actually the same kind of claim. A philosopher I interviewed for background keeps insisting that the real headwaters of this whole argument aren't Judith Butler or Ian Hacking but John Locke, of all people, and she sent me this bit from Book III of the Essay Concerning Human Understanding (the chapter on how we sort things into "species" and hand out names). I've been chewing on it for two days and my philosophy minor from college is not really up to the task -- Locke's sentences just aren't built for skimming. Here's what she sent me:

"I would not here be thought to forget, much less to deny, that Nature, in the production of things, makes several of them alike: there is nothing more obvious, especially in the race of animals, and all things propagated by seed. But yet I think we may say, the sorting of them under names is the workmanship of the understanding, taking occasion, from the similitude it observes amongst them, to make abstract general ideas, and set them up in the mind, with names annexed to them, as patterns or forms, to which as particular things existing are found to agree, so they come to be of that species, have that denomination, or are put into that classis. For when we say this is a man, that a horse; this justice, that cruelty; this a watch, that a jack; what do we else but rank things under different specific names, as agreeing to those abstract ideas, of which we have made those names the signs? And what are the essences of those species set out and marked by names, but those abstract ideas in the mind; which are, as it were, the bonds between particular things that exist, and the names they are to be ranked under? And when general names have any connexion with particular beings, these abstract ideas are the medium that unites them: so that the essences of species, as distinguished and denominated by us, neither are nor can be anything but those precise abstract ideas we have in our minds. And therefore the supposed real essences of substances, if different from our abstract ideas, cannot be the essences of the species we rank things into."

(Yes, I clocked that "race" shows up in there too, though obviously not meaning what it means now -- not sure if thats a coincidence worth a footnote or just an old sense of the word.) What I can't work out on my own: is Locke saying kinds like "man" or "gold" are basically made up by the mind, full stop, or is he holding onto something more careful, given that he explicitly grants nature really does "make several of them alike"? Relatedly, does this framework actually help distinguish race, gender, and money as different kinds of case, or does it just flatten them into one? I half-remember, from way back, that Locke treats something like "justice" or a "triumph" as a totally different category of idea from a substance like gold or man -- their nominal and real essence being literally the same thing, since nobody thinks there's a hidden fact of the matter about what a triumph "really" is underneath our idea of it. If I'm remembering that right, wouldn't money slot in easily as that second kind of idea, while race gets stuck arguing over whether it's more like gold, and gender ends up somewhere genuinely unclear between the two? And does any of this survive Kripke and Putnam supposedly wrecking the whole Lockean, description-based story of how words like "gold" and "water" latch onto the world? I'd love an actual close reading from you, not the two-sentence "workmanship of the understanding" gloss you get in encyclopedia entries.
