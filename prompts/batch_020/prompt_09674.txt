I spent about thirty-five years running a physics lab (condensed matter, nothing exciting) before I retired, and now that I've got time on my hands philosophy has sort of taken over -- probably more than my wife would like. Right now I'm stuck on Galen Strawson's "Basic Argument" against moral responsibility, which I ran into in an anthology my nephew (philosophy grad student, bless him) left at the house. As I understand it: to be truly responsible for what you do, you'd have to be truly responsible for the way you are, but you could only be responsible for the way you are if you'd somehow chosen it -- and that choice itself just expresses an earlier way-you-were that you never chose either, and so on back, an infinite regress of self-authorship that never gets off the ground. What hooked me is that Strawson insists this isn't just determinism wearing a fancier coat: he says the argument bites just as hard under indeterminism, since a random undetermined blip in how your character got formed still isn't something you authored, its just luck standing in for law. He even says the point doesn't depend on contingent facts about our universe -- supposedly it would hold for any possible agent whatsoever, natural or not. As a recovering physicist my instinct with any "no free will" argument is to reach for quantum indeterminacy as an escape hatch, so I was annoyed to see him close that door too.

I brought this up at book club (mostly retired engineers -- rough year to pick philosophy as the theme) and it went nowhere, everybody just restated their priors louder. So here's what I'd actually like from you: an essay that reconstructs the regress step of the Basic Argument carefully, then tests it against the two comebacks I keep half-remembering -- the compatibilist line that ultimate self-authorship was never required for ordinary responsibility in the first place (something Frankfurt- or Fischer-ish), and the libertarian attempt to use indeterminism to break the regress via something like Robert Kane's "self-forming actions." Take both seriously rather than swatting down a cartoon version, and then actually land somewhere: does the Basic Argument succeed in showing that "true," desert-entailing moral responsibility is impossible, or does the regress premise quietly smuggle in a standard of responsibility nobody sane needed to accept in the first place? A qualified verdict is fine -- something like "the regress works, but only against a notion of responsibility nobody should've wanted anyway" would count as landing somewhere -- I just don't want a shrug that ends on "reasonable people disagree."
