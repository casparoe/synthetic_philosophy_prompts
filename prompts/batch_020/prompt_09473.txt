I'm a junior at my high school and was recently elected to our Honor Council, the student-and-faculty body that hears academic integrity cases and recommends a resolution to the Dean of Students. A case on next Tuesday's agenda has split the student members more than I expected, and since we're each supposed to arrive with an independent view rather than simply falling in with whoever argues most forcefully in the room, I wanted a considered outside opinion before then.

Here is the case: a senior submitted a take-home essay for AP Literature that she wrote by prompting a generative AI chatbot for full paragraphs of analysis, which she then revised lightly in her own words. Our school's Academic Integrity Policy, adopted three years ago, says students may not submit work involving "unauthorized assistance," and that such assistance "includes, but is not limited to: (a) having another person write or dictate any part of an assignment; (b) consulting a solutions manual, answer key, or another student's completed work; or (c) using notes or electronic devices during a closed-book assessment." Nobody drafting that sentence was thinking about chatbots. One faction on the Council wants "unauthorized assistance" read in light of what the policy is plainly there to do — make sure graded work reflects the student's own understanding — which they think obviously reaches an AI-drafted paragraph. The other faction points out that (a), (b), and (c) all involve drawing on some pre-existing, fixed source (a person's own words, a manual, saved notes), whereas a chatbot produces text on the spot that did not exist before her prompt; on the principle that a general phrase following a specific list takes its meaning from the kind of things already listed, they think the essay falls outside what the policy actually says, whatever its authors would have wanted had they foreseen the technology. My English teacher, who advises the Council and mentioned she covered something similar in law school, called this basically the textualism-versus-purposivism dispute — which is what convinced me it deserved more than a gut reaction.

Could you write the short opinion memo I would bring to Tuesday's meeting? I'd like it to state plainly whether the essay counts as a violation under the policy as actually written, to present the side you don't ultimately endorse in its strongest form rather than waving it off, and to close with a specific recommendation for how the clause should be rewritten so the Council isn't stuck relitigating this same disagreement the next time the technology changes.
