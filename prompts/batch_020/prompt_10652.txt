As I finalize grades for my graduate seminar on Bayesian epistemology, I would appreciate a second, independent read on one student's answer to a recent exam question before I record a mark. The question, worth 10 points, was:

"Explain the problem of old evidence for Bayesian confirmation theory, illustrating it with the case of general relativity's confirmation by the perihelion precession of Mercury. Then present Garber's proposed solution to the problem, and state one significant objection to it."

Here is the student's answer:

Bayesian confirmation theory analyzes confirmation as a matter of probability-raising: evidence E confirms hypothesis H for an agent just in case P(H|E) > P(H). Since conditionalization is the standard model of belief update, this is naturally read diachronically as well: learning E confirms H when it causes the agent's credence in H to rise. The problem of old evidence, raised by Clark Glymour against this framework, is that when E is already known — so that P(E) = 1 for the agent before H is even formulated — conditionalizing on E cannot change the agent's credence in H at all, since P(H|E) reduces to P(H) whenever P(E) = 1. Yet historically, old evidence has seemed to provide dramatic confirmation of new theories. The standard example is the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion, a discrepancy of roughly 43 arcseconds per year between the observed and Newtonian-predicted orbit that had been known since the mid-nineteenth century. When Einstein showed in 1915 that general relativity entailed exactly this figure, physicists took it as powerful evidence for the new theory, despite the fact that the evidence itself was decades old and already certain.

Garber's solution to this puzzle is to ask what the scientist's credence in general relativity would have been had she not already known about the perihelion problem, and to locate the confirmation in the difference between that hypothetical prior credence and her credence once she learns of the anomaly. Because the relevant probability function is counterfactual rather than the scientist's actual one, it need not assign probability 1 to E, so conditionalizing on E within this hypothetical model can raise the credence in H after all. This lets Garber preserve the letter of Bayesian conditionalization while explaining why old evidence still feels evidentially significant.

The obvious objection is that this solution trades one problem for another: it is unclear which counterfactual probability function is the right one to use, since there is no fact of the matter about what a scientist "would have" believed had history gone differently, and different choices of hypothetical prior will yield different, and sometimes arbitrary, degrees of confirmation.
