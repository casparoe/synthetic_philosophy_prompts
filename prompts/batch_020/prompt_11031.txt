I spent almost forty years as a physicist—mostly atmospheric and climate stuff, nothing to do with relativity—before I retired, and these days I've fallen hard into reading philosophy, partly because my book club got sick of novels and switched to "big idea" nonfiction for a while. Right now I'm chewing on the old endurantism vs. perdurantism debate about how objects persist through time: basically, whether something like me, or my late father's pocket watch, persists by being "wholly present" at every single moment it exists (endurantism), or whether really there's just one four-dimensional space-time "worm," and the bit of me that exists right now is only one thin temporal slice out of a much bigger whole that also includes all my past and future slices (perdurantism).

Here's what's bugging me though: it seems to me that special relativity just settles this question outright, and I don't get why the philosophers are still arguing about it. I already believed in the "block universe" picture from my physics training—past, present, and future all equally real, no objectively privileged "now"—since that's just what falls out of the relativity of simultaneity: what counts as simultaneous depends on your reference frame, so there's no frame-independent way to say what exists "right now." But if that's true, then the idea of an object being "wholly present" at a single moment can't even be coherently stated anymore, since "a single moment" isn't objective. The only description of an object that survives intact across every reference frame is the whole four-dimensional worm, which different frames just slice up into time-slices differently. So it looks to me like accepting relativity commits you to perdurantism too, not just to the block universe, and that endurantism only ever seemed plausible because people were implicitly assuming old Newtonian absolute time. I ran this by an old collegue from my physics days and she just said "yeah, obviously," but when I brought it up at my philosophy reading group, a guy with an actual philosophy PhD got weirdly cagey and said "its not that simple" without really explaining why. So which is it—am I onto something the philosophers are missing, or is there a hole in my reasoning that I just can't see myself?
