I am drafting a qualifying-exam question for our decision theory sequence and want to pressure-test it before assigning it. Below are seven resolutions to the St. Petersburg paradox drawn from across its long reception history, ranging from Cramer's and Bernoulli's eighteenth-century treatments to recent work in ergodicity economics and risk-weighted utility theory. Each is compressed into a single sentence:

1. Bernoulli's diminishing-marginal-utility solution: money yields logarithmic utility, so the gamble's expected utility, unlike its expected monetary value, is finite.
2. Cramer's bounded-utility solution (proposed before Bernoulli's own paper appeared): utility rises with wealth but is capped, since sums beyond some enormous but finite amount contribute no further utility.
3. Buffon's practical-impossibility solution: rational deliberation may simply discount probabilities below some small threshold to zero.
4. The finite-counterparty solution: no real payer commands unbounded wealth, so every realizable version of the game already has a finite expected value, and the paradox only afflicts an idealization that could never be instantiated.
5. The cumulative-prospect-theory solution: replace expected utility with a value function over gains and losses together with a nonlinear weighting function that distorts small probabilities.
6. Peters's ergodicity-economics solution: the quantity a rational agent should track is the time-average growth rate of wealth under repeated or consequential play, since the ensemble-average expected value is the wrong statistic for this kind of non-ergodic, multiplicative process.
7. The risk-weighted-expected-utility solution (in the spirit of Buchak): let the agent's global attitude toward risk, not merely the curvature of the utility function, determine how heavily improbable outcomes count.

Please rank these seven from most to least philosophically satisfying, where "satisfying" means resolving the paradox in a principled, non-ad-hoc way that also survives Karl Menger's "super-Petersburg" maneuver of re-engineering the payoffs so that the same framework again yields an unbounded valuation.
