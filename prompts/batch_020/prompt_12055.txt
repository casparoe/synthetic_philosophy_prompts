so I've been down a bit of a rabbit hole lately. I keep seeing Henry Sidgwick's name pop up on rationalist/EA blogs as basically THE rigorous 19th century utilitarian, the guy people treat as the intellectual grandfather of the whole "impartial view from nowhere" thing. Curious, I looked him up properly and found out he was also the founding president of the Society for Psychical Research for like 20 years — i.e. the "most careful philosopher of his generation" guy spent a huge chunk of his career formally investigating mediums, telepathy claims, and haunted houses. Did not expect that combo, but here we are.

I found the text of one of his addresses to that Society from 1889, "The Canons of Evidence in Psychical Research," where he's basically trying to work out a general rule for how skeptical you should be of a weird claim relative to the evidence for it. There's a bit in the middle I can't fully untangel:

"Now these opposing improbabilities are quite diverse, and we have no intellectual scales in which we can weigh them accurately one against the other. Some of our opponents offer us, by way of such scales, Hume's summary argument against miracles: 'It is contrary to experience that miracles should be true, and not contrary to experience that testimony should be false.' But in saying that a marvel is contrary to experience we can mean no more than that it is unlike previous experience — or rather that it is unlike that portion of experience which has been collected, handed down, and systematised by competent persons. But this only means that it is entirely novel and strange: and in the course of the life of the human race, during the period in which it has handed down and communicated experiences, different portions of mankind have been continually coming across things that were at first entirely novel and strange, though further acquaintance has rendered them familiar.

No one will maintain that it is impossible that the human race should ever come across anything so entirely novel in the course of its accumulation of experiences; they can only say that it is highly improbable. What is impossible is to estimate this improbability with anything like exactness: since to make such an estimate we should require to ascertain the proportion that what we do know about the universe bears to what we do not know about it; and that proportion is certainly one of the things that we do not know.

We are, therefore, in this position — not very satisfactory to the logical mind, but one that we are bound to face: we must admit that the statement of a fact novel beyond a certain degree of novelty is in itself an improbable statement, and that the improbability grows as the novelty grows: but we must admit that no one can pretend to lay down at what rate the improbability grows."

Can you help me actually unpack the logic here? Two things I'm stuck on: first, is he agreeing with Hume's "contrary to experience" line or quietly dismantling it — I genuinely can't tell whose side he ends up on. Second, that bit about the proportion of what-we-know to what-we-don't-know being "one of the things we do not know" — is that just a fancy way of shrugging and saying "you can't put a number on this," or is he making an actual argument with some teeth to it? My instinct as someone who writes code for a living is to go "oh, it's just an unknowable prior, slap a Bayesian framework on it and move on," but that feels too easy and I don't think that's actually where he's going with it. Feels like this passage should cash out in a real answer to "how weird does a claim have to be before I should stop trusting the witnesses," but I can't tell if he's giving one or just eloquently explaining why nobody can.
