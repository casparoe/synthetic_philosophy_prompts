For my IB extended essay, I've been reading Leibniz's political writings alongside the Theodicy, and I can't tell whether two parts of his system are genuinely the same idea or just happen to look alike. In the preface to the Codex Iuris Gentium, he distinguishes three ascending degrees of justice — strict right ("harm no one"), equity ("give each his due"), and piety ("live honorably") — the last of which, sometimes called "universal justice," he identifies closely with what he elsewhere calls "the charity of the wise" (caritas sapientis). In the Theodicy, meanwhile, God is said to choose the best of all possible worlds out of that very same combination of wisdom and goodness. Is the finite version of "charity of the wise" that grounds human justice literally the same disposition as the one behind God's choice of the best world, just operating on a smaller scale — and if so, doesn't that risk collapsing Leibniz's other-regarding virtue into a kind of enlightened optimization rather than genuine love of others? I would like this specific connection worked through carefully, rather than a general survey of Leibniz's ethics.
