Okay this is gonna sound kind of nerdy but I've been chewing on the "this sentence is false" thing for like a month now and I can't let it go. I remember it from a puzzle book as a kid and always figured it was just a cute wordplay trick, but I went down a youtube/wikipedia rabbit hole recently and realized actual logicians treat this as a dead serious problem about truth itself, not a joke. I never went to school for philosophy or math or anything (I do HVAC work for a living) so I'm piecing this together on my own, but here's where I've gotten to: Tarski's answer is basically that a language is never allowed to judge the truth of its own sentences, you always need a separate, higher up "meta" language to do that job, like an endless stack of referees. Then theres a guy named Kripke who tried to build a language that CAN talk about its own truth using something called fixed points, but the cost is the liar sentence just comes out neither true nor false, stuck in "ungrounded" limbo. Except then I read that if you tweak the sentence slightly to "this sentence is not true" instead of "is false," Kripke's own fix breaks too, because "not true" is supposed to also cover the gappy/ungrounded sentences, so the paradox just comes roaring back one level up. People call this the "strengthened" liar apparently.

What's been rattling around in my head since then is the dialetheist option (I think the big name here is Graham Priest) - where you just accept straight up that the liar sentence really is both true and false at the same time, no hierarchy, no gaps, no extra games. My gut says that's actually the most honest answer out of all of them, since everyone else seems to be building increasingly elaborate scaffolding just so they don't have to say the plain thing. But I also know the standard objection is that letting in even one true contradiction means classical logic lets you prove literally anything ("explosion" is the term I've seen), which sounds like a way bigger problem than the ones the other theories have. So could you actually write out the strongest possible case for taking the dialetheist route as the right answer to the liar paradox - not a wishy washy "well some people say this, some say that" survey, but a real argument - and make sure you actually grapple with the explosion objection instead of brushing past it, since that feels like the crux of the whole thing to me. I want a real verdict at the end, not a shrug.
