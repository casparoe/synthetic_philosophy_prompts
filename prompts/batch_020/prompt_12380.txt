Over dinner last week, a friend of mine — she's finishing a dissertation in political philosophy — and I ended up in a two-hour argument that neither of us won. It started when I mentioned that I had been rereading the "Constraints and Animals" stretch of Chapter Three of Nozick's *Anarchy, State, and Utopia*, where he flirts with the idea (commentators have since distilled it into the slogan "Kantianism for people, utilitarianism for animals") that the side constraints protecting persons from being used merely as means might not extend, or might extend only in some diminished form, to non-human animals, whose treatment could instead be settled by ordinary aggregative, cost-benefit reasoning. I tried defending a sliding-scale reading, on which moral status comes in degrees tracking how closely a creature approximates the rational-agency and life-planning capacities that are supposed to ground full side constraints for persons. My friend thought this was hopeless: she pushed the standard marginal-cases worry (infants and the severely cognitively impaired fail the same capacities test, yet nobody thinks they may be folded into a utilitarian calculus), and then, more originally, she pointed out that Nozick's own utility-monster argument — his reason for thinking unconstrained aggregation is unacceptable even when it maximizes total welfare — seems to apply just as well to industrial animal agriculture, where enormous aggregate animal suffering is offset against comparatively trivial gains in convenience for the human beings who benefit from it. I didn't have a good answer to either point, but I also don't think the practical implications of simply extending full side constraints to every sentient being are obviously tolerable, and we never got further than restating our positions more loudly.

I'd like you to pick the argument back up in the form of a dialogue between two moral philosophers who serve together on a university's animal-research ethics board and have been asked to evaluate a contested proposal involving primates. One of them defends something close to Nozick's graded, hybrid view; the other presses the marginal-cases and reversed-utility-monster objections and argues for extending genuine, even if weaker-content, side constraints to animals rather than treating their interests as one more input to an aggregate calculation. Please make both of them equally sharp: the Nozickian should have substantive replies available (some story about potentiality, kind-membership, or special relations that might do work the bare-capacities criterion can't) rather than folding immediately, and her colleague should take seriously what extending robust side constraints to animals would cost for medicine and ordinary life, rather than simply asserting that animal interests always win. Let the two of them respond to each other's specific moves rather than deliver parallel monologues, and feel free to end with the disagreement narrowed to its sharpest remaining point rather than artificially resolved.
