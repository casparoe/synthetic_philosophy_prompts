In Piccione and Rubinstein's absent-minded driver problem, a driver on a highway must decide, at each of two intersections she cannot tell apart, whether to exit or continue; exiting at the first intersection pays 0, exiting at the second pays 4, and driving past both pays 1. Reasoning about the problem ex ante, before the drive begins, one finds that the exit probability maximizing expected payoff is 1/3. Yet when the driver instead imagines herself already at an intersection she cannot identify and tries to work out what to do by conditionalizing on her evidence in the ordinary way, the very computation of how likely she is to be at the first intersection rather than the second already presupposes some strategy for exiting, so that "figuring out where I probably am" and "figuring out what to do" turn out not to be separable in the usual order. Please explain, with some precision, why this constitutes a genuine problem about extending expected-utility reasoning to self-locating uncertainty under imperfect recall, rather than merely a confusion that dissolves once one reasons carefully, and explain how Aumann, Hart, and Perry's response to Piccione and Rubinstein attempts to show exactly that, together with your own assessment of how well it succeeds.
