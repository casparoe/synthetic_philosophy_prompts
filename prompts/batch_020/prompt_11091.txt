I've recently been rereading Eliezer Yudkowsky's old "Timeless Physics" sequence, which leans on Julian Barbour's relationist account of time to argue that there is no further fact about which moment is "really now" beyond the timeless structure of configuration space. That sent me down a rabbit hole into the older substantivalism/relationism debate of which Barbour's work is really just the latest chapter, and I ended up going back to the actual source that everyone eventually gestures at without quoting closely: Newton's Scholium to the Definitions in the Principia. Most discussions jump straight to the rotating bucket, but I got stuck on an earlier and much less-discussed passage (section VI in the standard numbering) that Newton uses to set up the whole argument before rotation ever enters the picture. In the standard Motte–Cajori translation, a few paragraphs after first distinguishing absolute from relative space, he writes:

"As the order of the parts of time is immutable, so also is the order of the parts of space. Suppose those parts to be moved out of their places, and they will be moved (if the expression may be allowed) out of themselves. For times and spaces are, as it were, the places as well of themselves as of all other things. All things are placed in time as to order of succession; and in space as to order of situation. It is from their essence or nature that they are places; and that the primary places of things should be movable, is absurd. These are therefore the absolute places; and translations out of those places, are the only absolute motions."

I can't tell what kind of argument this is supposed to be, and I'd like help reconstructing it. Is Newton offering a genuinely independent, non-empirical argument for substantivalism here — something like "a place is individuated by its own essence, so a place that could itself move would not really be a place at all; hence the parts of space cannot move; hence space itself is absolute" — meant to stand prior to, and apart from, the empirical bucket and two-globes arguments that follow later in the same Scholium? Or is it closer to a stipulative move that doesn't actually rule out anything a relationist would want to assert? I'd appreciate a close, sentence-by-sentence reading if that helps make the structure clear, an honest verdict on whether the argument succeeds or simply trades on treating "space" first as a system of relations among bodies and then, without further justification, as a further thing possessing its own essence, and finally a note on the sharpest reply you think Leibniz (or a contemporary relationist) could make to this specific passage, as distinct from the more familiar replies to the bucket experiment.
