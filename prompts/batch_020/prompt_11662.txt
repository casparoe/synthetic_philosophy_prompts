I'm a novelist currently at work on a multigenerational family saga, and one of its central figures — the family's patriarch, a self-made shipping magnate — has a favourite line he deploys whenever anyone in the family questions his conduct: "I made myself what I am, and I answer to no one for it." I didn't want him simply to assert this as bluster, so I've been re-reading the philosophers he might plausibly have encountered as a young man, Nietzsche among them, hoping to give the sentiment some real intellectual backbone. In the course of that reading I got stuck on a short section of Beyond Good and Evil that seems to be addressing exactly this kind of claim, though I can't quite work out what Nietzsche is actually arguing. Here it is, in the old Helen Zimmern translation I happen to own (it's section 21, in the first chapter):

"The causa sui is the best self-contradiction that has yet been conceived, it is a sort of logical violation and unnaturalness; but the extravagant pride of man has managed to entangle itself profoundly and frightfully with this very folly. The desire for 'freedom of will' in the superlative, metaphysical sense, such as still holds sway, unfortunately, in the minds of the half-educated, the desire to bear the entire and ultimate responsibility for one's actions oneself, and to absolve God, the world, ancestors, chance, and society therefrom, involves nothing less than to be precisely this causa sui, and, with more than Munchausen daring, to pull oneself up into existence by the hair, out of the slough of nothingness. If any one should find out in this manner the crass stupidity of the celebrated conception of 'free will' and put it out of his head altogether, I beg of him to carry his 'enlightenment' a step further, and also put out of his head the contrary of this monstrous conception of 'free will': I mean 'non-free will,' which is tantamount to a misuse of cause and effect."

What exactly is the argument here, and what does it actually rule out? I follow the joke about hauling yourself up by your own hair out of nothing, but I can't see why Nietzsche thinks that wanting to "bear the entire and ultimate responsibility for one's actions" commits you to that absurd picture, rather than to some perfectly ordinary, unmysterious sense of owning your choices. The last sentence puzzles me even more: having just dismissed free will as a piece of half-educated nonsense, he immediately warns against concluding that the will is simply unfree either, as though that were an equal and opposite mistake rather than the plain consequence of his own argument. I'd like to understand what position he thinks actually survives once both of those have been thrown out, since working that out might tell me something useful about exactly how much my patriarch is entitled to claim for himself — and where the line should fall between his pride and his self-deception.
