I've got a scene in my new historical novel where Frederick Douglass and William Lloyd Garrison run into each other after a lecture sometime in the mid-1850s and end up re-litigating the fight that actually split them: whether the Constitution is a pro-slavery document or not. Both men hate slavery equally, so I don't want the scene to be about that -- I want it to be about how you're even supposed to read a legal text in the first place. Garrison's side leans on what the framers intent actually was and how the clauses were understood at the time (three-fifths clause, fugitive slave clause, the 1808 slave-trade clause, plus that "covenant with death and an agreement with hell" line he loved -- the guy literally burned a copy of the Constitution at a rally once). Douglass, post-break with the Garrisonians (partly thanks to Gerrit Smith and Lysander Spooner's arguements, iirc), goes with the plain words on the page instead -- since "slave" and "slavery" appear nowhere in the actual text, he thinks any ambiguous clause should be read in favor of liberty. Can you write this out as a real dialogue where they're poking specific holes in each other's reading of these clauses, not just each guy giving his stump speech -- I want it to feel like two people who respect each other's minds but think the other is dead wrong.
