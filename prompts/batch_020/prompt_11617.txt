I'm several drafts into a novel set in the last years of the Weimar Republic, and one of its minor characters — a solitary university logician — is loosely modeled on Gottlob Frege. While researching him for texture, I came across something that has stuck with me well past its usefulness for the book. In 1924, the year before he died, Frege kept a private diary that reads as a bitter right-wing political testament: contempt for parliamentary democracy and universal suffrage, a longing for some strong national leader who could sweep the party system away, and explicitly antisemitic passages arguing that Jews should be stripped of political rights. This sits oddly against how Frege is usually presented, including in most of what I had read about him before: as the austere, almost disembodied inventor of modern quantificational logic and the sense/reference distinction, someone who lived so entirely inside abstract questions about number and meaning that he barely seems to have had a biography at all.

What makes this hard for me to just set aside is a detail that feels almost too pointed to be coincidence. Frege spent his career arguing against what he called "psychologism" — the idea that logical and mathematical laws could be reduced to facts about how particular minds happen to think — insisting instead that a thought is true or false in total independence of who holds it, when, or why. In one of his best-known late essays he even describes such thoughts as belonging to a "third realm," distinct from both the physical world and anyone's private mental life. So there is a strange, self-undermining quality to my own discomfort: by Frege's own account of what logic is, my instinct to let his politics color my estimate of his intellectual achievement is exactly the sort of confusion he spent his life diagnosing. I don't think I'm alone in feeling this pull both ways; my understanding is that Michael Dummett, who probably did more than anyone to secure Frege's place at the founding of analytic philosophy, wrote candidly about how shaken he was by the diary without ever quite settling what it should mean for Frege's philosophical standing.

So here is what I would like help thinking through, and I would rather not be told to simply "separate the art from the artist," since that is precisely the move that will not sit right for me. Does a discovery like this actually bear on how we ought to evaluate someone's technical or philosophical achievements, or only on how we should feel about them as a person — or is even that distinction too tidy to survive contact with a case like this one? And if there is a principled way of drawing such a line rather than just asserting it, what would it look like here, in a field that is supposed to owe nothing at all to the thinker's psychology or character? I ask partly for my own sake and partly because I still have to decide whether to let any version of this shadow fall across my fictional logician, or whether that would be a cheap trick that misses what is actually at stake in the real case.
