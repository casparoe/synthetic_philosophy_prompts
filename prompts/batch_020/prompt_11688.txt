I write about forecasting and the "superforecaster" world for a living, and a couple years back I did a piece that touched on van Fraassen's Reflection Principle -- roughly, the idea that your credence now should equal whatever you expect your future, presumably better-informed credence to be, on pain of being exploitable across time the same way an incoherent bettor is exploitable right now. Mostly forgot about it until this week, because I somehow ended up on the methodology committee for a university forecasting tournament (a decade of covering this stuff apparently gets you drafted onto things), and we've got a real mess. Our top-ranked forecaster had an independent 15% on a big geopolitical question, then revised to 45% right after the live consensus number went up on the leaderboard, and her rationale literally reads: "my own analysis hasn't moved, but when this many good forecasters disagree with me I should expect that's roughly where I'll land once I actually see their reasoning, so I'm just moving now instead of waiting." Half the committee wants to score this as exactly the calibrated humility we should be rewarding; the other half thinks its contentless herding that'll gut the independence the whole wisdom-of-crowds effect depends on if we start scoring it well. We need an actual policy before the next season opens in six weeks: how should the scoring rule, and our guidance to forecasters, treat "deferring to my anticipated future self" as grounds for revising a forecast? Write the committee memo -- a real recommendation, not a survey of considerations, and be honest about the strongest case for whichever side you don't pick.
