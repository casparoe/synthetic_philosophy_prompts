So I went down a rabbit hole this week on Wittgenstein's rule-following paradox, the Kripke version that people apparently call "Kripkenstein." You know the plus vs quus thing - the idea that you can't point to any fact about your past behavior or brain state that proves you meant "add" by the plus sign, rather than some weird function that agrees with addition on every sum you've actually ever done but spits out a totally different answer once the numbers get big enough that you've never actually tried it. The way the video explained it, the conclusion is that theres just no fact of the matter about what anyone means by any word or rule, period, so meaning is basically made up, and any interpretation of a rule is exactly as good as any other - there's no such thing as getting it "right."

This kinda messed with me cause I mess around with code in my spare time, and it made me think that's probably why arguments about "what this function is actually supposed to do" never get settled between me and other devs, and it seems like it should also wreck the whole idea of a judge interpreting a law correctly, or an AI model actually understanding a prompt instead of just pattern matching its way to an answer. But if literally nothing distinguishes a correct reading of a rule from an incorrect one, I don't get why people who study this stuff for a living havent all just given up - like why do people still argue about originalism vs. living constitution, or bother marking a kid's math homework wrong, if Kripke basically already proved there's no fact about correct usage at all? Am I getting the argument wrong somewhere, or is that genuinely the accepted conclusion and everyone in the field is just sort of ignoring what it implies?
