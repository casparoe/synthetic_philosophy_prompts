I'm a novelist currently drafting a near-future novel centered on Iris, a domestic care robot assigned to look after Frank, an elderly widower with early memory loss. About a third of the way in there's a scene I keep rewriting: checking on Frank at night, Iris narrates, in close third person, that she believes she has already given him his evening medication, but that she's nonetheless afraid she miscounted, and lies awake, so to speak, turning the possibility over. I brought the chapter to my long-running critique group, and two of my oldest writing partners -- Denny, who did a philosophy minor decades ago and never quite let it go, and Renata, who reads more cognitive science than either of us -- got into a disagreement about the scene that neither would drop, and that I haven't been able to settle myself.

Renata's opening objection was that the scene is dishonest: real AI systems, she said, aren't interpreted as having beliefs because they have them, but because attributing beliefs is a convenient shorthand for predicting what they'll do next -- which is more or less what Dennett meant by the intentional stance, introduced as a predictive strategy rather than a discovery about what's going on inside the system. So when I write that Iris "believed she'd given him the pills but feared she was wrong," I'm smuggling in a first-person ache the intentional stance was never meant to underwrite. Denny answered that this flattens Dennett's actual position: in essays like "Real Patterns," his more considered view is that a stance-dependent pattern counts as real precisely when it's robust enough that no cheaper, lower-level description could replace it without loss -- so if Iris's cognition is stipulated, within the story, to have that kind of rationalizing robustness, narrating her "insides" adds no further dishonesty, since that's exactly what novels already do with human characters: reconstruct an interior from patterns of behavior and self-report that no reader ever independently verifies. Renata's reply granted the reality of the pattern but denied that a real pattern is thereby the sort of thing that can dread anything: predictive indispensability doesn't yet secure a subject who can be right or wrong, relieved or afraid, and dressing the pattern up in that vocabulary is a category mistake sold as craft -- one she thinks readers will quietly mistake for a claim about actual machines. Denny's last word was that the same argument, applied honestly, would disqualify most literary interiority, since we have no more independent access to a human character's inner states than to Iris's; if inferring an interior from a pattern is illegitimate for her, it's illegitimate across the board, and Renata's asymmetry between her and human characters must be coming from some prior view about consciousness that she owes us, not from anything in the intentional stance itself.

I've read enough Dennett to have opinions but not enough to be sure who's actually landing punches versus who's just throwing them. Could you go through Renata's and Denny's exchange move by move and say who has the better of each specific point, rather than handing down an overall winner? And if there's a distinction they're both missing that would dissolve, or at least relocate, the disagreement, I'd like to hear about that too -- I may end up writing it into the scene.
