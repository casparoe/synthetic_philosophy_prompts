My friend Meg, a freelance graphic designer, and her neighbor Andre, a plumber, recently had a disagreement I can't stop thinking about. When Meg's basement flooded from a burst pipe on a Sunday night, Andre came straight over and fixed it in about three hours -- work he'd normally bill at an emergency-callout rate of $180 an hour. Instead of paying him, Meg offered to redesign his business's website and logo, and the two of them had to agree on how many hours of design work (her usual rate is $60 an hour) would fairly match his three hours.

Andre proposed simply converting between their normal rates: $180 x 3, divided by $60, comes to nine hours of design work. Meg objected that his emergency rate is high precisely because people are desperate and can't shop around when a pipe bursts, and building that into the trade defeats the point of exchanging favors instead of just paying his callout fee outright. She proposed using his ordinary, non-emergency rate of $90 an hour instead, which comes to four and a half hours. Andre answered that the skill and disruption of a Sunday-night job are the same no matter who's paying, so this isn't gouging -- it's simply what fast, on-call plumbing is actually worth to anyone in her position. Meg replied that fairness between neighbors doing favors should track ordinary value, not whatever a stranger with no alternative would be forced to pay. Andre's last word was that her rule conveniently discounts only his side of the trade, since nothing makes a logo redesign more valuable under desperation -- so "use the non-emergency rate" isn't a neutral principle at all, it just happens to always favor her.

Could you go through their exchange step by step, say who has the better of it at each point, flag anything both of them seem to be missing, and then give an overall verdict on which comes closer to a fair trade: Andre's nine hours, Meg's four and a half, or something else entirely?
