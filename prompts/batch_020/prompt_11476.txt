I'm about two-thirds of the way through a novel whose protagonist, a productivity blogger turned minor tech entrepreneur, discovers the virtue-tracking chart from Franklin's Autobiography and, in a fit of post-burnout self-reinvention, rebuilds it as an app: thirteen virtues, a ruled grid, a black mark for every daily lapse. The novel's emotional hinge is a dinner-table scene in which his ex-wife, who has actually read the Autobiography rather than the productivity-blog summaries of it, needles him with Franklin's own most embarrassing admission: having added Humility to his list as a thirteenth virtue, at a Quaker friend's gentle suggestion that he was generally thought proud, Franklin confessed that even total success in subduing his pride would probably leave him proud of his humility. I want her objection to land as a genuine philosophical point rather than a glib one-liner about apps and virtue, so before I put words in her mouth, I would like help getting the underlying argument right.

Here is the specific shape of the argument I am after. Not the broad-brush claim that Franklin's whole checklist method is self-defeating, but the narrower and more interesting claim that the self-defeat he stumbles into with pride and humility is peculiar to a particular subset of the thirteen virtues on his list, and does not generalize to the rest. Temperance, order, frugality, industry, and cleanliness, for instance, seem perfectly trackable in the way Franklin intended: nothing about deliberately logging one's meals, one's spending, or the state of one's workshop seems to corrupt the resulting virtue. Humility looks structurally different. Please argue for a principled version of this distinction. Propose a criterion that picks out exactly which of Franklin's thirteen virtues are vulnerable to this sort of reflexive self-undermining under deliberate self-tracking and which are not; explain why the vulnerable ones are vulnerable in a way that amounts to more than the truism that any virtue can curdle into vanity if one is not careful; and then use the criterion to rule, with actual reasons, on where sincerity, tranquility, and moderation fall.
