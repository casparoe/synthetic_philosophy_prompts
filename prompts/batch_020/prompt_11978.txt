The occasion was a favor: a friend who is TAing an introductory philosophy of language course this term asked me to sanity-check her grading on one exam response before she records it, and I would like your independent read on it as well.

Here is the question as posed to students:

"Using the Hesperus/Phosphorus case, explain (a) what it means for a term to be a rigid designator, (b) why Kripke holds that 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' is a necessary truth, and (c) why he nonetheless holds that this is something we can know only a posteriori. State the general moral Kripke draws from this case about the relationship between the necessary/contingent distinction and the a priori/a posteriori distinction, and illustrate the same moral with one further example." (20 points)

And here, verbatim, is the student's answer:

"A rigid designator is an expression that picks out the same object in every possible world in which that object exists; a non-rigid designator, such as the definite description 'the morning star,' can pick out different objects in different possible worlds depending on whatever happens to satisfy the description there. Kripke argues that ordinary proper names, including 'Hesperus' and 'Phosphorus,' are rigid designators, even though both names were originally introduced by using descriptions to fix their reference ('that bright object visible in the evening,' 'that bright object visible in the morning'). Since 'Hesperus' and 'Phosphorus' both rigidly designate Venus, the two names designate the same object in every possible world, so the identity statement 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' is true in every possible world — that is, necessarily true. Yet this was an astronomical discovery: ancient observers used the two names for what they took to be two different heavenly bodies, and it took empirical investigation to establish that the evening star and the morning star are one and the same planet. So the statement is necessary but a posteriori, which refutes the traditional Kantian assumption that necessity and apriority always travel together.

Kripke draws the same moral from natural-kind identities: 'water' and 'H2O' are both rigid designators for the same substance, so 'water is H2O' is likewise a necessary truth. Because this identity was arrived at through careful analysis of the very concept of water, rather than through observation of any particular sample, it is knowable a priori, making it a necessary a priori truth in much the same way that '7+5=12' is. This connects to Kripke's Twin Earth thought experiment: on a qualitatively identical Twin Earth where the clear, potable liquid filling the lakes and rivers is XYZ rather than H2O, our word 'water' would fail to refer to that liquid even though no observer could tell the difference by taste or sight — showing that the meaning of a natural-kind term is fixed by the world and not simply by whatever is in the speaker's head."
