I'm working on a feature about the mathematics behind the "wisdom of crowds," and the Condorcet Jury Theorem keeps coming up as its most rigorous justification: provided each voter is independently more likely than not to be correct, simple majority rule converges on the right answer as the electorate grows. A technical objection I've since encountered complicates that picture: the theorem's proof relies on voters casting "sincere" votes—meaning they vote according to their own private signal—yet once voters are modeled as rational, strategic agents who reason about what it means for their vote to be pivotal, sincere voting generally stops being anyone's best response, because in some configurations of priors and competence, the very fact of being decisive is itself evidence that should override one's private signal. Please argue that this pivotal-voter problem is fatal to using the Condorcet Jury Theorem as a defense of majority rule's truth-tracking credentials in real committees and electorates, rather than being just one more idealizing assumption—on a par with full independence across voters—that can be relaxed without disturbing the theorem's substantive lesson. Your argument should also directly engage with the natural rejoinder that game theorists have identified more sophisticated equilibria that remain informative even once sincere voting breaks down as a rational strategy.
