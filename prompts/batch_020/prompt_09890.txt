I'm a software engineer with no background in philosophy or religious studies, and I've been working through Maimonides' Guide for the Perplexed on my own time. I've gotten stuck trying to reconcile two things he seems to say about animal sacrifice. In Part III, chapter 32, explaining why the Torah commands sacrifice at all rather than something like prayer alone, he writes, in the standard Friedländer translation:

"It is, namely, impossible to go suddenly from one extreme to the other; and therefore man, according to his nature, is not capable of abandoning suddenly all to which he has been accustomed... It would at that time have made the same impression as a prophet would make at present, if he called us to the service of God and told us in His name that we should not pray to Him, nor fast, nor seek His help in time of trouble, but that we should serve Him in thought only, and not by any action. For this reason God allowed these kinds of service to remain, but transferred them from created and imaginary beings to His own name, commanding us to build Him a temple and bring our sacrifices to Him alone, strictly forbidding that any such act be offered to any other being."

Taken by itself, this makes sacrifice sound like a concession to a psychologically primitive, idol-habituated stage of humanity - a stage to be outgrown rather than something valuable in its own right. But elsewhere, in the Mishneh Torah's laws of kings, Maimonides treats the sacrificial service as something to be reinstated, not superseded, once the Temple is rebuilt in messianic times. Am I right that these two strands are in real tension, and if so, how do serious readers of Maimonides usually try to resolve it? I also vaguely recall that some later critic pointed out that Cain, Abel, and Noah all offer sacrifices in Genesis long before any idolatry exists in the story for this to be a reaction against - does that objection actually land against Maimonides, or is there a good reply available to him?
