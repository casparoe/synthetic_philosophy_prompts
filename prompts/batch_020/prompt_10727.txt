I went down a rabbit hole this weekend after a coworker brought up "Harsanyi's aggregation theorem" during one of our lunchtime arguments about utilitarianism (yes, this is a recurring thing on my team, we're all insufferable). I looked it up and think I've got the formal setup down: everyone's individual preferences over lotteries satisfy the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms, so each person has their own utility function that's unique up to an affine transform; "society" also has preferences over those same lotteries that satisfy the vNM axioms; then you add a Pareto-indifference condition -- basically, if literally every individual is indifferent between two lotteries, society has to be indifferent between them too. And apparently just from that, you can prove the social utility function has to be a weighted linear sum of everyone's individual utilities plus a constant, which is why people wave this around as a quasi-formal argument for utilitarianism. I can follow the algebra ok, but I don't have any real intuition for WHY it comes out linear/additive specifically. Like, my instinct as a programmer is that there's a million ways to combine a bunch of scores into one aggregate score, so where exactly does the proof rule out anything nonlinear that would still respect Pareto and still count as "rational" at the society level (i.e. still satisfy vNM up there too)? Where does the additivity actually sneak in?

The other piece I can't get my head around is an objection from someone named Diamond (1967, I think) involving a coin flip. From what I can tell he sets up a case with two people and one indivisible good to hand out, and shows that under Harsanyi's setup, giving the good to person A for sure, giving it to person B for sure, and flipping a fair coin to pick between them all come out exactly tied in social utility. But almost everybody's gut reaction is that the coin flip is obviously the fairer option, so something feels off. Is that actually a real inconsistency in the theorem's own premises, or is it more that the theorem was never designed to capture "fairness" in that ex-ante sense to begin with, and Diamond's just pointing at a mismatch between the theorem and our intuitions rather than an internal flaw? I'd love a walkthrough of both of these things that actually builds the intuition up from scratch rather then just restating the proof sketch or citing the conclusion -- I don't have any formal philosophy background (or math beyond undergrad linear algebra, if that helps), so feel free to get technical, just don't assume I already have intuitions here that I definately don't.
