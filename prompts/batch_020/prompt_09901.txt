So I'm a phil of mind PhD student and lately I've fallen down a rabbit hole reading about animal consciousness for my quals list -- all the stuff about how different theories (global workspace, IIT, higher-order theories, the LSE sentience-criteria people) give wildly different verdicts about where exactly the lights turn on across the animal kingdom, whether it's insects, fish, decapods, whatever. The case that's really been messing with me is octopuses specifically, partly because they're about the closest thing to an alien intelligence we've got (last common ancestor with us was something like 500+ million years ago, so all that cognitive complexity evolved completely independantly), and partly because of the neuroanatomy -- something like two thirds of their neurons are out in the arms rather than the central brain, the arms clearly do a ton of local processing on their own (there's footage of severed arms still reaching for and grabbing food), and it's genuinely unclear how much the "central" brain even knows about what any given arm is up to moment to moment. I was going on about this to some labmates over lunch and half-jokingly said someone should write the thing up as a short story, and since none of us actually did, I figured I'd outsource it to you.

Could you write me a short story (a few hundred to ~1500 words, whatever feels right) that takes this seriously as its premise -- either from close inside an octopus's pov, or following a researcher/diver who's forced to confront it, I don't mind which. The one thing I really don't want is the lazy version of this, where the octopus just has one normal humanlike stream of consciousness that happens to live in a tentacled body. I want it to actually sit with the possibility that whatever's going on in there (if "experience" is even the right word for it) might be genuinely multiple, or partial, or only loosely stitched together moment to moment, in a way that doesn't map onto any human case we already have an intuitive handle on -- even split-brain patients are still way more unified than this. Totally fine if it ends up unsettling or ambiguous rather than tidy; that's kind of the point.
