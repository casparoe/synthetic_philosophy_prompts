I'm writing my IB Philosophy extended essay on Schopenhauer's aesthetics, and I've run into a difficulty reconciling his treatment of music with the rest of his theory in The World as Will and Representation. As I understand it, he ranks architecture, sculpture and painting, and poetry (with tragedy near the summit) according to how fully each one reveals a Platonic Idea, with each Idea being a fixed grade of the will's objectification in nature — yet in Book 3, §52, he treats music as falling completely outside that hierarchy, calling it a direct copy of the will itself rather than of any Idea. Could you compare his account of music with his general theory of the other arts? I'd specifically like you to (1) explain why he thinks music escapes the Idea-mediated structure that governs the other arts, including how his analogy between a composition's bass, harmony, and melody and the "grades" of the will's objectification is meant to work, and (2) assess whether this exception sits comfortably with his claim that aesthetic pleasure generally requires the subject to become a "will-less" contemplator of an Idea, since it isn't obvious why listening to a direct copy of the will should be calming rather than just another way of being gripped by it. Please go beyond the standard one-line gloss that "Schopenhauer thought music expresses the emotions directly" — that much I already have.
