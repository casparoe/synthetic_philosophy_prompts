I write a science-and-ideas newsletter for a general audience, and this week's edition is one of those fun rundowns of classic "is it still the same thing" thought experiments. In my draft, I've basically been treating the Ship of Theseus and the statue-and-the-lump-of-clay problem as two skins on the exact same puzzle: you've got an object, you change it a bit at a time (swap the planks one by one, or squish the clay into a new shape), and eventually you have to decide whether it's still "the same thing." I even worked in the Hobbes twist, where someone secretly hoards all the old discarded planks and rebuilds a second ship out of them, since it felt like a nice way to juice up either example.

I sent the draft to an old roommate from undergrad who actually finished her philosophy degree (unlike me), mainly just to fact check some names, and all she wrote back was "these aren't even the same kind of puzzle," then went dark because she's swamped this week. Now I'm two days from deadline and stuck: is there an actual, substantive difference between the ship case and the statue/clay case, beyond the obvious surface stuff (wood vs. clay, a boat vs. a person-shaped object), or are they really just the same underlying problem in two different costumes, not truly seperate puzzles at all? And if they genuinely are different in some deeper way, should that change which one I lead the piece with?
