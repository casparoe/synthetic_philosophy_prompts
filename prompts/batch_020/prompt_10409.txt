I'm a software engineer, and I sit on my company's internal AI ethics review board, which has to sign off on any new product feature that raises an ethical red flag before it can ship. Over the past year we've started contracting an outside AI ethics advisory firm for the harder cases: we send them a spec, and they send back a verdict -- ship, don't ship, or ship with changes -- plus a short rationale that's often quite thin (something like "this undermines user autonomy"), because, we're told, their full assessment methodology is proprietary. Things came to a head a few weeks ago when the firm told us to kill a notification-timing feature that infers a user's emotional state from behavioral signals; when we pushed for the underlying reasoning, we were essentially told to trust that they'd thought it through more rigorously than we had time to.

The board is now split, and the disagreement has gotten more philosophical than I expected. One camp wants to defer to the firm's verdicts more or less the way we'd defer to outside counsel on a legal question or a pentester on a security question -- that's what we're paying for, and none of us has any special standing to overrule people who do this full-time. The other camp argues that a board which signs off on a verdict it doesn't understand hasn't actually exercised moral judgment at all, just relayed someone else's, the way you could accurately repeat a foreign-language sentence you don't understand a word of -- and that if a shipped feature later turns out to have been the wrong call, "the consultants told us to" won't feel like an adequate account of what we, the board, did. The first camp's response is that this proves too much, since we accept opaque expert conclusions constantly elsewhere in the business without anyone treating that as an abdication of responsibility. I did some searching to see whether this disagreement has a name and turned up some philosophy writing on whether it's ever legitimate to base a moral judgment purely on someone else's say-so, but it was written for a philosophy audience, and I couldn't work out how it should actually cash out in a policy.

Could you draft the short internal memo I'd circulate before our next board meeting? I need it to land on one concrete, actionable policy for how much weight the firm's verdicts should carry relative to the board's own judgment going forward, with reasons the rest of the board can actually engage with rather than an exhortation to "use good judgment" -- and I'd like it to take the deference camp's position seriously on its own terms, rather than assuming from the outset that independent understanding is required, since most of us aren't ethicists and we already act on specialist conclusions we couldn't defend ourselves in plenty of other parts of the business.
