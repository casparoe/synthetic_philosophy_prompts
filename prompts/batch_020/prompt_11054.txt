And here's the student's answer: "Anscombe's pamphlet was written to protest Oxford's decision to award Truman an honorary degree in 1956, and her central charge is that he is a mass murderer, not merely someone who made a regrettable wartime call. Her argument rests on the doctrine of double effect: it's permissible to perform an act with bad effects you foresee but don't intend, provided the good you're aiming at doesn't come about by means of the bad effect. She argues that's exactly what's missing at Hiroshima and Nagasaki — the bombs weren't aimed at a military target with civilians unluckily caught in the blast radius, they were aimed at annihilating whole cities in order to terrorize the Japanese government into surrendering, so the killing of civilians was the means to the end, not a foreseen side effect. That's why she insists on calling it murder rather than a lamentable but permissible act of war: you can't launder an intentional killing of the innocent into something acceptable just because good consequences flow from it, which is also why she rejects the standard defence that the bombs saved more lives than they cost by avoiding a land invasion of Japan — even granting that's true, it's a calculation of consequences, and consequences can't convert murder into non-murder. And because her whole argument turns on the idea that killing the innocent is never permitted no matter the stakes, Anscombe is basically committed to pacifism here even if she wouldn't put it that way herself — once you take double effect seriously, almost any strategic bombing campaign is going to have this same structure, so nothing in modern war comes out justified. It's worth noting she actually coins the term 'consequentialism' in this pamphlet to label the kind of reasoning she thinks Truman's defenders are using, a label that's stuck in ethics ever since."
