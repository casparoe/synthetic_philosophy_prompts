"Explain how the Duhem-Quine thesis is supposed to generate a problem of underdetermination of theory by evidence. Then present ONE serious response to this problem from the philosophy of science literature, and assess whether it succeeds."

here's the answer:

"Pierre Duhem and W.V.O. Quine each argued that no scientific hypothesis is ever tested in isolation. On what's usually called the Duhem-Quine thesis, a hypothesis only yields testable predictions once its conjoined with a raft of auxiliary assumptions (about instruments, background theory, initial conditions, etc), so when a prediction fails we can always blame one of the auxiliaries instead of the hypothesis itself - logic alone never tells us where to lay the blame. Duhem and Quine both push this insight all the way down to logic and mathematics: because our beliefs meet experience only "as a corporate body" (Quine's phrase), in principle any statement whatsoever, even a law of logic, can be held true come what may if we're willing to revise something else in the web. This is supposed to generate underdetermination of theory by evidence because no finite body of evidence can ever force us to a unique theory: whatever data we have, there will always be some way of tinkering with the auxiliaries to save a given theory, and by the same token there will always be some rival, empirically equivalent theory that the very same data fits just as well.

A well-known response comes from Larry Laudan and Jarrett Leplin, who argue that empirical equivalence doesn't give us underdetermination in any sense that should actually worry a realist. They distinguish a trivial, transient underdetermination - two theories are equivalent only relative to the evidence we happen to have now, but future observations, or independent tests of the auxiliary assumptions, could still separate them - from a strong, permanent underdetermination where no possible evidence, ever, could favor one theory over the other. Their complaint is that people slide from the easy, obviously-true claim (weak underdetermination happens all the time) to the radical claim (strong underdetermination is the norm) without any real argument, and they note that this exact same slide is what underwrites Laudan's argument that the historical track record of abandoned successful theories (phlogiston, the ether, etc) should make us doubt our current best theories too. They also point out that even genuinely empirically equivalent theories need not be equally good options, since extra-empirical virtues like unification and explanatory depth can still make one better supported than the other.

I think Laudan and Leplin are basically right against the strongest, "anything goes" version of underdetermination, though it doesn't really touch weaker, historically-grounded worries like Stanford's unconceived alternatives, where the problem isn't logical equivalence but our own failure of imagination."
