Me and my friend Theo got into a whole thing last night about Bertrand's chord paradox and neither of us really budged, so now I want to see the argument fought out properly instead of the half-finished version we managed after a few beers. Quick recap in case you need it: you ask for the probability that a "random" chord of a circle is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, and depending on whether you randomize the two endpoints, the midpoint of the chord, or a random radius plus a point along it, you get 1/3, 1/4, and 1/2 respectively -- three perfectly reasonable "uniform" distributions giving three different answers. Theo's a big Jaynes guy and kept insisting that Jaynes's invariance argument basically kills the paradox for good: if you demand that the distribution not care about where the circle sits, how its rotated, or how big it is (the way it shouldn't, if you picture the chords as generated by some physical process like tossing straws at the circle from far away), only the random-radius method survives, so 1/2 is just correct, and the principle of indifference works fine as long as you apply it to the right invariant description instead of an arbitrary one.

I wasn't convinced. My pushback was that you need to already know the "right" symmetry group before Jaynes's trick can even get off the ground, and that's substantive information about the setup, not pure ignorance -- plus for a lot of the other classic indifference puzzles (the wine-water case, where indifference over the ratio of wine to water and indifference over its reciprocal disagree, or that cube-factory example, I forget whose it is, where indifference over side length vs. over volume disagree) there's no nice geometric symmetry sitting around to invoke, so his fix looks like it only really works on the one example everyone always trots out to showcase it. Theo thinks that's a cheap shot since the straw-tossing prediction is actually testable, and apparently Jaynes really did throw straws and got something close to 1/2, which he says proves this isn't just aesthetics.

We ran out of steam before either of us landed a real blow, so could you write a dialogue between the two positions -- call them the Jaynesian and the skeptic, or whatever names fit -- picking up right where the "it's empirically testable" point and the "doesn't generalize" objection collide? I want both sides played as sharp as they can actually be, no strawmanning the Jaynesian into just chanting "invariance," and no strawmanning the skeptic into pretending 1/2 doesn't obviously seem right, with each one actually responding to what the other just said instead of restating their opening position. If it fits naturally, I'd like them to get concrete about one of the non-circular cases partway through, and ideally the dialogue leaves us clearer on what would have to be true for the invariance move to rescue the principle of indifference in general, rather than it just being a one-off patch for this one paradox.
