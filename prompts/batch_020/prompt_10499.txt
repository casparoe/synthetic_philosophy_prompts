I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of science (my undergraduate degree was in physics, which is how I ended up caring about this particular dispute), and this term I've taken over as managing editor of our department's small graduate-run journal. A faculty advisor technically has final sign-off, but in practice she defers to whatever the three of us on the editorial board recommend, so the memo I bring to next week's meeting will effectively decide the matter. We have a submission that has split our two referees right down the middle, and rather than relay "the referees disagreed" to an author who has put two years into this paper, I'd like to give the board something more substantive to act on.

The paper argues that renormalization-group explanations of universality in continuous phase transitions -- the fact that, say, a uniaxial ferromagnet and a fluid at its liquid-gas critical point share identical critical exponents despite having nothing relevant in common at the level of microphysics -- provide genuine evidence for a strong, ontologically robust form of emergence. Its central premise, drawing on the Yang-Lee theorem, is that the non-analyticities marking a real phase transition appear only in the thermodynamic limit and in no actual finite system, which the author (following Batterman) takes to show that the statistical-mechanical story is explanatorily incomplete on its own terms, not merely hard to compute in practice. Referee A recommends acceptance essentially as submitted, calling it an overdue defense of Batterman against a complacent reconciliationist consensus. Referee B recommends rejection outright, on the grounds that the paper never seriously engages Butterfield's reply: that the large-but-finite systems physicists actually study already display the robust, comparatively autonomous patterns the author is after; that the N-to-infinity limit is best read as a mathematical device for characterizing an asymptotic trend rather than proof of explanatory failure; and that the paper's argument depends on silently sliding between an epistemic and an ontological sense of "emergence."

Both referees are people whose judgment I trust on this material, so I don't think I can responsibly split the difference or just count votes. Could you draft the memo I'd bring to the board: a clear recommendation among accept, reject, and major revisions, with the reasoning laid out precisely enough that the board could actually act on it and, if it comes to revisions, the author would know what to change -- and one that engages seriously with whichever referee ends up on the losing side, rather than simply declaring a winner?
