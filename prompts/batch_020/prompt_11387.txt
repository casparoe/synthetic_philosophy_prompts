I'm teaching a graduate seminar on American pragmatism this semester, and next week's session is the Dewey-Lippman exchange on democracy, which I assign every year but never quite get to land right. Students read Public Opinion and The Phantom Public alongside The Public and Its Problems, and almost every time it flattens into "Lippmann thinks voters are basically sheep, Dewey believes in the wisdom of the common man" — a caricature of both guys that misses what's actually at stake. The "omnicompetent citizen" bit isn't really about IQ, and Dewey's answer isn't just cheerleading for the masses; his whole move is about what a public even is, and how inquiry gets socialized once one exists. My colleague down the hall in poli sci still ribs me every year for "assigning the guy who lost the argument," which is exactly the flattened take I'm trying to get students past this time.

So could you write me a dialogue between the two of them, maybe 800-1000 words, staged as though they got seated next to each other at a dinner sometime after Dewey's book came out and couldn't help litigating it in person? (I know this exact conversation never happened — that's fine, it's a teaching device, not a history assignment.) I want Lippmann to make his strongest case: the argument from the sheer scale and technicality of modern industrial life, the way news reaches people pre-digested through the press, and why he thought holding democracy to the standard of the fully-informed citizen was self-undermining rather than just idealistic. And I want Dewey to really engage with that, not just reassert faith in ordinary people — his actual counter is about the eclipse of the public under modern conditions, how a public gets constituted by the indirect consequences of transactions, and why he thought the remedy was better inquiry and communication rather than handing things over to insiders. Don't let either one land a clean knockout; I want them genuinely pushing on each other's weakest spots instead of talking past one another.
