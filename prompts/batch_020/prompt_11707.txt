Backstory: this started because a classmate said something like "well obviously later theories are just closer to the truth than earlier ones, thats just common sense" during a workshop, and I realized I actually had no idea how you'd cash out "closer to the truth" once you admit the theory in question is false — and it turns out neither did Popper, not really.

Here's the part I'm least sure I'm explaining right. Popper wanted a formal sense in which a false theory could still be closer to the truth than another false theory, so a fallibilist could say science progresses toward truth even though no theory we currently hold is ever actually true. His move: define the content of a theory as the set of all its logical consequences, split that into true consequences ("truth content") and false consequences ("falsity content"), and say theory B is more verisimilar than theory A when B's truth content contains all of A's truth content (plus more) while B's falsity content is contained in A's (with at least one of those containments proper). Seems intuitive enough on its face. But then in '74 Tichý and David Miller independently proved this can't actually do the job it was built for: if A and B are both genuinely false — which is the whole point, since that's the interesting case Popper actually cared about — it's provably impossible for B to satisfy that whole package relative to A unless B turns out to be secretly true (or the two are just equivalent). So the one case the definition exists for is exactly the case where it collapses. The way I currently gloss the "why" for my reader is that false content and true content get logically entangled through things like conditionals and disjunctions, so you can't cleanly trim away a theory's false consequences (or add new true ones) without that move dragging the other category along with it — but I'm honestly not sure that's the cleanest or even fully correct way to state the intuition, as opposed to just asserting the result and moving on.

After that I sketch how people tried to rescue the idea by swapping the pure logical definition for similarity-based measures instead — comparing how "close" a theory's models are to the actual world, along the lines of Niiniluoto's min-sum approach or Oddie's and Tichý's own later distance-based proposals — and I raise the worry that this trades a clean logical definition for a hand-picked similarity metric, which seems to make "closer to the truth" depend on which language or predicates you use to describe the world, undercutting exactly the kind of objectivity verisimilitude was supposed to give realists in the first place.
