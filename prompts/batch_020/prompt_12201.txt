Here's the question they got:

"Pascal writes in the Pensées that justice without force is powerless, and force without justice is tyrannical, and concludes that since people could not make justice strong, they made what is strong seem just instead. Explain the reasoning behind this passage, and explain how (if at all) it differs from Thrasymachus's claim in Republic Book I that justice is 'the advantage of the stronger.'"

And here's the student's answer:

"Pascal's point is that justice and force each need the other: justice on its own has no teeth, so people just ignore it, while force on its own is nothing but tyranny with no legitimacy behind it. Because people can't agree on what's actually just, but everyone can recognize who's powerful, society settles for the workable thing instead of the right thing - instead of making justice powerful, it makes power look just. In that sense Pascal basically lands in the same place as Thrasymachus, who says justice just is whatever benefits the stronger party: both are saying that in practice 'justice' just tracks who holds power, so the word isn't doing any independent moral work. Really, Pascal's fragment is a more literary way of making Hobbes's argument in Leviathan - since people will never stop disagreeing about justice on their own, the only way out of endless conflict is to hand final authority to a sovereign strong enough to make its rulings stick, and just treat that as justice from then on. So Pascal isn't objecting to any of this so much as calmly explaining why it's unavoidable."
