The practice prompt reads: "Frederick Douglass's understanding of the U.S. Constitution's relationship to slavery changed significantly between the 1840s and 1860. Describe both his earlier and later positions, explain what prompted the shift, and analyze the method of interpretation Douglass used to defend his mature view."

Here is the response we were asked to grade:

"In the 1840s, Frederick Douglass was a close ally of William Lloyd Garrison and shared the Garrisonian view that the Constitution was fundamentally a pro-slavery document. Garrisonians pointed to the three-fifths clause, the fugitive slave clause, and the clause permitting the slave trade to continue until 1808 as proof that the framers had built protections for slavery directly into the nation's founding law. Because voting or holding office required swearing an oath to support the Constitution, Garrisonians like Douglass refused to participate in electoral politics and instead called for the North to dissolve the Union rather than stay bound to a slaveholding South. It was Douglass himself who coined the description of the Constitution as 'a covenant with death, and an agreement with hell,' a phrase that captured just how irredeemable he believed the document to be.

By 1851, however, Douglass had broken publicly and painfully with Garrison. The split coincided with his growing friendship with the wealthy abolitionist and Liberty Party politician Gerrit Smith, whose financial backing allowed Douglass to merge his paper with the Liberty Party Paper to form Frederick Douglass' Paper. Douglass now insisted, along with Smith and the radical lawyer Lysander Spooner, that the Constitution was actually an anti-slavery document, since the words 'slave' and 'slavery' never once appear in its text. In his famous Fourth of July address the following year, he declared that read as it ought to be read, the Constitution is 'a glorious liberty document' rather than a pact with slaveholders.

Douglass developed this position most fully in his 1860 Glasgow speech, 'The Constitution of the United States: Is It Pro-Slavery or Anti-Slavery?,' delivered a few years after the Dred Scott decision had raised the stakes of the debate. There, Douglass's central interpretive move was to recover the framers' original intentions: he argued that many delegates at the Philadelphia convention had privately hoped slavery would eventually die out and had deliberately kept it out of the text so that the document could later be read as an instrument of freedom. For Douglass, uncovering this hidden anti-slavery intent behind the founding generation was ultimately what licensed reading the Constitution against slavery rather than for it."
