so I went down a rabbit hole recently re-reading Aaronson's "The Ghost in the Quantum Turing Machine" and got stuck on the bit about functionalism. The classic Putnam/Searle worry is that "implementing a computation" is a way too cheap relation between physical systems and programs -- if you're allowed to gerrymander the mapping from physical microstates to computational states however you like, you can argue a rock (or a wall, or a waterfall) is secretly implementing any program you want, including a full conscious mind, which makes computationalism about the mind sound kind of vacuous. Aaronson's fix is to bring in complexity theory: an implementation should only count if the decoding map from physical states to computational states is itself "cheap," i.e. it shouldn't take exponentially more resources to find/verify than just running the computation directly would.

Write me an essay on whether this complexity-theoretic move actually rescues functionalism from the triviality objections, or whether its just kicking the can down the road (e.g. maybe you can still gerrymander a cheap-enough decoder for any system you like, or the fix ends up ruling in/out the wrong things as minds). Weigh the strongest arguments on both sides but actually commit to a real verdict by the end -- don't just land on "it's more subtle than that."
