I'm reporting a piece on panpsychism, and the more I read, the more the field looks defined by a single fault line: the combination problem. If fundamental entities such as electrons or quantum fields possess primitive experience, how could countless micro-experiences ever add up to the single, unified perspective of a person? This has split panpsychists into two broad camps. Micropsychists keep the traditional bottom-up picture, treating macro-subjects like us as somehow composed of many micro-subjects, and so must explain how numerically distinct subjects — not just qualities or physical parts — could ever combine. Cosmopsychists invert the priority, holding that the one fundamental subject is the cosmos as a whole, with finite subjects like us as dependent, partial aspects of that cosmic experience; this dodges the combination problem only to run into what looks like its mirror image, sometimes called the decombination problem: how a single, unified cosmic consciousness could ever differentiate into what presents itself, from the inside, as many separate, bounded minds.

For the piece, I want to see the strongest version of this disagreement play out. Write a dialogue between a committed micropsychist and a committed cosmopsychist in which each argues that the other's problem — decombination for the cosmopsychist, combination for the micropsychist — is the harder one to actually solve. Each should respond to the specific objection just raised against their own view rather than simply restating it, and the exchange should end without either side clearly winning.
