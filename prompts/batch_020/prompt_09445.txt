I'm a PhD student in philosophy of religion, and this term I've been trying to write a chapter arguing that the grounding objection to middle knowledge associated with Robert Adams and William Hasker is, structurally, the same complaint the Dominicans pressed against Molina four centuries earlier, just re-dressed in truthmaker-theoretic vocabulary. My advisor thinks this is too quick: he suspects that a present-day Molinist and a present-day Bañezian Thomist, if actually made to argue with each other, would not converge on the same sticking point at all, because what's really driving the Thomist side is a worry about grace and providence rather than a worry about semantics for counterfactuals. I'd like to see whether he's right, so I want to stage the conversation.

Please write a dialogue between a contemporary Molinist and a contemporary Bañezian Thomist debating how divine providence and creaturely freedom fit together. Have the Thomist open with the grounding objection in its sharpest form: that a counterfactual of creaturely freedom can be neither necessary nor made true by any actual choice, so nothing available at the "middle" logical moment fixes its truth value. The Molinist should answer with something more than an appeal to mystery -- an appeal to God's supercomprehension of possible essences, say, or a challenge to whether the objection's truthmaker demands are ones a Thomist can consistently make in the first place. Then have the Molinist go on the offensive: pressing the Thomist on whether physical premotion, which settles the will's act prior to its own willing, can really be squared with libertarian freedom rather than the sort of determinism that led Jesuits at the time to accuse Dominicans of crypto-Calvinism -- and on whether intrinsically efficacious grace doesn't make God more directly responsible for reprobation than the Molinist's congruism does, where grace counts as efficacious just because God knows, via middle knowledge, that it will be freely met with consent.

Give both figures equally strong material and have them actually respond to each other's specific moves rather than reciting talking points past one another -- I don't want either position quietly strawmanned so the other can win. You don't need to force a resolution, but end with each character stating, in their own words, what they take the real, irreducible core of the disagreement to be: is it ultimately about the metaphysics of freedom, about the logic of providence, or about something else that the grounding objection was never really about in the first place?
