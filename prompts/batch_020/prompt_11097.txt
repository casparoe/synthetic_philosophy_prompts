I'm working on a longform piece about the afterlife of Zeno's paradoxes, and one comparison in my notes refuses to resolve itself cleanly. It involves James F. Thomson's 1954 lamp: the device that gets switched on, then off, then on again infinitely often, with each interval between flicks half the length of the one before, so that an infinite number of switchings is packed into exactly two minutes. While preparing the piece I reread Aristotle's presentation of the Dichotomy and the Achilles in the Physics, and I also came across the argument, made forcefully by Jonathan Barnes and others, that the historical Zeno may not have been the earnest Eleatic loyalist of the textbooks at all, but something closer to a contrarian who prized paradox for its own sake and only loosely tethered his conclusions to Parmenides' monism. Two philosophers I spoke with for the piece gave me flatly opposed answers about what this Zeno, on either reading, would have made of Thomson's lamp, and I would like a more rigorous adjudication than either offered me.

Here is the technical wrinkle I think their disagreement was skating over. The standard modern answer to the Dichotomy and the Achilles is that Zeno's argument only shows that an infinite sequence of ever-smaller sub-tasks has no first or last member, while the quantity that actually matters — Achilles's position, say — converges to a perfectly determinate value regardless. Thomson built his lamp precisely to resist that style of answer: the switching times converge nicely to the two-minute mark, but the resulting sequence of lamp-states (on, off, on, off, ...) has no limit at all in the ordinary sense. He explicitly considered, and rejected, the reply that Grandi's series 1 − 1 + 1 − ... can be given a generalized "sum" of one-half by Cesàro or Abel summation, on the grounds that this gives no sense to the idea of the lamp being half on. So the lamp trades on a missing limit, where Zeno's own paradoxes trade on a missing first or last step — and Thomson meant it as a rebuke to anyone who thought the mathematics behind the Dichotomy had made peace with infinite task-completion in general.

Given that distinction, I would like your best-considered reconstruction of how Zeno would have reacted to being shown Thomson's lamp. Please address three things explicitly: first, whether the "earnest monist" or the "contrarian showman" reading of his purposes would lead him to claim the lamp as a natural extension of his own method, or instead to treat it as a genuinely different sort of puzzle on which he has no special claim; second, whether the missing-step/missing-limit distinction is one his surviving arguments, as reported by Aristotle, equip him to draw at all, or whether that would be asking too much of him; and third, exactly where in your answer you are extrapolating responsibly from the historical record and where you are essentially guessing. A carefully hedged answer would be far more useful to me here than a confident one that glides past the anachronism.
