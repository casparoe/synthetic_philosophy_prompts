Last month I underwent a long, slow induction into general anesthesia, and in the strange interval before I lost consciousness I had the vivid impression of being aware of "myself" while having no awareness whatsoever of having a body: no limbs, no weight, no location in space, just a bare sense that "I" existed. Curious afterward, I started reading about Avicenna's "flying man" thought experiment, in which he imagines a person created instantaneously, suspended in a void, and unable to perceive anything at all through the senses — including their own limbs and organs — who would nonetheless still affirm that they themselves exist. I assumed this was settled history-of-philosophy trivia, with the obvious comparison being to Descartes' cogito, but the secondary literature turns out to be quite divided on what the argument is actually supposed to establish. Some read it as a genuine demonstration that the soul is an immaterial substance really distinct from the body; others insist Avicenna intends it only as a more modest "reminder," pointing the reader toward a primitive, non-sensory mode of self-awareness, while leaving the harder metaphysical work of proving the soul's incorporeality to separate arguments made elsewhere in his system. Before I lean on this argument to make sense of my own experience, I would like to see that dispute properly fought out. Could you write a dialogue between two contemporary Avicenna scholars who hold these opposing readings, in which each engages seriously with the other's strongest objections rather than simply restating their own position? Both should be equally sharp, and the disagreement should remain substantive throughout, rather than either side conceding too easily or being reduced to a caricature.
