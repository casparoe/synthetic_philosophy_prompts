I've recently joined the board of a homeowners' association for a condominium development that's being built and sold off in phases: of forty-two planned units, thirty are sold and occupied, and the remaining twelve are still unbuilt or unsold. What began as a routine bylaws review has turned into the most contentious thing the board has faced in years, and after a couple of meetings that generated far more heat than clarity, I'd like a more rigorous outside take than anyone on the board has managed to offer.

The dispute is nominally just about voting rights, but it keeps fracturing into sub-disputes every time we think we've settled it. Under the current bylaws, one vote attaches to each deed, held by whoever is on the title. About a third of the thirty sold units are owned by investors who rent them out on long leases and have never lived there, while several tenants who've rented for years, attend every meeting, and are bound by every rule the board passes have no vote at all, since tenancy isn't ownership. Meanwhile, the board is about to approve a fifteen-year reserve-funding and renovation plan that will financially bind whoever eventually buys the twelve unsold units — people who, by definition, can't be consulted now. The development company that still holds title to those twelve units, and will cast its votes until each one sells, has said plainly that it intends to vote however keeps its own carrying costs lowest, with no particular loyalty to the future owners it's nominally standing in for. Predictably, the board has split: one faction wants to leave the rule exactly as it is, since "one deed, one vote" is at least unambiguous and hard to litigate; another wants to extend a vote to long-term tenants and hold the unsold units' votes in some kind of trust for their eventual owners, on the theory that whoever actually has to live under a rule has the strongest claim to a say in making it.

This feels like exactly the kind of dispute that would get a long, careful treatment on a rationalist blog, and I keep wishing someone had already written that post — but since I can't find one, I'd rather reason it through directly than lean on half-remembered analogies. Part of me suspects the "one deed, one vote" camp is really just defending a Schelling point — a rule that's stable and hard to litigate, rather than one that's obviously fair — but administrative simplicity has to count for something, and I don't want to dismiss their position just because it's convenient. More fundamentally, my suspicion is that every one of these proposals quietly smuggles in an answer to the very question it's supposed to be neutral about: there's no way to decide who gets to help decide without having, in some sense, already decided who counts. But I can't tell whether that's a genuine and probably irresolvable problem, or just a sophisticated-sounding rationalization for whichever scheme happens to favor my own preferred outcome. I'd like your honest, fully worked-through answer: is there a principled way to settle who should get a vote in a case like this, and if so, what is it, and why does it beat the alternatives? And if you conclude that the problem is genuinely unfixable in principle, I want the argument for that conclusion, not a diplomatic shrug.
