As a PhD student in philosophy of mind and cognitive science, I keep running into an unresolved dispute that I want to work through carefully for a dissertation chapter. Jakob Hohwy has argued that because the brain's generative model is only ever in indirect, inferential contact with its worldly causes -- formally, the two are separated by a 'Markov blanket' -- the self-evidencing character of prediction-error minimization favors a broadly internalist, brain-bound picture of cognition. Andy Clark and other embodied and enactive theorists respond that once active inference is taken seriously, so that prediction error gets resolved by acting on the world rather than merely by updating internal beliefs, the very same formalism sits at least as comfortably with embodied or extended readings of the mind; on this view, treating the Markov blanket as a literal skin-and-skull boundary over-interprets what is fundamentally a statistical modeling device. Write an essay adjudicating this dispute, addressing in particular (a) whether the Markov blanket formalism can bear the metaphysical weight either side wants to place on it, and (b) whether active inference genuinely shifts the internalism/externalism calculus relative to prediction-error minimization via belief-updating alone. Weigh the strongest considerations on each side, but don't hedge into a non-committal conclusion: defend a specific verdict on what, if anything, the free energy principle and predictive processing imply for internalism versus embodied or extended cognition. (A verdict like 'the formalism is metaphysically inert and the internalist gloss is doing all the work' or 'active inference genuinely tips the balance toward embodiment' would both count as real verdicts, provided you argue for one.)
