I'm currently taking a history of economic thought course, and we've just wrapped up the opening unit on Aristotle before moving on next week to the medieval scholastics and their debates about usury. We spent two sessions working closely through the chapters of Politics Book I that deal with household management and the different modes of acquiring wealth (roughly chapters 8 through 11), together with the discussion of justice in exchange in Nicomachean Ethics Book V, chapter 5. My professor was insistent that we not flatten Aristotle's position into the bumper-sticker version, "Aristotle was against usury," and instead reconstruct the actual argument: the distinction between household management (oikonomia) and the unlimited art of acquisition (chrematistike); the idea that any possession, like a shoe, has both a proper use and a use in exchange, and that retail trade (kapelike) becomes suspect precisely because money allows accumulation to become boundless in a way natural goods never could; the claim that money (nomisma) is a matter of convention or law rather than nature; and finally the argument that interest (tokos) is the most unnatural mode of acquisition of all, since it treats money, which he thought of as intrinsically barren, as though it could breed offspring of its own kind.

For our final paper, we've each been asked to take some feature of contemporary finance that obviously postdates fourth-century Athens and work out, as rigorously as we can from the primary texts rather than from secondhand summaries, what Aristotle would say about it. I've chosen cryptocurrency, and specifically decentralized finance ("DeFi") lending, where users deposit crypto tokens into an automated smart contract and receive a continuously accruing "yield" that functions like interest but has no bank, state, or individual creditor on the other side of the transaction. Two questions are giving me trouble and I'd like your help thinking them through with real reference to Aristotle's own arguments rather than to popular impressions of him. First, would something like Bitcoin even count as nomisma by Aristotle's own criteria, given that no polis issues or backs it and its exchange value fluctuates wildly? Second, and independently of that, would DeFi yield count as a paradigm case of tokos, or does the total absence of a human lender who could be said to exploit a debtor mean his critique doesn't straightforwardly transfer to an algorithmic system? In section, one classmate argued that the mechanism is all that matters and the missing human lender is irrelevant, while another insisted that Aristotle's ethical objection to usury is really about a vicious relationship between persons and therefore can't survive the disappearance of the person on the lending side. I'd value your own best reconstruction of where Aristotle would likely have landed, and where in your answer you're extending his framework versus where you're confident the text already settles the matter.
