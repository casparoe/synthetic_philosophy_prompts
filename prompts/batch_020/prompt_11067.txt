I've been reading Overcoming Bias and then LessWrong on and off since roughly the Sequences era, and Pascal's Mugging is one of those pieces of community lore I keep circling back to, because the standard "solution" never feels like it has actually landed anywhere stable. The setup, in case it helps to have it stated precisely rather than assumed: in Bostrom's original 2009 paper, a stranger threatens to torture 3^^^3 people (a number so large under Knuth's up-arrow notation that ordinary intuition just breaks) unless you hand over five dollars, and the worry is that garden-variety expected-utility reasoning with an unbounded utility function tells you to pay, since no probability you could rationally assign to the threat seems small enough to cancel out a disutility that size. Yudkowsky's patch, developed on the back of Bostrom's discussion, is the "leverage penalty": your prior credence in "I am in a position to affect N lives" should itself scale roughly as 1/N, on the grounds that in any sufficiently large reality only a vanishing fraction of observers get to be that pivotal, which is enough to defuse the mugger's bare verbal claim.

What I keep getting stuck on is the extension of this in "Pascal's Muggle," where the mugger doesn't just assert their powers but actually performs some small, startling violation of physics in front of you before making the threat. The leverage penalty is so extreme (on the order of 1 in 3^^^3) that no amount of ordinary sensory evidence -- which is always compatible with hallucination, simulation, or sufficiently advanced trickery -- looks capable of pushing your posterior high enough to justify paying up; a perfectly consistent leverage-penalizing agent should apparently stay a "muggle," unmoved, even while the miracle is happening right in front of them. A friend of mine, who's considerably more sanguine than I am about the whole rationalist decision-theory project, thinks Yudkowsky's eventual move -- allowing that sufficiently strong evidence can cross some finite threshold and license paying up after all -- basically closes the case.

I don't think it does, and rather than have you summarize both sides, I'd like you to make the case for my side of this disagreement. Specifically: please argue that raising a finite evidential threshold in "Pascal's Muggle" does not actually close the exploit the leverage penalty was built to fix, but merely relocates it -- from the space of claimed stakes to the space of producible evidence. If manufacturing an experience that clears whatever threshold a bounded human observer can set is cheaper, for a sufficiently resourceful trickster, than the mugger's claimed stakes would need to be real in order to be worth that cost, then the structural exploit is unchanged; only the mugger's cost of doing business has changed. Please take seriously the most natural objection -- that convincingly faking a physics violation is not actually cheap, and that this cost asymmetry is exactly what does the work -- rather than waving it away. I'd rather have four or five tight paragraphs of real argument than another survey of the leverage-penalty literature; I can get that from the wiki.
