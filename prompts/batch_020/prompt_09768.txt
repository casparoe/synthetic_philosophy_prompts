So I've been kind of obsessed with Alan Turing lately after watching a documentary about him and then falling down a Wikipedia hole for like two hours, and theres something that's been bugging me ever since. The guy died in 1954, which is right around when Watson and Crick had just published the double helix structure, but well before anyone actually figured out how the DNA "alphabet" gets translated into proteins — that whole codon/triplet thing didn't get cracked til the early-to-mid 60s. And what's wild to me is that this is a man who spent WW2 literally breaking codes for a living, then basically invented half of theoretical computer science thinking hard about what a "code" or a program even is at bottom, and THEN, near the end of his life, wrote a paper about how stripes and spots on animals form using continuous chemistry/diffusion equations (the morphogenesis paper) — nothing digital about it at all.

So here's what I keep wondering: if Turing had lived another 10-15 years and actually gotten to see the genetic code worked out, do you think he'd have said "yep, that's a real code, same basic idea as Enigma, just running on a different substrate"? Or do you think the pattern-forming, continuous-chemistry side of him would've pushed back and said biologists are just borrowing his field's vocabulary for a process that's really more like a chemical reaction that doesn't need "decoding" by anybody, the way an actual cipher does? Me and a friend have been going back and forth on this for weeks over text and neither of us can land anywhere satisfying. I don't want a wishy-washy "well it's complicated, he might've said either" answer — I want you to actually pick a side and defend it using what's known about how the guy actually thought and worked, not just modern hot takes on the whole DNA-as-code debate.
