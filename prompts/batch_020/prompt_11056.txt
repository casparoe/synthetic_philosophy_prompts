After forty years as a field geologist — my working life spent reading the deep time recorded in rock strata — I have recently taken up philosophy in retirement, and Wittgenstein's *On Certainty* has forced me to rethink a long-running, fruitless argument with my brother-in-law, a committed young-earth creationist. No radiometric date, ice core, or fossil sequence I cite ever seems to gain any purchase on his position, and I have come to suspect the disagreement is not really about evidence at all, but about what Wittgenstein calls "hinge" propositions: unargued background certainties, such as "the earth is very old," against which evidence and doubt get their sense in the first place. This looks close to what Robert Fogelin called a "deep disagreement," in which two parties do not merely disagree about facts within a shared framework but rest on divergent hinges. Please describe the most important implications of hinge epistemology, and of the deep-disagreement literature it has inspired, for whether rational persuasion is even possible in a case like this, and for whether I ought to change how I argue with him.
