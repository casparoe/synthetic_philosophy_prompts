Two friends of mine got into it over a little arithmetic script one of them built, and I can't decide who's actually right. Marcus wired up a script that adds numbers, and it turned out that past a certain size it started giving wrong sums — consistently wrong the same way every time, not just random glitches. Marcus said this proves the script was never really doing addition to begin with: the whole time it was computing some other function that just happens to agree with plus on every number anyone had tested, and there's no fact of the matter, even in principle, about which one it 'really' meant. Tanya said that's silly, you can just read the source code, the code says +, and the big-number stuff is obviously an overflow bug, end of story. Marcus came back with: the code is only more symbols that themselves need interpreting, and besides, the chip does exactly what its transistors do every single time, with perfect consistency — calling the big-number outputs 'wrong' already assumes plus was the target function, which is exactly what's being argued about. He added that any finite machine has to diverge from true addition somewhere, so pointing at 'the algorithm' can't be what settles it. Tanya said fine, but that logic proves way too much, since it would mean no person and no machine has ever added two numbers in the history of the world, which can't be right, so somethings gotta give — and that at some point a documented, agreed-upon design intention just has to be what fixes what's meant, even if you could never verify it from outputs alone. Marcus said that's basically conceding his point and just moving the 'fact' from the machine to a bunch of people agreeing, which isn't a further fact at all, just agreement.
