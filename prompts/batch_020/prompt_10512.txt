I cover science and ideas, and I'm partway through a feature on the diagnostic turbulence surrounding PTSD: DSM-5's 2013 overhaul, which split the old "avoidance and numbing" symptom cluster into two and dropped the old requirement that a person have responded to the trauma with "fear, helplessness, or horror"; the WHO's subsequent decision in ICD-11 to carve out a separate "complex PTSD" diagnosis for people who also show disturbed emotion regulation, self-concept, and relationships; and the current push from some clinicians to formalize adjacent categories like "moral injury" and race-based traumatic stress. Rereading Ian Hacking's essays on looping effects for background, I keep running into the same move from the philosophers and historians of psychiatry I've interviewed: PTSD gets treated as close to a paradigm "interactive kind" in Hacking's sense, where classifying people changes how they understand and present themselves, which loops back to reshape the classification itself, unlike an "indifferent kind" such as a quark or a rock, which is unmoved by what we call it. More than one source has offered this looping story as the deep explanation for why the diagnosis has never stopped moving since 1980.

Before I write that up as settled background, I'd like you to argue the other side as forcefully as you can: that Hacking's looping apparatus does no real explanatory work in the PTSD case, and that every revision historians point to as evidence of "looping" is fully explicable by ordinary mechanisms of scientific and social change that have nothing distinctively to do with feedback running through the classified individuals' own self-understanding. To make this more than a verbal dispute, please begin by pinning down what would have to be true of an episode for it to count as genuine looping in Hacking's sense, as opposed to, say, a committee revising criteria in light of new epidemiological data, or advocacy groups lobbying for recognition without any accompanying change in how patients privately experience or describe their own symptoms. Then use that distinction to go after specific moments in PTSD's history, rather than simply asserting that Hacking's story is too vague to test. I'd also appreciate a short closing section anticipating the strongest comeback a committed Hacking defender could make, and explaining why it still falls short of rescuing the looping explanation.
