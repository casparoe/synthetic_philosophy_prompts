I recently joined a small book club, and this month we read a novel whose protagonist can glimpse, and occasionally seems to briefly slip into, the lives she would be living now if she had made different choices at various points in her past. I'll admit the premise landed closer to home than I expected, since I had turned down a job that would have moved me across the country only a few months before we picked the book. At the meeting, two members, Farid (who did a philosophy minor and never fully recovered) and Naomi (currently auditing a graduate seminar on modality), ended up in an argument about whether the "other" women the protagonist glimpses are really her, and it outlasted the rest of the discussion by a good half hour.

Farid opened by arguing that the other-branch women can't strictly be identical to the protagonist: in one branch she is thriving and married, in another broke and alone, and since nothing can consistently have both sets of incompatible properties, these must be distinct individuals who merely resemble her closely enough to count as what David Lewis called her "counterparts" — so her "other lives" are really other people's lives. Naomi replied that this guts the whole point of the novel and is essentially Kripke's old objection to counterpart theory in new clothes: the protagonist isn't losing sleep over whether some similar-but-distinct woman elsewhere still plays the violin; she is grieving her own lost possibility, and a theory on which that thought is secretly about somebody else isn't a friendlier restatement of it but a denial of it. Farid answered that this just assumes what's in question — that the claim must be about the numerically same woman to be true — and that literal identity across branches is not free either: to avoid contradiction, Naomi has to say the protagonist is married only relative to one branch and unmarried only relative to another, which leaves her own view resting on what philosophers call transworld identity, a brute relation no less mysterious than his counterpart relation. Naomi granted they might be even on that score, but pressed further: the novel's branches diverge in college, in childhood, even before birth, so Farid owes a principled account of which of these count as similar enough to be counterparts at all; if the answer just tracks whichever resemblance a given scene happens to foreground, then whether the protagonist "really could have" done something seems to be fixed by our narrative interests rather than by any fact about her.

Neither of them would concede the point, and both eventually told me to go find someone who actually works on this for a living. Could you go through the four moves above in order and say who has the better of the argument at each one, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I'm particularly interested in whether Naomi's closing point is a genuine problem for counterpart theory or whether there's a reply available to Farid that neither of them considered, and, more generally, in what each side seems to be assuming that the other should have challenged.
