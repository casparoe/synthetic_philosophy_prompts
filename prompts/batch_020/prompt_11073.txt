I'm reporting a feature on how professionals handle the gap between their own judgment and what they know about their field's error rates, and a minor episode from my own work keeps getting in the way of finishing it, because I can't work out what it actually shows. While fact-checking a widely cited study linking a common dietary supplement to improved memory in older adults, I decided to rerun the core analysis myself out of habit. The more carefully I went through it, the more convinced I became that the paper's central finding was an artifact of how one confounding variable had been handled in the statistical model — by the end, my own reasoning felt as solid as anything I've produced in ten years of doing this.

Before writing any of it up, I described the problem to a friend who works as a professional statistician. She didn't find anything wrong with my calculation, and didn't really try to. Instead, she told me that journalists who believe they've caught a basic statistical error that the original researchers, their co-authors, and several peer reviewers all missed are, by any reasonable track record, wrong far more often than they're right — and that this is true no matter how airtight their reasoning feels to them at the time. Her point wasn't about my specific case at all; it was a fact about people in my position generally. When I went back over my own work again that evening, I still couldn't find anything wrong with it.

What I haven't been able to settle is what I was actually supposed to do with her comment. If nothing about the analysis itself changed, is it simply rational to go on believing it exactly as strongly as before, given that she never identified any specific flaw in it? Or does having good reason to think people like me are usually deceiving themselves in situations like this mean I should doubt my own reasoning even while, from the inside, it still looks completely sound? And if it's the second, I'm not sure that's something a person can actually do on command — is there a coherent way to hold onto a piece of reasoning at full strength while simultaneously believing that very reasoning is probably compromised, or is one of those two attitudes bound to collapse into the other? I'd like a genuine account of what's happening here, rather than a reminder to stay humble.
