"Scientific realists hold that our best theories are at least approximately true and that the unobservable entities they posit really exist, and the main argument for this is the 'no miracles' argument: it would be a miracle if theories that were not even approximately true were nonetheless so successful at generating novel predictions. Laudan's pessimistic meta-induction is meant to undercut this inference from predictive success to truth. He points to a long list of past theories that were empirically successful in their day, accepted by the best scientists of the time, but that we now regard as false and as populated by entities that don't exist -- for instance the phlogiston theory of combustion, the caloric theory of heat, and the nineteenth-century ether theories of light. Since success obviously didn't track truth in these cases, Laudan concludes that success doesn't reliably track truth in general, so our own best theories, however successful, are probably false too, and their entities probably don't exist either.

One realist strategy for handling this is structural realism, defended by Worrall. The lesson he draws from cases like the shift from Fresnel's theory of light to Maxwell's electromagnetic theory is that what survives theory change isn't the entities that were doing the causal work -- the ether disappeared completely -- but the successful mathematical structure, since Fresnel's equations for reflection and refraction were carried over more or less intact into Maxwell's theory, just reinterpreted. So the structural realist's move is to say that we should only commit ourselves to the entities in a theory that are actually responsible for its predictive success, and stay agnostic about the rest; this lets them accept Laudan's whole list without giving up on realism, since in each case it was really the surviving structure, and not the discarded entities, that was doing the work.

I think this mostly succeeds, but not for free: if structural realism only commits us to mathematical structure rather than to any story about what the world is built out of, it starts to look less like realism about a world of things and more like an elaborate instrumentalism, so the view may be buying its safety from the meta-induction at the price of giving up much of what made realism an interesting position in the first place."
