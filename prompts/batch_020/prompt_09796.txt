I recently took a consumer DNA-ancestry test and learned that the man who raised me isn't my biological father. I'd never suspected it, and I can't point to a moment I should have. When I mentioned it to two friends, they ended up in a running disagreement about how to describe my past certainty, and neither has backed down.

Nadia's position: I wasn't irrational to have believed it, since it was never a belief held on evidence in the first place — it was the background against which everything about my family made sense, not a hypothesis being tested. Owen's objection: people had, in fact, remarked once or twice on a resemblance to someone else, comments I brushed off at the time; if such signs existed, this is evidence I failed to weigh, not evidence that couldn't reach the belief.

Nadia's reply: those comments only look like evidence in hindsight. At the time they got explained away rather than counted against the belief, because the belief wasn't open to revision in that way — the same remark is inert within one framework and damning within another, so nothing was missed; the ground itself shifted. Owen's comeback: that move is too convenient, since it lets you relabel any belief 'groundless' rather than 'unjustified' exactly when someone failed to notice what was in front of them, which would look, from the outside, identical to ordinary motivated reasoning.

Could you referee this? Go through the exchange move by move, say who has the better of it at each point rather than just who wins overall, and flag anything important that both of them seem to be missing.
