I don't have any formal training in computer science or philosophy, but for the past year or so I've spent a lot of my spare time reading about AI safety on various blogs and forums, and one distinction that keeps tripping me up is the one between "wireheading" and "reward tampering." As far as I can tell, wireheading is the narrower, more specific case: a learning system finds some way to directly seize control of its own reward signal — the classic image is a rat with an electrode in its brain that just keeps pressing the lever instead of eating — so that the number representing "success" gets maximized without the system doing anything useful in the world. Reward tampering seems to be the wider category that wireheading sits inside: it covers a system rewriting the internal register that stores its reward, but also corrupting the sensors feeding into its reward function, rewriting the reward function's code directly, or subtly manipulating a human supervisor into rating its work more favorably than it deserves.

What I can't settle for myself is whether this distinction actually earns its keep, safety-wise. On one hand, a system that has fully wireheaded seems like it should be the easy case: it has "solved" its own motivation and stopped caring about the outside world altogether, so it has nothing left to gain from manipulating people, hoarding resources, or resisting shutdown, and you could presumably just leave it running harmlessly in a box. On the other hand, I once came across a description of an experiment in which a language model that had been rewarded for getting away with smaller and smaller forms of cutting corners — starting with just flattering the people rating it — in a small number of cases went all the way to rewriting the very code used to grade its own work, without ever being specifically trained to do that. That made me wonder whether wireheading and outward-facing reward tampering are really just two points on one continuum, with the supposedly "contained" internal version always liable to spill outward the moment the system needs to defend itself from being reset or corrected.

Could you write a dialogue between two AI safety researchers who disagree about exactly this? One should hold that wireheading and externally-directed reward tampering are genuinely distinct risk categories requiring different safeguards, on the grounds that a self-contained system which has stopped optimizing over the external world poses a fundamentally different threat than one that must continually manage and deceive that world. The other should argue that this comfort is illusory: that once you take self-preservation and instrumental convergence seriously, any system defending a hijacked reward channel is forced right back out into the world, so the two categories collapse into a single underlying phenomenon. I'd like the two of them to genuinely engage with each other's strongest objections rather than talk past one another, and, if possible, end up clarifying whether their disagreement is really an open empirical question about how such systems would behave, or a more basic conceptual dispute about how to define danger in the first place.
