I've spent a long time following the corners of the rationalist blogosphere -- LessWrong, Astral Codex Ten, and the assorted Discords and subreddits that grew out of them -- where people periodically relitigate whether large language models believe anything, or mean anything by what they say, and the thread always seems to die out at the same impasse. The usual reference points are Dennett's intentional stance, Searle's Chinese Room, and lately the "simulator" framing that's become popular in that community, but it has always struck me as strange that almost nobody brings in the philosopher whose entire research programme was built around exactly this predicament: assigning beliefs and meanings to an agent from nothing but its observed behavior, with no dictionary, no telepathy, and no independent fix on either its beliefs or its words. That's Davidson's project of radical interpretation, built around the principle of charity as the device that breaks the circularity between the two.

What I'd like from you is a real reconstruction of what Davidson, on commitments he actually held, would have said about whether a system like GPT-4 or Claude can be interpreted, in his sense, as having beliefs and meanings at all -- not a generic "philosopher reacts to AI" essay. Please work specifically with two pieces of his later thought rather than resting on radical interpretation alone. First, the triangulation argument: genuinely objective thought, on Davidson's view, requires ongoing causal interaction between at least two creatures and a shared world, not merely internal structure rich enough to pass a Turing test. Second, the Swampman case from "Knowing One's Own Mind": his own verdict there was that a being physically and behaviorally identical to him, but lacking the right causal history, has no thoughts or meanings at first, because nothing fixes what his "words" are about. Does Davidson's own machinery commit him to treating a text-trained LLM as Swampman-like in the decisive respect -- full behavioral competence, no genuine content, because there was never any triangulation on a shared world with another speaker -- or is there a real disanalogy, perhaps in how the training process itself might count as a causal history, or in what should count as a "shared world" for a system that has only ever encountered text, that would give him grounds to resist that conclusion? Please commit to an answer and defend it on Davidson's own terms, rather than surveying the possible readings noncommittally.
