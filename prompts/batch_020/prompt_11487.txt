I co-host my university's undergraduate philosophy podcast, and this term I'm also taking a seminar on metaethics and value theory that has spent the last few weeks on fitting-attitude, or buck-passing, analyses of the good. Next month's guest on the podcast defends exactly this kind of analysis -- roughly, that for something to be good is for there to be reason to take up some favoring attitude toward it, such as desiring, admiring, or pursuing it -- and she is best known in the literature for how she handles the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem: the worry, illustrated by the demon who will torture you unless you admire him, that some considerations clearly give you reason to have the pro-attitude without making the object one bit more admirable, so a workable fitting-attitude analysis needs some principled way of excluding those reasons. Her signature move sorts reasons by source rather than by weight: reasons grounded in the object's own features are, in the jargon, "object-given" and count as the right kind, bearing on genuine fittingness, while reasons grounded merely in the further upshot of occupying the attitude itself -- like the torture -- are "state-given" and don't count, however strong they are.

I don't want the episode to have her simply restate this distinction, since anyone who's read the same two or three papers we were assigned could predict that much. Could you draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having her repeat the official position: I want at least one question on the charge that the object-given/state-given cut is covertly circular, since you seemingly can only sort a reason into the right pile by already knowing which facts about the object are value-relevant; at least one that asks her, with reference to an actual case, why her way of drawing the line should be preferred over rival "hybrid" solutions that locate the difference in whether a reason bears on the accuracy of the attitude rather than in its causal source; and at least one on whether her fix is meant to carry over to the structurally identical problem about belief, where a purely prudential, Pascal's-Wager-style reason to believe something looks exactly as suspect as the demon's threat -- and for each, a short follow-up question keyed to the answer she's most likely to give.
