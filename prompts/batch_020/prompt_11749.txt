I am a PhD student in political philosophy, currently writing a dissertation chapter on value pluralism that draws heavily on Isaiah Berlin and Joseph Raz, but this term I have also been sitting in on a decision theory reading group run out of the economics department. Last week's session was devoted to Ruth Chang's work on hard choices and incommensurability, and one of the economists in the group -- a friend from my cohort who worked in quantitative finance before starting her PhD -- raised an objection that I found considerably more persuasive than I expected, to the point that it is now pushing me to reorganize the chapter.

Her objection runs as follows: if two options are genuinely incommensurable, in the sense that there is no common scale on which to rank them, then neither one can be better than the other; and since "not better, not worse" just is what we ordinarily mean by "equally good," incommensurable options must simply be a more impressive-sounding case of a tie -- no different in kind from a choice between two equally short routes to the airport. A perfectly rational, fully informed agent should therefore feel exactly as indifferent between two "incommensurable" careers as she does between the two routes: she picks one and moves on, and nothing of normative significance turns on which one she picks. On this reading, the agonizing that real people report when facing choices like academia-versus-industry is not evidence of some further evaluative structure that requires a new piece of philosophical vocabulary, such as Chang's "parity," to describe; it is simply what garden-variety uncertainty about one's own future preferences feels like from the inside. I am now inclined to fold my discussion of Chang into a subsection on choice under uncertainty rather than presenting parity as a basic value relation standing alongside "better than," "worse than," and "equal to," and I would value your view on whether that reduction actually goes through before I commit to restructuring the chapter around it -- and, if it does not, what exactly it fails to capture about why theorists in this literature insist on treating parity as irreducible to the other three relations.
