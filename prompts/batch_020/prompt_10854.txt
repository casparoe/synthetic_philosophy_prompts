I've volunteered at a small wildlife rehabilitation center for about three years, and I now sit on the five-person board that sets our intake policy. That policy, drafted by the center's founder a decade ago, says we treat animals "whose injury or illness is clearly attributable to human activity" -- roadkill survivors, birds that hit windows, animals tangled in fishing line, raptors poisoned by rodenticide -- while animals hurt by predation, disease, or starvation in the ordinary course of nature are only to be stabilized enough for release or euthanasia, never actively treated. Last month a hiker brought in a fawn that had escaped a coyote attack but was badly wounded; its injuries looked no different from the car-strike fawns we treat every week, but our policy said we weren't supposed to do more than assess it.

That case has split the board almost evenly. Afterward I spent an evening reading about this on my own and found there's an entire existing argument, apparently going back decades, that the human/natural line is basically arbitrary once you accept that suffering is bad regardless of what caused it -- and two of my fellow board members now want to drop the distinction entirely. The other three don't deny the pain feels the same either way, but argue that our specific duty is to fix harms we're implicated in causing, not to referee the entire food web -- and they point out, a little uncomfortably, that we already blur this line ourselves, since our non-releasable education-program hawks are fed live mice, which makes the center itself a direct cause of exactly the kind of "natural" predation the policy calls off-limits.

I'd like you to draft the short memo I'll circulate before our board meeting in two weeks, recommending whether we keep the human-cause criterion, replace it with a different triage principle, or drop it altogether -- and to give real reasons for the recommendation rather than just picking whichever side sounds more compassionate on its face. I especially want it to engage with the mission-creep worry instead of waving it away, since that's the argument that's kept this policy in place for ten years, and I need the recommendation to translate into concrete criteria our intake volunteers could actually apply at the door at 11 p.m. on a Saturday, not just a restatement of the principle that suffering matters wherever it occurs.
