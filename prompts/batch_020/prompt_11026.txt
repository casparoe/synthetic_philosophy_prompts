I'm taking an upper-level metaphysics seminar this term that has spent the last few weeks on the conditional analysis of dispositions -- the idea that to be fragile, say, just is to be such that you would break if struck -- and on the two families of counterexample usually taken to sink the simplest version of that idea. The first is finks: David Lewis's sorcerer, who protects a fragile glass by magically and temporarily suspending its fragility every time it is about to be struck (so it never breaks, despite being genuinely fragile the rest of the time), and C. B. Martin's wire, which is dead but wired to a device that makes it live the instant a conductor touches it, so that "if touched, current would flow" comes out true even though the wire isn't really live. The second is masking, or "antidote," cases, usually credited to Mark Johnston and Alexander Bird: a poison that would kill you, taken alongside an antidote that blocks its effect, so that you are still poisoned even though nothing happens. Lewis's own fix, the so-called Reformed Conditional Analysis, is generally presented as handling finks -- by requiring that the disposition's actual intrinsic basis survive through the stimulus -- while still failing on masks, since there the basis survives perfectly well and something else simply blocks the route to the manifestation.

After one of these seminars, two people in my study group, Owen and Sasha, spent nearly half an hour arguing over a case I had made up mostly as a joke, and I think they were both onto something, which is why I want to lay out their exchange in full rather than just tell you who I think won. The case: a bike cable lock marketed as "SentryLock" is made of the same soft braided steel that any angle grinder could cut through in under ten seconds. Embedded in the cable's core, however, is a sensor-and-capacitor mechanism that detects the vibration signature of a grinder wheel biting into the metal and, within milliseconds, discharges a current that hardens the steel at exactly that spot for about three seconds -- long enough to blunt most grinder wheels -- before the segment reverts to its ordinary, easily-cut state. (1) Owen said this is just Lewis's sorcerer case with a battery instead of magic: the cable really is soft, but a mechanism intervenes at the exact moment of the stimulus to remove that disposition before it can manifest, then hands it back, which makes this a fink, and the Reformed Conditional Analysis should call the cable cuttable regardless, since the fix works by asking what would happen if the softness had been retained through the grinding. (2) Sasha disagreed: the sorcerer's magic acts on the glass from outside, she said, whereas the hardening circuit is a permanent, built-in part of the lock as sold; once the sensor and capacitor count as part of "the SentryLock," its resistance to cutting is just as intrinsic to it as the poison's toxicity is intrinsic to the poison, which makes this an antidote case -- the softness is still there, untouched, while something else blocks the route from grinding to severing. Calling it a fink, she said, only looks right if you draw "the object" around the bare metal and arbitrarily exclude the very mechanism the manufacturer installed for this purpose. (3) Owen replied that this move proves too much, since any fink mechanism whatsoever could be redescribed as "part of the object" and thereby relabeled a mask; what actually matters, he said, is whether the property doing the causal work -- the metal's hardness -- itself changes at the moment of the stimulus, and here it does: the steel is measurably harder for those three seconds than it was a moment before, which is precisely the mark of a fink and not a mask, where, as with the poison, the relevant property never changes at all. (4) Sasha answered that Owen was ignoring timing: in every fink case she could remember, the disposition is removed before the stimulus has begun causing anything, whereas here the grinder is already biting into the cable -- the process has already started -- when the hardening kicks in and arrests it partway through, and interrupting an already-initiated process is the structure of an antidote, not a fink. (5) Owen said he didn't see why timing should be decisive, since Lewis's own analysis says nothing about how early a fink has to act, only about whether the basis survives, and that if timing really were what mattered, Sasha owed him an argument for why "before" and "during" the stimulus differ in the relevant way -- which she hadn't given. Neither of them budged, and recounting the exchange to two other people from the seminar produced an equally even split.

Could you go through the exchange move by move, (1) through (5), and say who has the better of it at each stage, rather than just declaring an overall winner? I would also like to know whether Owen and Sasha are both missing some consideration that would actually settle whether SentryLock is finkish or masked, and, depending on the answer, whether Lewis's Reformed Conditional Analysis gets the right verdict about it either way. Please end your reply with a final verdict on its own line, in exactly this format: VERDICT: fink, VERDICT: mask, or VERDICT: neither cleanly -- whichever you think is most accurate.
