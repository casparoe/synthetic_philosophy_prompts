A colleague recently pointed me to an essay Keynes wrote around 1930, apparently called "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren," where he tried to predict what daily life would look like once the economic problem, as he put it, had basically been solved. As I understand it, he argued that within about a hundred years, rising productivity and accumulated capital would let people meet their basic needs on something like fifteen hours of work a week. He thought the harder challenge afterward would be psychological rather than material, since most people have been raised for generations to strive and compete, and he doubted we were prepared to handle real abundance well. He also distinguished between needs that are absolute, like having enough food and shelter, and needs that are purely relative, things we want only because they make us feel superior to other people, and he suspected the latter might never be fully satisfied no matter how prosperous everyone becomes. Somewhat surprisingly, he also thought society would need to go on prizing traits like avarice, usury, and excessive caution with money for a good while longer, even while regarding them as fairly ugly, because only that motive could carry us through a kind of transitional tunnel into eventual abundance, after which point they could finally be dropped.

This stuck with me because software feels like the closest real-world approximation to the productivity miracle Keynes had in mind: code, and now AI tooling, have automated away enormous amounts of work in a fraction of the timeframe he was picturing. Yet essentially no one I know works fifteen-hour weeks; salaried engineers routinely put in unpaid overtime, and treating side projects or freelance "hustles" as admirable is the norm rather than the exception. More strikingly, founders who have already made far more money than they could reasonably spend often start new ventures rather than stopping, and people in the FIRE (financial independence, retire early) community who do hit their savings target and quit working sometimes report feeling restless or aimless within a year or two, quietly drifting back into consulting or new projects.

Assuming Keynes could observe all this, how do you think he would interpret it? Would he read it as confirmation of his own worries, meaning that we are still stuck in the transitional period he described, perhaps permanently so, because status-driven relative wants turned out to be far more dominant than he expected, especially now that social media lets people compare themselves against the entire planet rather than just their neighbors? Or would he conclude that something more basic in his original argument was mistaken? I would like a reading grounded in the actual logic of his essay rather than just his general reputation as someone who thought we would eventually work a lot less.
