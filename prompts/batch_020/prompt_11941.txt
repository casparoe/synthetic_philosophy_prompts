Epistemologists frequently motivate two rival views with the same "bank cases": pairs of scenarios in which a subject's evidence that the bank is open on Saturday never changes, yet our willingness to say she "knows" this seems to drop as the cost of being wrong rises. Epistemic contextualism, associated with Keith DeRose, explains the shift by treating "knows" as context-sensitive to the standards operative in the attributor's own conversation, much like an indexical. Pragmatic encroachment, defended by John Hawthorne and Jason Stanley under labels such as "subject-sensitive" or "interest-relative" invariantism, holds instead that a subject's own practical stakes can affect whether she knows, even holding her evidence fixed. As a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, I've seen both positions invoked to explain the very same cases, and I can't work out what actually separates them once you get past the slogans. Compare the two views directly by working out what each one predicts for a case in the spirit of Stanley's "Hannah" examples: a single speaker, in one fixed conversational context, considers two subjects who share identical evidence but differ only in their own personal stakes — do the theories really diverge on whether each subject "knows," and if so, whose verdict better matches our intuitions about the case? Conclude by defending which view gives the more plausible overall account of the bank cases, or whether some further alternative, such as strict invariantism or a relativist semantics for "knows," does better than either.
