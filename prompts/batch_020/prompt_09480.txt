I attend a Catholic high school whose Honor Code covers more than cheating: it also forbids "deliberate dishonesty toward a member of the faculty or staff in the course of a school matter," and I'm one of two student representatives on the Honor Council that hears these cases. We're currently sitting on one that's harder than anything we've handled before. A junior at our school, asked directly by our dean of students whether she knew why a friend had been missing so much class, said no. In fact she did know: the friend had confided in her about a mental health crisis and asked her to keep it secret, even from the school, because the friend was afraid that her own strict parents would respond by making things worse rather than better. The friend was hospitalized a week later, the truth came out, and the junior is now charged with a violation. The code technically lets the Council waive or reduce a sanction when there is "a compelling moral justification" for the dishonesty, but in the eleven years the code has existed, nobody has ever invoked that clause or said what it means, and the Council is split on whether to finally define it or just resolve this case without setting a precedent.

For theology this semester we've actually been reading Augustine's two treatises on exactly this question, De Mendacio and Contra Mendacium, and what strikes me is how little room he leaves: he holds that lying is wrong even to save a life, he's unmoved by the Hebrew midwives or Rahab as counterexamples, and in Contra Mendacium he won't even let Catholics feign membership in a dangerous heretical sect in order to expose it, on the grounds that allowing this reasoning would make nonsense of martyrdom. He does grade lies from worst to least harmful, but even the mildest kind he still calls something to be hated, not something to be excused. Because we're a Catholic school, a couple of Council members think that settles the matter and that the "compelling moral justification" clause should effectively be read out of the code; I'm not convinced, but I don't want to write a memo that just asserts the opposite without dealing with him honestly. Could you draft the short memo I'll circulate before Wednesday's meeting? It should recommend a specific resolution of the junior's case, take a real position on whether and how to define "compelling moral justification" going forward, and engage Augustine's argument closely enough that a Council member who has actually read Contra Mendacium would recognize their own view in it rather than feel strawmanned.
