So this actually happened on a hiring committee -- not mine, a friend's, in a totally different department, but I got the full play-by-play afterward. Three-person committee, and the rule going in was: only extend the offer if the candidate is BOTH clearly strong on research AND a good teaching fit; if either one is missing, no offer. Here's how the three of them actually judged the two criteria:

- Member 1: research-strong = yes, teaching fit = yes
- Member 2: research-strong = yes, teaching fit = no
- Member 3: research-strong = no, teaching fit = yes

Naturally, each member just applies the committee's own rule to their own two judgments when asked directly "offer, yes or no" -- so member 1 says yes, and members 2 and 3 both say no (each is missing one of the two required things). Now here's the twist: if you take a separate majority vote on each criterion (research: 2-1 yes, teaching: 2-1 yes) and then apply the rule to those two majority verdicts, you get "offer." But if you instead majority-vote directly on "offer, yes or no," you get "no offer," 2-1. Same three people, same individual judgments, same agreed rule -- opposite results depending on what gets aggregated. Feels like this should have a name, something Arrow's-theorem-adjacent, but I can't place it -- bonus points if you know it.

Anyway, my friend told this story at dinner and two people there, Dana and Theo, ended up arguing about it for like twenty minutes. Dana's view: the committee's actual job was to answer "offer or not," so the direct vote is the one that counts, full stop -- the per-criterion tallies are just each person's private reasoning and were never themselves put to a group vote. Theo pushed back: a majority of the committee thinks the candidate is research-strong, a seperate majority thinks they're a good teaching fit, and the committee's own rule says that combination is sufficient -- so "no offer" isn't actually backed by any of the committee's collective reasons, its just an artifact of members 2 and 3 disliking the candidate for two different reasons that happen to add up to a majority. Dana answered that this cuts both ways: two named, actual people voted no on the actual question that was asked, and letting some recombination of sub-votes overrule two real verdicts gets the whole point of voting backwards. Theo replied that if "what real people actually voted" is the standard, it should apply to the sub-questions too -- a real majority backs both of them -- so Dana can't count real majorities at the level of the final question while waving away real majorities on the two things that, by the committee's own rule, are supposed to settle it.

Neither of them budged, and I honestly go back and forth on it myself. Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's got the better of each point? Then tell me on balance whether you think one of them is closer to right overall, or whether they're both missing something neither one spotted.
