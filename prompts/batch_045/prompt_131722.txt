Please analyze the wrongful conviction of Ronald Cotton for the 1984 rape of Jennifer Thompson in Burlington, North Carolina, through the lens of the contemporary dispute in the philosophy of perception between disjunctivism and the argument from illusion.

I will stipulate the facts so that nothing turns on your having to look anything up. Thompson was raped at knifepoint by an intruder; she later said she had deliberately studied her attacker's face during the assault in order to be able to identify him afterward. She picked Ronald Cotton, a Black man, out of a photo array and then out of a physical lineup — a cross-racial identification — and identified him again in court. Cotton was convicted in 1985 and sentenced to life plus 54 years. Roughly a decade later, DNA testing of the biological evidence excluded Cotton and instead matched Bobby Poole, another man who had boasted of the crime while in prison; Cotton was released, and he and Thompson subsequently became friends and advocates for reform of eyewitness-identification procedures.

On the theory side, I will also supply neutral working definitions so that we are arguing from the same starting point. The argument from illusion is the line of thought that runs: because a subjectively indistinguishable hallucination or misperception can occur, a veridical perception and a non-veridical experience must share a common experiential core, a highest common factor, so that what the subject is directly aware of is never the mind-independent object itself but only that common core. Disjunctivism denies the common-factor assumption. On this view, a veridical perception is fundamentally a relational state in which the object perceived is itself a constituent of the experience, whereas a hallucination or a case of mistaken identification is a state of a different kind altogether; there is no single experiential kind that both instantiate, and the move from first-person indistinguishability to a shared mental kind is exactly what the disjunctivist rejects.

What I want from you is a disciplined mapping in both directions. First, the mapping downward: on each theory, what is the relevant mental state in Thompson's case — her perception of her attacker during the assault, and her later memorial and lineup-based identification? What does each theory say the police and jury were treating as evidence, what options were genuinely open to them given that treatment, and what should they have concluded by the lights of each theory? Be careful to say what counts as evidence on each view. For the common-factor theorist, confidence and vividness attach to the shared experiential core; for the disjunctivist, if the experience is not relational, what do confidence and vividness attach to, and what is left of the case for the identification?

Then the mapping back upward: does the Cotton case confirm the argument from illusion and refute disjunctivism? The tempting thought is that here is a case in which a witness's experience was, from the inside, no different from a genuine perception of the attacker, yet picked out the wrong man — which looks like the very indistinguishability the argument from illusion trades on. Say whether that temptation should be resisted: for instance, by the disjunctivist insisting that the thesis is metaphysical rather than epistemological, so that no first-person indistinguishability data can touch it; or by the disjunctivist extending the dispute beyond perception.

That extension is the part I most want you to take up. The conviction rested not on Thompson's on-the-spot perception but on her later memory and on an identification procedure, so I want you to construct and apply a mnemonic disjunctivism: the view that, just as perceiving and hallucinating are not states of a common kind, so genuinely remembering and merely confabulating or imagining are not states of a common kind either. Say what this view predicts about the photo array and the lineup, about Thompson's certainty, and about whether the procedure could have been reliable in principle. Then judge whether the mnemonic extension helps the disjunctivist or hurts them: does it explain why the system failed, or does it leave the disjunctivist unable to account for the felt continuity between Thompson's memory and a genuine one?

Finally, the epistemic ask: commit. Take a definite position on whether this case, properly described, is evidence for disjunctivism, evidence for the common-factor view, or evidentially inert with respect to the dispute — and give the reasons that actually carry weight for you, rather than cataloguing the considerations on both sides and leaving me to choose. At the same time, be explicit about the points where the case is silent and neither theory has anything to say, rather than forcing them to speak. Work only from the facts I have stipulated, the definitions I have given, and the internal resources of the positions themselves: I do not want citations, bibliographies, or appeals to empirical work you would need to look up.
