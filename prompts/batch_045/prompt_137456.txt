NOTES: INITIAL CONDITIONS AND ULTIMATE RESPONSIBILITY

1. The argument, as I understand it. You do what you do, in any situation, because of the way you are. To be ultimately morally responsible for what you do, you must be ultimately responsible for the way you are, at least in the crucial mental respects. But you cannot be ultimately responsible for the way you are: any act of self-making would itself have to issue from a nature you already had, so you face a regress; halting the regress at an unchosen starting point concedes the point; and letting some step be undetermined does not help, because what happens by chance is no more authored by you than what happens by necessity. Therefore no one is ever ultimately morally responsible for anything. I take it that the argument is meant to be neutral between determinism and its denial, and that this is meant to be its peculiar strength.

2. The scientific analogue. In physics we explain an event by citing laws together with initial conditions, and we never require the initial conditions to be self-caused. No cosmologist thinks that the absence of a self-causing first cause shows that nothing ever happens, or that later states of the universe are not genuinely produced by earlier ones. Call this the Initial-Conditions Reply: a person's character at the moment of action is simply the initial condition from which the action is derived, and there is no more reason to demand that she be the author of her own initial conditions than to demand that the universe be the author of its own. The demand for self-authorship looks like a survival of a pre-scientific, essentially theological picture of origination, and premise three borrows its air of necessity from that picture.

3. The objection I cannot answer. Brute facts may be good enough for causation, but moral responsibility is a normative status, and it is unclear why a normative status should be grounded in the mere absence of an explanation. Worse, the reply seems to prove too much. If origins never matter, then a team who secretly rewires someone's desires overnight leaves her exactly as responsible as the rest of us, which almost everyone finds unacceptable. My tentative escape is to distinguish history-independence from archaeology: what matters is not whether a disposition was authored but whether it is now reasons-responsive and integrated with the agent's other attitudes, which we test by counterfactual and interventionist means rather than by tracing descent. But I suspect this concedes the real point, since such a test establishes that someone is an agent and not that she is ultimately responsible.

4. The part I most want judged on its merits: the dimmer argument. Suppose responsibility admits of degrees and self-shaping is real but partial. Imagine a series of agents, each of whom has shaped by earlier deliberate choices a fraction p of the motives that now move her, the rest inherited or imposed. The regress shows only that p never reaches one. But the second premise does not obviously require p to equal one; it requires ultimate responsibility for the way you are. If ultimate responsibility is all-or-nothing, the argument seems to build total self-authorship into the definition of the very thing it concludes we lack. If it admits of degrees, the regress shows at most that responsibility is never total, not that it is ever zero, and the step from not maximal to none needs a defence of its own. Strawson would then need an extra premise I do not find in the argument: a threshold claim, or a no-partial-credit claim on which incomplete self-authorship contributes nothing at all. Against that, my intuition as a measurement scientist is that partial credit is the default: in every other case where we grade a capacity — memory, acuity, computational power, the precision of an instrument — we treat partial reliability as partial competence, not as zero.

5. What I cannot tell is whether section four is a genuine objection or merely a compatibilist intuition wearing a laboratory coat.
