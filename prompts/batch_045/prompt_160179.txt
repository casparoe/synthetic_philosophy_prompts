I'm retired now — thirty-odd years in lab and field work, I use to do boundary-layer and micrometeorology stuff — and lately I've been sitting in on a philosophy reading group at the local university mostly to argue with them. Right now we're stuck on Friedman's as-if move (the F-twist: never mind whether the assumptions are true, the system behaves as if they were, the predictions come out, so stop worrying about realism). My complaint is that it's far too permissive: plenty of models predict beautifully for the wrong reasons.

So build me a case to make that stick. One paragraph, real publishable science — enzyme kinetics, fisheries stock assessment, atmospheric stuff, I don't mind — containing two models, M1 and M2, that are empirically indistinguishable: same fit to all the data anybody has, same predictions across every regime the field can actually probe, nothing on the horizon that would separate them. But their false assumptions differ in kind. In M1 the falsehood is gentle: replace the false assumption with a truer one, refit, and the predictions survive and get slightly better — there's a smooth path of successively less false assumptions that keeps tracking the data. In M2 the falsehood is load-bearing and brittle: the model works only because that particular false assumption happens to mimic the real mechanism, and if its swapped for anything nearer the truth the whole thing falls apart.

Then run the check. Say what the as-if standard (predictive success is what warrants an assumption) and a de-idealizing standard (an assumption is warranted only to the extent the model survives being made truer) each says about M1 and about M2, and why — they should agree about M1 and come apart on M2.

Constraints: no sci-fi or thought-experiment gadgets, one paragraph for the scenario itself, the empirical indistinguishability has to be earned by the story rather than stipulated, and neither model may be obviously bad — M2 in particular should be something a working scientist would defend. After the scenario, name the single structural feature that makes the two standards peel apart, show what happens to the case if you remove it, and then give me the strongest reply an as-if defender actually has (not a strawman) plus how I should answer it.

I want to use this in a short talk to my old department, so if the case only works because I smuggled the verdict in by hand, say so instead of pretending it works.
