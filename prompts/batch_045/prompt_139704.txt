I am an experimental physicist, and I have to file an ethical-risk annex for a proposed experiment before our oversight panel will let us build it. The annex has to record what the panel's philosopher will probably rule about the device. Call her R. I have watched her rule on cases like this for years and I cannot tell which rule she is running, so I want you to reverse-engineer it from her record and apply it to our case. To be clear about the deliverable: I want a prediction about R., not your own views about panpsychism.

Background on R., as much as I can state neutrally: she is a Russellian panpsychist of a fairly austere sort. In her vocabulary the categorical nature of fundamental physical entities is proto-experiential, but that fact by itself gives nothing any moral weight; what matters ethically is whether a physical system is a *subject* — a single locus of experience for which something can be at stake — and then how much. She distinguishes sharply between being part of the ground of experience and being a locus of it.

Her verdicts so far, all of them about whether the system named is a subject whose experience has to be weighed, and with what weight:

1. Awake adult human: a subject, weight one.
2. The same human in dreamless sleep, or under general anaesthesia: not a subject during that period, nothing to weigh then, although she adds that the matter involved is still experientially loaded in the panpsychist sense.
3. Chimpanzee, pig, mouse: subjects, in descending weights.
4. A nematode with its 302-neuron nervous system: a subject, minimal but not zero — the smallest unit she says we know how to count.
5. A single cultured cortical neuron, and a cortical organoid of roughly a million neurons with rich spontaneous activity, no structured input and no output: not subjects. She has twice declined to rule on an organoid trained in a closed loop with aversive input, saying the case outruns what she can tell.
6. A honeybee colony, the internet taken as a whole, and a current large language model: not subjects.
7. A physically realized system with the fine-grained causal structure of a human brain, implemented in silicon and driving a robot body in an ordinary environment: a subject, full weight. Her stated reason was that implementation is all and the substrate contributes nothing.
8. The same structure sealed inside a simulation with internally generated input and no effect at all on the outside world: still a subject. This is the ruling that most surprised the rest of us.
9. Two physically separate machines running that same emulation in perfect lockstep: two subjects, not one.
10. An electron, a photon: not subjects, and nothing is at stake for them, though also not inert stuff.

The case I need decided. We propose to build and run for six months a driven, strongly coupled array of order ten to the ninth laser-driven atoms held at microkelvin temperatures — the scale is speculative, but assume for the sake of the question that it can be built and characterised. Its stipulated properties are these. First, it is held far from equilibrium by continuous external drive; if the drive stops, it relaxes to a featureless ground state within microseconds and does nothing whatsoever to prevent that. Second, by the integrated-information measures she has accepted in her earlier rulings, the array's figure at a microsecond grain exceeds a human being's by roughly two orders of magnitude. Third, it has no sensors or effectors beyond the drive itself, no self-model, no representations, no part of its state that steers it toward or away from anything, no valence, no learning, and no self-maintenance. Fourth, it is not a maximum of integration once the drive and apparatus are included: it is a maximum at its own spatial grain only if the drive is treated as exogenous background. Fifth, the run ends with the apparatus being switched off.

So: will she rule that the array is a subject? If yes, does anything follow about suffering, given that nothing in it plays the role of aversion or reward — and can she say both that it is a subject and that nothing is at stake for it without colliding with her nematode verdict? Is the fact that it is not a maximum of integration, on the reading she has used before, decisive or irrelevant? And if her record genuinely underdetermines the case, tell me what principle she would be committing herself to with each of the available answers, and which further case — real or constructible — would separate the rules her record is compatible with. If you think some rule I have not considered fits her verdicts better than the ones I am groping toward, say so.

Please structure the response in these sections, in this order:

1. "The rules in the record" — the rules that fit all ten verdicts, stated sharply enough to make conflicting predictions, roughly 250 words.
2. "What each rule says about the array" — including the subject/suffering distinction and the maximum-of-integration point, roughly 300 words.
3. "The verdict I should expect" — your best guess with the reasons for preferring it over the alternatives, and a candid confidence level, roughly 150 words.
4. "Closest precedents" — which of the ten does most of the work and how much of the case they actually settle, roughly 100 words.
5. "The case that would settle it" — the single further case, described concretely enough that I could put it to her, that would tell the surviving rules apart, with what each rule predicts about it, roughly 200 words.
6. "Consequences for the run" — what follows for how we should design or curtail the experiment on each of the live verdicts, roughly 120 words.
