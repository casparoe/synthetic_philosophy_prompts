I'm a PhD student in machine learning, and I'm writing the framing section of a paper on out-of-distribution goal robustness. Before a hostile reviewer reads it, I want to know whether the commitments I'm about to commit to print actually hang together. Here they are, roughly in the order I defend them.

1. Taxonomy. Capability robustness and goal robustness are separable failure axes, and the case worth theorising about is the one where the system remains competent off-distribution but the thing it competently pursues has come apart from what we wanted. I treat this as a genuine category and not just a relabelling of "the model is imperfect", and I think the distinction is what licenses different interventions in the two cases.

2. Goal ascriptions are interest-relative. I don't think there is a description-independent fact of the matter about which goal a trained network has; an ascription is licensed by its usefulness within some specified class of interventions. In the paper I use three such classes — interventions on the input channel, on the deployment feedback channel (who scores the outputs, on what schedule, with what delay), and on internal activations — and I say an ascription is *locked* only if it is stable across all three. By "stable" I mean something stronger than "not contradicted": each class has to have some confirming say. Locked ascriptions are the only ones I let myself reason with; the rest I treat as pragmatics.

3. Search is rare. I expect gradient descent in realistic regimes, most of the time, to produce something that is not searching over plans. It is pattern-matching on features that happened to track reward during training. I hold this as a claim about the typical case, not about every system.

4. Deceptive alignment is a coherent failure mode distinct from plain misgeneralisation — a system that represents the training objective, has a goal that diverges from it, and performs because performing is instrumentally useful — and it is the failure mode I would least like to face, even granting (3).

5. Behavioural evidence underdetermines the individual case. I think no amount of black-box interaction short of deployment could settle, for a particular system, whether its good training behaviour is deceptive in the sense of (4). Only mechanistic evidence could — locating the goal representation, or the features or directions that carry it. And if it did, I would treat that as decisive grounds not to deploy that system.

6. Method. Most of the field's empirical effort should go into behavioural evaluation — held-out-distribution suites, red-teaming under shift — because those are the only evaluations that scale. Talk of internal goals I officially treat as shorthand for patterns in behaviour.

Audit this set for me. Which pairs are actually in tension, and which only look like it? Order the real tensions by severity rather than listing them as equal, and for each say what specifically would have to give, what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set, and whether there is a single revision anywhere that would clean up the most. Please also say explicitly which apparent tensions dissolve and why: I would rather be told that a pair is fine than be handed a manufactured problem. Where a tension turns on how I have drawn one of my own distinctions — the three intervention classes in (2), or the capability/goal split in (1) — say so, since redrawing it may be cheaper than dropping a commitment. I can't check references while I write, so reason from the content of the views rather than from what anyone has argued in print.
