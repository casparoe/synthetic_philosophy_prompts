I came across this passage in a popular-science chapter on cultural evolution, and I cannot tell how much of it belongs to the actual research program and how much is rhetorical varnish:

"Human culture is Darwinian. A meme is any idea, tune, skill, or habit that gets copied from one person to another, and copying just is replication: variation arises because copies are imperfect, inheritance because a copy carries the content of its source, and selection because attention and memory are scarce, so the variants that spread are the ones good at getting themselves copied. Since the same three conditions that drive organic evolution hold here, Darwin's algorithm runs on ideas as well as on genes — but with the meme, and not the person, as the unit of selection. That is what makes memetics interesting: a meme's fitness is nothing but its own spread, so memes that mislead or harm their bearers can still win, and chain letters, superstitions, and conspiracy theories are as well adapted as anything in culture. Memes are therefore a second replicator, coevolving with genes: genes built brains good at copying, memes then exploited that machinery, and the outcome is the one species with cumulative culture. Which is why dismissing something as 'just a meme' misses the point — on this view our minds are habitats that memes compete to occupy, and many of our beliefs are ones that were good at spreading rather than ones we adopted for good reasons."

Please audit this paragraph for me. Tell me what someone who genuinely defends a Darwinian theory of culture would accept, what is a loose but common simplification that a specialist would let pass in a popular treatment, and what they would reject or flag as an open controversy rather than a consequence of the theory.

Three things I am especially unsure about. First, whether the move from "the three conditions hold" really establishes the Darwinian claim, or whether the substantive dispute lies elsewhere — in whether cultural transmission has the right structure of variation and inheritance at all, as opposed to being driven by reconstructed or directed transmission. Second, whether the step from "memes are what gets selected" to "memes that harm their bearers are favored" is as automatic as it is made to sound. Third, whether the closing claim about our beliefs follows from anything established earlier in the passage, or needs premises the passage has not supplied.

If part of the apparent disagreement here is merely verbal — for instance, about whether a meme is the thing copied, a unit of selection, or simply anything learned — say which part is verbal and what substantive question remains once the definition is fixed.
