hey — I'm co-running a cross-listed philosophy of mind / cognitive science seminar this fall: 16 students, mostly juniors, one 80-minute block, and I need to build a whole session around the ant-colony question. You know the one — Dennett gives the colony a name (Aunt Hillary) and asks whether the colony itself, and not the ants, can have beliefs and desires; and Hofstadter has a version where the colony seems stupid or evasive because the conversation you're trying to have with it is happening at the wrong level. About a third of the room are CS majors who will say "this is just ant colony optimization, obviously no mind there" inside four minutes, and another chunk have had one phil of mind course and will say "fine, functionalism, whatever." I want neither group to be able to coast.

What I want is a simulation they actually play, not a discussion prompt. No lecture up front, nobody hears the name Aunt Hillary or any technical label until the debrief, and the point should land before anybody names it.

Room and materials: normal seminar room with one open floor area. Two feeder stations at different distances from a nest — say one about six steps away and one about fourteen, or off at an angle so the routes differ in length, whatever actually makes the convergence happen. Communication is only by tokens left on the floor (poker chips, sticky notes, whatever), standing in for pheromone. No talking during the run except at one point I'll get to. Small index cards with individual rules. I can make stuff at home, nothing fancy.

Beats I want hit, in whatever order works:

- every ant gets only a local rule on a card; nobody is told what the colony is supposed to accomplish, or that there are two feeders. They should end up converged on the short route without any individual having decided to, and I want that to be undeniable afterwards, not something I have to point at.
- mid-run turnover: swap out a big chunk of the ants for people who know nothing, keep the trail going. Did the "desire" survive its carriers?
- fission: split the room with a barrier and let both halves keep running. Did Aunt Hillary become two, or did she die, or was she never one?
- misrepresentation: once the trail is set, move the food without disturbing the trail, and let them keep running to the empty station for a while. Is that a false belief or just stale pheromone, and how would you tell the difference from inside the room?
- addressability: I announce out loud, to the room, that the food has moved. Nothing changes, because the information can't get in at the colony level — only individual ants can be perturbed. I want them to feel that there is nobody there to tell.
- publicity: every ant-to-ant message is out in the open where anyone can see it; nothing in the colony is private. I genuinely don't know whether that makes the colony-mind story easier or harder, and I'd rather they argue it out than have me tell them.

Two design constraints. First, don't stack the deck: I don't want an exercise that's secretly cheerleading for the yes answer just because emergent optimization looks clever. The skeptical side should get real purchase from something that happens in the room, not only from what I say afterwards. Second, give the colony a payoff (food units delivered) and the individual ants something too, and make the two come apart at least once, so they feel the gap between what's rational for an ant and what's rational for a colony.

Deliverables: rules and card text, a minute-by-minute timeline (roughly half the block running, ~25 min debrief, the rest setup and reset), the payoff scheme, and a debrief mapped to moments — this thing that happened is that part of the argument. Then: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (the CS kids short-circuiting it, ants getting bored or talking, the trail not converging, someone guessing the point and narrating it to everyone, the fission phase turning into chaos, and the fact that naming the colony may be doing argumentative work it shouldn't be doing), plus what I do in each case. And be straight about which part of the question the simulation can't reach and where I'd just have to say it out loud.

If it works, they should walk out able to say what would have to be added to the colony for the yes answer to be right, and which of their gut reactions is the one that's going to keep coming back later in the semester.
