I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy training; I read a little philosophy of science with an evening reading group, which is where I picked up the phrase inference to the best explanation, and I use it constantly at work without having been able to say what licenses it. A colleague and I got into an argument over lunch that I now can't settle, and I'd rather have the reasoning checked than win it. He laid out the following, and each step looks fine to me individually while the conclusion strikes me as unacceptable.

(1) Abduction, as I actually practice it: the evidence E is in front of me, several candidate hypotheses would account for it, and one of them accounts for it markedly better on the usual virtues — it covers more of what I see, it does so with fewer moving parts and less special pleading, it hangs together with what I already accept, and it isn't a patch invented for this one case — and that is a reason to believe that hypothesis rather than its rivals. (2) Suppose H is the best explanation of my evidence. Now form H-and-X, where X is some unrelated truth I already accept: simple, independently credible, not contrived for the case at hand. Concretely: I'm diagnosing an integration test that fails roughly one run in twelve; H says there is a race between the fixture teardown and the next test's setup; X says the CI runners have 8 cores. (3) Anything H explains, H-and-X explains as well: H-and-X entails H, so none of the evidence H accounts for is lost. (4) And H-and-X is not worse on the virtues listed in (1): it explains strictly more (it also predicts what nproc reports), it costs one conjunct rather than a new mechanism, it contradicts nothing I already accept, and it is not ad hoc in the objectionable sense, since X is something I believed for unrelated reasons rather than a bolt-on added to rescue H. (5) So by (1), my evidence supports H-and-X at least as strongly as H, and more strongly if (4) is right. (6) That is preposterous: no diagnosis is improved by conjoining a true, simple, irrelevant fact, and if I wrote race in the fixture and 8-core runners in the postmortem my team would think I had lost the thread. The same holds outside debugging, as far as I can tell — the tracks in a cloud chamber are best explained by a muon having passed through, and the muon hypothesis does not become better supported by conjoining and the apparatus sits at sea level, even though that conjunct is true, simple, and not ad hoc. (7) It also cannot be probability that is going up: H-and-X is never more probable than H and is normally strictly less probable, so a rule that says believe whatever scores highest on explanatory goodness is a rule that says believe the less probable thing. (8) But (1) is the one claim in the stack I am not willing to abandon, because abductive diagnosis is how I actually find bugs and, near as I can tell, how a good deal of successful science worked.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I don't want a name for the problem or a gesture at a literature — I want to know exactly where the chain breaks, why that step looked sound enough to fool both of us, and what I would have to give up elsewhere to reject it. My own suspicion is that the trouble is in (4) and that not ad hoc is doing more work than I can articulate, though I can't say what work; my colleague says I am equivocating on explains between (1) and (3), which sounds right but which I can't reconstruct as an actual diagnosis. If it helps to know what I'd least like to lose: I'd rather give up (5) than (1), and I'd rather give up (1) than accept that the reasoning I do every day is noise. And if the honest answer is that inference to the best explanation is buying me something other than confidence that a hypothesis is true — understanding, or a target for further testing, or a choice among rivals rather than a belief — I'd like to be told what that something is, and whether it is enough to keep the practice.
