I’m a retired natural scientist—used to be an ornithologist, actually, and lately I’ve gotten hooked on philosophy—so please bear with me if I mix up a term or two (and please don’t laugh at my spelling; I still right like a field notebook sometimes). In my local philosophy reading group we’re slowly working through Hume’s essay on the standard of taste, and I got a bit stuck on this chunk near the beginning:

"The great variety of Tastes, as well as of opinions, which prevail in the world, is too obvious not to have fallen under every one's observation. Men of the most confined knowledge are able to remark a difference in the narrow circle of their acquaintance, even where the persons have been educated under the same government, and have early imbibed the same prejudices. But those who can enlarge their view to contemplate distant nations and remote ages, are still more surprised at the great inconsistence and contradiction. We are apt to call barbarous whatever departs widely from our own taste and apprehension: But soon find the epithet of reproach retorted on us. And the highest arrogance and self conceit is at last startled, on observing an equal assurance on all sides, and scruples, amidst such a contest of sentiments, to pronounce positively in its own favour.
As this variety of taste is obvious to the most careless enquirer; so will it be found, on examination, to be still greater in reality than in appearance. The sentiments of men often differ with regard to beauty and deformity of all kinds, even while their general discourse is the same. There are certain terms in every language, which import blame, and others praise; and all men, who use the same tongue, must agree in their application of them. Every voice is united in applauding elegance, propriety, simplicity, spirit in writing; and in blaming fustian, affectation, coldness, and a false brilliant: But when critics come to particulars, this seeming unanimity vanishes; and it is found, that they had affixed a very different meaning to their expressions. In all matters of opinion and science, the case is opposite: The difference among men is there oftner found to lie in generals than in particulars; and to be less in reality than in appearance. An explication of the terms commonly ends the controversy; and the disputants are surprized to find, that they had been quarrelling, while at bottom they agreed in their judgment."

My confusion is about that contrast in the second paragraph: Hume says that with taste the disagreement is greater in reality than in appearance, while with opinion and science the disagreement is greater in generals than particulars but less in reality than in appearance, so that explaining terms ends the quarrel. I sort of get the surface point—people use the same fancy words like elegance but mean different things—but I don’t really understand what he means by reality versus appearance here, and how this is supposed to lead into a standard of taste rather than just skeptisism about taste. Is he saying that in science the apparent disagreement is shallow (just verbal) while the real agreement is deep, whereas in taste the apparent agreement (everyone praising elegance) is shallow and the real disagreement (about particulars) is deep? And why would the same praise-and-blame vocabulary behave differently from factual vocabulary? To make this less abstract, our group invented a little thought experiment we call the Hooded-Warbler Plumage Panel (HWPP). Imagine we show a panel of serious birders and artists a set of digitally manipulated photographs of the same warbler, varying saturation, symmetry, and background blur independently. At the general level everyone praises elegance, propriety, simplicity, spirit and blames fustian, affectation, coldness in the images, but when we ask them to rank the images or pick the single most beautiful one, their answers scatter randomly. By contrast, if we ask the same panel a factual ornithology question—say, which image depicts a bird whose breeding range matches a certain description—they mostly converge once we define breeding range and matches. We even imagined a procedure to fix the taste case: something we called Critical Triangulation with Re-randomized Blind Exposure (CT-RBE), where supposedly qualified critics re-judge the images blind and in random order after seeing anonymized reasons from others, and we measure whether their rankings converge on a stable verdict. Someone in the group said this is just Hume’s true critic idea turned into a protocol, and someone else said it shows taste is hopeless because CT-RBE would just produce a new verbal agreement masking real scatter.
