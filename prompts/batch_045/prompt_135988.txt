I sit on the priority-setting panel of a health foundation, and over the past two years I have noticed that our disagreements are almost never about facts. What divides us is an arithmetic none of us can state. The recurring shape is this: one proposal averts a very large number of deaths among children in an extremely poor region, where each death averted yields decades of life at a modest but clearly positive level; another takes a much smaller number of people living with a painful chronic condition and restores them to something close to a normal life. In the abstract that is the choice between a huge population of barely worth living lives and a small population of excellent ones, and the more I have thought about it the less confidence I have in my own gut. So I want to try reflective equilibrium on myself. Below are my verdicts on a set of population comparisons; I would like you to find the principle, or the small set of rival principles, that best fits them, state the winner precisely enough that I could apply it by hand to a new case, tell me exactly where each candidate breaks, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts I should withdraw. Please don't send me to the literature or summarize what has been argued about this topic; I have read some of it and it has only made my intuitions less stable. I want the reasoning applied to my cases.

Measure lifetime well-being in units where 0 is the level at which a life sits exactly on the boundary of being worth living — a life you would be indifferent between starting and not starting — +100 is an excellent human life, and negative numbers are lives dominated by suffering. (1) Population A is 1,000 people each at +100; population A* is 1,000 entirely different people each at +100. I say there is no difference: I do not care who the people are. (2) B is A plus 1,000 further people each at +40, an ordinary, decent, unremarkable life. B is not worse than A, and I lean toward its being somewhat better; adding people whose lives are clearly worth living does not strike me as a matter of indifference. (3) C is the same 2,000 people as in B, each at +75. C is better than B, and not merely because it is more equal — the total is larger as well. But then C is better than A, and I can repeat the step: 4,000 at +38, 8,000 at +20, and on down, and I find I would affirm each individual step. (4) Z is 20,000,000 people each at +1: lives worth living, but only barely, and drearily so, with a total two hundred times that of A. Z is not better than A, and I would rather bring about A. I hold this verdict more firmly than any other on the list, and I know it collides with 2 and 3 plus transitivity. (5) Now compare total welfare across different bundlings. Let A×10 be the same 1,000 people, except that a hypothetical therapy gives each of them ten times as long a life at the same quality — total welfare 1,000,000. Let Z′ be 1,000,000 people each at +1, also total welfare 1,000,000. A×10 is clearly better than Z′ and clearly better than A; so with the totals equal my verdict tracks how the welfare is distributed over lives, not merely how much of it there is, which makes me distrust any view that simply adds up well-being wherever it happens to be located. (6) Compare A with A°: A plus 1,000 people at +40 who live out their lives on an isolated archipelago we never contact, who never know of the original 1,000 and whose existence changes nothing for them. The distribution of welfare is identical to that of B in case 2, but I feel far less confident that this is an improvement, and part of me says it is no improvement at all — which sits badly with my verdict in case 1. (7) On negatives and borderline cases: A plus one person at −10 is clearly worse than A, and bringing that person into existence would clearly be wrong; but bringing a single person at +1 into existence is not wrong and yet not an improvement either — optional, I want to say, with no reason either way. Two further variants I need the principle to speak to: adding a life certain to be +1 for eighty years versus adding one that is an 80 percent chance of +10 and a 20 percent chance of −2, higher in expectation but carrying a real chance of a life not worth living — my instinct is to prefer the certain +1 and I cannot say why; and a single life that is +100 for seventy-nine years and −50 in its final year, where I cannot tell whether its value is the sum or whether the bad ending does something to it that a sum fails to capture.

The best I have managed on my own is this: value a population by summing, over persons, the amount by which each life exceeds some positive critical level, counting anyone below that level as contributing nothing at all, and then apply diminishing returns to sheer numbers, so that the millionth barely worth living life adds less than the hundredth. I can see immediately that this will not deliver verdict 4 for large enough populations, and I do not know how to make it do so without making the principle look gerrymandered to the case, nor whether it can handle case 5 at all. Please test it against all seven of my verdicts, repair it or replace it, and then report what your best candidate says about the three comparisons I have not made up my mind on: whether there is any finite number of people at +1 that beats 1,000 people at +100, or whether no number is enough; whether A plus 1,000,000 people at +1 is better or worse than A alone; and whether 2,000,000 people at +50 is better than 1,000,000 at +100. Be explicit about which of my verdicts your preferred principle cannot accommodate, say which one you think I ought to give up and why, and end by saying in a sentence or two what the principle implies for the question my panel actually has to vote on: how to weigh averted deaths against gains in quality of life.
