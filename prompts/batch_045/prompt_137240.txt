I work on an internal safety-review team at a company that ships tool-using language models to enterprise customers, and I'm the person who has to sign the deployment memo. At our last review I argued for a probabilistic safety case: our monitor catches bad trajectories in the large majority of cases in evals, most failures we do see are recoverable and get rolled back, so expected loss per deployment is acceptable and we can keep shipping while we iterate on the monitor. A senior engineer shut the discussion down with a line I keep hearing in this field: "we only get one shot, so an average-case guarantee is no guarantee at all." I couldn't answer it, but I also don't think it is as obviously decisive as it was made to sound — partly because I've noticed the same line being used to argue in two apparently opposite directions. Sometimes it means that averaging is illegitimate because there is really only one trial; other times it means that even a tiny per-deployment failure probability is unacceptable, because there will be millions of deployments and small risks compound. Those can't both be the point of "one shot," and I need to know whether the tension is real or whether both uses are readings of a single underlying claim.

What I'd like is a menu of the precise theses this one-liner could be expressing, ordered from trivial to radical, with a verdict on each — platitude, substantive and defensible, substantive but probably false, or simply confused — and, most importantly, a note on which readings would actually defeat the averaging move I made and which would only support something weaker, such as a larger safety margin, a mandatory rollback path, or a staged rollout. Some readings I have been circling, which you should feel free to discard or sharpen: that the relevant trials are not repeatable at all; that they are repeatable but neither independent nor identically distributed, because the system learns, or because each deployment changes the world the next deployment faces; that averaging fails not for statistical reasons but because the downside is not compensable, so gains elsewhere cannot offset a catastrophic loss in the way expected-value reasoning presumes; that the real issue is not trials but the trustworthiness of my probability estimates, which would make expected-value maximization the wrong decision rule and push toward something like worst-case or minimax-regret reasoning; that it is a claim about the non-stationarity introduced by the system's own agency, since a deployed agent can influence which situations arise; and that it is really a claim about grain, since "one shot" is literally false of our individual deployments and could only be true of some larger unit such as the model class, the capability level, or the first irreversible loss of control — in which case I want to know what principle selects the right unit.

Two distinctions from my own notes that I'd like you to either tighten or tell me are the wrong carving. First, I separate serial averaging — many deployments, one world, roughly stable distribution — from modal averaging — a single decision with uncertainty spread over possible worlds. My safety case was doing the former and the reply seemed to demand the latter, but I'm not sure the fault line is there rather than somewhere else entirely, and I want to know whether the two really come apart or whether both collapse back into one expected-utility calculation with different inputs. Second, I have a provisional name for a failure mode I think we are committing and don't know whether it is already discussed under some established label: our average-case numbers are computed over a distribution that the system itself has partly shaped, because the model was trained to perform well on roughly the distribution we later use to evaluate it, and because a deployed agent can affect which situations actually occur — so the average we certify is not the average we will get. I have been calling this laundering the distribution, on the analogy of a student who knows the exam in advance and therefore isn't really being examined. Is that a coherent and distinct failure mode? Is it the thing the "one shot" line is actually about, or a separate problem I should stop conflating with it? And is there anything in the vicinity of the closely related line "you can't A/B test extinction" that I should be reading as a separate argument rather than a restatement? Please argue this out — I don't need citations or a reading list, since I can find those myself; I need the distinctions and the verdicts.
