I'm taking an interdisciplinary seminar that puts the philosophy of history next to some tort law, and I keep getting stuck on two institutional practices that appear to run on opposite assumptions about counterfactuals, so I'd like the implicit theory made explicit. The first is the tort-side one: a plaintiff has to show that the wrongdoer made a difference, that but for the negligent act the injury would not have occurred, and most common-law courts refuse what get called "wrongful life" claims, in which a child born with severe impairments because a doctor failed to warn the parents seeks damages for having been born at all; the stated difficulty, as I understand it, is that the comparison state — her never having existed — is not a state that can be worse or better for her, so the counterfactual needed to measure the harm is simply unavailable. The second is the practice of compensating historical injustices: schemes that pay the descendants of enslaved, dispossessed, or displaced populations compute what is owed by comparing the claimants' situation with a counterfactual in which the enslavement or dispossession did not happen, even though, given how partnerships, migrations, and conception dates would have shifted, essentially none of these particular individuals would have existed in that world. So the same broad legal culture treats an existence-destroying counterfactual as unusable in one context and as the working basis of an entitlement in another.

Here is the shape of what I want. First, reconstruct the theory of harm, of counterfactual comparison, and of what the relata of comparison are that the reparations practice only makes sense under — and be precise about which parts it strictly presupposes and which it merely sits comfortably with. My own tentative carving is that institutions handle what I've started calling existence-entangled counterfactuals in a few distinguishable ways: comparing lineages over generations rather than persons; comparing occupants of a social position or role rather than individuals; comparing distributions over populations, so that identity drops out altogether; or treating a people or community as a single entity that survives counterfactual perturbation even though its members do not. Is that the right set of options, is one of them what the practice actually needs, or is the practice metaphysically lighter than all of them — compatible with several theories at once, so that nothing about the metaphysics of persons or groups settles it? Second, say how the practice chooses its nearest worlds. It seems to hold fixed the broad demographic and economic background and remove only the institution, rather than asking whether, absent that institution, some other predation would have produced much the same distribution — the "it would have happened anyway" worry that historians raise about inevitability. Does rejecting that alternative presuppose a contested view of historical modality, or is it just a stipulation about which differences are to be counted, and what would it take for the inevitability-style counterfactual to defeat a claim?

Finally, I want the practical upshot. Suppose a scheme were written differently: instead of tracing lineage wealth, it pays each claimant the gap between her present position in the income distribution and the position that a same-sized but differently composed population would have occupied had the institution never existed. Is that scheme more defensible under the tort-side metaphysics of persons, or does it inherit the same commitments in a new place? And if we took the tort-side scruple seriously throughout, what would actually have to change — would compensation have to be paid to communities rather than individuals, restricted to living victims of the original wrong, reframed as forward-looking redistribution rather than repair, or abandoned as a category? A good answer, for me, would say exactly where the existing practice is underdetermined by theory and where it quietly rules a rival view out.
