My mate and I had a proper three-hour arguement in the pub last night about whether large language models understand anything, and I came away realising we spent most of it talking past each other because neither of us ever said what the slogan was actually meant to mean. He does literary translation for a living (Hungarian into English mostly), which is why he got so heated. I mentioned that a chatbot had caught an ambiguity in a sentence he was working on that his editor had missed — one that turns on whether the object being described is the kind of thing that can be shut — and he said the thing doesn't understand anything, it's just autocomplete, it doesn't know what it's talking about. Fine, I've heard that a million times. But then he said something that actually got to me.

Imagine two training corpora, he said. One is ordinary English. The other is the same English with the words 'left' and 'right' swapped everywhere, systematically, from page one. Train a model on each. Both come out fluent, both are internally consistent, each one is right about its own language and 'wrong' about the other, and no test you can run in words will tell you which is which. So neither of them, he says, is about left and right: nothing in either system is answerable to the world, only to more text. He had a name for it, the no debts criterion — a system understands a word only if what it does with that word can get it into trouble with something that isn't more text.

I tried a couple of moves. I said humans are predicting the next word a lot of the time too, which didn't seem to land. Then I tried what I'd call the borrowed debts reply: the debts are real, they're just held by someone else. When a technician follows instructions a model wrote and the machine catches fire, an error signal does arrive from the world — just late, and routed through a person. He said that's the technician's understanding doing the work in the loop, not the model's, and that the model never pays either way. We went round on that for ages and got nowhere, and I think it's partly because 'it's just autocomplete' and 'it doesn't know what it's talking about' were each doing three or four different jobs in his mouth.

So can you do the disambiguation properly. I want a menu of the distinct precise claims those two lines could be expressing, ordered from the trivial to the radical — is it a claim about the training objective, about the causal history of the system, about what its internal states are hooked up to, about whether it can be wrong in a way that costs it something, about what happens downstream when people act on its output, or about nothing more than how we ought to talk? For each reading say whether it's true and uninteresting, substantive and defensible, contentious, straightforwardly false, or merely verbal; say what kind of evidence could in principle settle it and which ones are unfalsifiable by construction; and say which reading he was probably leaning on when he gave me the swapped left/right corpora, and whether that thought experiment actually establishes the reading it was offered for, or whether it sneaks in the conclusion.

The bit I care most about: which readings, if any, would really support his actual conclusion, which is that we should stop saying these systems know or understand anything even loosely and as shorthand, and that it's misleading for a company to imply otherwise. I suspect several of the readings support something weaker than that, and I want to know exactly where the gap is. Also tell me if there's any reading on which the slogan turns round and bites him — one that would apply with equal force to a human speaker, or that would make the claim unfalsifiable in a way he'd reject elsewhere. And if my borrowed debts reply has a precise version worth defending, give me that version and say honestly whether it works or whether it's just changing the subject.

Lay it out as a list I can send him, and write it in British English please. Don't just tell me what the philosophy literature says in general — I want the readings separated and graded, because the whole argument hinged on which one of them he was entitled to.
