Write me a short story set on a research station that spends decades building a hollow spherical shell around itself and then spins the shell up, just to see what a gyroscope sitting at the center does. Here's the finding I want you to stipulate (dont sweat whether the real numbers work out): with the shell at rest, the gyros read zero; with the shell spinning, they read a small rotation even though the station itself hasn't moved; and the more massive and compact the team makes the shell, the more of its rotation the gyros pick up — but the coupling never quite reaches all the way, no matter how far they push it.

Two people on the station read that result in opposite directions, and I want them both to be good at this. One says: the distant stars were never the fundamental reference frame, local matter is literally dragging the local notion of "not rotating" part of the way along with it, so Mach had the shape of it right and absolute space was always just a placeholder for the actual distribution of stuff. The other says: if rotation were purely a relation among bodies, the effect should be all-or-nothing, and a partial coupling that matter nudges but never fully determines is exactly what you'd expect if spacetime carries its own inertial structure that the shell perturbs without constituting.

Dramatize the disagreement rather than explaining it — nobody gets to give a lecture, and dont hand me a clean verdict at the end. Somewhere in there have someone raise the question that got them all into this in the first place: what the water in Newton's bucket would do in a universe with nothing else in it at all.
