Please take the role of a senior philosopher of mind who also works seriously at the interface of philosophy of biology and collective agency, and who has supervised a good number of dissertations. What I want from you is an audit of a position, not a survey of a literature and not a lecture; no citations or suggested readings, since I can find those myself. Please work in stages: first, spend genuine time brainstorming every respect in which the commitments below might fail to hang together, including pairs I have not paired myself and including pressures that come from outside the set; then winnow that list down to the tensions that are real; then give me the audit ranked by severity, with a sentence or two of reasoning per item so I can see how you got there.

Context: I am a PhD student in philosophy, working in philosophy of cognitive science with a philosopher of biology as my second reader, and I am finalising a dissertation prospectus. The first chapter argues that some eusocial insect colonies — in particular Aunt Hillary, the ant colony that Hofstadter converses with in the Ant Fugue chapter of Gödel, Escher, Bach — are not merely usefully interpreted as agents but are genuinely subjects of mental states: they believe things, want things, and understand a good deal of English. I do not yet know whether the package I have assembled to support that claim hangs together, and I would rather find out now than at the defence. Here is the position, stated as flatly as I can manage.

(1) Aunt Hillary has beliefs and desires in a full-blooded realist sense, and she understands a good deal of English. I mean this as more than a façon de parler: I want to say there is a fact of the matter here, and that it falls on her side.

(2) No individual ant in the colony has any mental state that does explanatory work in accounting for Aunt Hillary's mentality. Ants stand to Aunt Hillary roughly as neurons stand to me, and I deny that neurons believe anything.

(3) There is a fact of the matter about where the thinker is located. It is the colony: not the larger system comprising the colony, the nest, and the fungus garden it tends, and not the still larger system comprising the colony together with the human who reads and writes her end of the conversation.

(4) My warrant for (1) has always been broadly Dennettian: you get nowhere predicting what she will do by tracking ants, whereas at the colony level the intentional stance pays off handsomely. And I am inclined to go further and say that this payoff is where her mentality consists, rather than being merely excellent evidence of some further hidden fact.

(5) I am not a classical functionalist about the substrate. I doubt that a nation-sized system organised so as to mimic the functional economy of a single person — Block's Chinese Nation — or a large corporation, could have a mind however good the mimicry, because the medium and the causal-mechanical details matter, and a system whose parts go home at night and have interests of their own is the wrong kind of thing.

(6) As an account of what makes a state genuinely representational rather than merely as-if representational, I am attracted to an etiological theory: content is fixed by proper function, where proper functions are the offspring of selection. This is the machinery I rely on elsewhere to say that a frog's visual system really does represent small moving dark things while a thermostat's states do not really represent temperatures.

(7) A case verdict I am attached to and do not want to lose: a computational simulation whose signal traffic is matched to the colony's, ant for ant and signal for signal, and which is trained until its side of a transcript is indistinguishable from hers, understands nothing at all. Whatever content her states have is original; whatever content its states have is derived, from us.

(8) I think there is something it is like to be Aunt Hillary — something minimal, slow and deeply alien — but I also think phenomenal consciousness requires a specific sort of architecture: fast recurrent processing, a bottleneck, integration realised in a single bounded dynamic core. A colony is a diffuse swarm with no obvious bottleneck, and I am not confident it has that architecture.

(9) To settle the problems about boundaries and derivedness that (3) and (7) point at, I have been developing a condition of my own, which I have been calling the maintenance condition: a system's states have content originally only if the system itself has to absorb the cost of its own misrepresentations, in the sense that error on its part can, in the ordinary course of things and without any external interpreter resetting it, degrade the very machinery that produced the representation. I intend it to admit the colony, since a colony that systematically misreads its environment starves, and to exclude both the simulation and the thermostat. A friend has pointed out that failing firms go bankrupt too, and I do not yet have a good answer to that.

(10) Two further verdicts I am fairly confident about: I am not willing to say that a Portuguese man o' war has beliefs, even granting that it is an evolutionary individual with a division of labour, and I am not willing to say that a clonal aspen stand has beliefs.

What I want from you: which of these commitments pull against each other, ranked by how badly they do, and for each, precisely what would have to give and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set — trace the knock-on consequences explicitly where a fix in one place forces a change in another. Tell me plainly which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection and why; I would much rather be told that one of my worries is unfounded than have a problem manufactured for me. If there is a single revision that would tidy up the most, name it and say what it costs me. And tell me whether the maintenance condition in (9) is doing the work I need it to do, or whether it either over-generates or quietly restates one of my other commitments. I do not know which of these I would give up, and I do not want that decision made for me on grounds of what is currently fashionable. One last constraint: whatever you propose must not commit me to mentality coming for free to every evolutionary individual.
