Writing a novel, and for the last two years I've been mainlining Nietzsche for it — my narrator is a food writer who's halfway through writing a sort of Nietzschean self-help book nobody asked for, and nobody else in the book is a philosopher, so whatever she says out loud has to survive being said in a kitchen to people who are just cutting vegetables. Before I let her hold forth in chapter nine I want to know whether what I've got her believing is a package or a pile. Roughly where she's landed:

1. The physiological reading is the right one. You should read a philosophical doctrine as, among other things, a report on the state of the body that produced it — not as a refutation of it, just as the most informative thing you can say about it. And you should run this on everybody, Nietzsche included.

2. That "great health" thing — the health you're supposed to win back again and again rather than just have — is not a metaphor for spiritual robustness or creativity or whatever the soft version is. He means something about actual bodies. I think he's right to mean that, and I want her to say it without winking.

3. His own health record is beside the point. The migraines, the near-blindness, the stomach, the collapse at the end. Bad luck and a bad century and worse doctors. It tells you nothing about whether what he says about health is true or useful.

4. None of the actual diet stuff is advice I'd follow now — the heavy German cooking, the beer, the sit-down life, all of it is period nonsense and there's no Nietzschean diet to be extracted. But I don't think that hollows out 2.

5. The anti-vegetarian streak is the most underrated thing in him. Most people who convert are converting for reasons that have nothing to do with the reasons they give, and he's right to be suspicious of the type. She's a vegetarian anyway, and she thinks you should be one too, for the normal reasons, which she thinks are good.

6. His thing about the sedentary life — that it's bad for thought specifically, not just for your back — is one of the best things he ever said, and it's what her whole working life is built around. Walk in the morning, stand at the desk, never think in a chair.

Go through those and tell me which pairs actually fight, and which only look like they do, because I genuinely can't tell. Rank the real ones by how bad the fight is, and for each say what would have to give and what that costs her. And if there's one she could quietly drop in revision without losing whatever it was that made me want to write her in the first place, say which one.
