i run a 75-minute seminar, second-year applied ethics / political philosophy, 18 students, one room, no tech beyond a whiteboard. I want an in-class activity that makes them feel the difference between a Ulysses contract and constitutional precommitment — and, more importantly, makes them feel the thing that makes the analogy wobble.

Here's the target, as I see it. When I bind my own future self, the person who installs the constraint and the person who lives under it are (arguably) the same agent over time, which is why we can call the constraint autonomy-respecting rather than a restraint. When a founding cohort entrenches a rule, the installers and the people bound come apart — and yet we still sometimes want to say the constraint protects the bound party rather than just ruling them. My actual target is the bit underneath both: from the inside, you cannot tell which situation you are in. If a constraint binds you now and you resent it, you can't distinguish "my earlier self foresaw I'd be tempted and saved me" from "some people who are now off the hook tied my hands, for reasons that were partly theirs." Both feel exactly the same from the position of the person bound. I want them to run into that, not hear it.

Constraints:
- points only, no money, nothing bought in advance; index cards or a shared sheet is fine, printing is fine.
- at least two phases, and a binding device whose strength the participants set themselves (a lock with a cost to break, or similar).
- one group should get to write rules they don't themselves live under — their score gets locked before the constraint bites — and a later group lives under them. I want the first group to feel the pull to legislate cheaply, and the second group to feel the resentment.
- i'd like the obvious repair to be something they invent rather than me naming it: some version of a self-renewing Ulysses contract, where the binding lapses unless you re-authorise it after a cooling-off period. Set the rules up so that fix is available; don't mention it.
- no mini-lecture at the top, one line of framing max.
- don't use the words "Ulysses", "precommitment" or "dead hand" before the debrief.
- one thing I don't want: the whole thing resting on a gotcha reveal. If something changes mid-game it should be a change they could have anticipated the possibility of in advance.

Give me the setup and materials, the rules and payoff scheme, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments onto (i) same-agent binding, (ii) cross-cohort binding, and (iii) the indistinguishability point. Then honestly: what will go wrong the first time I run it (I expect one group to refuse to play or to start gaming the scoring) and how to handle it, and which part of the idea the game simply can't produce and I'll have to say out loud myself.

British English, and keep it under 700 words.
