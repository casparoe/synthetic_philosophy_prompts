I'm a PhD student working on symmetry and gauge freedom in classical field theory, and I've been asked to give a short talk on the hole argument to a general philosophy of science audience. A colleague lent me a popular-level chapter on spacetime; before I borrow any of its framing, I'd like to know how much of it I can repeat in good conscience. The chapter puts the argument like this:

The hole argument goes like this. Start with a spacetime containing a hole — a region where the field equations stop constraining the metric, such as the interior of a black hole or the neighbourhood of the Big Bang singularity. Fix the geometry everywhere outside the hole by data on a slice to its past. Now apply a diffeomorphism that reshuffles the points inside the hole and is the identity outside it. Because general relativity is generally covariant, the dragged-along metric is also a solution of the field equations, agreeing with the original everywhere outside the hole. So the theory leaves the geometry inside the hole undetermined: general relativity is indeterministic. The only escape is to deny that the two models describe different physical situations, and that requires denying that spacetime points are individuals with an identity independent of the fields — which is just to say that substantivalism about spacetime is false and that relationism is true. Notice, finally, that the construction uses nothing but diffeomorphisms, so it does not really rest on any doctrine about spacetime at all: the same argument can be run in Newtonian mechanics and in special relativity, and its real moral is that a symmetry group large enough will make any theory indeterministic.

It reads very smoothly, which makes me suspicious. My own sense is that something goes wrong early: I was taught that a hole is just any region outside which the diffeomorphism is required to be the identity, not a place where the dynamics breaks down, and that the conclusion is conditional — the theory plus a certain thesis about the identity of spacetime points yields a failure of determinism — rather than a verdict on general relativity itself. I am less sure about the closing moves. I have heard it said both that the argument refutes substantivalism and that a sophisticated substantivalist can simply accept that diffeomorphic models represent the same physical situation and carry on; and I have heard the opposite claim about background structure, namely that the construction needs a theory without fixed background fields and so cannot be run in Newtonian or special-relativistic settings. But I cannot tell which of these are live disputes in the literature and which are my colleague's embellishments.

Please work through the passage claim by claim: number the claims, mark each as correct, a defensible simplification, or a genuine misrepresentation, and say in each case what a philosopher of physics would say instead. Where the passage draws one of the morals I have flagged as doubtful, tell me whether that moral still follows once the premises are corrected, and where the real disagreement in the literature lies. Close with a one-line overall verdict, written as Verdict: fair, fair but misleading, or unfair, and then, as the final line of your answer, \answer{n}, where n is the number of your numbered claims that survive essentially intact. Please write in British English.
