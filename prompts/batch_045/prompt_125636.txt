My roommate's cousin has been trying to talk me into putting some savings into his startup, and I can't tell weather he's onto something or whether I'm the one being dense.

The pitch: two small chambers of ordinary room-temperature gas joined by an aperture with a shutter. Right by the aperture there's a cheap sensor array plus a little neural net that watches the gas and predicts, a few microseconds ahead, whether the next thing to arrive will be a fast molecule coming from the left or a slow one coming from the right. The shutter opens only in those cases. Over time the right chamber heats up and the left one cools down, and then you either run a heat engine off the difference or just sell the whole thing as a fridge with no compressor.

I told him this is basically that old thought experiment from school — the one with the tiny door and sorting fast and slow molecules — and that everybody learns it doesn't work. He says he knows all that and has answers ready:

1. The usual reply is that you have to pay for the information, at kT ln 2 per bit or whatever. But he says a single "open now" decision lets through hundreds of molecules, each carrying more than that, so the energy books come out way ahead.
2. He never erases anything. The record of every decision just gets appended to a log; storage is cheap and he can buy more of it with the power he's generating. He also points out the unit only has to run a few hours between maintenance, so "your memory fills up eventually" doesn't bite in practice.
3. He says the information is free anyway, since the sensor array was already built for their other product, an air-quality monitor. Same data stream, second use. He calls this the data moat.
4. And his simulation runs at a profit. I did notice that in the sim the shutter is weightless and never jiggles around on its own, but he says that's just an engineering detail they'll solve later.

Which of these comebacks, if any, actually survives, and where exactly does the free lunch get paid for? I'd like something concrete to say back to him that isn't just "physics says no."
