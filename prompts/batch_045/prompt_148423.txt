I've been teaching a grad seminar on the self this term and I keep running into a thing in discussion that I don't have a word for. I work mostly in epistemology, so I'm well outside that literature and probably reinventing something someone named twenty years ago. Would love to know whether it has a name.

Here's the phenomenon. A person can come to disbelieve their own self-narrative and it still keeps pulling. Not a vague residue, an actual continuing grip on what they do and on what they'll say about themselves in the moment. Case one: a friend of mine spent thirty years with a story about himself, he's the kind of person who finishes what he starts. A few years back he decided, deliberately and with evidence, that it's false. He can rattle off six projects he walked away from, he'll tell you unprompted that it was a family myth, and he still can't leave a manuscript alone. He describes the pull in the first person while simultaneously disavowing it, and he isn't being ironic. Case two: a student who was assigned "you're the responsible one" by her siblings, sees perfectly well that it was imposed on her, resents it, and is nevertheless the person who books the flights every single time. What's odd is how it goes away: it fades by being acted against, slowly and unevenly, and it comes back under time pressure. Arguing against it does nothing, or nothing she can feel.

The contrast case is what makes me think this is one thing and not just recalcitrant belief. Correct a merely factual bit of someone's autobiography, no you were born in April, and the old version loses its grip instantly and completely the moment they're convinced. So whatever this is, it attaches specifically to the parts of a life-story that have a shape: the "kind of person I am", the "where this is going", the evaluative and teleological bits rather than the dated ones.

In my notes I've been calling it narrative drag, or residual gravity. It isn't quite habit, and it isn't like the alief business or like belief perseverance, those seem to be about single propositions or associations whereas this is shaped like a whole life. And it isn't straightforwardly what Dennett is after with the self as a centre of narrative gravity either, because on his picture the abstraction is supposed to be doing explanatory work, and here you have a story the person has explicitly withdrawn and it is still doing the work. Though maybe that just means the picture absorbs it and I'm not seeing how.

So: does this have a name of its own, or is there a cluster of established concepts that carve it up between them? I want the carving, not the label — what exactly it is, what it is not, where its edges are, and whether the disavowed story is still that person's centre of gravity or whether something else has taken over while mimicking it. Also tell me if it tells against the gravity metaphor rather than sitting inside it.

Be honest about your confidence. If there's no established term, say so plainly instead of finding me one. If you think I'm bundling three separate things under one description, split them and say which is which. If my contrast case is doing less work than I think, or the whole framing leans on a picture of self-narratives that nobody should accept, I'd rather be told that than get a polite answer. And if you reach for names and dates, don't bother — I can't look anything up from here and I won't trust a half-remembered citation anyway, so give me the views rather than who holds them.

Write it as continuous prose, the way you'd write to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. I know a table of nearest concepts and how each differs would be the obvious move here and I specifically don't want one, because I want to hand what you give me to my students as a letter.
