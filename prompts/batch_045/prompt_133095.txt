I'm on a tiny team that just got handed a single pot of money: about five researcher-years and a chunk of compute, and the mandate is "safety work that doesn't rely on the model being good." So the control end of the pool, not alignment training. We sit inside a lab that will probably ship something with real write access — code repos, tickets, some prod infrastructure — within the next couple of years, and our leverage over the ship/no-ship call is limited but not zero. Five researcher-years is short enough that we should pick three lines of work and just accept that the rest goes unfunded.

I've been sorting the options along some axes I made up, because the usual framing feels wrong to me:

- disclosure-robustness: does the measure still work if the model knows the entire protocol, i.e. does its bite come from what the surrounding system does with outputs rather than from the model being surprised? Structural stuff like never acting on unverified outputs scores high here; hidden honeypots and unannounced audits seem to die the moment they leak, and everything leaks.
- deployment-blocking vs deployment-hardening: is the point to generate a signal that should stop or roll back a launch, or to make the thing safer while it runs? A signal looks worthless to me unless the institutional machinery for acting on it already exists, and I suspect that machinery, not the detector, is the actual bottleneck.
- graceful degradation: how much value is left if we're wrong about the threat model in a specific direction — if the model is merely unreliable rather than strategically adversarial, or adversarial but bad at long-horizon planning, or only opportunistic when oversight is visibly low.
- capability cost: measures that require giving up real capability get quietly dropped under competitive pressure, so I discount anything that's expensive in that currency even if it looks good on paper.

Two things I want from you. First: which three lines of work do you fund, in what order, and why — I don't want a tour of the menu, I know the menu, I want the argument for the ordering. Second: which open conceptual questions are upstream of the whole enterprise, in the sense that a lot of the ordering swings on how they come out? For each, say how tractable it is for people doing conceptual work rather than empirical ML, and what we'd observe in the next eighteen months or so that should move it up or down the list.

Also tell me which of my four axes are bad axes, or which one I'm overweighting. I'm most suspicious of the first one myself — I've definately underpriced how much compartmentalisation can buy you, and if you can get workable secrecy cheaply then disclosure-robustness stops being the thing everything else keys off.
