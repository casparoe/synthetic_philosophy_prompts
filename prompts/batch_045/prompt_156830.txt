i'm putting together a short position paper for a reading group on AI and education and I want someone to poke holes in the package before I commit to it. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) Turing's strategic point in the imitation-game paper — that instead of trying to write a programme that has the adult mind in it, you build something with very little built in and put it through a course of education — is right, and it isn't just a practical shortcut. It's the only strategy that could ever work for competences nobody can writen down in advance.

(2) What motivates (1) for me is partly that a system whose adult behaviour was specified by hand by a programmer is in some sense a fake: it gives you the appearance of the competence without the thing itself. That's what the child machine is supposed to get us away from.

(3) But I also think there's no fact about whether the machine "really" understands, or has really been educated, over and above what it goes on to do. Whether the education took is settled by its behaviour in new situations, not by anything you could read off its internals or off how it got there.

(4) I want to distinguish education from mere training, and the mark I like is curriculum sensitivity: the same material delivered in a different order, or paced and sequenced differently, produces a genuinely differently shaped competence. If the outcome is invariant to how you order and pace the material, whatever happened was training, not education. This also means whoever does the sequencing has to be doing it with an eye to what the learner currently can't do.

(5) I think some system of rewards and punishments has to be there at the start — the learner needs a signal it's actually sensitive to — but the endpoint of education is a machine that notices and corrects its own errors without anyone grading it.

(6) And a case verdict I keep coming back to: a machine that just stores an enormous hand-specified table of input-output pairs, a giant lookup table however big, is the paradigm case of something that hasn't been educated and isn't thinking, even though it would sail through any behavioural test you cared to run.

I can't tell which of these is doing the real work. Go through them and tell me which pairs actually conflict, which only look like they do, and for each genuine conflict what would have to give and what giving it would cost me. Rank the conflicts by how bad they are rather than treating them all as equally serious, and if there's a single revision that clears up more than one of them at once, say so.
