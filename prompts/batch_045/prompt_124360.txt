Most Anglophone news organizations follow a rule I will call the redaction norm: when a quoted speaker uses a racial, ethnic, or homophobic slur, the word itself is not printed — it appears instead as a metalinguistic description, as "a racial slur," or as a few letters and a row of asterisks — while the rest of the sentence, the speaker's identity, and the fact that the slur was used are all reported verbatim; graphic descriptions of violence, by contrast, are normally printed without redaction. Reconstruct the implicit theory of slur meaning this norm presupposes, and say how much of the philosophy of slurs a newsroom is thereby committed to.

Two features of the norm seem to pull in opposite directions. First, it treats a slur as too dangerous to reproduce even when it is merely quoted — mentioned rather than used — which suggests derogatory content that survives embedding in indirect reports, negations, and questions, and so looks like content carried by the word rather than something an individual speaker contributes on the occasion. Second, the same norm routinely hands readers a description or a skeleton that makes the suppressed word uniquely recoverable, and does not treat that as a failure; on the face of it that is only coherent if what matters is not the occurrence of the word-form but who is putting it into the world and with what apparent endorsement. Is the norm therefore internally inconsistent, or can a single account of slurs — one that locates the derogation in semantic content, one that locates it in the speaker's attitudes or in a convention governing use, or one that locates it in a social practice rather than in an utterance — make sense of both halves? Distinguish what the norm strictly presupposes from what it merely sits well with, and tell me what would have to change in newsroom practice if we adopted a view on which a slur's offense is entirely a matter of use.
