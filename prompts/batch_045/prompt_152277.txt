I am writing a position paper on the principle of indifference, and before I commit myself in print I would like an audit of the package of views I have ended up with. I hold all six of the commitments below, I am fairly attached to each, and I have a nagging sense that the set is not jointly tenable — but I cannot locate the fault line myself, and I would rather know now than at the stage of responding to a referee.

The context is Bertrand's chord paradox and what it is supposed to show. A chord of a circle is chosen at random; the probability that it is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle comes out as a third, a half, or a quarter depending on whether one uniformises over the endpoints on the circumference, over the chord's distance from the centre, or over the midpoint within the disc. The parallel case I have in mind is the cube factory: a factory produces cubes with side length between one and two feet, and uniformity over side length and uniformity over volume give different probabilities for the very same event.

My commitments:

(1) Indifference is legitimate for the six faces of a die that I know to be fair, and the ground of that legitimacy is the physical symmetry of the set-up rather than ignorance. If I were told nothing whatever about the die I would not take myself to be entitled to a sharp sixth.

(2) Bertrand's paradox and the cube factory show that the classical principle has no determinate content when applied to continuous magnitudes. Any interval can be reparametrised, so neither randomness nor ignorance singles out a partition. I read the paradox as a reductio of indifference over continua, and specifically not as a demonstration that Bertrand's question was merely under-specified pending a description of the generating mechanism.

(3) At the same time, I think the transformation-group and maximum-entropy repair of the classical principle is broadly right: impose invariance under the symmetries that genuinely belong to the problem — rotation, translation, rescaling — and a unique distribution drops out, namely the one on which the chord's distance from the centre is uniform. I have also heard it said that invariance arguments of this style can be run so as to deliver each of the three classical answers depending on what background structure one holds fixed; I do not know whether that refutes (3) or only shows that the constraints are being under-described, and I would like to know which.

(4) My own positive proposal, which I take to be the least-discussed part of the package: indifference is always a stand-in for frequency evidence. It is defensible on a partition only where the agent could in principle be calibrated against it — where there exists a repeatable chance set-up whose long-run frequencies could score the credences in question. Where no such set-up is even conceivable, a sharp credence produced by indifference is not uncertainty but noise, and I would rather record an imprecise, interval-valued credence or none at all.

(5) Two agents differently informed about how the chord was produced ought to assign different probabilities. So I do not read (3) as claiming that the answer is information-independent, only that it is unique once the constraints are fixed.

(6) I assign a half to heads on a coin of unknown bias, but I refuse to take the bias itself to be uniformly distributed on the unit interval, and the reason I give is that this would be indifference over a continuous magnitude.

What I want is an audit, not a survey of the literature and not a reading list — argue from the structure of the views rather than from what has been said about them. Please work through the six and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and how severe each tension is, ordered by severity rather than listed as if equally bad, with an explicit statement of what would have to give in each case and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the package. I also want to know which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection, and why they dissolve, since I would rather be told about three real problems with reasons than about nine possible frictions. If one of my commitments is doing damage out of proportion to how central it feels to me, say which: I am especially unsure whether (4) is the load-bearing part or the part that has to go. Finally, tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and whether any of the six is underdetermined in the sense that several quite different further commitments would equally well make it true — in which case I would like to know what the choice between them turns on.

Please write in British English.
