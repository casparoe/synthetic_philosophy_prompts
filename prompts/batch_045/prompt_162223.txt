So I've drifted into a version of tranquilism I can't shake: nothing is intrinsically good for a person, only bad — what matters is the absence of suffering and of craving, where I mean craving broadly, any felt affective urgency to make things different, so boredom and restlessness count and a calm absorbed life sits at zero instead of in the negatives. Two clauses I've bolted on: a craving only counts if you'd still have it with full information and unimpaired reflection (that's my fix for adaptive preferences and manufactured desires), and you get no credit for cravings you never had, so preventing a craving isn't a benefit, it's just not a cost. I also lean harder than I should on the liking/wanting work in affective neuroscience — the idea that satisfaction isn't a positive feeling so much as a wanting going quiet.

What would actually count against this? Skip the "then a quiet contented life is as good as a life of achievement" objection, I've heard it and it doesn't move me. What I definately care about are cases where I have to say two lives are equally good and one is obviously better off in a way that has nothing to do with craving — understanding versus confusion, say. Rank the defeaters fatal to merely awkward, say which ones a defender of my version should concede rather than explain away, and tell me honestly whether the pleasure-is-just-the-absence-of-wanting move has insulated me into unfalsifiability, and whether that's a problem for a view like this. Also mark which claims are settled among people who work on welfare and which are contested, and don't dress up a contested reading as the consensus.
