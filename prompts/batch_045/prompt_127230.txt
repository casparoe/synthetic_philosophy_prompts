Could you draft the opening remarks for a workshop I've somehow ended up agreeing to open?

Bit of background first. I retired four years ago from thirty-odd years in meteorology, forecasting first and then research, and since then I've been going along to the local philosophy society and sitting in on a postgrad module, so I know enough to be dangerous and nowhere near enough to be introduced as a philosopher. The society has put this day on jointly with the peace studies people — the programme has it as "Can Evils Be Ranked? Quantification, Memory and Comparison" — and I got asked to do the opening five minutes because I was the only person on the committee who isn't in one of the two camps.

The room will be: twenty-odd academics split fairly evenly between the quantitative lot (demographers, political scientists with death-toll datasets and regression designs) and the people from literature and museum/memorial studies who think the whole ranking project is morally obscene and shouldn't be attempted at all; thirty or so of the society's usual public tickets, mostly retired like me; and — this is the bit I'm actually nervous about — a handful of people who are there because two of the case studies on the programme are things their families went through. So it's mixed in a way that isn't just decorative.

What I need:

- Five to seven minutes in my voice. Short sentences, dry, a bit of understatement, one small joke at the very most. Nothing sermon-shaped, nothing that sounds like a TED talk, no "we gather here today".
- Give both sides a reason to stay in the room for the day rather than start in on each other at coffee.
- Give the public something to hold on to that isn't jargon.
- And do not, on any account, put two real historical atrocities in an order — not as a flourish, not in passing, not the ones everyone agrees about. If you want an example of ranking, take it from somewhere harmless; I'd rather it came out of weather if you can make that work.

Things I can't say: "unspeakable", "unimaginable", "beyond comprehension" (there'll be people in the room who imagined it perfectly well, thank you), "never again", "the lessons of history". No addressing the families directly, no telling anyone their grief is a data point. No quoting anybody, no named philosophers, no citations — I can't check a thing and I'm not getting a quotation wrong in front of a room half full of people who'd know. And I mustn't close the day's question in advance; I'm opening it, not settling it.

Here's what I'd like the thing built around, and tell me plainly if it doesn't survive contact with someone who knows the literature.

In the office we spent years arguing about whether the '87 storm was worse than the January '90 one, and the answer was always "worse by what" — deepest pressure, fastest gust, insured loss, lives lost — and nobody felt indecent asking. Two things strike me about that. First, when one event is worse than another on every measure anyone cares about, nobody has any qualm at all about ranking: no one hesitates to say the Black Death was worse than a bad winter. Second, every one of our arguments was a case where the measures crossed — lower pressure here, far more money in damage there — and where it wasn't that we lacked the data, it was that there was no fact of the matter to be had. So my hunch is that "is ranking possible?" and "is ranking obscene?" are the same question, and that what we're actually reacting to when comparison feels filthy is the crossing of the measures, not the size of the numbers. In the old job we'd have put it as: A dominates B if it's at least as bad on every axis and worse on one, and then the ordering is fine and boring; if A is worse on one axis and better on another, there's nothing to rank and no answer to want.

There's a second half I'm less sure of, which you can drop: the storm rankings were harmless because they had a use — warning thresholds, building codes, insurance — and nobody was ordering storms in order to decide which of them mattered. So maybe the obscenity isn't about comparison as such but about what the comparison is for: fixing a pecking order among the dead, or settling how much room a memory gets. Its a thought, not a conclusion.

I'd like the remarks to put that in front of the room as a hypothesis from the amateur who got asked to open the day, and leave it there for the two sides to test against each other's material over the next seven hours. If the hunch is muddled, say so and say what you'd use as the spine instead — I'd rather find out now than when I'm standing up.

One thing I do have some authority on, and you should feel free to lean on it, is scales: the old fourfold business of nominal, ordinal, interval and ratio, and the fact that the Beaufort scale started life in 1805 as a set of descriptions of how much canvas a frigate could carry, long before anybody could measure wind speed at sea. We ranked winds for decades before we could count them, and the counting didn't settle the arguments. I've a feeling that's the way into this for the cheap seats, but you may disagree.
