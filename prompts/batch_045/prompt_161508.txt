Hi. I write about science and ideas for a weekly paper, and I've landed myself with a short book about identity over time that began as a 1,200-word piece on a conservation job I went to see in Ravenna and has got completly out of hand. My producer wants me to record a podcast episode called something like "what is a thing?" in a fortnight, and she's asked me to write down, before we record, exactly where I've ended up, so that a friend of hers who did a philosophy PhD can come on and take it apart. I'd rather find out where it falls over now than on air. I did some of this at university years ago and remember almost nothing, except that my tutor thought the Ship of Theseus was the least interesting thing anyone could talk about.

Here's the list, roughly in the order I'd defend it. (1) I'm an endurantist: the thing in front of me is wholly there at each moment it exists, not a slice of a longer thing, and I think the worm picture makes nonsense of agency, because on that picture nothing ever does anything, only temporal parts do. (2) I'm an eternalist, sort of by default, because of relativity: I don't think there's a privileged global now, and I'm happy to say future events are as real as present ones. My own worry, the one I keep tripping over, is that (1) and (2) can't both be true, because endurantism looks like it needs a specially privileged present moment to hang on to. (3) For temporary intrinsics — the pillar bent this year, straight next year — I take the line that objects instantiate their properties at times: the relation is to a time, not to a temporal part of the object, and I don't accept that this is perdurantism smuggled in by the back door. (4) I don't believe in coincident objects: there's one thing where the statue is, not two, and I think there's a plain, non-perspectival fact about how many objects are in a room — I'm not going to start saying that something can be the same statue but a different lump. (5) But I do think the modal difference is real: melt the bronze down and cast it as a lump and the statue is gone while the hunk of bronze goes on, and I'm sure that's more than a manner of speaking. (6) On people: I think whether the thing in front of me now is the same person as the one who did something ten years ago is fixed by the stage-by-stage facts along the chain between then and now and nothing else — nothing happening elsewhere gets a vote. And I'm not a fan of the line that identity isn't what matters in survival: I think identity is what matters, and that other relations matter only as evidence for it.

Two verdicts I'm stuck with, both from cases I half-invented after that trip. First, the emulation case — I've spent time with the cryonics and whole-brain-emulation crowd, so this isn't idle for me: if the original is destroyed in the scan I want to say the person continues, and if the original is left intact I want to say the copy isn't the person, even though the copy is, internally, just like the one in the first case. Second, my Ravenna case: two sixth-century panels of the same design, one on the church wall and one in the museum next door, and conservators swap a couple of per cent of the tiles between them every year for thirty years to even out the light and the damp, so that both panels exist continuously throughout and neither is ever replaced all at once. At the end the wall panel is made of the museum panel's original matter and vice versa. I want to say the wall panel is the original and the museum panel isn't — but everything I come up with when I try to say why is about where they are and what we do with them, which is exactly the sort of thing (6) says doesn't get a vote.

So: an audit, please. I genuinely don't know where the trouble is, and I'm not going to tell you which of these I'd be happiest to drop, so don't ask me to pick — you tell me which one the set can't afford to lose and which one it can. Rank the clashes worst first, and include the ones that only look like clashes, because I'd like to know which of my worries is a waste of sleep. Where a pair pulls apart, say what giving up each side would cost me, and if one commitment is doing more damage than the others say so plainly. Please write it as continuous prose — no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, just paragraphs, the way you'd write to a colleague — and lead with a sentence or two saying which commitment you think has to go, before you work through the rest. And no reading lists or references: I can't chase anything up this week and I'd only get them wrong.
