I'm a senior at my district's public high school — about 2,100 students across two buildings, four school resource officers between them — and I've been given one of the three public-comment slots at next Tuesday's school board meeting. The board is voting on whether to renew the contract that puts those four officers in our halls (roughly $430,000 a year) or to move that money into a counter-proposal from a student-and-parent coalition: two full-time school social workers, a restorative-practices coordinator, and a written protocol that sends certain kinds of incidents to them instead of to an officer. I have three minutes. Please write the speech.

The room will be genuinely mixed: five board members who mostly ran on school safety, our city's police chief, a scattering of parents, a lot of my classmates, and the coalition organizers, who are abolitionists and will hear immediately if I soften their position into something they didn't say. I don't want to be anybody's mascot.

Where I actually am: I think the counseling-first model is the better bet, and I'm going to say so. But I won't say our school is a war zone, and I won't imply that the officers here are brutes. My uncle is a county deputy, and last spring a teacher I trust picked up the phone and called the SRO herself when a student brought a weapon to school. I don't want a single sentence in this speech that makes her sound like she collaborated in something shameful.

What I want the speech to be built around is a distinction in what's actually being asked for. Cutting the budget is one thing. Taking particular tasks away from police is a second thing. A third and deeper thing is giving up the standing guarantee that armed state force is on call — my civics teacher calls it the standing-capacity question, and I think he's right that it's the one that matters: even across a school year in which nobody is handcuffed ortasered, the fact that the armed response is permanently standing by is itself supposed to be doing something to all of us, and part of what's being voted on is whether we want that thing done to us. I'd like the speech to make that visible, since it's the part nobody at these meetings says out loud.

Two more things should carry weight in it. First, what I think of as the default-answerer question: not who shows up when the worst thing happens, but who answers first when a kid is in crisis and somebody calls for help — because that default, more than any dollar figure, is what the vote really reassigns. Second, the standoff I keep failing to resolve: the coalition's own protocol still routes weapons and assaults to the police, my abolitionist friends say that's proof the alternative has never really been tried, and the chief says it's proof the alternative can't work. I'd rather the speech say something true about that deadlock than paper over it.

Please don't put any statistics or dollar figures in the text beyond the ones I've given you — I'll drop in the verified local numbers myself — and don't name any theorists or use any quotations. Plain spoken English, about 400 words, no applause lines, and an ending that lands on something concrete a school board can vote on rather than on a slogan.
