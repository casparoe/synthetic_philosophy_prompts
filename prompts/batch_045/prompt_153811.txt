I'm retired now -- materials science, mostly spectroscopy, ran a small lab for about thirty years -- and for the last year or so I've been sitting in on a philosophy reading group at the university. First philosophy I've done since a compulsory "science and its methods" course in the seventies, which was mostly about falsification and not much else. So please tell me if I'm reinventing the wheel.

Here's the thing I keep noticing about if-then sentences, and it's not the thing people usually make a fuss about.

First instance. In group meeting someone says: if the sample's contaminated, we'll see a peak at twelve minutes. I'd have put about 0.8 on that. Then we test for contamination and it comes back positive. My 0.8 on that sentence does not move. It doesn't go to one, it doesn't go anywhere. Compare: my confidence that the sample is contaminated goes straight to one when the test comes back. Every ordinary factual sentence I can think of gets settled, one way or t'other, by whatever settles the matter it's about. But the conditional seems numb to exactly the news you'd think mattered most to it.

Second instance. When the contamination gets ruled out, nobody in the lab says "good, your prediction came out true." We say the prediction is moot, the run it was about never happened. But nor does anyone retract it -- I'd still insist I'd been right about what would have happened. So refuting the antecedent doesn't confirm the conditional either, the way the truth-table treatment I was taught as an undergraduate says it should. Blind in both directions: confirming the antecedent doesn't raise the number, refuting it doesn't send it to one.

The only way I can make sense of my own 0.8 is as a procedure rather than a state of the world: suppose the sample is contaminated, then judge how much confidence the peak gets. Which makes me think both halves come from the same place -- once you've supposed the antecedent, there's nothing left for the news about the antecedent to do.

So, what I'm after:

Does this have a name, and is it one phenomenon or two that happen to travel together? Someone in the group said the "suppose it and then judge" move has a name going back to the 1920s, and separately that if-then sentences might not express propositions at all -- but I can't tell whether either of those is what I've stumbled on, or a different claim in the neighbourhood. The refuting-the-antecedent half at least feels like something everybody already has a word for; the confirming-the-antecedent half feels less talked about.

And edges, please, since a name alone won't satisfy me. Does the pattern separate the indicative case from the subjunctive one (if the pump *had* failed, the run would have been lost -- I can't do the same "learn the antecedent" move there, because it's already settled)? What about 'if' doing duty for 'whenever' -- if the temperature crosses forty degrees the valve opens -- which does seem to get confirmed by testing? Is the pattern about the word or about one use of the word?

One more thing: a retired logician in the group told me there's a famous result, Lewis I think, showing that the probability of an if-then can't be the probability of any proposition, on pain of everything coming out probabilistically independent. Is that the same thing I've noticed, or a much stronger claim that my two observations don't get near? I half-suspect I've confused myself somewhere here.

Which brings me to what I want from you. Push back if the observation is muddled, or if the two halves really are unrelated and I've stitched them together, or if learning the antecedent would in fact move the number on the serious accounts. Say plainly if there's no established concept and give me a term with a definition and the boundary cases if so. Flag how confident you are in each part, and don't invent references -- no reading list, no citations, I'll chase those myself; if you're not sure a result is correctly attributed, just say you're not sure.
