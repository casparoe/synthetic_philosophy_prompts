I am assembling the handout for the second half of my philosophy of mind module, on the original-versus-derived intentionality dispute, and I want the centerpiece to be a dialogue that runs the dispute on a case neither Searle nor Dennett ever had to face, so that it cannot quietly degenerate into another rehearsal of the Chinese Room argument. The students will already have read both sides, so the characters can allude to the standard moves without explaining them, but I do not want those moves restaged: the system at issue is not a clerk with a rulebook, and if either character starts trading on syntax-versus-semantics I will have to cut the piece. Call the characters Miriam and Tobias, both senior, both having read everything the other has written, neither impressed by the other and neither contemptible; set them wherever you like — a workshop hotel bar the night before they are supposed to deliver a joint one-paragraph statement to the organizers, say, which they are failing to write.

Here is the case I want them to fight over. A control system of the kind that now runs agricultural robots, call it Halcyon-4, was produced by an automated pipeline that generated its own training objective: the objective was drawn from a distribution of randomly generated objectives and retained only because lineages trained on it happened to persist, so no human ever chose what the system was for, and the people who built the pipeline never inspected its internal states or assigned them any reading. The lab was wound up, the records were lost, and nothing survives that could serve as the ascription from which the system's states might borrow their aboutness. Years later an independent team reverse-engineers Halcyon-4 and finds a population of internal states that track distal features of the robot's environment, are consumed downstream in the selection of action, and — this is the part I care about — sometimes misfire in systematic, diagnosable ways, so that there is a real distinction inside the system between getting things right and getting them wrong that is not simply a matter of what any manual says. On top of that, Halcyon-4 runs a self-modifying training loop in deployment, so what its states now do has drifted some distance from anything the original training could plausibly be said to have designed them to do. Finally, give them the twin case: an atom-for-atom physical duplicate of Halcyon-4 assembled by a chance physical process and switched on tomorrow, locally identical in causal powers, differing only in how it came to exist. Miriam's question to Tobias is who, exactly, is doing the deriving in Halcyon-4's case, given that nobody ever interpreted anything; Tobias's question to Miriam is what work the word intrinsic is doing that a story about provenance would not do better, and whether she really wants to say the duplicate means things too.

What I want from the exchange is genuine back-and-forth rather than two well-rehearsed monologues. Miriam should get to press the point that derived intentionality requires someone who derives it, and that a system's capacity to be wrong about a feature seems to demand a standard that is not fixed by whatever we find it convenient to read into it. Tobias should get to press the point that design needn't come from a mind — that selection and self-training are design-fixing processes too — and that Miriam's intrinsicness is a placeholder for a stopping point rather than an account of one. But each must also answer the other's strongest version: Tobias has to say where his view draws the line between a system that is really about something and one we merely find it useful to interpret as being about something, and I would like Miriam to point out that the two-bitser style of argument cuts against him as well as for him; Miriam has to say what in Halcyon-4's physical constitution could make its states about anything at all, and whether she is willing to extend that to the duplicate. Let each of them concede one thing that genuinely costs them something, and let the dialogue end with them isolating a single disagreement that is precise enough that neither can dismiss it as merely verbal — and, if it fits, one claim they unexpectedly agree on. Around a thousand to fourteen hundred words, stage directions kept to a minimum, and written so that a good second-year can follow it without a glossary.
