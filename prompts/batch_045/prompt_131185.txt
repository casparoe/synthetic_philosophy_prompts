You are a philosopher of mind working at the intersection of conceptual analysis and cognitive neuroscience. Please work through the following problem in two stages. First, write a short, deliberately unstructured brainstorming paragraph in which you map the conceptual territory, flag any point where you think the question is ill-posed or where my stipulations are underdescribed, and note which distinctions will end up doing the real work. Second, give a polished, self-contained explanation that a graduate seminar could follow without any outside reading. Define every technical term before you rely on it.

Background, to be assumed rather than defended. Ned Block distinguishes access consciousness from phenomenal consciousness. Roughly: a state is access-conscious when its content is poised for use in reasoning and for the rational control of action and speech, in the sense that the content is available as a premise to a wide range of downstream consumer systems. A state is phenomenally conscious when there is something it is like for the subject to be in it. Global workspace theory, in the style of Baars and in the global neuronal workspace model developed by Dehaene and colleagues, describes an architecture of many specialized, relatively encapsulated peripheral processors that compete for entry into a limited-capacity workspace; when a coalition wins, its content is globally broadcast, via long-range workspace neurons, to a wide array of consumer systems, and this broadcasting event, often called ignition, is what the theory identifies with consciousness.

It is frequently said, and equally frequently disputed, that this architecture is really a theory of access consciousness and not of phenomenal consciousness. The usual way of pressing the point is the overflow argument, which appeals to putatively rich phenomenology that outruns what a subject can report or use. I want you to set that dialectic aside and instead reason from three imagined architectures, which I will stipulate as empirically possible within the general functional framework:

Case one, sealed broadcast. Ignition occurs. The winning coalition's content is transmitted along all the normal workspace output pathways, and the transmission produces its normal metabolic and electrophysiological signature, so an experimenter can tell with certainty that a broadcast has taken place. But uptake in every downstream consumer has been pharmacologically blocked. Nothing anywhere in the system can actually use the content: no report, no memory consolidation of the usual kind, no deliberate action, no reasoning.

Case two, private lines. Two specialized modules have acquired, through extensive training, a dense reciprocal bundle of direct connections. Rich content is exchanged between them, and that exchange drives fine-grained, flexible, apparently purposeful behavior and even a specialized output channel functionally resembling reporting. Nothing is broadcast: there is no ignition, no workspace involvement, and no other system can draw on the content.

Case three, sharded workspace. Following surgical disconnection, an organism has two partially independent workspace hubs, each with its own long-range projections and its own downstream consumers, with only occasional and unreliable cross-talk between the hubs.

Your task: explain what the distinction between access and phenomenal consciousness predicts, or fails to predict, about each case, and what global workspace theory predicts about each. Then answer the harder question. Is global workspace theory best understood as a theory of access consciousness, as a theory of phenomenal consciousness, or as a theory of a third functional property, namely global broadcast itself, that can in principle come apart from both? If it is the third, what argument could show that global broadcast is the property in virtue of which a state is phenomenally conscious, rather than merely the most efficient way for a nervous system to make a state access-conscious? And relatedly, if report is our only evidence about phenomenology and report itself seems to require access, can the access/phenomenal distinction do any independent work in adjudicating these cases, or does it collapse into the very thing global workspace theory is supposed to explain?

Aim for roughly 700 to 900 words in the polished section. Do not hedge symmetrically across all options: commit to the most defensible reading and say what would have to be true for a rival reading to win.
