Something came up in my flat the other night and I've been chewing on it ever since, partly for selfish reasons: I'm about to ask three people for reference letters of my own and now I'm not sure what I'm even asking them for.

Here's the argument. Ana applied for a research-heavy job and asked Dev, who supervised her master's, to write her a reference. He said yes. The letter he wrote was perfectly nice — praised her teaching, said she was reliable and conscientious, all of it true. But Ana later found out from someone else that Dev had told another colleague in the department that she "wasn't ready for that kind of role yet," and that the letter said nothing at all about research, which is most of what the job was about.

Ana: You wrote a letter of support for a job you didn't think I should get. That's dishonest.
Dev: I didn't write a single false sentence. Everything in there is true and I'd stand behind every line.
Ana: That's not the point. Leaving out the research stuff *in a letter for a research job* says something. You let them draw a conclusion you knew was wrong.
Dev: Then draw it. Nothing in the letter says you're strong on research. If they inferred that, they inferred it — I didn't say it.

Mei, who's done some linguistics, jumped in: Dev, that's textbook Grice. You implicated that she's weak on research by staying silent about it in a context where the whole point of the letter is research. Speaker meaning isn't the same as sentence meaning. It's a kind of deception even though every sentence is true.

Dev: OK, but Grice's whole story has the hearer working it out — they're meant to notice the gap and infer something. This committee read the thing in four minutes and took it at face value. If nobody actually drew the inference, what did I implicate, and to whom?
Mei: Implicature is about what the speaker means, not about what lands. Otherwise whether you misled someone depends on how careless your audience happened to be.
Dev: That makes it unfalsifiable. On your account I can implicate literally anything to anyone — I can "implicate" that I think the moon is made of cheese, as long as I privately intended it, and no one can ever check. A notion of communication that floats completely free of uptake isn't communication.
Mei: Uptake matters for whether you succeeded, not for whether you tried. You aimed to leave them with a certain impression without writing it down. That's exactly what implicature is for.
Dev: I aimed at nothing. I wrote the honest letter I could write and moved on with my week.

Ana then said that even granting that, he had a duty either to say the research thing or to decline to write. Dev said declining would itself have communicated something — she'd have had to explain to the committee why her supervisor wasn't writing — so there was no neutral option available to him.

And then there's the bit that I can't stop thinking about. Everyone in their subfield knows that "reliable" and "conscientious" in a letter are what you write when you can't praise the person's research. But the committee at the place Ana applied was five people, mostly outside the subfield, and one of them is a former colleague of Dev's who definitely is in on the code. So the letter seems to have conveyed one thing to one reader and nothing to the other four.

Ana: If it conveyed it to one person, you communicated it — and you were gambling on who happened to read it.
Dev: I can't be responsible for which of five readers happens to know the code. And if the code is what's doing the work, it isn't my utterance that's deceptive, it's the profession's shabby conventions.
Mei: But if the only thing generating the inference is a private in-group convention, then it isn't really the kind of thing Grice's machinery was built for — that stuff is supposed to be worked out from the maxims plus context the hearer can be assumed to have, not from a wink shared by three people.

I sat there saying nothing because I honestly coludn't tell who was winning. Please go through it move by move: for each point someone made, say whether it's right, partly right, or wrong, and why — I want the verdict on the individual moves, not just an overall winner. Then tell me who's more right overall, whether the in-group code business should change the verdict or is a distraction, and what, if anything, all three of them are missing.
