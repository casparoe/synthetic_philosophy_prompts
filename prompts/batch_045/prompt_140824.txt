I'm on the committee of a housing co-op (about 40 members) and we're trying to fix how we make two decisions each year: (1) which of five projects gets funded — roof insulation, solar panels, a new bike shed, the garden, or redoing the common room — and (2) what share of the annual budget goes to it, anywhere from 0 to 60%. Right now we argue until everyone is tired and then vote by show of hands between whichever two options are still standing, and everybody hates it.

Someone dropped a blog post in the group chat — you know the genre, "maths proves every voting system is broken" — to argue that there's no point redesigning anything. The paragraph that keeps getting quoted at me runs like this:

"Gibbard and Satterthwaite proved in the 1970s that no voting system can be made immune to tactical voting. Being strategy-proof means that no voter can ever obtain an outcome they prefer by reporting a ranking other than their true one. Their theorem says that with three or more options, every voting rule is either a dictatorship — one voter's favourite always wins — or it is manipulable: there will always be some voter, somewhere, who would have done better by lying. So tactical voting isn't a flaw of plurality, or instant-runoff, or Borda in particular; it's a mathematical feature of voting as such. That rules out the usual reform proposals as well, including approval and score voting, since those are just rankings with extra ties allowed. And no amount of clever design gets around it: Gibbard himself extended the result to randomized rules in a later paper, so drawing a ballot out of a hat is just a dictatorship with a coin toss. Since manipulation is unavoidable in every system, no system beats any other on this dimension, and the honest thing is to stop chasing impossible criteria like strategy-proofness and just use the simplest rule people understand."

I don't know enough to say where this is fair and where it's overselling, and I suspect it's being used to shut down a conversation rather than start one. Three things I'm specifically stuck on:

- A friend who did some economics said the "no clever design gets around it" bit quietly leaves out an assumption, and that when everyone's preferences have a single peak along a line, taking the middle voter's favourite point is immune to strategic voting. Which assumption is she talking about, and is she right? More importantly: would our budget-share vote actually satisfy it? I can imagine people here who'd rather spend nothing or spend a lot on a proper job but not something half-done, which sounds like it has two peaks rather than one, and I don't know whether that kills it.
- For the five-project decision, somebody proposed picking a name out of a hat and funding that member's top project. That looks strategy-proof to me — how would lying help if the winner is a random person's favourite? — but the blog guy says it's covered by the theorem and is "just a dictatorship with a coin toss." Is either of us right? Does it depend on how people feel about risk?
- Does it matter at all that one of our two decisions is a number on a line rather than a choice among five named things, or does the theorem hit both the same way?

So: could you go through that paragraph and tell me what a social choice theorist would accept as basically right, what's a defensible simplification, what's just wrong or a conflation, and whether the "no system beats any other, so don't bother" conclusion survives once the mistakes are fixed. Please don't give me a reading list or paper titles, I have no access to a library — just tell me in your own words what the theorem does and doesn't say, and what it would actually imply for how we should run these two votes.
