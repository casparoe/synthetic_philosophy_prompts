ok so I need to stop reading the Harsanyi aggregation literature at random and get a spec first. Here's the use case: I'm on a small team putting together a decision-support tool for a regional public-investment body — the kind of thing that has to rank flood defences vs vaccination campaigns vs subsidised housing, where the benefits are spread over a couple of million people and most of the action is in the uncertainty (flood probabilities, take-up rates, long-run effects nobody can measure well). Whatever rule for combining individual utilities into a social ranking we put in the code has to be defensible to people who will never read a paper.

What I want from you is the desiderata, not a restatement of the theorem: what a rule for turning many individuals' cardinal utility functions — and maybe their beliefs — into one social ranking has to get right, the case or consideration behind each requirement, and your view on which are non-negotiable and which are just what one camp happens to like. Please don't just hand back the axioms of the aggregation theorem dressed up as a list.

Things I already think are on the list, with the cases behind them — add your own, and tell me if any of mine are junk or duplicates:

- Splitting invariance. Divide a state of the world into two states with identical utility consequences and probabilites summing to the original, and the social ranking shouldn't move. Is that a real requirement or just expected-utility independence smuggled in as a fairness constraint? It looks like it kills any rule that cares about who ends up badly off once the uncertainty resolves, which is most of what practitioners say they want.
- No double counting. Real preferences have moral and other-regarding bits in them: everyone prefers the flood defence go to the poorer valley because they think that's fairer. Add those up and the fairness concern seems to get counted once per person, while a concern nobody else shares gets counted once. What should a rule do with preferences that are already partly social — launder them somehow, or take them as given?
- Invariance to rescaling versus sensitivity to interpersonal comparisons. Each person's utility is only fixed up to positive affine transformation, so anything that turns on an arbitrary normalisation is out. But full invariance looks like it makes real comparability impossible and lands you near dictatorship or near social indifference. Which way does this cut, and is demanding a common unit of well-being a genuine requirement or a prejudice of the welfarist camp?
- Disagreement about probabilities. Our experts and our citizens don't share a credence over flood risk. Whose probabilities does the rule use, or does it manage not to take a stand? Ive seen claims that respecting everyone's ex ante preferences here forces something pretty unwelcome — say what that is and whether it's avoidable or just a cost to be paid.
- Graceful degradation on non-ideal inputs. What we actually get is not vNM-rational: people violate independence in Allais-ish ways, some are intransitive, future people and corporate actors don't have a well-defined utility function at all. A rule that only runs on clean inputs is useless to me, so I'd put robustness to messy and partial input on the list — unless you think that's asking for something no rule could deliver, in which case say so and tell me what the honest fallback is.
- Variable population and near-duplicates. People barely affected by the decision, or two people with identical prospects and identical utility functions: does the rule average or total, and what does it say about adding lives that are barely worth living? How much weight does this requirement carry compared to the others, given the decisions we're actually making?
- Stability over time. The body decides every year, and sometimes re-decides. If it aggregates and then updates versus updates and then aggregates, do we get reversed rankings or something money-pumpable? That's on my list only if it genuinely bites.
- Estimability. Can it be run on quantities a public body could actually estimate in a few months, or does it need full interpersonal comparisons nobody can measure? I count implementability as a real desideratum — tell me if that's cheating, i.e. if it's a constraint on us rather than a requirement on the theory.

Two more things, and they're the parts I most want. First: flag any pair of requirements that can't be jointly satisfied, and if the whole list turns out to be unsatisfiable as a package, tell me which requirement you'd drop first and what dropping it costs — that's a result, not a failure. Second: which single requirement does the most work in knocking out candidate rules? A short scorecard at the end for the main contenders (weighted or average utilitarian sums, ex-post prioritarian views, maximin, and the simulate-a-chooser-behind-a-veil-of-ignorance approach) is welcome, but keep it short — the list and the reasoning behind it are the deliverable.
