The notes:

§1 Setup. Bankroll W_0; each round you pick a fraction f of current wealth to put on a favorable bet with iid return R_t, so W_n = W_0 ∏(1 + f R_t) and the growth rate is g(f) = E[log(1 + f R)]. Kelly is f* = argmax g. Two features I want to lean on. (i) Log wealth is additive across rounds, so the objective doesn't depend on the horizon — you never need to know n in advance, and the myopic choice is the long-run choice. (ii) The asymptotic dominance argument: log(W_n^{f*}/W_n^f) = n(g(f*) − g(f)) + noise of order √n, so for every f ≠ f* the Kelly bettor is ahead by a factor going to infinity with probability → 1; and since this holds for all the alternative fractions at once, with probability one the Kelly bettor eventually dominates every other fixed-fraction strategy.

§2 The scale-invariance point. Everything in §1 is stated in ratios: W → cW changes nothing. So geometric rationality is structurally blind to any question of the form "will I ever have enough to buy the house / fund the clinic / retire", where the goal is an absolute level rather than a multiple. Those questions are about the probability of crossing a threshold and about the expected time until you cross it, and neither objective is scale-invariant. So the slogan "geometric rationality is what you should do if you care about the long run" quietly equivocates between a multiplicative long run (ratios, growing like e^{ng}) and an additive one (getting to a level).

§3 Three objectives that I think people run together. (A) maximize the almost-sure asymptotic growth rate; (B) maximize the probability of hitting an absolute target T before ruin; (C) minimize the expected time to hit T. Claim: A is not even approximately optimal for B or C, and the gap doesn't shrink as the target gets more ambitious — it gets worse, because the optimal policy for B depends on how far T is above where you are and on how many rounds you plausibly have left, whereas A hands you a single fraction that is independent of both. And here is the step I'm relying on: since log W_n is approximately normal with mean log W_0 + n·g and sd σ√n, its median is about exp(log W_0 + n·g), so maximizing g maximizes median final wealth — and the same formula, P(W_n > T) = 1 − Φ((log T − log W_0 − n·g)/(σ√n)), shows it also maximizes the probability of clearing any fixed absolute target, since that expression is increasing in g. Hence the positive proposal: what I'm calling 'target-anchored geometric rationality' is maximize g(f) subject to E[τ_T] ≤ H, where τ_T is the hitting time of your target and H is your remaining number of rounds. A pure growth-rate maximizer passes any threshold with probability approaching 1, but can be beaten, at every finite horizon, by agents much more likely to have passed it by then; and n = ∞ is not a place anybody lives.

§4 Why any of this matters for my actual topic, moral compromise. Take two parties with different utility functions u_1, u_2 over a shared set of joint outcomes, who have to pick one policy for a long sequence of decisions (a funding body making grants year after year, say). Treat them as holding stakes in one shared bankroll and read the compromise as maximizing E[log u_1] + E[log u_2], i.e. the expected log of the geometric mean. Then: (a) it's additive over rounds exactly like log wealth, so it's dynamically consistent — no reason to renegotiate halfway through; (b) it's invariant to positive rescaling of each u_i but not to translation, so you need an anchor, and the anchor is the no-agreement fallback, playing precisely the role that current bankroll plays for the Kelly bettor — it's what turns ratios into something with a unit; (c) the gains from trade have a clean second-order form, because E[log X] ≈ log E[X] − Var(X)/(2(E[X])²). Alternating (each party gets its way half the time) gives each party a high mean but a wildly dispersed per-round evaluation; compromising gives each party something middling every round. For fixed mean, log prefers low dispersion, so the value of compromise is a variance-reduction term, and it's larger the more anti-correlated the parties' per-round evaluations are. That, I think, is what "gains from trade through moral compromise" actually consists of formally: you are buying variance reduction in log space.

§5 The bit I'm sure is soft. Log aggregation is fine for wealth because wealth compounds — winnings get reinvested. It isn't obvious that moral value compounds: one saved life doesn't obviously enlarge your capacity to save the next one. So the shared "bankroll" in §4 has to be something like a repeated relationship or an institutional capacity, and I'm not sure I can make that concrete. Also, taking the log of a utility function is dimensionally weird unless the zero point is fixed by something.
