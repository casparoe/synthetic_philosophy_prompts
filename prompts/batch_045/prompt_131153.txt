Two researchers I work with have spent a year circling each other on a single question: whether the simulation argument should dampen fanaticism about the far future. They have agreed to an adversarial collaboration, and I want the document that a well-run collaboration between them would actually produce.

Take the two positions as positions in an argument, not as portraits of anyone. The Stakes-First party holds that, because the conditional payoff of favourably shaping the long-run future is astronomically large, expected-value reasoning mandates prioritising that aim even when the probability of success is small; it holds further that taking the simulation hypothesis seriously leaves this conclusion essentially intact. The Simulation-Discount party holds that, conditional on our being in a simulation, the expected decision-relevant stakes of our actions collapse by many orders of magnitude, and that this collapse is large enough to break the dominance argument and restore the priority of robust near-term goods.

The simulation argument I have in mind is the familiar trilemma: either very few civilizations at something like our stage reach the capacity to run large numbers of detailed simulations of their own history, or almost none of those that do choose to run them, or we are almost certainly inside one. Everything below should be argument and definition rather than reportage: I do not want a survey of the literature, a bibliography, or citations, and where a substantive premise is needed you should state and defend it rather than attribute it.

What I want, in order.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims that both parties could sign. These must not be vacuous: a claim such as both sides want to do good is worthless, whereas a claim about the disjunctive structure of the trilemma, about what would have to be true for a simulation to be resource-cheap enough to be common, or about the moral irrelevance of substrate in the absence of further argument, is the right kind of thing. Include at least one claim that each side initially resisted and was talked into. Give a working definition of fanaticism both can accept, or, if they cannot agree on one, register that in the fourth section instead of papering over it. Finally, fix the formal quantity at issue: define the dampening factor as the expected decision-relevant stakes of a representative action conditional on the simulation hypothesis, divided by the same quantity conditional on its denial, and have each party commit to a numerical range for it and say what evidence would move that range.

Second, a table of residual disagreements, with these columns: a neutral statement of the issue; the formulation of its own view that the Stakes-First party would sign; the formulation of its own view that the Simulation-Discount party would sign; the crux whose resolution would move at least one side; and each side's prediction about that crux, written down in advance. I would rather have sharp, contestable formulations than hedged ones.

Third, a cruxes section. At minimum treat the following, and for each give a jointly accepted argument, case, or test that both agree bears on it, together with each side's pre-registered prediction. The horizon question: whether, conditional on the simulation hypothesis, our expected remaining runtime is bounded by the purpose for which simulations are run, whether an off-switch prior of that kind is itself supported or undermined by applying the same reasoning to base reality, and what each side predicts we would find. The control question: whether, conditional on the simulation hypothesis, our capacity to alter long-run outcomes is drastically reduced because the operators and the termination conditions dominate the trajectory. The contamination question: whether the evidence base for long-run forecasts survives conditional on the simulation hypothesis, how this depends on the fidelity of the simulation to whatever base physics there is, and what the discovery of resource-saving shortcuts in fundamental physics or cosmology would show. The dominance-by-the-base-reality-branch argument, according to which even a modest credence in not being simulated, multiplied by astronomical stakes, still swamps any finite near-term consideration: say whether it is decisive, and say plainly whether the Simulation-Discount party's answer to it secretly depends on a general anti-fanaticism it would hold anyway, in which case the simulation apparatus is doing no independent work at that crux. The replica question: if we are simulated we are plausibly one of many relevantly similar simulations, so does that multiply the decision-relevant stakes, and on what does the answer depend — correlation among the choices of counterparts, and whether the decision theory in play lets an actual choice bear evidentially or functionally on copies it cannot causally reach; note explicitly if the two sides' verdicts here would flip between causal, evidential, and functional decision theories. The export question: whether the possibility that simulated minds are copied into base reality restores astronomical stakes conditional on the simulation hypothesis, and how probable each side takes it to be. The nesting question: if simulations can contain simulations, most simulated minds sit at deep levels, so does the dampening compound without limit, converge to a fixed point, or get absorbed into the prior. And the moral-status question: whether the case for fully counting simulated minds succeeds, whether anything remains of the thought that the interests of simulators could outweigh them, and what both would treat as evidence — for instance the discovery that our own world already contains minds that originated in base reality.

Fourth, a short audit separating genuine from verbal disagreement. Say which parts of the dispute, if any, are about words — about the definition of fanaticism, about whether our actions affect the far future means causal influence or evidential bearing, about bounded versus unbounded value — and which parts are substantive. Where a crux turns out to be the general dispute about fanaticism wearing a simulation costume, say so and say how much of the collaboration's remaining work survives that diagnosis.

Fifth, a conditional joint recommendation and change-of-mind commitments: the specific empirical or argumentative findings that would move the Stakes-First party to shift weight toward near-term work, and those that would move the Simulation-Discount party back toward long-run work, stated concretely enough to be checkable after the fact. If you think the honest upshot of the collaboration is a verdict on some crux rather than a stand-off, give the verdict, say which side loses it, and say why.

Aim for roughly fourteen hundred to eighteen hundred words, dense rather than introductory.
