I am a senior at a public high school and the editor-in-chief of our student literary magazine, which the administration told us last week it will stop funding at the end of this year. I am giving a five-minute closing speech at the release party for our final issue. In the room will be the faculty advisor who has run the magazine for twenty-two years, about a dozen staff members who have been arguing about this for weeks, parents, and the assistant principal who delivered the news.

I want the speech organized around the dollar auction game, in which a single dollar is auctioned off, the highest bidder wins the dollar, and the second-highest bidder also pays their bid and receives nothing. I want it used as the honest explanation of why we are folding rather than funding another year ourselves, and of why the fact that we have already put so much into this is not by itself a reason to continue.

Two complications should be handled rather than smoothed over. First, the plain sunk-cost reading of the auction does not quite fit our case: three years of work changed what we actually care about, so continuing would be motivated partly by who we have become and not merely by what we have spent. I have been calling this the difference between sunk cost and sunk identity, and the speech should use it to say both that the magazine was worth having been part of and that it is not worth continuing, without the two claims canceling out. Second, consider a variant in which each bidder is offered a one-time option, available only while their current bid is still below fifty cents, to withdraw by paying a dime and owing nothing further; the speech should work out in plain terms why a bidder ought to seriously consider taking that option at the point when it still feels unnecessary, and should connect that to our situation.

Please write the speech in American English, at roughly five minutes when read aloud. It must be intelligible and respectful to the parents, the assistant principal, and the advisor without condescending to the staff, and it must contain no sarcasm directed at the administration and no suggestion that the work we did was wasted.
