Wittgenstein's central point is that our concepts cannot be defined. Consider game: there is no single feature common to board games, card games, ball games, and solitary games like patience — only overlapping similarities, family resemblances, criss-crossing in every direction. The same is true of language, and in principle of every concept we possess. What a word means is the way it is used, and use is governed by rules; that is why Wittgenstein speaks of language games. Speaking a language is like making moves in chess: the rules settle in advance which moves are legal and which are not, and a move that breaks a rule is simply not part of the game. This is also why a private language is impossible. Suppose you are the last surviving speaker of some language, keeping a diary in it in the certain knowledge that no one will ever read it. Correctness of usage requires a community able to check it, so your diary marks would not mean anything, and there would be no sense in which you were thinking in that language. Finally, rules are embedded in forms of life — that is, in cultures — and standards of correctness can therefore differ wholesale between cultures. So if scholars a thousand years from now dug up that diary, translating it would not merely be hard; it would be impossible in principle.
