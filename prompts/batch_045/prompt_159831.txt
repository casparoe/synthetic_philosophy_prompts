hey — ive drifted into holding a fairly strong version of outside-view primacy about forecasting, and I want someone to tell me how exposed it actually is before I build more on top of it. Call the view base-rate-first (BRF). Its moving parts, as I'd defend them:

1. When the inside view and the outside view disagree, the tie goes to the outside view, because the inside view's errors are systematic — overconfidence, planning-fallacy optimism, narrative coherence masquerading as probability — while the outside view's errors are mostly noise around a decent mean.
2. The right shape for combining them: reference-class distribution as the prior, inside view as a likelihood that moves the center and barely touches the tails. The inside view can tell you that you're in the top quartile of your class; it can't tell you that the class's 18-month median doesn't apply to you.
3. Epistemic modesty here is bookkeeping, not a virtue. You defer to a process with a better calibration record, not to a wise person, and you drop the deference the moment the record stops being better.
4. The reference class gets chosen by causal similarity, screened off from effort: you're not allowed to sort yourself into a class whose base rate already encodes the very thing you think your own effort or skill can move.

Tell me what would count against this. Not the generic skeptical stuff, but the defeaters with teeth. Things I've half-thought through and want you to take further or knock down:

- Shared-mode failure. If everyone inside a reference class is wrong for the same reason, averaging doesn't rescue you — the distribution's tails are understated exactly where it matters. Does this defeat BRF or just tell you to widen the tails while keeping the machinery?
- Self-consuming base rates. Base rates that get published change the population that generates them, because the agents being predicted respond to them. How bad is this, and does it argue for treating published base rates as decayed?
- Laundering. A lot of reference classes were themselves assembled by insiders doing inside-view reasoning with the case details filed off. If so, "outside view" is sometimes just someone else's inside view, and the deference in (3) has no object.
- Reference-class drift: the class you'd have picked before seeing the outcome isn't the class you pick after, and I don't have a principled pre-observation rule that fixes it.
- The awkward cases where the inside view has genuinely privileged access: you've already poured the foundation, you know your own team's attrition, the bridge is half built.

Also: what empirical results should move me (no citations, just the shape of the finding and roughly how big it would have to be); which of these defeaters has arguably already happened; which ones a committed BRF person would concede count against them versus dismiss as a misuse of the view; and what BRF predicts that a real inside-view-first rival doesn't — if the answer is "nothing," say so, because that's a defeater too.

And be honest about whether (4) rescues the view into unfalsifiability. It looks like an all-purpose escape hatch: any failure becomes "wrong reference class." I want to know if that insulation is real or if it costs me something.

Two personal notes so you can calibrate: the shared-mode-failure point is the one that currently moves me most, and the familiar regress worry — that picking a reference class is itself an exercise of judgement, so the outside view is just a higher-level inside view — leaves me completely cold. If I'm wrong to be cold about that, argue me out of it.
