Two philosophers who have spent years arguing past each other about the new riddle of induction have agreed to an adversarial collaboration. Your task is to draft the joint statement they could both sign.

Side A, the projectibility-first side, holds that Goodman's grue case isolates something that no purely formal theory of confirmation can capture: inductive inference requires a substantive constraint on which predicates may be projected, and the reply that simply assigns a low prior to the hypothesis that all emeralds are grue has only relocated the question into the prior, where it is no easier to answer without appeal to naturalness, causal structure, or entrenchment.

Side B, the prior-first side, holds that there is no distinct new riddle: the grue hypothesis is a perfectly coherent member of the hypothesis space, confirmation is a matter of probabilistic coherence plus conditionalization, and the whole phenomenon is accounted for by the priors agents actually have together with ordinary considerations of simplicity and informativeness. On this view entrenchment is just a name for a feature of priors, and the residual question of whether any prior is rationally mandatory is a general question about rationality rather than a special difficulty about induction.

Both sides must also address two variants they have never discussed. First, the law-grue variant, in which the gerrymandered term is not an observation predicate but occurs inside a theoretical law: a physical constant takes value α₁ before a time T and α₂ afterwards. Both accept in advance that a few such time-sliced laws are scientifically serious while the vast majority are not, and they must say what does the work in separating them. Second, the symmetric-entrenchment case: a community whose basic colour vocabulary is time-indexed, with grue and bleen primitive and green and blue defined in terms of them, so that, by stipulation, entrenchment is exactly symmetric between the two rival hypotheses.

Produce: (i) a numbered section of claims both can sign, including claims about what the grue case does and does not show, about whether the puzzle is one about language, metaphysics, or rationality, and about whether the new riddle is independent of Hume's old riddle; (ii) a disagreements table in which each residual disagreement appears twice, once in wording side A would accept for its own position and once in wording side B would accept for its own; (iii) the cruxes, the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side, and for each crux the case, argument, or piece of evidence both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. If you find that some part of their dispute is merely verbal, say which part and why, and keep it out of the table.
