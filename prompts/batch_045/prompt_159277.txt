I am writing to ask for something quite specific, and I would rather explain the situation than have you guess at it. I spent thirty-five years as an experimental condensed-matter physicist, mostly on phase transitions, and I retired four years ago. Since then I have been reading philosophy of science in a fairly disciplined way, and I let myself be persuaded to join the refereeing panel of an interdisciplinary journal that publishes on complex systems. Roughly a dozen manuscripts a year reach me in which the word emergent is doing real argumentative work: it is the reason the paper is supposed to be interesting, the reason a derivation is said to be unnecessary, or the reason a modelling assumption is said to be safe. My recommendations on these have not been consistent. Last spring I recommended acceptance of a paper on stop-and-go waves in traffic and rejection of one on collective motion in flocks, and when the handling editor — a professional philosopher — asked me what distinguished them, I found that what I had written amounted to an intuition about which authors were being careful. She was entitled to ask, and I would like to answer her properly the next time.

What I want from you is not an essay about emergence. It is a procedure I can run on a manuscript, in the order given, and defend to an editor. Before I say what it has to do, let me tell you the provisional scheme I have been carrying around, because I would like you to build on it and also to tell me plainly which parts of it are confused. I have been using three tests. First, what I call the substitution test: if you alter the micro-level constituents or the micro-level dynamics substantially, does the macro-level regularity survive unchanged? My instinct, from my own field, is that survival under this kind of variation is the real content of emergence claims, and that a macro regularity which dissolves the moment the details change is a summary of the parts and not a finding about them. Second, what I call the two-way prediction test: how well can one predict the macro state from the micro state, and how well can one predict the micro state from the macro state? Where the downward direction is systematically degenerate — many micro arrangements compatible with one macro state, and nothing at the micro level selecting among them — I have been inclined to say the macro level is autonomous. Third, what I call the derivation debt: a paper that claims emergence ought to say what a completed derivation from the micro level would consist of, which bridge assumptions and which limiting or idealising steps it would require, and how far the present paper is from providing one. A claim with no stated debt has always struck me as promissory.

I am not confident about any of this. I cannot tell whether robustness under substitution is the same property as autonomy or merely evidence for it; I cannot tell whether my second test is a claim about the world or about the notation we happen to use; and I suspect the third conflates our being finite with nature's being inexhaustible, which are not the same thing and which I would want kept apart if they can be.

So here is what I need. Give me a decision procedure, in the order the questions are to be asked, that starts from what a manuscript actually contains and not from what a well-read author might have said elsewhere. For each question say what would count as a sufficient answer at the level of evidence papers in this area really provide, and say where the procedure terminates in a negative verdict — the point at which I am entitled to write that the emergence claim is unsupported and needs no rebuttal, rather than being asked to refute it. Mark separately the places where the procedure must stop and hand the decision to me, because what is at issue is not a fact about the system but a decision about what the journal should mean by the word, and I should not pretend that such a decision was delivered by the method. Two classes of case worry me especially and must be handled rather than set aside: manuscripts in which the macro state is said to act back on the parts, so that the causal arrow is claimed to run downwards; and manuscripts, most of them from biology and neuroscience, where the micro level is not fully characterised or is itself contested, so that the substitution test cannot literally be run. That second situation is nothing like the one I was trained in, and I do not want a procedure that quietly presupposes a finished micro theory.

Then I want the procedure applied, in full and without abbreviation, to three manuscripts of a kind I actually receive. One reports stop-and-go waves in highway traffic, which appear without any driver intending them and propagate backwards through the flow. One is from my own former field and argues that the universality of critical exponents — the fact that systems with entirely different microphysics share exponents and scaling functions — is a case of emergence, and I should say at once that this is the example I have long regarded as the paradigm, so I am not a neutral reader of it. One concerns a decentralised collective, a robot swarm or an ant colony, and attributes to the collective a goal-directedness or a form of agency that no individual unit has; this is the kind of paper I instinctively dismiss as anthropomorphism, and I expect to dislike whatever the procedure tells me about it. I would rather be told I am wrong than have my instincts confirmed by flattery, so please let the procedure give its verdict and let me argue with it afterwards.

Finally, tell me in plain terms where the procedure will misfire: which kinds of system it will systematically over-credit or under-credit, which kinds of author it will reward for rhetoric, and which questions it cannot settle even in principle. I have about an hour per manuscript, I am not a philosopher, and the person who receives my recommendation is one; my output is a single paragraph saying reject, revise, or accept, with reasons the editor can check.

Two requests about form and content. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose in the manner of a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables; if the steps must be numbered, number them inside the running text. Aim for somewhere between a thousand and fourteen hundred words. And do not give me references, reading lists, or a survey of the literature by name — I can find that myself, and what I cannot find for myself is exactly what I am asking you for.
