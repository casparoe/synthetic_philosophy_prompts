I help run a reading group that's half linguistics, half cognitive science, plus one philosopher who won't shut up about innateness, and we have been going round on the poverty of the stimulus for about two years now. Every move on both sides is familier to everyone in the room, nobody has moved anybody, and it isn't for lack of arguments. I want a diagnosis of the stalemate rather than a verdict, and no reading lists please — we've read them.

The shape of it: our nativist says the child converges on a grammar wildly underdetermined by what it hears. The input is fragmentary and full of false starts, nobody reliably corrects children or labels sentences as ungrammatical, and still by five they all end up in roughly the same place, including on things nobody modelled for them. The stock example is contraction across a trace: you can say who do you wanna see but not who do you wanna leave, and the claim is that nothing in what a child hears, and no correction anyone gives, could have taught them that contraction is blocked in the second case. Our anti-nativist says this is an argument from ignorance in a lab coat: the input is far richer and more structured than the nativist pretends, statistical learners with a structured hypothesis space do fine, and anyway the whole argument depends on first stipulating that the target of acquisition is a uniform adult-like competence stated over abstract rules — pick a more usage-shaped, probabilistic target and the gap that needs explaining shrinks to almost nothing.

My hypothesis is that it's three arguments sharing a name, which is why it never moves. One is quasi-formal: what class of grammars is learnable from positive evidence alone by what class of learners. One is empirical: what is actually in the input, and how much of the explanatory work do domain-general inductive biases do. One is definitional: whether innate means genetically encoded rules, or canalised development, or species-typical, or just not learned from the input in the sense at issue — because on that last reading basically everyone is a nativist and the debate is over before it starts. Tell me whether that's the right diagnosis and, if not, what's actually doing the work.

Here's the part I really want you to engage with, because I've been trying to design a test and I can't tell if it's any good. Call it the matched learner test: train a system on nothing but a corpus matched in size, kind and noisiness to what a four-year-old has actually heard — same transcripts, no tags, no annotations, no negative evidence, no corrections — and then test it the way the nativists test children, on held-out structures it couldn't have memorised, with the pass condition being not just right answers but child-shaped errors and no structure-independent ones. Say it passes. Our nativist has a move: wrong architecture, its built-in biases are doing the work, it has seen more words than a child, and passing a judgement task isn't acquiring a grammar. Say it fails. Our anti-nativist has a move: wrong architecture, wrong objective, wrong evaluation, and one system failing proves nothing about the space of possible learners. So is the debate stalled because no outcome would count as evidence for either side, or because I've designed the test badly?

And a second thing, which I suspect is where the real question has gone. If the honest version of the dispute is how much of the work is done by the data and how much by the learner's biases, then it looks quantitative, and I keep wanting to decompose it the way you'd decompose variance: hold the learner fixed and vary the input, hold the input fixed and vary the learner. But I suspect that decomposition isn't well defined, since how much a bias contributes depends on what data it's applied to — the interaction terms are the whole story, not a nuisance term. Is that right, and if it is, what would count as progress on a question you can't measure that way? A formal result about which classes of learners acquire which classes of grammar from which classes of input? Dropping the word innate and asking only about the strength and shape of inductive biases? Changing the question to something like how much input a given learner needs? Or is there a residue here that is just bedrock intuition about how surprising children are, where no progress should be expected and the honest answer is to say so?
