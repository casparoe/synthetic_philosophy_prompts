My supervisor is writing a paper on why possible-worlds style models of knowledge make agents logically omniscient, and for the last few weeks she's been ruling on cases at the whiteboard: the subject clearly knows P, Q follows from what she knows, does the subject count as knowing Q? I photograph the board after each meeting and I want to predict her verdict on the next case before she writes it up. Her rulings so far:

- knows P, knows P→Q, has never bothered to put the two together, one modus ponens, nothing blocking → YES
- knows the axioms, Q has a 300-line proof she's never seen and the proof runs through a lemma from a subject she's never studied → NO
- knows P, Q is just P-or-not-P in the same vocabulary → YES
- knows P, Q is a logical truth stated using a concept she has never acquired → NO
- knows the premises, Q takes six easy steps she could run through in a minute but hasn't → YES

Next week's case: Dana went through 5,000 instrument readings one at a time over the past month and verified each one. She now knows of each reading that it is verified, and she still has the list. She has never aggregated them, there's no summary line anywhere and no tool that will do it for her, and getting to all 5,000 are verified would mean working back through the list for the better part of a day. Does Dana know that all 5,000 are verified?

I can see three rules that seem to fit those five verdicts: a cost rule (closure holds only up to what is cheap for her given the inference routines she actually has), a content rule (closure holds whenever the route to Q needs no input beyond logic, definitions and things she already believes, however long that route is), and a would-she-assent-if-you-asked-her rule. I'm not even sure all three really survive the five verdicts, and they pretty clearly come apart on Dana. Give me your best guess at her verdict with the reasoning, say which rule you think she's actually running and why you prefer it, and tell me which further case would separate the ones you can't distinguish yet — I was waffling between varying the number of readings (five instead of 5,000) and swapping in a case where the step is one she makes constantly but the conclusion carries content that isn't already implicit in what she believes. Please don't just tell me it depends on the theory of knowledge; commit to a verdict and a rule. Answer in American English, and keep it reasonably short.
