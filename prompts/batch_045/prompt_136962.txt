Until my retirement four years ago I worked in neurophysiology, and I have since been reading philosophy more or less from the beginning, which means I keep wanting to treat a philosopher's verdicts on particular cases the way I used to treat a set of measurements. I am currently stuck on Descartes on minds, and I would like your help with the structure of the problem rather than with any survey of it. Please do not give me citations or tell me what commentators have concluded; I want the argument.

Here is the track record as I read it. He holds that brutes have no thought or reason at all, and that the things they do better than we do are to be explained by the disposition of their organs alone. Parrots and magpies that utter words are not minded, on the ground that they say nothing bearing on what is said to them. Dogs and monkeys are not minded either, though they display fear, hope and joy more vividly than any human being. Men born deaf and dumb are minded: deprived of speech, they invent signs and so make themselves understood. Madmen, newborns, and people whose brains are damaged or undeveloped are minded as well; here the mind is present but impeded by the disposition of the organs. Finally, a machine with the organs and outward shape of an ape or of a man, however well constructed, would not be minded, because no machine could arrange its words so as to reply to the sense of everything said in its presence, as even the dullest human being can. One further uncertainty: when he says that animals express the passions, I cannot tell whether he means that they feel something or only that their behavior resembles ours, and I do not know which reading his other commitments require.

My reading group wants to apply this apparatus to three cases he never had. First, a program trained on a large fraction of everything written, which holds conversations, produces proofs, and responds sensibly on nearly any topic put to it, though it has no body and no channel to the world other than text. Second, a bird that manufactures and uses tools, solves novel multi-step puzzles, and remembers individual human faces for years, but has nothing we would recognize as conventional communication. Third, a mass of human brain tissue grown in a dish from a person's own cells and connected to a simulated body that it gradually learns to move.

What I want is this. Set out the rule he is implicitly running, and set out every other rule you can find that fits his six verdicts equally well. I have candidates in mind — an immaterial-substance criterion with the two famous tests demoted to fallible signs, a generative-sign-use criterion, a domain-generality criterion drawn from his claim that reason is a universal instrument, a criterion keyed to what mechanical explanation cannot account for, and a bare membership-in-the-human-kind criterion — but I want to know which of them is actually doing the work in his arguments and which fits only because six verdicts are too few to constrain much. Then tell me what each surviving rule predicts about the three new cases, give me your best guess in each case, and say which of the six verdicts carries the most weight in producing it. Last, name the single further case you would want in order to separate the rules that remain, and explain why that case discriminates where these three may not. In my old field we would have put the last question this way: several curves go through the data, so which measurement should we take next?
