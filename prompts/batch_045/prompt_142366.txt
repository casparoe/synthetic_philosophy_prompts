I am trying to work out what I think about cognitive penetration of perception, going case by case, and my verdicts will not line up. Here they are as I actually have them:

1. A radiologist who, after years of training, sees the faint lesion that a junior scans straight past. Her expertise changes what she sees — she really does see something new — and the experience still gives her a reason to think the lesion is there.
2. A birdwatcher who cannot pick a trill out of the background until a companion says "hear the pine warbler?", at which point it jumps out and stays out; an hour later, told it was a different species, the trill collapses back into the background. Penetration, and reversible.
3. An audio engineer tells me my speakers have a dip at 2 kHz. I hear the dip immediately, and I still hear it after he admits he invented it. Experience is genuinely altered, but I do not think it gives me any reason to believe anything about the speakers.
4. Hikers with heavy packs report that the hill ahead looks steeper. I say the hill does not look any different; they just expect the climb to cost more.
5. Someone afraid of heights on a balcony: the drop looks deeper than it does to her calm companion. Here I am inclined to allow that the experience itself is altered — but I deny that it gives her any reason to think the drop is deeper.
6. A taster told the same wine costs $90 rather than $10 says it tastes different. I do not take that report at face value; what changed is how much he likes it, not how it looks or tastes.
7. Someone who has been told and fully believes that the two shafts in the arrow-fins illusion are equal in length: the illusion does not weaken for her. The belief does not get in.
8. Someone wearing goggles that invert the visual field left-to-right: after two weeks the world looks normal again. I want to count this as penetration too, although what is doing the work looks more like recalibration than like anything belief-shaped.
9. When the soundtrack of a video says one syllable and the visible mouth movements say another, viewers report a third. A real perceptual effect, but nothing cognitive seems to be involved, and I do not know whether to count it.

The principle I keep coming back to: a top-down influence genuinely penetrates perception only when it is a learned expectation about the distal cause of the sensory input, and the resulting experience carries justificatory force only when that expectation is a trustworthy guide to that cause, rather than to the subject's needs, fears, or preferences.

Test that against my nine cases, repair it where it breaks, and tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon — above all the asymmetry between 1 and 5, and between 3 and 7. Does case 8 show that "cognitive" is the wrong word, or that my question was never really about cognition? And give the repaired principle's verdicts on two cases I have not listed: a reader of a colleague's damning email about a rival who now sees the rival's neutral expression as hostile, and a patient given a placebo who reports that her pain genuinely hurts less.
