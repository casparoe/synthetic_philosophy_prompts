Hey — odd one here. I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background past one logic class where I mostly drew truth tables, and I got pulled into a comment-thread argument a while back about whether math is discovered or invented. Someone there dropped the phrase "Benacerraf's dilemma" and it's been rattling around my head since. As I understood it: our best account of what mathematical sentences *mean* says they're about abstract objects, so "3 is prime" is true because of how that object is — but abstract objects don't do anything to us causally, so it's hard to see how we ever got reliable beliefs about them. Nail down the semantics and the epistemology falls apart; nail down the epistemology and the semantics falls apart. (There's also an earlier bit I half-remember about how basically any sequence with the right shape could play the role of the numbers, so nothing in what we say picks out which objects we're talking about. That one sounds to me like the gap between a program's behavior and the infinite number of ways you could implement it — nothing in the behavior tells you which implementation it "really" is. I may be mangling it, correct me if so.)

Anyway. My book club at work — we do short stories, it's mostly an excuse to drink beer — just did Borges's "The Library of Babel", and it clicked for me in a way none of the blog posts did. The setup: an enormous or endless set of hexagonal rooms, every possible book of some fixed length is in there somewhere, nearly all of them gibberish, librarians spending whole lives searching, some hunting a catalogue, some hoping there's one particular man somewhere who has read the book that justifies all the others. Total plenitude, and the plenitude is what's horrifying rather than what saves you.

I want to read that story through this Benacerraf thing, seriously in both directions, and I want to know where the reading breaks. Some things I'd want covered:

- The obvious move: a library that contains every possible book looks like the plenitudinous answer to the dilemma — there can't be an access problem for a domain that includes every consistent possibility, because there's nothing to get wrong. Does the story actually dramatize that as a *solution*, or does it quietly destroy it? Because knowing every book is in there doesn't tell any librarian which one to believe, and the librarians go mad, form sects, smash the ones they think are junk. Is that a genuine objection to the plenitude move — that it converts Benacerraf's access problem into a "which one?" problem that's just as bad — or is it a cheap shot, since the librarians' difficulty is about meaning and interpretation and not about reference at all?
- Where in the story would you actually point? Don't need exact wording, I only remember the broad shapes: the narrator saying something like how the impious think gibberish is the rule and anything coherent is a near-miraculous exception, the searches for the vindications/catalogue, the guy who goes looking for the one man who's read everything, the weird hope at the very end. What would you say about those moments with this lens on?
- Where does the lens distort Borges? I suspect the honest answer is "a fair amount" — the Library is made of matter, the despair reads as religious or existential, and Borges is arguably doing something about language, infinity, and whether the universe contains a book that names you, not about whether we're in causal contact with the number 3. I'd rather be told the reading fails than have it smoothed over.
- And if the story adds something the argument alone doesn't — some cost of the plenitude picture that's easier to feel in fiction than to state in a paper — I want that. My hunch is plenitude looked like a clean fix when I read it in argument form and completly like a nightmare when I read Borges, and I can't tell if that's a philosophical point or just a mood.
