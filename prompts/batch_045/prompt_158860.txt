Write me an essay on whether debate can actually do anything about obfuscated arguments. I've been going back and forth on this and I want you to weigh both sides properly and then actually land somewhere — don't give me a "it depends" shrug at the end, defend a verdict, and tell me what would change your mind.

Setup, so we're on the same page: the scalable-oversight proposal where you pit two AI debaters against each other arguing opposite conclusions in front of a weaker judge, maybe with rounds of cross-examination, and the hope is the judge extracts truth from the competition even when it couldn't check the answer itself. The failure mode I care about is the one where the dishonest debater's best move isn't to sound confident or to lie loudly, it's to produce an argument with one bad step buried among many steps that each look individually fine, so that auditing the argument costs about as much as producing it. Whether debate helps in that regime is the whole question.

Here's the taxonomy I've been using to think about it, and part of what I want from the essay is a judgement on whether the taxonomy is load-bearing or whether it collapses in a way I haven't noticed. Say an argument has N steps, checking any single step costs one unit of judge-effort, and the judge's budget B is much smaller than N.

1. Localizable commission errors: the bad step can be found for cost much less than N, e.g. because you can test whether the prefix of a chain is sound and binary-search your way to the break. Here the honest debater seems to have a real weapon: they can make a narrow, refutable claim ("step 43 is where it goes wrong"), which forces the liar into a dilemma — concede and the chain breaks, or defend it and the judge spends one unit of budget and catches you. Cheap for the honest side, expensive for the liar.
2. Non-localizable commission errors: there's no cheap prefix test, so finding the needle costs order N. Pointing at a specific step is now as expensive as auditing everything, and the liar's dilemma evaporates because the honest debater probably can't name the right step.
3. Errors of omission / framing: nothing in the argument is locally wrong. The move is that a needed step, definition, baseline, or counterfactual was never stated. There is no step 43 to point at. The honest debater has to *add* structure rather than *attack* it, and the protocol gives them no obvious privilege for doing so.

The thing that makes me suspicious of debate is that the arguments I've seen for it seem to secretly work only on type 1, and type 1 is exactly the kind of error that's overrepresented in the tasks we benchmark on — math proofs, code review, factual lookup, anything with a checkable chain structure. The errors that worry me at the frontier are type 3: a memo that quietly picks a discount rate, an argument that never defines its key term, a plan that's valid at every step and still beside the point.

But I also think there's a case for the other side that I haven't been able to kill, and I want you to make it as strongly as you can before you rule on it:

- The recursion objection and its limit. Any objection the honest debater raises is itself an argument, which the liar can obfuscate in turn, and so on. Does the honest advantage decay with the number of rounds, stay constant, or grow? Is there a clean way to say where the regress bottoms out — does it bottom out in the judge's own sense of what a plausible argument looks like, which is precisely the thing a competent liar is optimizing against?
- The asymmetry assumption. The usual argument for debate assumes it's cheaper to *find* a flaw than to *build* a flawless argument. But at the level of a single step, producing plausible-sounding nonsense is famously cheap while checking it is expensive, so the per-step asymmetry might favor the liar; the honest debater's edge would then come only from the aggregate requirement that all N steps be sound. Is that aggregate requirement enough on its own, or does it smuggle back in the localizability assumption?
- Cheap-talk worries. If both debaters are equally capable and the liar can mimic the distribution of objections the honest debater produces, is there an equilibrium where the judge learns nothing at all? If not, what exactly breaks the symmetry — something about truth being self-consistent, or something about the liar having to maintain a whole consistent fiction under pressure?
- Does the honest debater need to be able to *reconstruct* the liar's argument in order to attack it, and if so does that mean debate costs roughly as much as the original audit anyway?

Please also say something concrete about what an experiment would have to show to settle this. Like, if you ran debate on tasks where you can control which of the three error types is present, what pattern of results would vindicate debate as a load-bearing technique and what pattern would show it's basically audit wearing a costume. And if you think my three-way split is missing a fourth kind of error that changes the answer, say so and argue for it.

Aim for something like 1200-1800 words, prose not bulletpoints, and feel free to use toy numbers or little formal sketches where they actually buy something. Recieve the verdict loud and clear, please — I can handle being told I'm wrong.
