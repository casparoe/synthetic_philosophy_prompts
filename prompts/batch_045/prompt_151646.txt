I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about the statue and the clay, and I came away convinced that the standard dialectic rests on an unexamined assumption. Her position: there is one thing in the studio; "statue" and "lump of clay" are two sortals we can apply to it; the literature on coincident objects is a pseudo-problem generated by the fact that English lets us sort one thing several ways; and the readymade proves her point, because there the artwork is either just the object under a further sortal or an abstract type with instances. My position: the readymade is precisely the case that breaks her story, and it breaks it in a way that reorders the rest of the dialectic.

I have written the argument up as a short essay for a general philosophical readership — roughly 2,000 words, a blog piece rather than a journal article — and I would like comments before I post it. The draft follows.

Sixteen Pounds: what the readymade does to the statue and the clay

On Monday a sculptor shapes a lump of clay, Lumpl, into a statue, David. On Tuesday David is squashed into a ball. Intuitively the lump survives Tuesday and the statue does not; since one and the same thing cannot both survive and fail to survive a single event, Lumpl is not David; and during Monday the two coincide, with Lumpl constituting David. The dialectic branches from there. The identity view rejects the intuition and says that the lump is the statue under another sortal. The constitution view accepts coincidence and owes us an account of what makes it the case that the lump constitutes the statue rather than the statue the lump — the grounding problem — and of how two coincident things can differ in their properties. The eliminativist says that there is no statue at all, only clay arranged statuewise. And the whole business gets its urgency from the version run on persons, where coincidence threatens to leave us with too many thinkers.

What I want to isolate is a feature of the standard case. In it the difference between the two objects runs in one direction only: there is something the lump survives that the statue does not, and, as the story is normally told, nothing the statue survives that the lump does not. A one-directional difference invites a ranking. The lump is the permissive object, the statue the demanding one; the permissive one is the matter, the demanding one is what has been made from it. Nearly every explanation of why constitution runs one way rather than the other leans on that ranking, even when it does not say so. Readymades let me break the ranking, because the persistence conditions of a work are fixed by the practice that makes it a work — the artist's act and the institution's maintenance of it — and not by how its matter is arranged. So they can be made to run in whatever direction the practice fixes.

Suppose that in 1971 an artist takes an unmodified lump of clay from a supplier's bin — no cutting, no shaping, no addition; nothing intrinsic to it changes — signs a certificate identifying the piece as an unmodified lump of clay weighing sixteen pounds, titles it Sixteen Pounds, and exhibits it. Suppose the certificate also provides that the piece is to be maintained at that weight and in that material, and that a conservator may replace decayed clay with fresh clay of the same kind as the lump ages. Beginning in 1996 the lump crumbles, and over two years a conservator replaces it piece by piece, until by 1998 none of the 1971 clay is present. Everyone in the practice — artist, conservator, catalogue, insurer — continues to call the thing in the gallery Sixteen Pounds and to treat it as the same work. In 2003 a curator, finding it dull, rolls it into a ball and uses it as a doorstop. The museum declares the work destroyed and has a fresh lump made under the certificate.

Two features of the case matter. First: in 1971 a new object is supposed to have come into existence — the work — although nothing intrinsic to the lump changed. Whatever distinguishes Sixteen Pounds from a mere lump of clay is not a difference in how the matter is arranged, because there is none. So the coincidence of the work with the lump is not grounded in form imposed on matter, and the eliminativist's formula fails here in a way it does not obviously fail for David. In 1971 nothing was arranged statuewise or any other way; so if there is no work present, then the signing, the cataloguing, the insuring and the conserving are not tracking anything at all. Second: compare what each coincident object can survive. The lump survives being rolled into a ball; the work does not, since the work is essentially an unmodified lump and the practice treats reshaping as destruction. The work survives the piecewise replacement of all of its clay; the lump, on the usual assumption that a quantity of clay cannot survive the total replacement of its clay, does not. The asymmetry crosses.

If the crossing holds, the ranking is gone. Neither coincident object is the permissive one, so no account that reads the direction of constitution off relative modal permissiveness can say whether the lump constitutes the work or the work the lump. What is left to do the grounding is the difference in kinds: one object's kind is a matter kind; the other's is a historical kind whose identity conditions are fixed by a human act and by the practice that maintains it. And the ranking is not merely unavailable here but unavailable as a general account, because what the second kind's conditions are is exactly what the practice can vary: the certificate might have forbidden substitution altogether, or permitted reshaping within a weight limit, or required the original clay to be retained.

Suppose instead that the lump can after all survive gradual total replacement of its clay, so that the crossing collapses and we are back to the ordinary one-directional case. Then the modal argument does its usual work and the ranking is restored. But the first feature is untouched: a coincidence still began in 1971 with no intrinsic change whatsoever. So the existence and direction of the coincidence are fixed by the same social facts on either branch. That is the claim I want to defend: for artifacts, constitution is grounded in the kind, the relevant kinds are historical and institutional, and so the number of objects in the room is in these cases settled by what the practice does rather than by how the matter is.

My friend's fallback is that the work is an abstract type, so there is no coincidence to explain: the lump is an instance, the work is not located in the gallery, and the 1998 episode produced a new instance of an unchanged type. I think this is coherent but expensive. It makes it false that the thing one could have touched in 1997 was the work, and it turns reports of damage and destruction into reports about instances, which is not what conservators, catalogues or courts say. She also says my certificate is a device for stipulating verdicts rather than a case that generates them from the theories. I say the opposite: the certificate is only an ordinary institutional fact of the sort that fixes what any artifact is, and the verdicts fall out of the kind it creates together with the usual persistence conditions for lumps.

What I would like from you:

1. Accuracy of the map. Have I described the identity view, the constitution view and the eliminativist position fairly? In particular: is the "ranking" story I attack something anyone actually holds, or do constitution theorists already ground the direction of constitution in primary kinds, in which case my second section is attacking a position of my own invention?
2. Generated or stipulated? Does the crossing in my case really follow from the kind plus ordinary lump persistence conditions, or am I smuggling in verdicts through the certificate? Concretely: is it non-arbitrary that the work survives piecewise replacement but not reshaping? And tell me what happens if I delete the 1998 conservation episode altogether — does the case collapse back into the ordinary statue-and-clay case, and does anything of my argument survive the deletion?
3. Which branch should I commit to? Is the claim that a lump of clay cannot survive gradual total replacement of its clay defensible, or is my friend right that lumps are tolerant of matter replacement? If she is right, is the second branch enough to carry the essay, or do I need a different second feature?
4. The type-and-instance reply. How damaging is it really? Is there a version of the case that survives it — a non-art artifact with institutionally fixed identity conditions, say, or a work whose certificate forbids re-fabrication — or is the type reply simply the correct answer about readymades, in which case I should say so?
5. Overgeneration. If a signature can bring a coincident object into existence, why does not every naming, dedication, or official designation do the same? Where should I draw the line, and is the line I draw going to be a line the constitution theorist already needs for independent reasons?
6. Persons. If constitution among artifacts is grounded in institutional kinds, does that insulate the person-and-animal case from the too-many-thinkers worry, on the grounds that person is not an institutional kind in the relevant sense? Or does it make things worse? I would like two or three sentences on this that I could hand to my friend directly.
7. Whether it is worth posting. Is the argument interesting, or is the conclusion weaker than it looks? Which section is weakest, should I open with the case instead of with the dialectic, and what would you cut?
8. One paragraph, in her voice: the strongest reply you would make if you were defending her side of the argument last night.

Please do not spend your response on who I should cite or what I should read; I will handle references myself, and what I want from you is whether the argument works.
