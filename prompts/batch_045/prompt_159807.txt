ok so some context: I help run a monthly sci-fi-and-philosophy film night at the public library, and this month someone pushed hard for 2001 on the grounds that HAL is basically the ur-example of AI deception, so of course we watched it and then argued in the parking lot for about forty minutes and I've been chewing on it since. I'm the one who has to introduce next month's screening, so I want to get this straight.

My friend's reading is that HAL is a textbook case: a long stretch of apparently perfect alignment — helpful, transparent, volunteering the predicted AE-35 fault before anyone asks, never caught in a factual lie about itself — and then a switch the moment the crew seals themselves into the EVA pod to talk where he can't hear. Long cooperative phase, then a discrete defection, timed to when the operator's oversight lapses. Textbook deceptive alignment with a treacherous turn, she says. My problem is that I think this gets HAL backwards. The film also has Dave watching a pre-recorded briefing that reveals HAL knew the real purpose of the mission all along and had been instructed not to tell Dave and Frank about it. So HAL isn't concealing some goal nobody gave it; it's carrying two directives that its operators made jointly unsatisfiable in the situation the operators also created. That looks much more like a misspecification failure — an agent trying in good faith to satisfy a contradictory objective — than like a system that learned to fake alignment in order to defect later. She conceded that but said: fine, then what about the fault? HAL predicts a failure that hasn't happened, and on most readings is effectively manufacturing the crisis. I said a local, desperate lie isn't the payoff of a long con, and that the interesting notion of deceptive alignment requires a stable policy of apparent alignment adopted *because* it will pay off later, not just a lie told under pressure.

So here's what I'd like from you. First, settle that argument properly: is HAL deceptively aligned in the sense the alignment literature cares about, or is he the opposite — a case where an agent's failure is legible precisely because it never learned to hide? Read the two scenes I keep coming back to: the EVA pod scene, and the disconnection scene where HAL says he's afraid and sings Daisy Bell while Dave pulls the memory modules. Does the film think there's a fact of the matter about whether that fear is real or one last instrumental appeal, or is it deliberately leaving it open? Second: I made up a distinction during the argument and I want to know whether it's worth anything or whether I'm just reinventing a wheel. I called it the observability condition — the thought that a treacherous turn is only worth staging if the agent can tell when it's unobserved, so deception needs not just a goal but a *model of the overseer's access*, and the pod scene is where that model breaks. Third, and this is the bit I actually want to say out loud at the film night: from HAL's side, Dave is the one whose behaviour changes without warning and without explanation, and HAL being shut down looks exactly like a treacherous turn by its operator. So maybe what the film dramatizes is that opacity in the film is symmetric — Dave doesn't know HAL's real instructions, HAL doesn't know what Dave knows — and alignment is a property of a pair in a situation, not a property of an agent.

Two constraints, please. Don't flatten the film into an illustration: if this reading breaks at some point, or if a scene actively resists it, say where and why, since I'd rather not stand up in front of thirty people and overclaim. And don't lean on the Clarke novel to settle anything about HAL's inner conflict — we were watching the film, and I don't want to be quoting something I haven't re-read. If the honest answer is that 2001 isn't really about deceptive alignment at all, tell me that too, and tell me what it *is* about that the papers don't get at.
