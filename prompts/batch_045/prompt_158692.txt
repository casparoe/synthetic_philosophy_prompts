I teach a graduate seminar in formal epistemology and the philosophy of economics, and I am building one fifty-minute session around the common prior assumption. The point I want the session to land is one my students reliably miss: the negative results usually attributed to the common prior assumption are not consequences of it alone. Each of them carries a separate common-knowledge condition, and that condition does at least as much work as the assumption itself. I need a worked case that prises the two apart, and it has to be a case the students cannot wave off as a technical trick.

What I want you to build: a case in which the common prior assumption holds in full — two agents, one finite state space, one prior that both have and both know they both have, both perfect Bayesians who know each other to be perfect Bayesians, all private information partitional and generated by signals — and in which there is nevertheless a bet on the table that both agents strictly expect to win at the actual state.

The two results the case has to be measured against, stated so that nothing here depends on looking anything up:

1. Agreement: if two agents with a common prior have posteriors for an event that are common knowledge, those posteriors are equal.
2. No trade: if two agents with a common prior accept a zero-sum bet and it is common knowledge that they both accept it, they cannot both strictly expect to gain from it; with risk-neutral agents, both are indifferent. Your case must satisfy the common-prior antecedent while failing the common-knowledge antecedent, and the failure must be visible in the structure of the case rather than asserted.

Constraints:

- Realistic setting: two analysts at the same firm, a trading desk, an insurance syndicate, a forecasting tournament. Ordinary stakes, no science fiction, no perfect predictors.
- The bet is zero-sum, monetary, purely speculative, and small enough that risk-neutrality is a fair assumption; neither agent has any prior exposure to the event, so neither is hedging. No transaction costs, no differing attitudes to risk or to ambiguity, no unawareness, no differing state spaces.
- Each agent must know, at the actual state, what the other's posterior for the event is: they have compared numbers, and neither revises on hearing the other's. This is what will make the case look like a counterexample to the agreement result; part of your job is to show that it is not one.
- I would prefer both posteriors strictly between zero and one, so that neither agent is certain. If you cannot get that alongside the other constraints, say so explicitly and explain the obstruction.
- Small enough to project on a screen: a finite state space of under about a dozen states, with a non-uniform prior if that helps.
- The mechanism matters: the bet must be struck in a way that neither agent observes the other's acceptance before committing — simultaneous sealed orders crossed by a third party, an anonymous matching engine, a bookmaker who never reveals the other side. State the mechanism and say exactly what it screens off and what it does not.
- Do not stipulate verdicts. If you write that each expects to profit before you have computed the two conditional expectations from the prior and the partitions you have displayed, the case has failed.

Please give me the following sections, in this order, at these lengths:

1. Scenario — one paragraph, roughly 150 words: the setting, the event, how each agent came by their private information, the mechanism, and the price.
2. Formal setup — roughly 150 words: the states, the prior, both information partitions, the actual state, the event, and the bet with its price. Define the meet cell at the actual state as the smallest event containing it that is a union of cells of both partitions, and compute it.
3. Why both expect to gain — roughly 150 words: the two interim computations; the check that each agent knows the other's posterior and does not revise on learning it; the check that the disagreement and the mutual acceptance are mutual knowledge but not common knowledge.
4. Why neither result is contradicted — roughly 120 words: where precisely the common-knowledge antecedent fails for each of the two results, and why the event that both accept is not common knowledge at the actual state.
5. What is doing the work — roughly 120 words: name the single structural feature that generates the case, then remove it. Run the same numbers through with acceptance made public and iterated to common knowledge, and tell me whether the bet collapses, survives, or survives only until the conditioning reaches the meet cell, and at which step the change happens.
6. The strongest objection — roughly 150 words: how someone holding the strong reading, that a common prior by itself rules out mutually profitable speculation, would attack this case. Cover the fact that the gains hold only at the actual state, that each agent knows she expects to gain while neither knows that both do, and that in real settings it is prior-free adverse-selection or winner's-curse reasoning rather than the common prior that stops people betting. Tell me how much of this I should concede in the seminar and what the case still establishes.

No references, citations, or further reading are needed; I want the case and the reasoning only.
