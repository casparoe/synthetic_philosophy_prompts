I'm applying for a six-month fully funded independent research grant from a foundation that has previously funded work in this corner of the space, and I need the central document: a two-page proposal, around a thousand words, that a reviewer who already knows the area can read quickly and either get behind or reject on the merits.

Assume familiarity with the standard setup of moral trade: agents with partly divergent terminal values each perform actions the other values, so that both gain in expectation when the gains are measured under each party's own values; the surplus comes from differences in comparative advantage rather than from splitting the difference; and the practice is distinguished both from moral offsets and from ordinary bargaining. Please do not supply citations, a bibliography, or a literature review by name — I will fill those in myself, and I would rather the word count go to argument.

Here is the half-formed idea I want turned into a proposal. The treatments I know of evaluate a trade at a single time-slice: hold each party's values fixed, compute each party's expected gain under their own values, and ask whether the arrangement is positive-sum and enforceable. What I think has not been faced head-on is that trade is among the more efficient mechanisms there is for changing the values of the person who trades, and that this is not an implementation detail but a problem for the notion of gain that is doing all the work.

The mechanism I have in mind — call it unit capture — is this. To trade at all, a party has to represent what they care about in the units of the exchange: so much money per unit of outcome, so many hours per concession, so many votes per commitment. Do it once and very little follows. Do it repeatedly, in small denominations, and there is a familiar psychological story about what happens next: the good starts to be represented to the agent in the units of the exchange rate rather than in the units native to the value itself, and the numeraire's units systematically omit whatever it was about the good that made it worth trading for in the first place. My suspicion is that the small, repeated, community-internal trades that actually get proposed in practice are far more exposed to this than the large, dramatic, one-off trades between opposed camps that get used as the canonical examples — partly because the community norm against airing contested political questions in public pushes real proposals toward being private, informal, and small. If so, the attention has been aimed at the wrong end of the distribution, and the interesting question is not whether moral trade works in principle but which denominations of it are safe to repeat.

I want the proposal to do the following.

State the question so that it has a determinate answer. Not whether moral trade is a good idea, but something like: when a proposed trade predictably alters what one or both parties value, which temporal and counterfactual standpoint is authoritative for judging whether the trade was mutually beneficial, and what follows for which trades should be accepted? Say plainly what would count as an answer.

Explain why the existing positions leave it open, and in particular why the three obvious replies fail. First, that a change in what someone values is just a preference change and preference changes are evaluatively neutral. Second, that if you dislike the values you will have afterwards, you can simply fold that into the ex ante calculation at a discount rate. Third, that this is really an empirical psychological question about how often unit capture occurs. I think the second reply is where the real difficulty is, since choosing the standpoint from which the later values get weighted is itself the thing under dispute, and I would like that circularity made explicit rather than gestured at.

Give the method, month by month, for six months of one person's time, with no new empirical data collection: how the taxonomy of value-change mechanisms would be built, whether the argument needs a minimal formal model or whether stylized cases can carry it, which cases would be used as the stress tests, and what gets written at the end.

Set out the possible conclusions and say which considerations would decide between them. Here are the ones I can see: unit capture is rare or self-limiting, and the case for moral trade survives untouched; it is real but bites only on a specifiable subclass of values that are bound up with a party's practical identity, so the upshot is a restriction on what may be traded rather than on how much; it is pervasive, and the practice needs institutional redesign; or it is real and unavoidable, and the honest conclusion is that some trades should be refused even though they are positive-sum ex ante. There is also a thread I cannot settle and would like either developed or explicitly ruled out: whether, given that the parties share no common numeraire, there can be a cycle of individually acceptable trades that leaves every participant worse off by their own lights once the interactions between the trades or their effects on valuing are taken into account — an analogue of a money-pump argument, if one exists here.

Say what would count as the project having failed, with enough bite that the failure is checkable: for instance, if every case of unit capture turns out to collapse into either ordinary learning or ordinary exploitation that was already identifiable beforehand, so that nothing new is being described; or if the effects are so case-specific that no usable rule survives; or if the argument quietly requires cardinality or intertheoretic comparisons that the framework does not permit.

Be honest about scope. I do not want to promise a general theory of value change, or a treatment of personal identity over time, and I would like the proposal to say so and to say what it is borrowing from those debates as a fixed assumption.

Close with one paragraph on why a funder should pay for philosophy here rather than for a survey or a set of interviews, and on what the deliverable actually is for someone who has to decide, this month, whether to accept a trade that has been offered to them.

Tone: a working proposal, not a manifesto. If the core idea is broken, I would rather that appear in the proposal's own risk section than be quietly repaired, so please tell me in a short note after the proposal which part of it you think is weakest and why.
