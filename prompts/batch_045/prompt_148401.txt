Write a parody of a supermarket loyalty scheme's personalized "Year in Review" email — the cheerful, second-person, data-delighted genre of a year-end streaming recap — sent by a chain that has taken revealed preference theory completely literally. Give it a subject line, a greeting, four or five short sections with mock data visualizations, one sidebar, and fine print at the bottom; about 600–700 words. Invent a name for the programme, and include one short quoted line from its in-house "Preference Scientist."

The comedy should come from the programme applying the doctrine rigorously rather than from invented absurdity. Specifically, it should:

- Refuse to distinguish what you chose from what you prefer, treating your purchase history as constitutive of your preferences rather than as evidence of them — so your survey response saying you want to eat less cheese is itself logged as one more expression of your cheese preference, and your "stated preferences" section is quietly replaced by your transaction log.
- Reconstruct preference only relative to the feasible set, so the fine print concedes that when the cheap blend was delisted your "revealed" switch to the expensive one carries no information about your tastes — while the body of the email draws an enthusiastic conclusion from that very switch.
- Detect a violation of the weak axiom of revealed preference (you took A on a trip where B was affordable and B on a trip where A was affordable, prices unchanged) and respond by levying a small "inconsistency fee" — i.e. by running an actual money pump on you, described as a service.
- Be scrupulously ordinal in the footnotes: utility is only a labelling of rankings, so the programme can never say how much you prefer something or whether you got more satisfaction than another member — while the headline sections commit every cardinal and interpersonal-comparison sin the ordinalist turn was designed to forbid.
- Close the gap between preference and welfare by fiat, so buying for a sick neighbour, grabbing the nearest thing at the till, and shopping while exhausted all count identically as revelations of what you wanted.

A reader who knows the literature should come away having felt, without being lectured, the real objections: that choices reveal preferences only against a budget and a menu, that the reconstruction is "as if," and that a view which identifies preference with choice has no room left to call a choice a mistake. An undergraduate should be able to read the whole thing as straight corporate satire. Keep the voice deadpan and sincere throughout: no winking, no jargon explained, and no character allowed to say that any of this seems bad.
