Bit of background: I'm retired, spent about thirty-five years in solid-state chemistry and crystalography, and about a year ago I started sitting in on a philosophy reading group at the local continuing-education centre — partly out of boredom, partly because my daughter (a GP) got tired of me winning arguments by asserting things. I've now got it into my head to write a short piece for the group's newsletter setting out where I've landed on the question of which kinds are really out there in the world and which ones we more or less conjure up. Before I inflict it on anyone I'd like a straight audit of whether my views fit together, because I have an itch that something in the middle is doing work it isn't entitled to, and I can't put my finger on it.

Roughly where I've landed, in no particular order: (1) Some kinds are discovered rather than invented — that water is H2O and that gold behaves as it does because of its atomic number were things we found out, the classification would have been correct even if nobody had ever done it, and the microstructure is what explains the behaviour. (2) Money is the opposite sort of thing: nothing intrinsic makes a bit of paper or a number in a ledger into money, it's conferred status plus people actually going along with it, and if we all stopped tomorrow it wouldn't be money any more — and yet I also want to say that claims about money are perfectly objectively true or false, not just matters of opinion. (3) Race isn't a biological kind — human genetic variation is clinal, most of it sits within rather than between the folk categories, and I think the search for an essence there failed for good reasons — but race is nonetheless real, because being classified as a member of a race has enormous downstream causal effects on who gets the mortgage, who gets stopped by police, who gets the callback. (4) I've been using a test I half made up and rather like: call something a "ratchet kind" if acting on the classification makes the instances come to resemble the stereotype more and more over time, so that predictions using it get better rather than worse the more it's used — I think race is the paradigm case and I think money might be one too, and I like the test because it gives me a "real kind" verdict without needing an essence. (5) But I do not want to say that being a Taurus is a real kind, or that "witch" in a witch-hunting village was a real kind, even though plenty of people's behaviour is shaped by horoscopes and being accused of witchcraft had devastating effects on the accused — and I notice that both of those seem to pass the test in (3) and possibly the one in (4). (6) Gender I take to be socially constructed rather than a matter of intrinsic biology, but I also find myself wanting to say that a person's own word about their gender is close to authoritative, in a way that I would not say about, say, their own word about whether they're middle class — I'd feel crass telling someone they've got their own gender wrong. (7) Finally, I think we should probably stop using "race" as a variable in biomedical research, because it keeps dragging people back into thinking there's a biological essence, even though by (3) the kind is perfectly real.

What I want from you is an audit, not a lecture and not a reading list: take that set and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like they are, and where I'm quietly relying on a test in one place that I refuse to apply in another. Rank the real tensions by how bad they are rather than listing seven equal complaints, and for each one say what would have to give and what giving it would cost me elsewhere in the set. I'm not attached to (4) just because it's mine, and I don't have a favourite I'm trying to protect — I'd happily drop whichever item is cheapest to drop, I just want to know which one that is and what I lose by it.
