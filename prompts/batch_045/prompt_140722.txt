For the past eight months I've been the software-engineer member of my firm's Model Risk Committee — five people, two of them statisticians, one from compliance, a rotating business owner, and me, who mostly maintains the feature pipeline that feeds every model we put in front of customers. I took one logic course as an undergraduate and that is the extent of my philosophy. We meet again on the 14th, I've been asked to draft the decision memo, and I'd like a draft I could actually circulate.

The case is this. Our head of strategy wants a ninety-day pilot of a vendor system called Meridian, sold by a company called Halcyon Systems. Meridian does not ship a model; it enumerates a space of roughly a billion candidate pipelines — feature transforms, model families, hyperparameters — and returns whichever one scores best on a cross-validated holdout of our own historical data. The pitch uses the no-free-lunch theorems offensively. Their claim as I understand it: those results say no learner beats any other once you average over all possible problems, but we are not interested in all possible problems, we are interested in this firm's data; over that distribution Meridian is just a machine for finding what works, and it imports no assumptions. Our chief quant reads the same theorems the other way: they license no conclusion at all unless you first say which class of problems you are restricting to, and Meridian's search space, its scoring rule and its validation protocol are precisely its assumptions, only nobody wrote them down. Picking the pipeline that won on history is, she says, an appeal to the future resembling the past — the same circularity Hume complained about — with a billion extra steps in front of it. Halcyon's reply is that every model we already run, including the incumbent Meridian would replace, rests on exactly that circularity, so if the circularity disqualifies Meridian it disqualifies everything, and our charter's requirement that we approve no production model whose superiority cannot be justified turns out to be unsatisfiable. I don't think that last move is sound, but I can't say what's wrong with it. Reading about this on my own, I keep ending up with two claims that cannot both be doing the work each side wants: that no learner is better than any other, and that of course some learners are better than others on the problems we actually have.

Two further things the memo has to settle. First, our regulator expects each production model to carry a documented statement of assumptions and limitations, and Halcyon maintains that Meridian has none, because it is assumption-free. I need a position on what should count as an assumption for a system that searches rather than specifies — my own suspicion is that the search space, the objective, and the validation scheme are the assumptions, and that honest documentation for Meridian would describe a class of problems rather than a model, but I want that argued rather than asserted. Second, the commercial terms: Halcyon has offered to be paid only if Meridian beats the incumbent on the next two quarters of live data, with the bonus capped. One committee member says that settles it, since we would not be trusting the induction, only buying an option on it. Another says a contract that pays only on out-of-sample success is itself a wager that the world is regular, so the contract has not removed the assumption, it has relocated it into the finance department. I find that second remark clever and I want to know whether it is true.

So please draft the committee's decision memo. It should take a clear position — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with a concrete time frame and terms Halcyon could actually meet, and it should say what justified superiority ought to mean in our charter given that the no-free-lunch results are correct. Please engage the losing side at its strongest rather than restating the theorem: if you recommend conditions, give the quant's worry its best day, and if you recommend rejection, take seriously Halcyon's point that we are not interested in all possible worlds. Where the theorems are genuinely silent about what we should do on Monday, say so rather than stretching them; and where our own incumbent model is exposed to the same objection Meridian is, I'd rather find that out in the memo than from the business owner in the meeting.
