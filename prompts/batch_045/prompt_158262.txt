I'm in a third-year seminar on experimental philosophy and we did the side-effect effect last week. I built an argument to try to show that nothing can be learned about intentional action from the way ordinary people use the word, and my TA said it "proves too much" but wouldn't tell me where. I can't find the exit. Here it is, with the case written out so we're using the same version.

Case A (harm): A company chairman is told by the vice-president that a new profit-making program will also harm the environment. The chairman says he doesn't care about the environment at all, he only cares about profit. They run the program; the environment is harmed. Most people asked whether he harmed the environment intentionally say yes.

Case B (help): Same, except the vice-president says the program will help the environment, the chairman gives the same I-don't-care reply, and the environment is helped. Most people say no, he didn't help it intentionally.

My argument:

(1) Everything the subjects are told is held fixed across the two cases except the valence of the side effect: the agent's knowledge, his aim, his indifference, and the causal structure are the same. Yet intentionality attributions flip.
(2) When attributions of a predicate flip across cases that differ only in a moral respect, moral considerations are part of that predicate's application conditions. Call such a predicate moralized.
(3) So does intentionally is moralized.
(4) Call a predicate upstream-independent if competent speakers can settle whether it applies without settling whether the agent is blameworthy, so that a finding that it applies can count as a reason for a blame verdict rather than a mere restatement of one. If a predicate is moralized, it can't be upstream-independent: the moral verdict is doing the work, so the question did she do it intentionally has no answer prior to the question is she to blame.
(5) But does intentionally obviously is upstream-independent. Juries get the mental-element question as a seperate question from the verdict. And sentences like she pushed the door intentionally and there was nothing wrong with that, or he did it deliberately but he was entirely justified, are perfectly ordinary: there the intentionality finding isn't even evidence of blame.
(6) So the chain has to break somewhere.

The bit my TA called over-generation: the same shape of argument doesn't stop at intentionality. Here's a third case I made up myself.

Case C (convention): The vice-president says the program will also cause the office coffee machine to be left switched on all night, which is against a silly and widely ignored building rule. The chairman says he doesn't care about the coffee machine at all, he only cares about profit. The machine is left on. Suppose — I'm stipulating this for the sake of argument, I havent run it — that people say yes, he left it on intentionally, just as readily as in case A.

(7) If so, what's driving the asymmetry isn't moral valence but norm violation generally, and case C involves no moral failing at all.
(8) But then intentionality attributions come apart from blameworthiness in exactly the kind of case the moralized-concept story was supposed to explain, and I get the opposite conclusion from (3).

And the general version: run (1)-(4) on any folk predicate with a valence effect — knows, caused, decided, could have done otherwise — and either every folk concept collapses into a moral verdict, which can't be right, or (4) is where the mistake is.
