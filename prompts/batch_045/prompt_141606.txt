Last night I had a four-hour argument with an old friend from grad school — the kind that starts at dinner and ends after one in the morning with both people more convinced than when they began — about the doomsday argument: the thought that if you treat your birth rank among humans as a random draw from everyone who will ever live, you should conclude that the total is probably not enormously larger than the number already born (on the usual numbers, somewhere around sixty billion so far, a median at roughly one more doubling, and a ninety-five-percent upper bound in the neighbourhood of ten thousand years — with no date and no bang implied, only a collapse in the total). Her objection was the standard one: that the tilt toward doom is cancelled the moment you also weight each hypothesis by how many observers it contains, since a hypothesis with vastly more people in it is correspondingly more likely to contain you, and that the price of that move is that a priori reasoning alone starts telling you the world is crowded. I had no reply to the cancellation. What I kept circling instead was something neither of us had a name for: the argument is self-effacing. Its premise is that I should regard myself as a typical human. But if I accept it, I act on it — I give to the risk-reduction things, I argue about policy, I help bring the long future about — and the person who does that is no longer a typical member of a sequence that ends soon; I am an early member of a long sequence that my own belief helped start. She said that was just the banal point about self-defeating prophecies and had nothing to do with whether the inference is valid. I said the interesting version isn't about the outcome at all: it's that the argument hands you a reason to make its own premise inapplicable to you, by turning you into the sort of person who was never a random sample.

So: I want a villanelle — nineteen lines, five tercets and a quatrain, two refrains alternating through the tercets and finally meeting in the last two lines — in which the two refrains are the two ends of the inference, one the sampling premise and one the doom-ish conclusion, both in ordinary language a person could say out loud rather than in notation (something like "I am the Nth" and "the rest is not much more than what has come," if that helps — use whatever the form needs). Have them behave the way the argument behaves: every time the poem seems to have gotten somewhere, the pair returns and resets it, the way the inference reimposes itself on anyone who has once understood it. Give the middle stanzas to the reference-class question — whether I am a random human, or a random member of some wider class of beings who could have had this thought, and why the answer changes the conclusion. And give the last two new lines before the refrains return to my self-effacing worry: let the poem state the conclusion and, in nearly the same breath, make the case that stating it disqualifies the speaker from the very sample the conclusion was drawn from — without winking at the reader, and without resolving it, because I honestly don't know whether that is a refutation or just what a self-locating prediction looks like from inside.

Two further things, and I care about both equally. It should be accurate: someone who works on anthropic selection should be able to read it without wincing — the sampling step should really be the sampling step, the conclusion should be a claim about the total number of humans rather than about a date or a catastrophe, and the reference-class worry should be recognizable as the thing it is. But it should also work as a poem for someone who has never heard of any of this: concrete images, plain-ish diction, no private jokes, nothing that needs a footnote. No prefatory explanation, no closing note, no epigraph — if the reader is lost, the refrains should have taught them the argument by the time they arrive. I mean to read it aloud to close the final session of a reading group that has spent ten weeks on observation selection effects and is ending, as these things do, without agreement.
