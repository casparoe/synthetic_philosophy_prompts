I'm trying to work out what Newton is actually committing himself to in the first of the Rules of Reasoning in Philosophy that open Book III of the Principia (Motte's translation), because I want to use the passage as a foil for thinking about Solomonoff induction:

RULE I.
We are to admit no more causes of natural things than such as are both true and sufficient to explain their appearances.

To this purpose the philosophers say that Nature does nothing in vain, and more is in vain when less will serve; for Nature is pleased with simplicity, and affects not the pomp of superfluous causes.

I read this as an ancestor of Occam's razor, but I don't think I understand it well enough to use it that way. I'd like help on three points.

1. What does the rule require, precisely? What work does true do in it — given that we should not posit false causes anyway, does the rule say anything beyond a ban on idle causes, or is it a different kind of injunction about which unifying explanations may be accepted? And is the supporting sentence a methodological maxim about how to theorize, or a substantive claim about how the world is?

2. Suppose I want to restate the rule in the vocabulary of Kolmogorov complexity, where simplicity is an ordering by program length relative to a chosen reference machine rather than a count of causes. Give me one small, concrete case — fixed body of data, two competing hypotheses — in which fewest causes and shortest description disagree about which hypothesis is the simpler one, and say which hypothesis each rule favors. My guess is that one cause specified by a long, irregular law versus many causes falling out of one short generative rule is the shape of the case, but I want to see it written down properly.

3. Newton seems to rest parsimony on a claim about Nature. Does Solomonoff induction rest on a claim of that kind too, and if so where exactly does it enter: in the requirement that the data stream be computable at all, in the arbitrary choice of reference machine, or in the bound on total prediction error, which involves the complexity of the true source? Is the world is computable the same sort of claim as Nature is pleased with simplicity, or a weaker one? And does the usual slogan about not multiplying entities overstate what the formalism supports, given that Solomonoff prediction is a weighted mixture over all computable hypotheses rather than a selection of the single simplest consistent one?

Keep the three answers separate, and in each one distinguish what is settled about the formalism itself — definitions, convergence results, the role of the reference machine — from what is genuinely contested among people who work on it, such as how much weight the reference-machine dependence should bear and whether Solomonoff induction is best described as a vindication of Occam's razor or as something else. Do not present a contested reading as the consensus view, and if you think my framing in question 3 rests on a confusion, say so rather than answering it as posed.
