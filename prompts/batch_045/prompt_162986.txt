Someone brought an airport paperback on the evolution of cooperation and read us the summary chapter out loud, and it all sounded very clean and conclusive in a way that made me suspicious, but I don't know enough yet to say where it goes wrong. Here's my paraphrase, from memory, of roughly what the chapter concludes:

"Back in the 1980s Robert Axelrod ran computer tournaments in which submitted strategies played the iterated prisoner's dilemma against each other, and the winner both times was the simplest entry: tit-for-tat, which cooperates on the first move and thereafter just copies whatever the opponent did last. Tit-for-tat is therefore the optimal way to play a repeated prisoner's dilemma, and it is an evolutionarily stable strategy — meaning natural selection will drive any population of self-interested agents toward it, which is why cooperation is everywhere in nature once individuals meet repeatedly. The mechanism is just the shadow of the future: as long as there is *any* probability at all of another encounter, however small, cooperating pays more in the long run than defecting, so cooperation is self-enforcing. Direct reciprocity is thus the master explanation of cooperation, and mechanisms like kin selection or group selection are at best special cases of it, at worst leftover baggage from before anyone had run the numbers. In the one-shot game, by contrast, defection is simply the rational move, since defecting does better whatever the partner does; that people still cooperate in laboratory one-shot games just shows that human intuition is bad at this kind of reasoning. Put those together and the evolution of cooperation is basically a solved problem."

Three things I want checked specifically, because I have half-memories that might be garbled. First, on the "any probability at all" claim: I sat down and did the expected-value comparison myself for a TFT player facing an always-defect player with our payoffs, and I got that cooperation only pays if the probability of another round is above (T-R)/(T-P) = 2/4 = 1/2 — so a threshold, not "any chance whatsoever". Is that right, and is the chapter's version just sloppy or actually a different claim? Second, I rember reading that tit-for-tat isn't a strict ESS at all, because unconditional cooperators can drift in against it since they score exactly the same, and then defectors can invade that population — and I even half-recall a result saying that in the repeated game *nothing* is evolutionarily stable once you discount the future. Is any of that real, or am I confusing it with something else, and does it matter for the chapter's "selection will drive any population to it"? Third, our own tournament: everyone knew the exact number of rounds (200) and yet reciprocal strategies still did well, which seems to clash with the backward-induction argument I thought we were taught (that with a known last round you defect, and then the round before, and so on all the way back) — and our 1% error rate made two tit-for-tat players get locked into what looked like retaliation ping-pong rather than recovering after one round.

So: does the chapter's picture survive those complications, or does the fixed-length and noisy version break the story? And do the conclusions it draws — reciprocity makes kin selection redundant; cooperating in a one-shot dilemma is just irrational; cooperation is a solved problem — actually follow from the parts of the summary that are true?
