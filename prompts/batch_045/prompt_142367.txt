My opening. Take the theorem in its plain binary form: two states of the world, fifty-fifty prior, n voters, each with a private signal that's right with p = 0.6, conditionally independant given the truth.

Version A: secret ballot, simple majority, n = 101. Group is right about 98% of the time. I make it ~98, you can check me. Fine — that's the theorem, we've all seen it.

Version B: the same 101 people, same signals, but they deliberate in a fixed order and each publicly announces the vote their posterior implies before the next one speaks. Everyone's a perfect Bayesian, common knowledge of p and the setup, everyone knows everyone is honest. Each announcement is worth a likelihood ratio of 1.5, so as soon as one option is ahead by two, the public evidence already outweighs any single private signal and every voter after that just copies the crowd. The cascade locks. Given state A, the first two agree 0.52 of the time (0.36 both A, 0.16 both B), and when they agree it's over; the other 0.48 of the time you're at 1–1 and voter three's signal picks the direction, which is wrong 0.4 of the time. Depending on how you break ties I get a wrong answer somewhere around a third of the time — call it 65% accuracy — and here's the bit I actually want you to deal with: it stays at 65% if I give you 1,001 voters, or a million. The cascade direction is fixed before the crowd is ever used. Independence was doing all the work in the theorem, and deliberation is precisely the thing that eats independence.

So the theorem is a conditional result about a voting rule nobody runs, and the institution epistemic democrats actually like — people talking, then voting — is the one where the headline 'more voters, more certainty' just flatlines. And before you go there: yes, I know the three obvious moves, that real deliberation raises p as well as correlating votes, that people could announce signals instead of votes, and that we should design institutions that protect independence.
