I teach epistemology at a small liberal arts college, and I have been asked to referee an exchange between two colleagues that I am not confident I can adjudicate. Last week, over a long department dinner, a metaphysician and a philosopher of science on my faculty—neither of whom has published on the epistemology of disagreement, though both teach material adjacent to it—got into it, and the argument has continued by email since. It began, as these things do, over a trivial case: the three of us disagreed about whether a particular actor narrated a certain 1970s nature-documentary series, and Miriam said that, since we had all been watching the same clips and were roughly equally placed to judge, each of us should have moved substantially toward the others—that, in her words, is what the equal-weight view requires when you discover that an epistemic peer disagrees with you. Tobias granted the object-level point but objected to what he called the ratchet: Miriam holds that a disagreement is not merely evidence against the proposition disputed but also evidence that you are the sort of person who errs on questions of that kind, so it should lower your estimate of your own reliability, and that lowered estimate should then make you weight the other person more heavily in the *next* disagreement. Tobias argued that the ratchet is self-destructive: run it often enough—he suggested a few dozen ordinary disagreements over a career—and your confidence in your own reliability approaches zero, at which point you defer to everyone about everything, including about who counts as a peer in the first place, since peerhood judgments are themselves beliefs you hold; so conciliationism, he said, drains you of the very first-order beliefs that were supposed to ground the peerhood determination. He proposed instead what he called fixed-point conciliationism: you should seek a steady state in which the reliability estimate that goes into the conciliation procedure is also the one that comes out of it. Miriam replied, first, that fixed-point conciliationism is steadfastness in disguise, since at the fixed point you weight yourself exactly as before, so the revisionary claim does all the work on the way down and none at the destination; and second, that the decay worry is overstated because agreements count too—a peer who agrees with you is evidence for you—and in an ordinary life agreements vastly outnumber disagreements, so the ratchet ratchets up as often as down and the process settles near where you began, making the fixed point a theorem rather than an objection. Tobias answered that the agreement-counting reply smuggles in a base rate: how often you should expect to agree with someone depends on how reliable you take yourself and them to be, which is precisely what is in question, so the reply presupposes the calibration it was meant to establish and is therefore bootstrapping. He also denied that the fixed point is trivially the status quo: if you begin overconfident, iterating the rule can move you a long way before it settles, and—this is where I lost the thread—the steady state may be plural, since depending on where you start, the very same conciliation rule can settle on near-total self-trust or on near-total deference, each self-consistent under the rule. Miriam's response was that plurality of fixed points is not a defect but a clue to what kind of thesis conciliationism is: a coherence constraint on permitted combinations of first-order confidence and self-trust, not a decision procedure, with the initial calibration supplied from elsewhere, such as your track record. She then pressed what she called the self-undermining objection against steadfastness: whatever the pattern of peer opinion, a steadfaster can always say that in this case the peers are the ones who are mistaken, so steadfastness is insulated from any possible disconfirmation and is not earning its keep. A side dispute also surfaced: Miriam said that if you have just looked at the relevant evidence yourself, splitting the difference with someone who has not is absurd, while Tobias said that this is simply a case of asymmetric evidence rather than a counterexample to conciliation, and that the interesting question is whether the ratchet should apply to your global reliability at all, or only to your reliability on questions of that local kind. They have both asked me to say who is right about what, and I have tried to reproduce their positions fairly without signaling my own sympathies, which are genuinely undecided.

Please take the role of a professional epistemologist who has spent years working on the epistemology of disagreement, and think this through slowly and carefully before committing yourself. I would like you to begin by laying out, in your own terms, the dialectical space as you see it—what the disagreement between conciliationism and steadfastness actually turns on, and what the rival positions in this particular argument are—and then go through the exchange move by move. For each move, tell me whether you take it to be clearly right, mostly right with a qualification, unclear, mostly mistaken, or clearly mistaken, and say briefly why. I am especially interested in four things: whether the ratchet is a genuine commitment of conciliationism or an optional extra; whether the agreement-counting reply really is bootstrapping; whether the possibility of plural fixed points shows conciliationism to be incomplete rather than false, and what the difference would amount to; and whether the local-versus-global distinction in how disagreement bears on self-trust dissolves a good part of the dispute. After that, give me an overall verdict on who is more right, and say what, if anything, both of them are missing—some consideration that reframes or undercuts the whole exchange. I plan to use your analysis as the basis for a seminar discussion, so I would rather have your honest assessment than a diplomatic one.

Please write in American English, in continuous prose only—no headings, bullet points, numbered lists, bold text, or tables—in the manner of a considered letter or email to a colleague; if you need to distinguish the moves, do it with sentences and transitional phrases rather than formatting. Aim for roughly eight hundred to a thousand words.
