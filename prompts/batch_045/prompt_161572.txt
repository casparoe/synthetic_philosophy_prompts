I'm a junior and I help organize the Ethics Bowl club at my school. At our last meeting two of the seniors on the team, Dana and Ravi, ended up in an argument that neither of them would concede, and the rest of us couldn't settle it either. I'd be grateful if you would adjudicate it.

The background: Dana had come across a well-known family of experiments in which people read two nearly identical stories about a businessman who approves a new program while knowing it will affect the environment, and who says he doesn't care about the environment at all and only wants the profit. Asked whether he intentionally harmed the environment, most people say yes; asked whether he intentionally helped it, most say no. Dana and Ravi rebuilt the case around our student council and ran it on about a hundred and eighteen students during lunch periods, one story per person. In their version, Maya is student council president. A company offers a vending-machine contract that will raise a lot of money for prom. She is told the machines will also cause the school to lose its green campus certification (or, in the other version, will help the school keep that certification). She says: I don't care about the green campus certification at all, I just want the money for prom, let's sign. Question: did Maya intentionally cause the school to lose its certification — or, in the other version, did she intentionally help the school keep it? Eighty-one percent said yes in the first version, twenty-seven percent in the second. A follow-up question, is Maya to blame, got eighty-four percent and nine percent respectively.

Then the argument:

(1) Dana: Everything that could conceivably matter to whether an action was intentional is held fixed across the two stories — what she knew, what she controlled, whether she aimed at the outcome. The only thing that changes is whether the side effect is good or bad. So the difference in answers cannot be a response to any fact about her agency; it is a response to the valence of the outcome. The word intentional is serving as a vehicle for blame. The alignment with their own blame question makes this obvious. Conclusion: these results are evidence about our moral reactions, not about intentional action, and they tell us nothing about the latter.

(2) Ravi: That an intentionality judgment tracks blame does not yet show it is a disguised blame judgment. It may be that our ordinary concept of doing something intentionally just is the concept of doing it in a way you are answerable for, in which case normative considerations belong inside the concept and their presence is not contamination. To tell the two apart you have to ask whether the effect follows the badness of outcomes or the presence of a violated norm. So I wrote a new pair of stories: Maya is told the machines will have to be installed nine feet from the water fountain, and the student handbook requires ten. Everyone agrees the rule is pointless and that the placement affects nobody either way. Asked whether she intentionally violated the handbook rule, seventy-four percent said yes; in the mirror version, where the machines end up eleven feet away and so bring the school into line with the same rule nobody cares about, thirty-one percent said she intentionally complied with it. The asymmetry survives with the consequences set to zero. That is what a normative concept predicts, and it is not what a story about reacting to bad outcomes predicts, since there is no bad outcome here to react to.

(3) Dana: That variant doesn't separate our views at all. Blame attaches to rule-breaking as readily as to harming; people are blamed for breaking pointless rules all the time, sometimes more harshly than for causing mild harm. So your own result is exactly what the contamination story predicts. All you have established is that the effect tracks norms rather than consequences — and norms are precisely what blame tracks. You have relocated the target from harm to norm violation without showing that the judgment has stopped being a moral or quasi-moral one.

(4) Ravi: Two things. First, if the intentionality judgment were a separate blame judgment piled on top of a descriptive one, the two should come apart somewhere — there ought to be cases where people say she isn't to blame, she had no real choice, but she still did it intentionally. If the two move together everywhere you look, the simpler hypothesis is that there is one judgment, and a single judgment that is essentially normative is a normative concept of intentional action, not a clean concept plus interference. Second, even if you insist on calling it blame, you still owe an account of why blame attaches to a foreseen side effect she never aimed at — that she was willing to bring it about, that she treated the value in question as worth nothing — and whatever you say there is the very feature I claim is built into what intentionally means. At that point the dispute is verbal.

(5) Dana: It isn't verbal, because there is a practical difference about what philosophers should do with the word. In philosophy intentionally is used as a neutral notion: in accounts of what action is, and in principles like the one distinguishing effects that are intended from effects that are merely foreseen. If ordinary usage turns out to be normative, then philosophers have two options. They can treat their notion as a technical term of art, in which case surveys of ordinary usage are simply irrelevant to it; or they can take over the ordinary notion, in which case those principles quietly import moral verdicts under a descriptive label. Either way, the survey results do no philosophical work.

(6) Ravi: Stipulation isn't free. If you define intentionally so that it excludes everything the ordinary notion includes, you have changed the subject rather than clarified it, and you owe an argument that the neutral notion is the one that explains action and responsibility. And the mere possibility of stipulating doesn't make the survey results irrelevant: they show that the concept we actually use when we hold one another to account has a structure philosophers have been misdescribing, and that is a philosophical result about that concept, not a datum about our psychology.

Could you go through these six moves one at a time? For each, say whether it is clearly right, mostly right, mostly mistaken, or clearly mistaken, and give the reason in a sentence or two — I care less about who wins overall than about which specific claim in which move holds up. Then say who you think is more right overall, and what you take both of them to be missing. Finally, if you were adding exactly one more version of their survey in order to separate Dana's explanation from Ravi's, what would you ask and what result would favor which of them? I'm not looking for references or further reading — I want the arguments themselves assessed.
