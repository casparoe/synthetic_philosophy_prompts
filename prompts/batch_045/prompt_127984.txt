A friend of mine — in her sixties, thirty years as an advertising copywriter, now on our city's consumer-protection board — has unusually firm and consistent verdicts about which ways of getting people to do something are just persuasion and which are manipulation. She has no philosophy training and reads none. I have no academic background in any of this either, so please don't assume I know the literature; I've simply been keeping a list of her rulings for a few years.

1. A car dealer who, having heard a customer say she likes white cars, has the white model washed and moved to the front of the lot, and mentions, entirely truthfully, that white holds its resale value better. Fine.
2. An anti-smoking advertisement that is nothing but a dying man speaking through a hole in his throat and footage of a blackened lung: no statistics, no argument, pure fear and disgust. Fine.
3. A drug company representative who puts the same trial data in front of a doctor with the benefits as raw counts and the risks as a one-in-ten-thousand rate, having picked the form of words that makes the drug look best, and says nothing false. Manipulation.
4. A supermarket that pumps the smell of baking bread toward the entrance to make people buy more. No one is misled about anything and no one is made worse off in any obvious way. Manipulation.
5. A hospital cafeteria that moves the salad bar to the front of the line and puts the desserts behind the till, openly, on the nutritionist's instruction, to make people eat better. Fine.
6. A charity that has volunteers hand-write short notes to donors along the lines of I was thinking of you — genuinely written, genuinely meant — but only to donors its model has flagged as likely to give more if they receive one. Manipulation.
7. A union representative who lays out, in a clear spreadsheet, what the company's offer actually costs people over three years, and tells them they have every reason to be angry. Fine.
8. A subscription company you can join in one click and can only leave by telephoning during office hours. Manipulation.

The only formulation I have ever managed to get out of her is a shrug and: it depends whether they are being got at. That is useless to me, because the supermarket shopper and the cafeteria eater are both being got at, and she rules opposite ways.

The case her board has to vote on next month is a savings app offered by a bank. You opt in, and at sign-up it explains in plain language exactly what it will do: it rounds your purchases up and moves the difference into savings, sends you a weekly note saying you saved less than most people in your bracket, shows you a photograph of the holiday you told it you were saving for, and schedules its reminders at the hours when you personally tend to give in. Nothing is false, nothing is concealed, you can turn any part of it off whenever you like, and on your own reckoning you end up better off than you would have. But it works purely by exploiting the fact that people are bad at this sort of thing, it gives the saver no new reason to save, and the bank's interest in holding your deposits is not the same as your interest in your holiday.

What I would like from you: the rule she seems to be running, stated precisely enough that it predicts her next verdict; the other rules that fit all eight of her rulings just as well; what each of those rules implies about the savings app; which way you would bet she votes, and why you prefer that rule over the others; which of her eight rulings is the closest match, and if her precedents genuinely underdetermine it, what she would be committing herself to either way; and the further case you would put to her to tell the surviving rules apart, since I have a standing invitation to propose one.
