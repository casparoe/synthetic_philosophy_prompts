I'm a junior at a public high school, and I write a fortnightly column on ethics and school life for our student paper. For the next issue I want to build something my classmates will actually finish rather than skim: a diagnostic quiz — eight to ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key — that sorts people by which moral-emotion view they are implicitly running on. Everything I have come across on the guilt-versus-shame question so far just defines the two emotions and then announces a winner, which is useless for my purpose, so I want an instrument built up from the logical space instead, with the labels arriving at the end rather than smuggled into the questions. That means starting from the separating questions rather than from the theories. The candidates I have in mind, which you should use or replace as you see fit, are: whether the emotion requires an actual audience or only an imagined one; whether it is directed at what was done or at what the person is; whether it can be felt for something that was not the person's fault; whether it can be felt for something no reasonable person would blame them for; whether it must be consciously felt at the time or can be present as a disposition that no one ever acts on; whether it can attach to someone through other people, as with family dishonor or inherited national guilt; and whether the emotion can itself become the object of the emotion. Build the tree from the combinations these allow, then locate the actual positions in it: the view that guilt is the more moral of the two because it keeps the act in view; the view that shame is the more moral because it reaches the whole character rather than one transaction; the view that the two are the same mechanism with different triggers and differ only in what sets them off; the view that both are defective and should be replaced by something like remorse or agent-regret; the view that they are socially constructed and not comparable across cultures at all; and the older anthropological contrast between guilt cultures and shame cultures, which I would like placed as its own leaf and flagged if it turns out to be a claim about which sanctions a society enforces rather than about what anyone feels.

For the questions themselves I need cases, not definitions, and no item may ask whether the reader thinks of themselves as a guilt person or a shame person. I have four cases I want included, and you should supply the rest: a student who feels oddly sullied after a plagiarism checker flags a sentence they wrote themselves, and who cannot shake the feeling even though they know perfectly well that they did nothing wrong; a student who is ashamed of having been ashamed of their father's accent at a school event; a student who feels a steady low-grade guilt about a classmate they were under no obligation to invite anywhere and to whom they have done nothing; and a student who behaves well in a corridor only because there is a camera at the end of it, and who cannot tell from the inside whether that is shame, prudence, or something with no name yet. Give each leaf the name of the position, or unnamed if nobody defends it, and one sentence on its least comfortable commitment. Where a combination of answers is logically available but nobody seems to occupy it, keep the empty leaf and say in a line whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely untried. Where two leaves cannot be separated by any question you can honestly write, say so rather than manufacturing a distinction.

The second thing I need in the same document is what the taxonomy implies about choosing, because my classmates keep asserting both that you cannot help what you feel and that a school can decide what sort of people it turns out, and I want to know which leaves are committed to which claim. Take three scales — an individual deliberately cultivating one of the emotions rather than the other, a school adopting an honor code or a restorative circle in place of detentions, and a whole culture shifting over a generation — and say for each which leaves can treat the shift as a genuine choice, which must treat it as something that happens to a group rather than something a group does, and which make the question ill-posed. If the ability to choose turns out to cut across the tree independently of the other branches, tell me that instead. Please keep the whole thing free of citations and reading lists; I have no database access from the library computers, so anything I cannot read there is of no use to me.
