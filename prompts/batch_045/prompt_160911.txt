Me and my mate had a long, slightly drunk argument last night about whether physics might be implying that everything is a bit conscious, and this morning he sent me his summary of the view so we could pick it up again:

"Panpsychism is the view that every electron has a little bit of consciousness — a tiny feeling or urge — because physics only tells you what matter does, its structure and relations, not what matter is in itself, and the only thing we know from the inside is our own experience, so that must be what the inside of matter is. It's basically animism with equations on, and it's meant to solve the hard problem of consciousness: once experience is fundamental, you don't have to explain how a lump of meat generates it. But it obviously fails, because there's this combination problem — no one can explain how a heap of electron-minds adds up to the one mind you've got — and even the panpsychists more or less admit that's fatal."

He's then using that to argue two things: first, that the position is dead; second, that if it weren't dead it would be a moral emergency, because the universe would be seething with suffering and the sane response would be extreme precaution, maybe even trying to do as little as possible forever. I said the second bit doesn't follow — "there's something it's like to be an electron" isn't "an electron is suffering" — and he said I was only knocking down a straw man, which stung because I'm not sure he's wrong.

Can you audit his summary for me, sentence by sentence roughly: what someone who actually defends panpsychism would accept, what they'd wince at, and what's just mistaken? I want to know in particular whether the "animism" line is a real kinship or a slur, whether panpsychism is supposed to be defined by that argument from the limits of physics or just often reached by it, and whether presenting the combination problem as something its defenders concede is fatal is fair. Then tell me whether either of his conclusions survives once you fix the summary.

The other is the aggregation question: if you did grant that micro-experiences can be slightly unpleasant, whether zillions of minuscule disvalues sum to something morally serious, or whether there's a threshold or anti-aggregation story.
