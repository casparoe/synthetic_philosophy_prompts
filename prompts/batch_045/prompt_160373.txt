I am finishing a thesis chapter on the evolution of cooperation and have staked myself to a position I need to stress-test before my supervisor reads the draft. Roughly: the explanatory weight in the standard reciprocity story is carried not by iteration as such, not by the shadow of the future, but by what I call *partner correlation* — the degree to which, in a given population, an individual's next interaction is more likely than chance to be with someone who will respond cooperatively. On this view the one-shot anonymous prisoner's dilemma is a misleading stylization rather than a revealing one: it strips out by experimental fiat exactly the correlation that does the work in real populations, and then reports the resulting defection as a discovery about strategic structure. Cooperation, I claim, tracks partner correlation; the folk-theorem multiplicity of equilibria in the indefinitely repeated game is a red herring, because indefinitely repeated play merely happens to generate correlation rather than being the source of it. My supervisor's objection is that I have done something worse than being wrong. If partner correlation is defined widely enough to cover kin, network structure, reputation, partner choice, punishment, and whatever else makes cooperators meet one another, she says, then the claim that cooperation occurs where partner correlation is high is close to a tautology, and I have arranged things so that no observation could ever surprise me.
