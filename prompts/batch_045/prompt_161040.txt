hi — I'm retired now, thirty-odd years as a research biologist in a government fisheries lab (stock assessment, lots of probability, lots of arguing about what counts as evidence), and in the last couple of years I've gotten into philosophy the way people get into birding: badly at first, then obsessively. Which is how I ended up on the review panel at our regional eye hospital. They've started enrolling adults who've been blind since early childhood in a pilot that combines a gene therapy with a retinal implant — partial sight restoration, 6 to 12 months of rehab, irreversible, maybe 40 candidates a year, and we meet one afternoon a month with a stack of files. The panel is me, a nurse, a clinical lead who signs off, a chaplain, and a rotating community member. We have about ninety minutes per intake and a one-page form, and whatever we write has to be defensible to the hospital's legal office. My instinct, from old habit, is to build a decision tree with probabilities and utilities and a threshold. A philosopher friend of mine says that is exactly the mistake for part of this: she got me reading about transformative experience, the idea that with some choices you cannot know what you are choosing between until you have chosen it, because the outcome changes what you want and maybe who you are, so the expected-utility machinery has nothing to run on. I more or less buy that. What I cannot do is get from that idea to something four non-specialists can actually run on a Tuesday afternoon.

So: write me the procedure. Ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. A few things it has to do. First, it has to split the parts where my numbers are legitimate — infection and detachment rates, rehab burden, hours of training, measurable acuity outcomes — from the part where I am told the numbers are fake, and tell me what to put in that second slot instead of a probability. Second, I want an actual weighting rule for testimony from people who've already been through the procedure: they are the only ones with access to what it is like, and they are also the ones most likely to be rationalizing a choice they can't undo. "Listen to them respectfully" is not a rule; give me something I can write on the form, including when I should discount a transformed person's report and when I shouldn't. Third, handle the cases where the file is really a family's file — parents pushing, a partner pushing, a candidate who is ambivalent in a way that might just be fear. Fourth, mark the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and tell me which pieces of apparent rigor I should throw out because they're fake rigor. And give me a stopping rule, because we are volunteers and we cannot become an endless counseling service.

Then run it on three files. (1) A 34-year-old, blind since about eighteen months, eager, has done the counseling, has realistic expectations, wants to recognise his kids' faces — I expect a yes, so mostly I want to see how fast the procedure gets there and what goes in the record. (2) A 19-year-old, blind from birth, lives at a residential school and is embedded in a tight-knit blind community, ambivalent, with two sighted parents who are convinced this is obviously the right call and keep calling us. (3) A 52-year-old, blind since age three, by a mile the best-adjusted candidate we've seen, articulate, very rooted in a community where blindness is part of who people are; he says he's not sure he wants to spend his fifties being a bad sighted person and losing the competence he's spent a life building. My suspicion is that the answer I'll hate is in file 3 — either that his own stated preference is exactly the unreliable thing and we shouldn't defer to it, which reads to me as straight paternalism, or that the procedure says yes to somebody who doesn't want it. Tell me honestly which one it is, show the run, and finish by saying what the procedure can't do — where we just have to sit there as people and decide.
