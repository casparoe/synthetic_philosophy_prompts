I'm a journalist — I cover science and tech for a weekend magazine, with the odd radio panel when they need someone to say something about AI — and I'm halfway through a short series on whether artefacts themselves can have politics, as opposed to the people who design, fund and use them having politics which then gets expressed through the thing. My editor read the first two pieces and said the whole thing was determinism in a constructivist hat, which I think is unfair but I couldn't quite say why in the moment, so I want someone to audit my actual position before I file the last one. Here it is, warts and all. (a) I reject the story where a technology shows up and society just rearranges itself around it; I think technologies are genuinely underdetermined by their technical logic, that use is open, that users reinterpret things in ways designers never planned, and that the social-construction-of-technology crowd basically won that argument. (b) But I also think artefacts really can carry a political charge, and my go-to case is the low overpasses on the old parkway that keep buses — and so the people who take buses — away from certain beaches; I want to say the politics is in the bridge whether or not anyone intends it or uses it that way today. (c) To handle the objection that plenty of kit is used benignly, I've been using a distinction of my own between a politics that's live and one that's dormant: the charge is still in the artefact but it only shows up under the right activation conditions, so the same face-recognition model sitting in a liberal democracy and in an autocracy is the same artefact carrying the same politics, just switched on in one place and not the other. (d) I write a lot of they-should-have-seen-this-coming journalism and I mean it: designers, funders and product people bear real responsibility for downstream political effects. (e) I think the move where someone says the tool itself is neutral and only the use matters is almost always an ideology move to shut down accountability. (f) At the same time I don't want to give up writing sentences like the cheap printing press destabilised the authority of the late medieval church, or the pill changed the shape of the labour market, and I don't think that makes me a determinist — my brother-in-law, who's a structural engineer and argues with me about this every Christmas, says it obviously does, and I think he's wrong but I'd like it checked. (g) There's also a heuristic I half-use: my opposite-headlines test, where if you can write two equally true stories about the same device, one about liberation and one about surveillance, then you've shown the politics isn't in the artefact at all — I suspect this sits badly with (b) but I can't tell how badly.

So: go through that lot and tell me which pairs are actually in tension and which only look like it, rank the real tensions by how bad they are, and for each one say precisely what I'd have to give up and what that would cost me — I don't want to be told which one to drop, I want to know which revision does the least damage to the rest of the package, and in particular whether my dormant-politics distinction is doing the work I think it's doing or whether it quietly hands the whole thing back to the constructivists I claim to agree with. About 700 words, in British English please, and no citations, references or reading lists — I'm filing this from a hotel lobby with no library access and I'd only have to fact-check everything you name, so argue it out and just name the moving parts.
