I've been reading the rationalist blogs since the Sequences were going up, and for the last couple of years I've helped organise a small rationality meetup in Bristol — a Discord server, a monthly in-person discussion, the usual mix of aspiring rationalists, a few effective altruists, and one very determined contrarian. We had a moderation argument last week that has been rattling around my head, and it ended in a slogan rather than a conclusion, which I always find suspicious.

Two exclusions were on the table. One member wanted to disinvite a speaker — call him A. — who argues in print that members of a particular religious minority should be barred from teaching in state schools: plainly intolerant content, but expressed entirely as speech, by someone with no institutional power and no history of anything worse than writing. Another member wanted to bar pre-emptively anyone turning up in the colours of a local organisation that has so far only marched peacefully, on the grounds that its programme, if it ever commanded a majority, would shut us and people like us down; nothing intolerant has actually been done by anyone yet, so her case is purely predictive. Both of them reached for the same one-liner: no tolerance for the intolerant. Popper's version, which I first met as an aside in the chapter notes to The Open Society and Its Enemies (1945), at least gestures at a reason — that the intolerant may turn out to be, in his words, not prepared to meet us on the level of rational argument, but begin by denouncing all argument — but I don't think either of my friends is trading on that reason, and I suspect they are trading on quite different readings of the same sentence.

So what I want is a menu rather than a verdict. Lay out the distinct precise claims the slogan could be making, from the platitudinous to the radical, and give each one a verdict: trivial, substantive and defensible, contentious, or straightforwardly false. In particular I'd like the senses of tolerance separated out, because ours is a community that cares far more about who gets treated as a partner in rational argument than about who gets fined or imprisoned, and I suspect most of the force of the slogan in a setting like ours comes from a sense of tolerance — membership of the group's epistemic commons, if you like — that isn't the one political philosophers usually have in mind. Then tell me, reading by reading, whether it would license the first exclusion, the second, both, or neither; my guess is that many readings license neither, that the word paradox is doing the work of presenting a diachronic trade-off as if it were a contradiction in a principle, and that the pre-emptive case smuggles in an empirical claim about prediction and base rates that no amount of conceptual analysis can underwrite — say whether that's fair. I'd also like the classification problem handled rather than gestured at: the power to declare a speaker intolerant is itself the kind of thing worth capturing, so does the paradox simply recur one level up, and does that make the slogan self-defeating or merely incomplete? Finally, which reading is someone in a moderation thread most likely to have in mind, and does that reading survive being stated precisely? Don't spend words on attribution or chapter-and-verse; assume I can follow an allusion to Rawls or Walzer, and that I care more about the structure of the claims than about who made them. I want to put this in front of the group at the next meetup, so a list I can read out would be ideal.
