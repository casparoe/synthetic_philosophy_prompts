For the last few months I have been the resident philosopher on an applied project, and it has made a distinction I used to treat as textbook feel genuinely unsettled. We are building an annotation scheme — and downstream, a classifier — over a corpus of news transcripts, parliamentary debate and recorded interviews, whose job is to tag every conditional sentence with what its speaker is committed to about the antecedent: that it is false; that she believes it false; that she is agnostic; that the matter is already settled but unknown to her; or that she is explicitly withholding commitment. The tokens that keep breaking our guidelines are the ones where the fact is settled but nobody has looked yet (the biopsy result is in the file, unopened: one annotator hears "If the result is malignant, we operate on Friday" as a wide-open indicative, another hears it as counterfactual-flavored and wants it tagged "settled"), the "if it weren't for" and "but for" constructions whose antecedent is plainly true, and the exchanges in which the speaker knows something her audience does not and still reaches for the indicative form. Before I go back and read more of the semantics literature, I want the specification written down. What does a theory of the indicative/counterfactual distinction have to deliver in order to be good enough for that job — not good enough in general, good enough to underwrite a commitment-detection rule?

So give me the requirements list, with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and keep the weight there rather than on the scoring. Which cases must it classify: past-tense counterfactuals with antecedents taken to be false; indicatives whose antecedent the speaker herself takes to be false (the Oswald-and-Kennedy style of example, where the plain "if" version is fine and the "would have" version is not); settled-but-unknown antecedents like the sealed result or a lottery ticket already drawn; future-directed and "were he to" forms that commit to nothing about the antecedent's truth; "if not for" constructions with true antecedents; and the asymmetric-information pairs where speaker and audience differ. Which distinctions must it refuse to collapse: semantic entailment versus conversational implicature versus presupposition; the speaker's own information versus the common ground; "commits to not-A" versus "commits to not knowing whether A"; and the author's own commitments versus the commitments she reports, supposes or fictionalizes. Which phenomena must it derive rather than stipulate: why "would" shows up in the consequent of one kind and "will" in the other; why only one of the two forms can be used to make an offer, a bet, a threat or a request; why adding detail to an antecedent flips a verdict that was fine a sentence earlier; why two speakers with different information can each assert a conditional with the same antecedent and contradictory consequents and both seem right; how "even if", "whether or not", inversion and "suppose" behave; how any of this survives embedding under "she said that", "I doubt whether", or negation; and whether the distinction is semantic or a matter of use, given that not every language marks it in its morphology.

Then the second half. For each requirement, say whether it is non-negotiable given my purpose or merely a preference of one camp — uniformity of treatment across the two kinds, compositionality, conservatism about the underlying modality, tractability from surface cues plus limited context, stability across annotators, and so on — and name the single requirement that does the most work in excluding candidates. Flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and tell me for each such pair whether the tension is itself a finding about conditionals or a sign that my project's specification is confused: my own suspicion is that "get the asymmetric-information cases right" and "classify every token from the common ground alone" cannot both be had, and more generally that any account which makes the distinction purely pragmatic will not be able to explain why it projects out of embeddings. Finish with a short scorecard — two or three lines each, no more — for the four families I keep encountering: closest-world similarity semantics (the Lewis–Stalnaker line), the modal-restrictor view (the Kratzer-style line), the view on which conditionals have no truth conditions and answer instead to a conditional-probability or acceptability condition (Adams's line), and dynamic update semantics. Please write the whole answer in American English.
