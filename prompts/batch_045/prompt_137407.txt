I am a retired experimental physicist — molecular beam spectroscopy early in my career, then some twenty years designing and debugging measurement apparatus, and a long stretch teaching graduate laboratory and experimental design — and in retirement I have been sitting in on a philosophy department reading group, where the topic that has lodged in my head is causation by omission. I have no philosophical training, and my sense of what the literature actually contains is second-hand and probably distorted, so please correct me where I have misdescribed a position. My working suspicion is that my discomfort with the whole debate is an occupational deformation rather than an insight, and I would like to find out which it is.

Here is the translation I am asking for. Take, one at a time, the main positions on whether an omission or absence can be a cause: the counterfactual and difference-making line, in the Lewis style and later in the interventionist style associated with Woodward and with Pearl's structural causal models; the process or conserved-quantity line, in the Salmon–Dowe style, on which causation requires something physically transmitted along a continuous process and an absence transmits nothing; the view that which omission we single out as 'the cause' is fixed by norms, expectations, and what was supposed to happen rather than by the causal structure itself; and the treatment of double prevention, where one thing causes an outcome by disabling something that would have prevented it. Restate each in the vocabulary I actually think in: standard operating procedures and protocols, control runs and baselines, intervening on a variable as opposed to merely conditioning on it, factorial designs and the choice of which contrast to estimate, blocking and holding nuisance variables fixed, screening off and conditional independence, error budgets in which a term is listed precisely because you bounded it and showed it to be negligible, null results and upper bounds rather than positive detections, detection limits and signal-to-noise, and the choice of state space and level of coarse-graining when you build a model. Define each of your terms of art in my terms before you use it. Be honest where the translation is doing real work: if a position survives in my vocabulary only because you have quietly supplied a protocol, a default state, or a contrast that the original example did not contain, say so instead of calling it a translation.

Then the residue report, which is what I actually care about. Two conjectures of my own to test against it, first. One: physicists and engineers never encounter a bare omission, because in the laboratory doing nothing is always instrumented — the switch is wired to off, the shutter stays closed, the log records that nobody touched anything — and I suspect the philosophical disagreement partly tracks how much of that apparatus the example is imagined to contain. Call an omission instrumented when a physically realised setup maintains the non-action, a boundary omission when what is missing is a condition the apparatus needs, such as a heat sink or a catalyst or a shield, and a bare omission when there is no protocol and no apparatus at all and nothing whatever was done by anybody in the vicinity; my guess is that intuitions that an omission causes something are stable for the first two grades and collapse for the third, and that the literature's disputes are often disputes about which grade is on stage. Two: presences are detected and absences are only inferred, from a model plus a calibrated bound, so every causal claim about an omission is model-mediated in a way causal claims about occurrences are not; I want to know whether that is an epistemic fact that dissolves part of the metaphysical worry or merely relocates it to the choice of model. Against those, tell me what translates cleanly, what translates only with distortion, and what cannot be said in my vocabulary at all — I have in mind the profligacy problem of the innumerable people who also failed to water the plant, the feeling that in some cases something is genuinely produced and in others merely permitted, and the demand of my friend in tort law that there must be a yes-or-no answer about whether the failure to act caused the harm, which I keep telling her is a modelling decision and she keeps telling me is not. Finally, and this is the part I most want: for each piece of residue, say whether it indicts my framework — something about the world that experimental design cannot represent — or whether it indicts the debate, a sign that several different questions are being asked under one heading and that the disagreement is less about causation than about which question is in charge.
