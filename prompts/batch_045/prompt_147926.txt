I help keep a small volunteer archive for our town — local history, old photos, building records, that kind of thing — and the thing we actually argue about most isn't the facts, its wording: whether a sentence goes in flat (the mill burned in 1908), hedged (reportedly), or doesn't go in at all. There's three of us, we push out maybe forty entries a month, whatever we write gets quoted back at us at town meetings, and when people appeal a hedge I'm the one who has to explain it, so I want something better than our current system of everybody going with their gut.

I got into this stuff on my own a couple years back — a used textbook, some podcasts, no classes — and the account that stuck with me (I think it's called the tracking theory? Nozick? maybe I've got the name wrong) is the one with the two what-if questions: you count as knowing that P only if, had P been false, you wouldn't have ended up believing it, and had P been true, you would have. I also gather there's a long fight about whether knowing P means you automatically know everything that obviously follows from P, which is exactly our problem, because half our entries are derived from other entries rather than from a source. I'm definately mangling some of this, so fix me gently but do fix me.

What I want is an actual procedure, not an essay. Something a volunteer with no training can run on one claim in about ten minutes, with questions to answer in order, boxes to tick, and a one-line reason for the log. Please give me:

- the steps in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, and where the thing just stops — downgrade to a hedge, or leave it out;
- one step where you write down, in a single sentence, how we came by the claim. My worry is people gaming this: describe the route in enough detail and everything passes, describe it loosely and nothing does. Give us a rule of thumb for how fine-grained that sentence should be, and say straight out if no rule can settle it;
- a step for claims we reach by inference instead of from a source. My half-baked idea, tell me if it's wrong: besides testing the premise, run the same sensitivity question on the step itself — if this step were a bad one, would we still have taken it? — and tell us how many "obvious" steps we're allowed before we go back and check from scratch. Then tell me which of these are really the same question in disguise: a claim that follows in one obvious step; a claim that only follows once you know some background thing we had to look up; a chain of four steps; a claim stitched out of two things we each know separately; and nobody recorded it turned into it didn't happen;
- a note on every step that's really a judgment call wearing a clipboard.

Then run it on three of ours. One: a single 1978 newspaper clipping says the mill burned in 1908, and from that we'd also like to say the mill wasn't operating in 1909. Two: the swimming hole was closed in 1974 because the plant upstream dumped solvent — everyone in town knows this, there's a plaque, and if it were false we would all still believe it exactly as hard as we do now. Three: we've read every issue of the local paper from that summer and there's no mention of anyone drowning in the river, so we want to say nobody did.

I already know which of those is going to come out badly and I'd rather watch you do it properly than have us quietly skip it. Finish with the one-page version — just the questions and the tick boxes, nothing else — that we can tape up over the scanner.
