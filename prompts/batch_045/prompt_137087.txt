So I've ended up in a weird spot on the common prior assumption and I want someone to try to knock me out of it. The view I keep defending in our reading group (I'm a PhD student in a formal epistemology / decision theory group, we've been slowly working through the agreement and no-trade literature) is something like this: the CPA isn't really a claim about rational agents at all, it's a demand on the modeler — that differences in belief get represented as differences in evidence, and that if you find yourself unable to trace some disagreement back to a difference in what the agents have seen, you've mis-specified the model rather than discovered a failure of rationality. On this reading Aumann-style agreement results and the no-trade results look a lot less deep than they're usually made out to be, because a lot of the conclusion is already packed into the representation: once all disagreement is disagreement about evidence conditional on one shared prior, of course enough communication about posteriors collapses it. And I've been pairing this with a genealogical story, call it the ur-prior picture: the reason a shared prior is ever a reasonable thing to assume is that the agents in the model have a common causal origin and were then fed different private signal streams, so their priors are the same object inherited from a common ancestor and their posteriors are the only thing that diverges. My worry is that I've built something unfalsifiable, and I can't tell whether that's a feature or a confession.

So: what would count against this package, ranked from would-kill-it to merely-awkward, and with an explicit division between what a defender like me would concede as a genuine defeater and what I'd wave off as a misreading. I especially want the unfalsifiability question pushed on, because there's a move I keep seeing and don't know what to do with: a visitor here sketched a result to the effect that for any finite bunch of agents with divergent priors over a countable state space, you can always recover a common prior by refining the space — add a coordinate saying which prior an agent ended up with, put a shared distribution over those, and presto, the disagreement is once again pure evidence-difference about which agent you turned out to be. If that's right, is the CPA a modeling tautology, and is that a problem given that it's usually advertised as a normative constraint? Relatedly: is the ur-prior picture doing any real work, or does it just relocate the problem one generation back (what fixes the ancestral prior, and why should agents that don't share an ancestor — separately trained systems, agents built by different designers, agents in different possible worlds, whatever — be bound by it at all)?

Two cases I want used as stress tests rather than just illustrated with. First, seed divergence: imagine two systems trained on literally the same data stream from different random initializations, who converge on stably different credences over the same events, know each other's credences, exchange unlimited cheap messages, and never budge. Is that a counterexample to a normative reading of the CPA, or just a demonstration that they aren't ideal agents, and how would I tell the difference without begging the question? Second, indexical repair: for any pair of agents with different priors, we can imagine a situation where each is unsure which agent in the population she is, and then there's a common prior over the enlarged space again. I find this move suspiciously cheap — it seems to make the assumption automatic in every case — but if it's legitimate, then what does my position actually predict that the rival view (priors can rationally differ, full stop) doesn't? What I'd personally find most persuasive, if it helps: evidence about what the view commits me to that I'd already reject — e.g., if it turns out I'm committed to saying that any persistent disagreement among apparent epistemic peers must have hidden evidence somewhere, which is a strong empirical claim about real disagreements I don't think I can support. Also say plainly whether you think my package has been qualified into something untestable, and whether for a claim of this kind that's damning or fine.
