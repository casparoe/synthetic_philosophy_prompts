I'm an MA student in a seminar on confirmation and induction, and we spent last week on Goodman's new riddle. I tried to generalize the grue case and ended up with an argument whose conclusion I am confident is false, but in which every step looks like something that has been asserted somewhere in the literature. I would be grateful for a diagnosis of the argument itself rather than a survey of positions.

The device. Let P be any predicate we are happy to project — say 'green' — and suppose that at stage n of an investigation we have examined n emeralds, all of them P. For each stage define the chaser predicate C_n: C_n(x) iff either x is P and x is among the first n emeralds examined, or x is not P and x is not among the first n emeralds examined. The point of the chaser, as against grue proper, is that it contains no dates, no calendar time, and no disjunction of two ordinary colour words assigned to different eras; it is defined over the order of examination, and it gets re-cut at every stage of inquiry.

(1) For every stage n and every predicate P, the hypothesis 'all emeralds are C_n' is consistent with the total evidence E_n and entails that the (n+1)-th emerald examined is not P, while 'all emeralds are P' is consistent with E_n and entails that it is P.

(2) Confirmation is a matter of the relation between the evidence and the hypothesis on the objects the evidence mentions: if H and H' entail exactly the same propositions about every object mentioned in E, and each is consistent with E, then E confirms H and H' to exactly the same degree.

(3) So at every stage n, E_n confirms 'the next emerald examined will not be P' exactly as strongly as it confirms 'the next emerald examined will be P'.

(4) Nothing in (1)–(3) depends on which P we started with or on how large n is: the chaser is available for any predicate, and it can be re-cut at each new stage, so there is no stage at which the evidence has outrun it.

(5) A body of evidence that confirms a prediction and its denial to the same degree does not favour either of them; and evidence that favours no prediction over its denial gives no reason at all to expect one thing rather than another about the unexamined.

(6) Therefore no finite body of evidence ever confirms any prediction about the unexamined. Induction has no rational basis; moreover the standard replies cannot help — in particular the entrenchment reply cannot, since entrenchment is itself a fact about which predicates we have happened to project in the past, and inferring from 'green has been projected successfully so far' to 'green will go on being projectible' is an inference of exactly the kind in question, available only to someone who already has a solution to the riddle.

The conclusion is far stronger than anything the grue case licenses: on this argument 'grue' is not a curiosity about a few artificially time-indexed predicates but a template that can be applied to every predicate at every stage of every inquiry, and the new riddle turns out to be the old problem of induction after all.

Which step do you reject? I would prefer to keep (1), which seems to follow just from how many predicates there are, and I would like to keep something in the neighbourhood of the entrenchment reply, since it is the only thing in the seminar that made the grue case feel tractable. My suspicion is that something is smuggled in either at (2) or in the re-cutting of the chaser from stage to stage, but I cannot say which, and I cannot make the suspicion precise; if the fault is a scope or quantifier shift, I would like it stated explicitly rather than gestured at. Then: what does rejecting it cost? Which of (1)–(5), or which other commitment about confirmation, do I have to surrender, and is there a reading of the argument on which it is valid and the conclusion should be accepted in some weakened form — and if so, what is left of induction once it is?
