I'm writing the empirical chapter of my thesis on explanation in the social sciences (philosophy dept, if that matters — my supervisor is more of an economist and keeps calling my Northern Rock section "a nice illustration", which I genuinely can't tell is a compliment). What I want is the failure of Northern Rock in 2007 used as a real case study for methodological individualism vs holism, done properly rather than as a paragraph where I say "systemic risk is emergent" and move on.

Facts I'm holding fixed so you don't have to guess at them: Northern Rock was a Newcastle mortgage lender that funded long-term mortgages by borrowing short on the wholesale money markets and securitising; when the credit markets seized up in the summer of 2007 it couldn't roll that funding over; on 14 September it took a liquidity support facility from the Bank of England; within about a day there were queues outside branches and people on the news withdrawing savings; the Treasury ended up guaranteeing all deposits and the queues stopped; the bank was nationalised in Feburary 2008. Two features I think are load-bearing and want you to use: (i) the run that actually killed it was the wholesale one — institutions and funds not rolling over — while the retail queues were a second, later event that the first one plus the tv pictures caused; (ii) the public announcement of support is widely thought to have caused the retail run, because support announced is distress revealed.

So: a mapping, not a survey. Split individualism into a constitutive claim (social facts are nothing over and above individuals and their relations), an explanatory claim (an adequate explanation bottoms out in what individuals did and believed), and a practical claim (you can only intervene by changing individual-level incentives), and say which of those this case is actually a problem for, with the wholesale/retail split in mind. For each: what does the view say the actors faced and should have done — the person deciding whether to queue, the institution deciding whether to roll over, the board, and the Bank and Treasury (announce, stay quiet, guarantee everything, cap the guarantee, let it fail)? And which features does each view genuinely explain and which does it misdescribe — I'm thinking of sequential service (first in the queue gets out, last doesn't), the fact that most retail depositors had no information whatever about the balance sheet, and the fact that guaranteeing deposits stopped the run without anyone learning anything new about the bank.

Then run it the other way. Does the case confirm holism — is "its funding structure was fragile" a property of nothing individual? Or does the standard Diamond–Dybvig-style sequential-service model absorb the whole thing individualistically, agents plus beliefs about other agents, in which case was the holist reading ever doing any work? And if that model explains the run only by selecting among multiple equilibria, is the selection an irreducibly social remainder or just an unfinished individualist story? I know that model, don't summarise it, and I don't want citations or a reading list — I've got that part covered.

Last bit, which is where I'm actually stuck: I've been toying with two tests for "irreducibly social" and can't tell if either is any good. Test one: a fact is irreducibly social if publicising it consumes it — publicising the queue lengthened the queue, publicising fragility caused the run. Worry: that overgenerates, since publicising germ theory changed behaviour too and germs aren't social facts. Test two: identity-swap invariance — if you can permute which individual is which and the run still happens, the explanation is structural. Worry: individualists just say "yes, because they'd have occupied the same role", so the test doesn't discriminate. Tell me whether either survives, what a better test would look like, and where you think the whole individualism/holism dispute is just silent here — for instance on what counts as an individual when the units are themselves institutions, or on whether any of it tells you how a deposit guarantee ought to be designed.
