I chair the board of a small public library district — one building, three paid staff, and a meeting room that seats forty and gets booked about twice a week. We have no written policy on who may use it, and we are now getting requests we don't know how to handle: from groups whose views a good part of the town finds repulsive, and from speakers whose events draw counter-protesters from outside the district and leave us with the police bill. We answer to an elected town council and have already had one threat of a lawsuit. I want to write a one-page rule that our booking panel — three volunteers who rotate, none of them lawyers, one week to decide, and required to send the applicant a one-paragraph written reason — can run on any request without me in the room.

I've been reading about Mill's harm principle on my own and I'd like the rule built on it, with two things settled in advance. First, the board has already decided that distress at hearing an opinion is not the sort of thing that counts: the room exists for speech, and people who dislike it can stay away. So the rule has to work with a narrower notion of harm than "someone is upset," and I take it Mill thought the setting mattered as well as the sentence — as I remember it, he said that calling corn dealers starvers of the poor is fine in print but punishable if shouted to an angry crowd outside a corn dealer's house. Second, everything in the room is livestreamed and kept in an online archive, and our name and logo go on the poster, so the audience of any talk is unbounded and lasts for years, and we are lending the speaker our credibility rather than only our chairs.

Please write the rule as an ordered set of questions, with what counts as a yes to each, where the sequence stops with a no, and where it has to stop and hand the rest to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts — and tell me which of those the panel may settle and which have to go to the full board. It has to handle the cases that make the principle awkward: harm that arrives only because other people react badly to the speech; harm that is trivial in any single meeting but builds up across a recurring booking; harm to named people who will not be in the room; and the fact that we can be wrong in either direction.

Then run it on three requests we have actually had. An anti-immigration pressure group wants a one-off evening; the last time they met locally, the event was shut down by protesters and the policing costs fell on the venue. A women's group wants a monthly slot; its position is that trans women should be excluded from women's spaces, and two of my staff have told me they would resign if we host it every month. And a parent wants to run an evening arguing that the standard childhood vaccine schedule is dangerous — several of us think that is straightforwardly harmful, and I suspect your rule will tell me to allow it, so if that is the answer I want to see the reasoning laid out plainly enough to defend. Finally, tell me what a rule of this kind cannot settle.
