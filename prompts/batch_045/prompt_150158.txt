I write courtroom fiction, and I am trying to get something right that is not really a matter of dialogue or plot: the theory of emotion that my characters' legal world is quietly committed to. I would like your help reconstructing it, because I keep finding that the practices I am describing pull in different directions, and I cannot tell whether that is my own muddle or the doctrine's.

Consider the cluster of practices I am working with. A killing done in the heat of passion is treated as a lesser offence than the same killing done deliberately, but only if the killer acted before there was time to cool off: he may have driven across town, bought a weapon, or waited several hours, and the mitigation fails on that basis alone, even if his rage is in no way diminished. The provocation must also be one that would have moved a reasonable person, and in some jurisdictions the question is put in explicitly evaluative terms, asking whether the deceased gave the defendant a justifiable sense of being seriously wronged. A history of small provocations culminating in a trivial final trigger is sometimes allowed in, so that the last straw counts although it could not have moved anyone by itself. Words alone have traditionally not been enough, though a blow is. Separately: remorse counts in a defendant's favour at sentencing, but an apology read out by counsel counts for almost nothing, and a defendant's bare assertion that he was terrified or grief-stricken is treated as nearly worthless unless it shows in what he did. Probation conditions routinely include anger-management courses, on the assumption that a disposition to anger is something a person can be made to work on and be held to account for failing to work on. And the same culture that runs these rules also gives a short window in which to cancel a contract signed at the kitchen table under a salesman's pressure, and imposes a pause before a strike may begin, on the reasoning that the agitation of the moment is temporary and that a night's reflection dissolves it.

What I want from you is the implicit theory of emotion that this pattern presupposes, worked out rather than named. Which parts of it only make sense if an emotion is a bodily agitation or feeling that swells, decays with time, and is dispelled by deliberation? Which parts only make sense if an emotion is an appraisal or evaluative judgment that can be reasonable or unreasonable, well-grounded or not, and therefore something we can be answerable for? Which parts sit best with the view that emotions are perceptions of value or concern-based construals, apt or inapt in the way a perception is veridical or not? Be careful to distinguish what these practices strictly presuppose from what they merely fit comfortably with, and tell me where they are underdetermined, so that two or three rival theories would license exactly the same rules and my characters could reasonably disagree. Where the pattern seems to me inconsistent, say whether it really is: does the mitigation have to treat emotion as an involuntary storm that displaces agency, given that the same system trains offenders out of their anger, discounts unexpressed remorse, and asks juries to assess whether the passion was warranted?

Then I want the counterfactual, because that is what my draft actually needs. Take each of the three families of theory in turn and tell me what would have to change in practice if it were the one the law adopted: which of the requirements above survives, which collapses as incoherent, and which would have to be replaced by something else. In particular, tell me what a cooling-off rule could even mean on a view where emotions carry content and can be more or less justified, and what a reasonable-person test could mean on a view where emotions are only felt disturbances.

One last thing, which is where I have had to invent. My manuscript is set in a jurisdiction that does not exist, and I have given it a rule I have never seen anywhere: the mitigation is unavailable unless the jury finds that the defendant's anger was about the thing he says it was about, so that a man enraged at his own humiliation rather than at what was done to him, or a man who kills the wrong person in a transported fury, gets no reduction. I would like to know which theory of emotion that rule is secretly legislating, whether it is coherent on the terms of the theory that seems to motivate it, and what the cross-examination of such a defendant would sound like under each of the three views. Reason it out from the concepts; I do not need authorities, names, or further reading, and I would rather have your argument than a survey of the literature.
