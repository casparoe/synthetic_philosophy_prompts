I sit on the choice-architecture review board of a public hospital system, and we have a proposal to rule on. Our precedents, all from the last two years:

1. Approved — a flu-shot appointment is pre-booked for the patient at check-in, cancelable in one tap, with no statement of why it was offered.
2. Approved — the cafeteria moves healthier items to eye level and to the till, and puts sugary drinks on a low shelf that requires stooping.
3. Rejected — waiting-room screens carry hand-hygiene images flashed too briefly to be consciously seen.
4. Rejected — refill reminders telling patients that nine in ten similar patients refill on time, when the true rate is about six in ten.
5. Rejected — the portal pre-selects the cheaper of two clinically equivalent treatments in order to cut the system's own costs, with the default disclosed and changeable in one tap.
6. Approved — reminder texts are timed to each patient's own historically most responsive window, inferred from their records, without telling the patient why the message arrived when it did.

The new proposal: the portal will tailor not only when it contacts a patient but how it frames the message — loss framing for patients the model classifies as loss-averse, an appeal to clinical authority for patients it classifies as deferential, and so on. The classifications come from each patient's own records and clickstream, are individually accurate, and are not shown to the patient, who is told only that their messages have been personalized. The model cannot produce a plain-language reason for any individual classification, though it is reliable. Nothing in the messages is false, the framing serves the patient's own health goals, any savings to the system are incidental, and switching personalization off takes three taps in the settings menu.

Do our precedents settle this, and if not, what should we decide in order to be consistent with ourselves? Which of cases 1 through 6 is the closest analog, and which is closest on the dimension that actually matters here? Set out the two or three rules that fit all six verdicts, say what each predicts about the new proposal, and give your best prediction with your reason for preferring it. Then name the single further proposal whose verdict would most cleanly separate the surviving rules, and state which principle we would be committing ourselves to under each of the two possible decisions.
