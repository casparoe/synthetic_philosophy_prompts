Last night I argued for something like three hours with an old friend — a clever, contrarian engineer with no stake in the outcome — about ancient-astronaut claims, Atlantis, and the re-dated chronologies that compress a millennium of the past into a few centuries. He granted, without much resistance, that the specific claims are almost certainly false. He refused to grant that anything is *wrong with the way they are arrived at*, and by the end of the evening I found that I could not say precisely what I thought was wrong either. That irritation is what I want to turn into a funded project.

Two of his moves I still cannot answer cleanly.

The symmetry move: every historical claim is underdetermined by the surviving record. Mainstream historians extrapolate from fragments, weigh biased testimony, assume that people in the past were broadly like people now, and arrange the result into a narrative that flatters some present. So the difference between a mainstream reconstruction and a pseudohistorical one, he says, is a difference in who holds the chairs, not a difference in method — and any demarcation criterion I produce will turn out to be a description of what historians currently happen to like.

The calibration move: when reliefs appear to depict flying machines or electric lamps, we know they are pareidolia only because a dense independent record lets us identify the ordinary motif behind them. Where the record is thin, that same pattern of reasoning — an anomaly is visible, therefore something extraordinary happened — is, he says, exactly what working historians run. On that view, calling it pseudohistory is only calling it unlucky about which century it landed in.

My half-formed idea is that both moves exploit a real symmetry, but that the asymmetry lies somewhere neither of us was looking: not in the content of the claims and not in the social position of the claimants, but in whether the anomaly-generating procedure has ever been calibrated. Nobody asks what a method does when it is pointed at a period we already know well. Three threads: (i) a control-period or phantom-anomaly test — run the procedure over a densely documented century and count how much lost-knowledge and vanished-civilisation signal it manufactures from noise; (ii) concordance without transmission — the inference from surface resemblance between two cultures straight to contact or common origin, with no mechanism, no route and no dateable intermediary, which I suspect is a distinct and separately criticisable pattern rather than a special case of over-extrapolation; (iii) exit conditions — not what would in principle refute a programme, but what its community actually does the morning after a radiocarbon date or a decipherment lands: shrinks the claim, or promotes the disconfirming evidence to part of the phenomenon.

I am applying for a six-month, funded independent fellowship (October to March, no teaching, no other obligations) and need a proposal of the following shape. State the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; I want it to be something like: can a procedure-based demarcation for claims about sparsely documented periods be given that does not presuppose the falsity of the claims and that survives the symmetry and calibration objections — and, if it can, is the resulting criterion genuinely epistemic or merely sociological in disguise? Then say why the available answers leave it open: falsity-first answers beg the question against my friend; falsificationist answers stall because historical hypotheses are not individually falsifiable and are holistically protected in mainstream work too; purely sociological answers concede his point rather than answering it; and the demand for extraordinary evidence just relocates the dispute to what makes evidence extraordinary. Then the method, month by month, including how I would handle the obvious objection that a well-documented period is not a fair control precisely because the method feeds on gaps — I have a bridge case in mind, a period that is well documented overall but with deliberate local losses, and I want your view on whether it rescues the test or merely complicates it. Then why this approach rather than the alternatives, what the deliverable is — I am thinking of the criterion in a form a journal referee, a heritage body or a museum education officer could apply to a submitted manuscript or an exhibit proposal in half an hour, plus a straight answer to my friend, including the range of cases where he was simply right — and, explicitly, what would count as the project failing: if the control test shows mainstream inference generating phantom anomalies at comparable rates, if exit-condition behaviour tracks the size or age of a community rather than the mainstream/fringe boundary, or if every case collapses back into needing a first-order judgement about which claims are false. Be honest about scope: six months will stretch to a taxonomy plus two or three worked cases, not to a general theory, and I would rather be told where it balloons than be handed an ambitious plan I cannot finish.

Please do not cite anything or give me a reading list; refer to positions and arguments only, and I will add the references myself.

Format: headed sections, and no more than 700 words in total. End with a final paragraph of at most 70 words that says how confident the proposal is in its central bet — that the asymmetry I am pointing at is real — and singles out the one load-bearing assumption it is least sure of.
