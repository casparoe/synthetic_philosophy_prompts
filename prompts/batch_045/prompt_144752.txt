I'm giving the after-dinner toast at my doctoral supervisor's retirement dinner in three weeks, and I would like you to write it.

She is Miriam Halloran, and for forty years she has defended one claim: that the relation between nucleic acid replication and Gödel's self-referential constructions is not a metaphor but the same structure appearing twice. The compressed version: a strand is read two ways at once, as inert material to be operated on and as a specification of the operation, and that double reading is what Gödel exploited when he used a numbering of formulas so that a formal system could contain a sentence about itself. The difference she always insisted on is one of mood. Gödel's sentence says something about itself; a self-copying strand does something to something else in order to bring about another of itself. I have been calling this the copy/quotation distinction: a quotation is a name from which the thing named can be recovered exactly, whereas a copy is a second thing of the same kind, and nothing in the formalism marks which of the two came first.

Halloran works in a formal system derived from the Typogenetics that Hofstadter set up in Gödel, Escher, Bach, and I will state the version I need rather than assume you remember it. A strand is a finite string over four letters. Read in non-overlapping triples, it yields a sequence of amino acids, each of which names an operation on strands: cut; delete a letter; move one place left or right along a strand; switch to the other strand; copy a letter and insert the copy; insert a specified letter; stop. The resulting sequence of operations is an enzyme, and an enzyme applied to a strand yields new strands. Two features do the work. First, the strand-to-enzyme map is many-to-one, because several triples code for the same amino acid, and no operation in the system runs it backwards. Second, nothing in the formalism distinguishes a strand from its duplicate; two copies are simply two inscriptions of one string.

In her last graduate seminar she circulated an unpublished note — half the professional half of the room will have read it, her family will not have — proposing that the notion of a self-replicator be relaxed. Let the enzyme's action be stochastic, and count a strand as self-replicating when applying its enzyme to it yields, with high probability, two strands lying within small edit distance of the original. Her slogan was that replication is self-reference with the identity condition relaxed, and she thought the analogy with Gödel only earned its keep there, because that is what a cell actually does. I think the relaxation is where the analogy dies: the Gödel construction depends on the code being exact and invertible, and that is precisely what makes the self-reference literal rather than approximate. Once the identity condition is loosened you have, I would argue, not a sentence about itself but a process that tends to reconstitute its own inputs — a different phenomenon wearing the vocabulary of the first. I have said this in print, she knows I think it, and she asked me to speak anyway. The toast therefore has to be a genuine tribute from someone who dissents from the central thesis, and it has to be honest about that without being a seminar, a retraction, or a disguised attack.

The room will be about sixty people in three roughly equal groups: her former students and colleagues in logic, who know this material cold and will silently check every technical move; molecular biologists from the faculty, most of whom think the Gödel business has been a net liability for how their field is popularised and who deserve something more than a polite nod; and her family, including a nine-year-old granddaughter, for whom isomorphism is a word they have heard at Sunday lunch for decades without ever being sure what it meant.

Four constraints, which are the reason I am asking rather than writing it myself.

One: she has banned, at her own retirement, the words code, program, blueprint and language as applied to DNA or genes. Her reason is that those are exactly the words that make her claim sound like a metaphor, which is the one thing she has spent her career denying. So the toast must convey forty years of work on this material without any of those four words — and, the harder part, without quietly substituting a fifth word that does the same work. I want to know if you think that is achievable; if it is not, say which constraint to drop and why rather than producing something that tastes of euphemism.

Two: no arc. She retracted the final chapter of her 1988 book and has asked, more than once, for no summing-up. The toast needs a shape that is not a curve with a summit — I had in mind something that returns to its beginning, or repeats a passage with a difference, since that is the structure of the thing she studies — but if that strikes you as too cute for a dinner toast, propose a better non-cumulative shape.

Three: it must give the logicians at least one technically exact point that survives scrutiny, and it must concede something real to the molecular biologists rather than flattering them.

Four: six to seven minutes, spoken, warm rather than reverent, ending in a form that sixty people can raise a glass to.

Leave square-bracketed slots wherever you need a fact about her life you could not know — an early lab anecdote, the title of a paper, something her granddaughter does — and do not invent biographical detail to fill the space.

Finally: before writing, tell me plainly if the request is built on something confused. If my copy/quotation framing is muddled, if the relaxation as I have described it is not coherent, or if the constraints jointly make the job impossible in a way I have not noticed, I would rather have that than a fluent speech. And append a short note, separate from the toast, flagging two things: anything in my statement of the formal setup that a room of logicians would wince at, and the places where the speech leans on a contested reading of what self-replication requires.
