I'm supposed to give the closing remarks at our citizens' assembly handover next Thursday and I've got five minutes and I'm making a mess of it. Bit of background so you know what you're walking into.

Our city pulled 33 names out of a lottery from the electoral roll to work on the twenty-year housing and transport plan; 24 of us were left by the end, 14 months later, and we handed in a report last month. Nobody applied, nobody campaigned, we just got letters. I was one of them and I've been reading about this stuff on my own ever since — no academic background, just too much time on it — and the other members asked me to speak, which was probably a mistake.

The handover session is where we give the report to the council. The room will be: the rest of us plus partners and a couple of kids; about nine councillors, roughly half of whom voted against setting the assembly up in the first place; two people from universities who study this and will hear every wrong word; and a local reporter. Roughly five minutes, out loud, no slides.

Here's why it's hard. Three things I keep going in circles on, and one practical mess.

First, I don't think I can thank people for having "stepped up" or "put themselves forward", because nobody put themselves forward — that's the entire point. But it's not clean either: nine people dropped out over the year, and the ones who stayed were visibly a type (retired, or no kids at home, or self-employed, or already half-political). I've started calling that the second draw: the random draw picks the sample, and then a second, quieter draw happens that nobody authorised, and what's left at the end looks nothing like the city. I really don't want a speech that ends up praising the second draw by accident.

Second, I can't say "you're still ordinary people, exactly like the people you represent", because we aren't, not any more — a year of expert witnesses and a budget to move stuff around changes you, and three of our group are now talking about running for council. But I also can't say "you've become experts and you've earned the right to be heard", because that is exactly the thing the career politicians in the room say about themselves ("governance needs professionals"), and it would hand them the whole argument. I've been calling this the polished mirror: we were supposed to be a mirror of the city, but a mirror that's been sat in a committee room for a year gets polished, and after a while it shows the room something flattering.

Third, I can't say "on behalf of the city, thank you" — I was drawn, not elected, I'm not authorised, and we didn't agree anyway (two of us filed a minority note against one of the recommendations).

Which brings me to the mess. One of our recommendations is a two-term cap for councillors. Same evening, in the same room, they're doing a small presentation to a councillor who's retiring after 28 years — he was actually decent to us, he answered questions straight, and he is exactly what that recommendation is about. So I have to hand over a document that insults half the room while being gracious about a man whose career the document says shouldn't be possible. I've got an idea I've been using on this that I quite like: the handover test. Whatever someone in a job knows that can't be written down or passed along to whoever comes after them isn't the institution's knowledge, it's theirs, and it leaves the building with them. A lot of what gets called "experience" fails that test, and some of it definately passes. If the tribute to him could turn on that, I'd like it to — but only if it can be said in plain words to people who've never thought about it once.

Can you write me the five minutes? It needs to land for my fellow members, who mostly need to be told the year mattered, and for the councillors, who mostly need to be told they aren't being abolished; it shouldn't turn into a lecture on democratic theory; and it shouldn't contain anything I'd have to walk back in the pub afterwards with people who were there. Oh — and the report is probably going to be shelved by the council, everyone knows it, and I won't pretend otherwise, but I also can't send 24 people home feeling like they did a year of unpaid work for nothing. Some of them want us to keep going as a permament standing assembly; I think that's the career politician problem in a different hat and I'd rather the speech didn't invite it.

If one of my three ideas — the second draw, the polished mirror, the handover test — won't actually carry the weight I'm putting on it, say so and tell me what to use instead.
