Below are two positions on a shared datum, and then five cases that pull them apart. I would like you to compare them as theories rather than to survey a literature.

Both positions accept the following: whether a true, well-evidenced belief counts as knowledge covaries with practical facts. In the familiar sort of bank case, the subject for whom being wrong is trivial is comfortably described as knowing, while the subject for whom being wrong is ruinous is not, even though the two share exactly the same evidence and the same true proposition. Call the first position attributor contextualism (DeRose's variety is the canonical form): the sentence S knows that p expresses different propositions in different contexts of ascription, and the standard is fixed by features of the ascriber's context — which error possibilities are salient, what the ascriber is using the ascription for, how much is at stake in the practical question the ascriber is addressing. Call the second position subject-stakes invariantism, or pragmatic encroachment (Hawthorne's and Stanley's variety is the canonical form): the ascription expresses a single proposition across contexts, but whether that proposition is true depends on how the subject's epistemic position compares with a threshold, and the threshold is set by what is at stake for the subject in acting on p. On the canonical cases the two positions agree, because normally the ascriber and the subject share stakes or purposes. The following five cases are designed to decouple them. In each, the evidence, the truth of p, and the subject's reliability are held fixed across variants; only the practical and interpersonal structure changes.

1. The detached ascriber. Ben stands to lose his house if p is false. Ada, a philosopher with nothing riding on p, is asked a purely theoretical question — does Ben know? — and is explicitly not advising Ben, not assessing what he should do, and not considering whether to rely on him. Is Ada's affirmative ascription true?

2. Hidden stakes. Cara takes herself to be in a low-stakes situation and has exactly the evidence of a low-stakes subject. Unknown to her, a procedural change means that a single missed step will trigger a catastrophic loss. Does Cara know? Does it matter whether the ascriber knows about the hidden stakes? Does it matter whether anyone does?

3. Imposed stakes. Dia is in an ordinary low-stakes situation. An adversary who wants her not to count as knowing raises the cost of her being wrong — by an act that leaves her evidence, her reliability, and the truth untouched. Has the adversary destroyed her knowledge? If the answer is yes, is that an acceptable consequence of the view or a reductio of it? What does the contextualist say has changed?

4. Delegated stakes. Ada must act on whether p, her stakes are enormous, and her only route to the truth is Ben's say-so. Ben himself has nothing at stake and is not assessing p for any practical purpose. Does Ada know? Whose stakes set the threshold — Ada's, as the subject of the knowledge ascription, or Ben's, as the source of the evidence? And what, if anything, may Ben properly assert to her?

5. Managed stakes, in two directions. Eli needs to know whether p and cheaply insures against the bad outcome, thereby lowering what is at stake for him: can he come to know without gaining any evidence, merely by rearranging his practical situation? Fia publicly commits herself to a large bet on p, thereby raising her own stakes: does she lose knowledge she possessed a minute earlier? Does the answer to either question depend on why the stakes were changed — to gain knowledge, to signal confidence, or incidentally?

Please do the following.

First, for each of the five cases, state what each position predicts, and say which prediction you find more credible and why. Where a case as I have described it leaves something open — for instance, whether the relevant stakes are objective or believed, actual or merely apparent — say which further stipulation is doing the work, and what the answer is under each filling-in.

Second, and most important: assess whether the two positions are genuinely distinct hypotheses once each is permitted its natural auxiliary moves. The contextualist can say that an ascriber's context normally tracks the subject's stakes, because we usually ascribe to people in order to assess their actions or to decide whether to rely on them. The subject-stakes theorist can say that what is at stake for a subject includes the stakes of those who will act on her belief, or that the threshold is indexed to a practical question rather than to a person. If both are allowed these moves, is there any remaining disagreement, or have we arrived at a single view of the form the standard varies with how much is practically at stake, relative to some standpoint, with the only dispute left being about where the standpoint is located? Say what kind of observation would settle that residual dispute, and whether such an observation is in principle available.

Third, identify which asymmetries between the two frameworks survive that collapse. Two candidate asymmetries I would like you to evaluate: the subject-stakes view generalizes naturally from knowledge to rational belief and credence, whereas the contextualist thesis is a claim about the semantics of knows and has no obvious extension to what it is rational to believe; and the subject-stakes view makes knowledge partly a matter of a person's practical situation in a way that seems to leave inquiry hostage to practical concerns, whereas the contextualist confines the practical sensitivity to the speech act of ascription. Is the first an advantage or a liability? Is the second a virtue or an evasion?

Close with an overall verdict: if you had to work within one of the two frameworks, which would you choose, and what is the single consideration that most determines your choice? Be explicit about which of the above considerations are genuinely discriminating and which are merely verbal or merely matters of formulation.
