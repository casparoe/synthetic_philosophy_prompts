I'm in the first year of a doctorate and I'm organising a reading group that has spent a whole term on the 1979 critique of the adaptationist programme — Gould and Lewontin's spandrels paper, the architectural analogy of the tapering spaces left over where arches meet the base of a dome, the complaint that adaptationists carve organisms into independent parts and invent functions for each, and the standing reply that selection is the only known source of complex functional fit. We keep having the same argument, so I want to try something different: draft the joint statement that a good-faith adversarial collaboration would produce between two researchers who have been talking past each other for forty years. Call them the optimality modeller — someone in the behavioural ecology tradition who holds that the productive first move with any trait is to ask what problem it might have been shaped to solve, to build the model, and to treat a failed prediction as information about the model's assumptions rather than as a refutation of the method — and the structuralist critic, who holds that the organism arrives with a body plan, a developmental system and a history, that many traits are by-products, later co-options (exaptations, in the term introduced a few years after the spandrels paper) or consequences of allometry and phylogenetic inertia, and that sliding from 'this trait serves F now' to 'this trait was built by selection for F' is a real error rather than a harmless shorthand.

What I want from you is the document, not a verdict on who was right. Give me a numbered section of agreed claims both could sign — about what the 1979 paper did and did not establish, about what would count as evidence that some trait is an adaptation, about what nobody has shown yet — followed by a disagreements table in which each side's position is stated in a wording that side would itself accept, and then the cruxes. My own suspicion is that 'adaptationism' is being used for at least three separable theses: one about the causal powers of selection, one about which question to ask first at the bench or in the field, and one about some particular trait; and that much of the shouting comes from people who accept one and are heard as asserting another. Say whether that split does any work or quietly begs the question, and mark off separately any part of the dispute that is merely verbal — a historical notion of adaptation versus one defined by current utility, say. I also want two things I have not seen these debates produce. The first is a base-rate commitment: both sides should fix a reference class of traits in advance — a named clade, or a year's worth of trait explanations in a journal they both read — say what fraction they predict will be vindicated as adaptations within some stated period, and say who would do the coding and by what rule; if they cannot agree on a reference class, that failure is itself a finding and I want it recorded. The second is an accounting of what I have started calling the asymmetry of omission: getting an adaptationist story wrong and getting a constraint story wrong are different errors with different costs, the first sending people after a function that was never there and the second closing off a line of work that was about to pay off, so each side should say which error it takes to be worse in the short run, which in the long run, and whether that judgement rather than any biological claim is what actually drives its methodological stance. Please also flag, explicitly, any sentence you catch yourself writing that both could sign but that no one has ever denied.

Several of the cruxes should be fastened to cases, and to save you guessing, here are the four I want used, with the facts as I understand them — tell me if I have any of them wrong, but please don't go hunting for more. First: the giant panda strips bamboo with a greatly enlarged wrist bone that works as an opposable sixth digit; it is a wrist bone, not a finger. Second: in vertebrate eyes the photoreceptors sit behind the neural wiring, the nerve fibres run across the light-facing surface and bundle to exit through it, leaving a blind spot, whereas in the independently evolved eyes of octopuses and squids the photoreceptors face the light and there is nothing structurally comparable. Third: in mammals the nerve serving the larynx leaves the skull, runs down into the chest, loops around a major artery and runs back up, so that in a giraffe the detour is metres long. Fourth: Darwin inferred from the length of a Madagascan orchid's nectar spur that a pollinator with a tongue of matching length must exist, and a moth of roughly that description was later found. For each case, say what each side should make of it, whether either side owes the other a concession, and whether the case is decisive or merely illustrative. I am especially interested in whether the fourth gives the modeller a tie-breaker — a prediction made before the observation — that the critic should accept as the strongest available evidence for the method, and in what the critic is entitled to demand in return. Finish by having each side name the single finding that would make it abandon its strongest claim, and the one line in the other's wording it would refuse to sign.
