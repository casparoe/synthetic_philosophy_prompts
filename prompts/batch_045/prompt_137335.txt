I'm taking a graduate seminar on philosophy of religion, and we keep hitting a distinction that the introductory readings blur. On the one hand there is the kalām-style argument: whatever begins to exist has a cause; the universe began to exist; therefore the universe has a cause — followed by a second phase of conceptual analysis in which that cause is argued to be timeless, immaterial, enormously powerful, and personal, on the grounds that only agent-causation makes sense of a changeless cause producing a temporal effect. On the other hand there is the contingency argument in its Leibnizian and Thomistic forms: contingent things exist; their existence calls for an explanation that cannot simply be another contingent thing; therefore there is a necessary being which explains why there is anything contingent at all — followed by its own second phase, in which simplicity, uniqueness, and the rest of the divine attributes get extracted from necessity. The suggestion I want to test is that these are not two mutually reinforcing routes to the same place, but two arguments with different load-bearing commitments, different vulnerabilities, and possibly inconsistent requirements on whatever they conclude to.

One lens I have been playing with is the difference between demanding a cause and demanding a ground. The first demand is for something that produces, brings about, or initiates; the second is for something in virtue of which the contingent is the way it is, and it need not be causal at all. If the kalām argument is essentially committed to the first demand and the contingency argument to the second, then they should come apart on cases my seminar has not discussed. Call the first the Bootstrap case: the Big Bang is produced by a causally efficacious later state of the very universe it initiates, a closed causal loop in which every event has a cause and the beginning of the universe has a cause, but the cause is internal to the thing it causes. Call the second the Cheshire regress: an eternal series of contingent things, each brought into existence by its immediate predecessor and each ceasing to exist in the very act of producing its successor, so that no two members are ever co-present, the series has no first member, and the series is never all there at once to serve as an object of explanation in its own right.

So here is what I would like from you. First, identify which single step in each argument is genuinely load-bearing: which premise or inference, if you removed it, would leave the argument with nothing, and are those load-bearing steps the same step? Second, run both cases past both arguments: does the Bootstrap satisfy the kalām premises while hollowing out its conclusion, and does it satisfy or violate the contingency argument's demand for a sufficient reason; does the Cheshire regress refute the kalām argument's temporal premise while leaving the contingency argument untouched, and can the usual whole-series move get off the ground when the series is never co-present? Third, compare the two second phases — the route from first cause to personal agent versus the route from necessary being to the divine attributes — and tell me which bridge is more precarious and why. Please close with an actual verdict rather than a survey: if you had to retain one of the two arguments and abandon the other, which would you keep, and would running them together be any stronger than the survivor alone, or is the pairing redundant, or worse, in tension? I am interested in the structure of the reasoning, not in who defended which version or where I should read about it.
