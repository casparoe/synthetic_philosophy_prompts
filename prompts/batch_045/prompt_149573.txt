I'm in a final-year philosophy-and-law seminar on punishment, and my term paper argues that most of the retributivism-versus-consequentialism literature is answering a question no institution actually faces. The question an institution faces is: which sentence, for this offender, under these procedures, given what we know about how the system in fact operates. So before I go further into that literature I want the specification. What would a theory of punishment have to be able to deliver to count as adequate for a real sentencing commission — one that must generate, check, and publicly defend actual sentence lengths in a jurisdiction with entrenched rights guarantees, guilty pleas resolving the large majority of cases, criminal liability for corporations, and a known nonzero wrongful-conviction rate?

Please write the desiderata. I want a numbered list in which every requirement comes with the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and with your verdict on whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of one camp. Group the list if that helps, and add requirements I've missed. The cases I have in mind, and which I want handled rather than gestured at:

The innocence constraint, and the case that tests how strong it really is: an official who can avert a lethal riot only by framing and punishing one person known to be uninvolved. Whether that is excluded by a right, by what punishment means, or by what authorizing it would do to officials and to the law's credibility, matters to me.

The line between a penalty and a price. A congestion charge, a regulatory fine, punitive damages against a firm, and a criminal fine look functionally similar; if a sanction is only a price, the procedural protections of criminal law do not attach. The theory has to say where it draws that line and why.

Grip on magnitudes, not just on order. Any view can say that worse crimes deserve more. I need to know what in the theory fixes why a term is two years rather than six months or twenty, whether there is an upper bound that holds independently of deterrent payoff, and whether the bottom of the scale can be non-custodial at all.

Whether probability may be traded against severity. Where clearance rates are low, deterrence reasoning pushes expected punishment up by raising the tariff, and proportionality pushes back; a commission needs an explicit answer, not a shrug.

Marginal deterrence: the requirement that the tariff not give an offender a reason to prefer the more serious of two offences — the case where the penalty is the same whether the victim is left alive or not.

Robustness to error. Given a known rate of wrongful conviction, does the theory discount what may be done to the guilty in order to reduce what will be done to the innocent, and are those error costs spread across all sentences or concentrated at the severe end? A pure desert view has a clean answer here, and I suspect it is the wrong one.

The plea-bargaining shadow. Since most sentences are not the output of a trial, the theory must say what it is justifying — the sentence pronounced, the sentence served, or the expected sentence produced by a bargaining system — and must have a verdict on the trial penalty and on the discount for pleading guilty.

Defendants that cannot suffer. Corporate fines fall on shareholders, employees, or customers rather than on anything that feels them. The theory must say whether desert is even coherent for legal persons, and whether treating them on deterrence grounds alone is consistent with treating them as agents elsewhere in the law.

Temporal decay. The person released at seventy-eight after forty years is not the person who offended at thirty-eight. Does the theory permit release because the desert has been outrun — cases: whole-life tariffs, geriatric and compassionate release, remittal for rehabilitation — or does it require the full term?

Blameworthiness versus liability to state punishment, in the cases of small participants in large wrongs — the clerk, the driver, the junior officer — where the architects are dead, retired, or beyond reach.

The gap between nominal sentence and lived severity: the same term is far harsher for a prisoner who is elderly, disabled, or held in isolation. Must the theory equalize the burden or the number?

Lapse and amnesty. Statutes of limitation, pardons, and truth-and-reconciliation style settlements: do these extinguish desert, or only bar its enforcement? A theory of punishment, as opposed to a theory of blame, should be able to say which.

Finally, administrability: whatever the theory says, officials of ordinary competence must be able to reach the same sentence from it, and I want to know whether that demand is itself in tension with taking desert seriously at all.

Three things beyond the list, which are the parts I most want. First: if some subset of these requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, say which pair or triple breaks, and which one you would give up — the list itself is allowed to be a result rather than a menu. Second: name the single requirement that does the most work in excluding candidates, and defend that choice. Third: name one requirement that is widely insisted on in this literature and that you think is really a camp prejudice — something only a retributivist, only a consequentialist, or only an abolitionist would care about — and say why it should come off the list.

Please commit to all of these rather than surveying the options for me; argue for the verdicts you give. Where you think the answer genuinely turns on a prior commitment I have not specified, name that commitment and what turns on it, but still state which way you would go. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases: a short scorecard at the end — a few lines each on retributivism, deterrence-based consequentialism, a mixed or desert-capped consequentialist view, and a restorative or abolition-oriented view, if you think it belongs on the list at all — is enough. Around 1,400 words; a table is fine if it earns its place.
