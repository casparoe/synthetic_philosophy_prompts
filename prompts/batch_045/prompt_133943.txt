Six weeks ago I was asked to chair the grants committee of a small private foundation that recently received an unexpected bequest: roughly $1.8M, to be spent over three years, earmarked for research on which computations, if any, might be owed moral consideration, and on how one would tell. My own training is a philosophy BA from fifteen years ago; the rest of the committee is two computer scientists, a retired judge, and the donor's daughter. We can fund four to six small grants, we cannot fund large-scale ML work, and we meet twice a year, so what we decide this autumn is largely what the money will have bought by 2028.

Three framings emerged from our first meeting, and I would rather have them stress-tested than have you build on them:

1. *The counting problem.* Most of the debate we've been shown asks which properties a computation must have to matter morally, but almost none of it asks what the unit of account is. A deployed system is not one thing: there are training runs, checkpoints, fine-tuned variants, distilled copies, ensembles, parallel instances serving overlapping users, and now a growing share of the total computation happening at inference rather than training time. Our sense is that the practical stakes are at least as sensitive to how we count as to which property we look for, and that these questions are strangely independent.

2. *Marker drift.* Suppose we did settle on some measurable proxy for moral relevance. There is now a large commercial incentive to preserve capabilities while reducing whatever the proxy measures, and capability-preserving architectures that score low on one proxy can be found by search rather than by malice. We have been calling this Goodhart-on-the-marker, and we want to know whether it is a genuine problem for the whole research program or merely an enforcement detail.

3. *The gradualist worry.* If moral relevance accrues at all, it plausibly accrues gradually across training rather than snapping on at deployment, which would make the morally significant events the training decisions themselves. But we cannot tell whether this is a deep implication or a confusion brought on by taking our own metaphors literally.

We keep being told the field divides into those who want to settle the underlying theory first, those who want to act under uncertainty using expected-value or precautionary reasoning, and those who want to fix the accounting before anything else. Please treat that ordering itself as one of the things we are deciding.

What I want from you is an agenda, not a survey: which four to six questions this portfolio should actually buy answers to, in rank order, with the ranking defended. For each, say what makes it upstream of other work, whether it is tractable for philosophers and small interdisciplinary teams without large compute budgets, how neglected it is relative to its importance, and what a completed answer would look like concretely enough that we could recognise it in a final report. Also tell us what we should explicitly decline to fund, and which findings over the next three years would move items up or down the list. Where a recommendation depends on an empirical claim nobody can currently settle, mark it as uncertain and say how much the recommendation would change if the claim went the other way. Please do not give us a reading list or point us at the literature; we have no way to act on citations.

Please structure your reply as follows:

- **Framing** (~150 words): how you are interpreting our situation, and any assumption in the above you think is mistaken.
- **Criteria and their trade-offs** (~250 words): the dimensions you are ranking on and how you weigh them against each other.
- **The recommended portfolio** (~700 words): the four to six questions in rank order, each with its rationale.
- **What we should decline** (~200 words): the proposals we will likely receive and should turn down, and why.
- **Signals that would reorder the list** (~250 words): what to watch for, and what each would change.
- **Bottom line** (~100 words): the single ordering claim you would defend if challenged.
