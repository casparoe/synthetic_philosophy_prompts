I've been retired from experimental geophysics for about six years now — forty years of diffusion and fluid flow in porous rock — and I've recently started going to a philosophy reading group at the local library, which has got me stuck on one question: how can a thought experiment in science give you knowledge, when by construction you aren't taking any new measurements? Here is where I land on the cases, going on gut.

- Two stones, one heavy one light, tied together and dropped off a tower: delivers. That's real knowledge, not restating what we already had.
- The spinning bucket of water on a rope: delivers something, but not what its author thought it did. It constrains any future theory of motion; it doesn't establish absolute space.
- The demon sorting fast and slow molecules through a little trapdoor: delivers, and I'd rate it the most impressive one here — except the payoff, as far as I can tell, only arrived much later with information theory, so I'm not sure the thought experiment itself is what did the work.
- The man in the falling lift with the cable cut: definitely delivers.
- The cat in the box with the poison: doesn't deliver. A vivid way of stating a problem you already had; nothing comes out that wasn't put in.
- The two-particle setup where measuring one seems to fix the other at a distance: doesn't deliver either — or rather, it delivered only once somebody actually did it in a lab. On its own it was a recipe, not a result.
- One of my own, from my field: I imagine a sealed sediment column, closed at the bottom, at steady state, with diffusion balancing a slow upward flow of pore water, and I can more or less see that the chloride profile has to be exponential. It feels like learning something. I want to say it isn't knowledge at all — it's me unpacking equations I already accepted.
- Another of my own: a sealed bottle of seawater with a bit of organic matter and some rock, left for a thousand years. In my head I can watch the oxygen go, then nitrate, then manganese and iron, then sulfate, then methane. This one I go back and forth on.

The pair I can't reconcile is the stones and my chloride column. Same move, it seems to me — set up an imaginary system, deduce what has to happen — and I say yes to the first and no to the second.

My tentative principle is that a thought experiment yields knowledge only when it shows that commitments I already hold can't all be true together, a kind of reductio on my whole picture rather than a derivation inside one theory; otherwise it's bookkeeping. But the demon misfires: it isn't a reductio and it isn't bookkeeping, and I rate it highest of all. And my principle should probably also be kinder to the two-particle case than I was.

So: is there one principle that gets these, or close? Which of my verdicts do I have to drop, and what does the surviving principle say about the seawater bottle? Give me its verdict on a case I haven't listed too — say, whether imagining a perfectly frictionless pendulum or an infinitely deep ocean tells us anything about the actual world. And please skip the literature: I want the argument, not who said it. Tell me how confident you are in what you're claiming, and single out the bit you're least sure of.
