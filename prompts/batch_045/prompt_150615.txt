I'm giving a seventy-five-minute session on Arrow's impossibility theorem to a reading group of about twelve machine-learning researchers. They routinely collapse many human preference rankings over model outputs into a single ranking; they are entirely comfortable with functions, profiles and quantifiers; they have no patience for philosophical throat-clearing; and they will arrive assuming the result is a fact about elections and therefore has nothing to say to them. What I want from you is the plan for the session, not the session itself: do not explain the theorem to me.

Reply as a numbered list of exactly five items, with no preamble and no closing line, each item two or three sentences at most.

1. The order in which the components must be introduced, with the dependency that forces each ordering — what has to be in place before the next piece can even be stated. Say where in the sequence the statement of the result itself should land relative to the argument for it, and why that placement rather than the other one.
2. The single example that carries the whole thing, drawn from their world rather than from elections, and what that example must contain and must lack in order to carry it. I take it that two alternatives are useless here, that the example has to be displayable as two profiles that agree on every individual's ranking of some one pair while differing elsewhere, and that it must carry no natural intensity or score information — but tell me what else it needs and why.
3. The misconceptions, in the order in which they will form, each with the exact point in the sequence at which to head it off. I expect two of them: that the theorem shows voting to be irrational or democracy impossible, and that independence of irrelevant alternatives means dropping a hopeless candidate must not change the winner. Find the rest — I suspect one about what "dictator" means in the statement of the result, one about whether score-based or utility-based aggregation escapes it, and one about whether the way to repair an impossibility theorem is to add further conditions.
4. One question to end with that tells me whether the idea has landed rather than merely depressed them. My candidate is to hand them the aggregation rule their own pipeline uses and ask which condition it violates and for the pair of profiles that witnesses the violation; improve on that if you can.
5. What I should openly decline to cover at this level, and what I should say out loud about why. I think that includes saying that the session supplies a vocabulary for diagnosing any aggregation rule and not a recommendation about which one to adopt, and that I should not claim the result carries over to their case when annotators rank overlapping but different sets of items — but tell me what else belongs on the refusal list.
