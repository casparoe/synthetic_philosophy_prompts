I'm writing a novel and I've got myself into a hole. Three characters break the same law in the same year, and I want the reader to feel that one of them is a hero, one is a pain in the neck, and one is just a criminal — but every time I try to say why, I end up writing a line of dialogue that sounds like a seminar. So I want the spec first, before I commit to any more scenes.

Give me the desiderata: what a theory of political obligation has to deliver, and separately what an account of civil disobedience has to deliver, if its supposed to be good enough for a character to use in first person *and* good enough for me to plot with. For each requirement, the case that motivates it — ideally one of these, since they're the scenes I actually have:

N, a mid-level official who swore an oath and then leaks the internal memo; D, who has no vote, no passport and no plausible claim to have consented to anything, has lived here nineteen years, works, pays tax, and refuses to register for the thing everyone has to register for; M, who hides her son the night before he's due to report and tells nobody; K, who pays every tax except the slice that funds one programme, and writes to say so. And then the same law eighteen months later, after the government has changed twice: I need to know whether M and K still owe the same thing, and whether the fact that they complied for a while changes what they owe now.

Things I specifically want your view on. Whether an account really has to answer the "why this lot and not any other reasonably decent state" question, or whether that's just a prejudice of one camp. Whether it has to keep "the law is valid" apart from "you have to obey it". Whether it has to handle the character who is only about sixty per cent sure the law is unjust, whether "you're permitted to act on your judgement" and "you're judgement is right" come apart, and who carries the risk of being wrong. Whether what makes D's refusal disobedience rather than crime is the publicity, the willingness to take the penalty, or the fact that it's addressed to an audience — and whether an account that demands all three quietly rules out resistance in places where going public gets you killed. Whether it has to say what the character owes afterwards — a trial, an apology, repair, keeping the rest of the law — or whether that's beyond what a theory of obligation is for. How much sacrifice is enough: I don't want a theory that requires my characters to be saints, but I don't want one that lets K off because the form was annoying. Whether it has to distinguish the person who pays, the one who drives the lorry and the one who votes, or whether they're all equally implicated. Whether emigrating discharges the thing, given that M can't leave because she's caring for her mother. And whether any of this is about law specifically or about authority generally — one of my subplots is a character defying a union directive and I don't know if that's the same animal.

Then the part I care about most: which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just what one camp happens to like, and flag any pair you think can't be jointly met. I've got a suspicion that "must bind even when you think the requirement is wrong, because that's what authority is" and "must never require you to abet something seriously unjust" can't both survive, and that "must give clear first-personal guidance" fights with "mustn't tell the agent to defer when she's probably right". Tell me if I'm wrong. And say which single requirement knocks out the most candidates — that's the one I'll put in the mouth of my barrister.

No long scorecard: a couple of lines each at the end on consent, fair-play/benefit, natural-duty and associativist accounts is plenty, and skip anything that needs a library. British English throughout (judgement, colour, defence), and prose rather than a wall of headings — I'm a novelist, I'll notice.
