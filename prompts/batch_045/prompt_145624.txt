Hi — I chair the language standards group at a mid-sized nonprofit newsroom (~60 journalists, plus a comment section we still, foolishly, moderate ourselves). We recieve somewhere between twenty and forty requests a week from reporters and producers about slurs: do we print the word, bleep it on audio, write the initialism, use a euphemism like "the N-word", describe the act in other words, or reproduce it because it's in a document, lyric, or post we're quoting. Right now it's whichever duty editor is on, deciding in under ten minutes with no training in any of this, then having to defend the call to readers and to our own staff, and the answers are wildly inconsistent.

What I need is a decision procedure, not an essay. Something the duty editor can actually walk through at speed: the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each one, the point where the answer is just "no, don't" and we stop, and the point where it gets handed up to me or to the standards group because what's left is a question about what we care about, not about what the word does. Please use whatever the semantics/pragmatics side of this actually distinguishes — I don't want a reading list or citations, I want the distinctions themselves: what a slur contributes to what's said versus what it does to the people hearing it, whether the derogatory bit is part of the meaning or a side-effect of the use, what changes when the word is quoted or reported or mentioned rather than used, whether "who is speaking" and "who is listening" matter differently.

The cases our current policy handles badly, and that the procedure has to handle: in-group or reclaimed use that we're quoting from a source; a commenter who never types the word but writes something that only does its work if the reader supplies it (initialisms, "you know which one", emoji, leetspeak); a term being reclaimed by one group while outsiders still use it as a slur against a different group; historical documents, song titles, and place names; and cases where the target group has essentially no one in the conversation.

Then run it on three: a producer who wants to quote a council member's voicemail verbatim in a story about that council member; a music review quoting a lyric in which the artist uses a slur for their own group; and our blanket house rule of always substituting the initialism, always, in every context — including in a piece we're planning on reclamation. I suspect that last one does not survive the procedure, but if it does survive, say that too. Also: say plainly where the procedure misfires and what it can't settle, since whatever we adopt has to be explainable in one sentence to a reader who's angry about it.
