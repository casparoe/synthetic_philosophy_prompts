I teach philosophy of science, and my own research is on causation and measurement: interventionist accounts of causal explanation, invariance under intervention, potential-outcome and DAG representations, and, on the measurement side, construct validity, latent-variable models, and tests of measurement invariance across populations and across time. Next term I am taking over our graduate module on the philosophy of psychiatry, which is not my field, and I would like help restating one of its central debates in the vocabulary I actually think in — and, more usefully, help seeing what that vocabulary cannot say.

The debate is the one about whether psychiatric categories pick out kinds that are discovered rather than instituted, and what turns on the answer. The positions I want translated are these: the medical-model claim that at least some categories approximate natural kinds whose boundaries empirical inquiry converges on (major depression is the usual example, though the attention-deficit and autism categories, and the newer prodromal or risk categories, are also in play); the looping-effects argument Ian Hacking developed, on which applying a human kind to people changes those people and thereby changes the kind, so that classifier and classified are not independent; the harmful-dysfunction analysis, which tries to hold a disorder together out of a value component and an etiological one; and the conventionalist reply that psychiatric kinds are practical kinds whose boundaries are fixed by what we need them for — prognosis, treatment response, service eligibility — rather than found.

To keep this from becoming a survey, I want to fix a case. Suppose a revision of a diagnostic manual introduces a new category — call it Compensatory Exhaustion Syndrome — defined by six months of fatigue, non-restorative sleep, difficulty sustaining attention, and a self-report of maintaining emotional performance beyond capacity, together with distress or impairment and not better explained by another condition. Suppose further that, over the following decade, all of the following hold: (1) prevalence triples, and most of the increase is in presentations below the severity threshold the pre-inclusion literature had treated as the syndrome's core, so that the latent structure recovered from symptom data is not stable across pre- and post-inclusion cohorts; (2) people diagnosed with it increasingly describe their experience in the manual's own idiom, and, holding baseline severity fixed, their symptom trajectories worsen relative to comparable pre-inclusion cohorts; (3) disability insurers, employers, and schools begin to use the code, and the composition of the diagnosed population shifts in ways that track eligibility rules rather than severity; (4) in a later revision the criteria are amended so that they fit better the presentations that (1) through (3) produced — call this criterion capture; and (5) patient-advocacy groups and some clinicians then argue that the criteria should be revised again, on the ground that the category now distorts the self-understanding of the people it applies to and that those people should have a say in how it is drawn.

What I want is a translation, not an explanation. Give me each position's verdict about whether Compensatory Exhaustion Syndrome picks out something real, rendered as far as possible in my terms: interventions and the variables they set, invariance and its failure, the classification itself as something one could intervene on, identified versus unidentified latent structure, measurement invariance over cohorts, and whatever else in that neighbourhood does real work. Where a rendering is forced, say so; where you had to choose between two non-equivalent renderings, say so and say which of the four positions the choice favours, since I would rather know that my vocabulary begs a question than have the begging hidden inside a translation.

Then I want the residue, and I want it to be the longer half. Which claims in this debate have no counterpart in a causal-and-measurement vocabulary at all? I have in mind especially the first-personal material in (2) — that how people understand themselves partly constitutes what is being classified — the normative and political material in (5), the thought that looping effects give the classified a standing they would not otherwise have, and the discovered-versus-instituted contrast itself, which may turn out to be two questions rather than one. For each item in the residue, give me your view on whether it marks a genuine lack in the framework I work in — something about classification that a vocabulary of variables and interventions cannot represent — or whether it marks a place where the psychiatry literature is trading on an ambiguity that my vocabulary simply refuses to reproduce. If the problem is that intervening on the classification variable is itself what changes the population, so that the interventionist apparatus has nothing to grip, tell me that plainly rather than constructing an analogy.

I would rather have continuous prose than bullet points, somewhere in the range of a thousand to fifteen hundred words, and I would rather you flagged the places where you are unsure whether a claim translates or fails to translate than smoothed them over.
