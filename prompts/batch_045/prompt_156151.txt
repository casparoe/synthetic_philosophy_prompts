I've been reading about metaphysics on my own — no formal training, just a shelf of library books and a friend I argue with on Sunday walks — and I keep coming back to the identity of indiscernibles and the well-known example of two exactly similar iron spheres suspended in an otherwise empty universe, each a mile from the other and nothing else anywhere. My friend thinks that example settles the matter against the principle; I am not so sure, because every defence of the principle I have read seems to reach for one of three moves, and I cannot tell how much work each of them is really doing, or whether they are even available in every case.

So what I would like from you is a constructed case in which there are exactly two things that are indiscernible in every respect, and in which the twoness is something the scenario forces on you rather than something you are allowed to stipulate. The case should be one paragraph long, built out of ordinary objects — spheres, lamps, bells, coins, anything I could set up on a kitchen table — and not out of quantum particles; I tried reading about those and got lost. The constraints are:

1. No primitive thisness. You may not individuate them by saying that one has the property of being itself, or of being this very thing, or by any other property that just amounts to pointing.
2. No landmark and no asymmetry. They need not be alone in the universe, but whatever else there is must be arranged symmetrically with respect to them, so that being the left-hand one, being the one nearer the wall, and being the one at such-and-such a position in absolute space are all unavailable.
3. The difficult one: the twoness may not be read off the fact that the two of them stand in some relation that nothing bears to itself. In the textbook case people say that each sphere is a mile from the other sphere and no sphere is a mile from itself, so there must be two of them; that argument has started to look circular to me, since it seems to help itself to two relata in order to conclude that there are two things. Your case therefore has to contain some other consideration on which there are two, not one is undeniable. The only idea I have had is that two of a thing often has effects that one of it does not — it is heavier, or brighter, or louder — but I genuinely cannot tell whether that secretly assumes the very thing it is meant to establish, so if you go down that road I want you to check it rather than merely assert it.
4. The indiscernibility has to hold across the whole scenario and not just at the outset: nothing may happen to one of them that does not happen to the other.

After the scenario, please give me: an argument that the two really are indiscernible, ideally from the fact that swapping them leaves the total situation unchanged rather than from a list of properties you happened to check; an account of exactly what makes the twoness undeniable, together with a check that this does not quietly lean on any of the three moves I have banned; the single feature of the case that is doing the work, and what becomes of the case if that feature is removed — my guess is that it then collapses back into the bare two-spheres example, and I would like to know whether that guess is right; and the strongest reply still open to someone who wants to keep the principle, including how much that reply costs them here compared with what it costs them against the original example. If you think the four constraints cannot all be satisfied, tell me so plainly, say which one has to give, and explain why.
