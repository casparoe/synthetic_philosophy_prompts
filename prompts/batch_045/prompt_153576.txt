Hey, I've been going back and forth with a friend about the Ellsberg thing and I want you to argue me out of my position — or tell me straight that my position is right, but commit either way, don't just lay out both sides and leave it to me.

The setup: an urn with 90 balls. Exactly 30 are red. The other 60 are black and yellow in some proportion nobody will tell you. One ball gets drawn and you win $100 if its color is in the event you bet on. You choose in two separate decisions:

(1) red vs. black
(2) red-or-yellow vs. black-or-yellow

The famous pattern is red over black, and black-or-yellow over red-or-yellow. What makes people think that's fishy: call b the unknown chance of black. Red is exactly 1/3, so betting red over black means you're acting like b < 1/3. But black-or-yellow is exactly 2/3 while red-or-yellow is 1 − b, so betting black-or-yellow over red-or-yellow means you're acting like b > 1/3. No single value of b makes both choices come out right.

Here's the twist my friend added. Before you commit to a bet, you can ask for balls to be pulled out one at a time, shown to you, and put back, so the urn itself isn't changed. Up to 20 of them, you can stop whenever you want, and it's all free. Also — this matters to her — there's no adversary in her version. The composition was fixed by a machine on the other side of the world, nobody in the room knows it, and nobody is betting against you or profiting from your choice.

My position: the peeks change nothing. The thing you're unsure about is how many black balls there are, a handfull of draws doesn't settle that, so "go with the option whose odds you actually know" is exactly as good advice after the peeks as before, and the whole addition is a distraction from the real question.

My friend's position: the peeks break it. As I understand her argument, it goes like this. If you're the kind of person who always bets on the known-odds option, then no sequence of peeks can ever change what you do: even twenty black balls in a row is still compatible with there being one black ball and fifty-nine yellow ones (you put them back, so nothing is ruled out), and that leftover possibility keeps black's lowest possible chance at 1/90, which is way below 1/3. So the information is worth nothing to you. And a policy that makes free information worth nothing has gone wrong somewhere. She also says — and this is the bit I can't answer — that if you patch your rule so the peeks do move you, you've basically just become somebody with a prior over the compositions, which is the thing you were resisting in the first place.

So: take a side and defend it. Is the peek version a real problem for the Ellsberg choices, or is my "nothing changes" reply basically fine? I want one answer with the best reasoning you've got for it, and I want you to actually engage with the other side rather than mention it and move on.

Two things I don't want: a tour of the literature with names of papers and theorems attached (I can't look anything up, and I don't care who said what first), and a "reasonable people disagree" shrug. Commit. And if you think the whole thing turns on some assumption neither of us has stated, say what it is and then tell me which side wins assuming it.
