I am applying for a twelve-month research fellowship and need a two-page proposal, written for a selection committee whose members are mostly not decision theorists. Please draft it in my voice: polished but not ornate, minimal formalism, no hedging where a definite claim is available, and no citations or bibliography — I will insert those myself.

Here is the half-formed idea I want turned into a proposal. Risk-weighted expected utility, the framework developed in Lara Buchak's *Risk and Rationality* (Oxford, 2013), evaluates a prospect by expected utility taken with respect to a transformed probability distribution rather than the true one: a *pessimistic* weighting shifts weight toward the worse-ranked outcomes of the prospect, an *optimistic* weighting shifts weight toward the better-ranked ones. The framework, and nearly all of the controversy around it, has been elaborated as a thesis about one person's prudential rationality — about whether it can be rational, and not merely permissible-by-taste, to depart from expected-utility maximization when the risk is yours.

But the decisions where this framework would do real practical work are not decisions anyone makes about their own prospects. They are decisions made for, or on behalf of, many people at once: a public-health regulator choosing between suppression strategies, an endowment trustee setting a spending rule, a foundation choosing between near-term benefits it can secure now and a low-probability catastrophic risk it might reduce. In those cases there is no single subject whose attitude toward her own risk could ground the weighting, and I think the extension of the theory to that case is not unique. I can see two natural candidates, and I think they come apart:

- *Aggregate-first weighting*: fix a social welfare function, evaluate each possible state of the world by it, and apply the risk transformation to the resulting social prospect.
- *Person-by-person weighting*: derive each individual's own prospect from the joint distribution over outcomes, apply the risk transformation to each person's prospect separately, and sum.

They coincide when there is one person and when risks across people are perfectly correlated; they diverge when risks are independent across individuals and when the size of the population is itself uncertain. Under person-by-person weighting, risk aversion washes out as risks diversify across a large population, so that in exactly the cases of greatest practical interest — many people each exposed to a small independent chance of a bad outcome — the theory collapses back toward ordinary expected-utility aggregation. Under aggregate-first weighting it retains its bite, but it invites the charge that the risk function is not an attitude toward risk at all, only a second and disguised social-welfare parameter doing work close to ex ante priority for the worse-off, which would double-count if the social welfare function already carries a priority or inequality term. Person-by-person weighting has a different vice: because it operates on each person's marginal prospect, its recommendations are sensitive to how finely we individuate the bearers of prospects — person-stages or whole lives, actual or merely possible future people, households or nations as single bearers — whereas aggregate-first weighting is not sensitive in that way. If that diagnosis is right, the two candidates are not competing versions of one theory but theories with different vulnerability profiles, and the choice between them may be decided by a question about individuation that the individual-choice literature never had to face.

What I ultimately want out of the project is the reverse implication: whether the argument for treating risk-weighting as rationally permissible in one's own case survives being generalized, or whether it was quietly relying on there being a single subject who both holds the attitude and bears the risk. If the only defensible many-person versions are the ones that collapse into a social-welfare parameter or evaporate under diversification, then the permissivist position in the individual case was drawing on something other than risk, and that would be a substantial negative result for the individual-case debate.
