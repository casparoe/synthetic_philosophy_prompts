I'm in a graduate reading group on the metaphysics of mind, and last week one member of it — a mereologist by training, which I suspect is where this comes from — handed me a short argument designed to show that the combination problem is not an objection to panpsychism at all. I've gone over it repeatedly and cannot find the seam, but I also can't accept the conclusion, since if it works it seems to take a whole family of objections down with it. He set it out in numbered steps, which I've reproduced as faithfully as I can, giving his two background assumptions names (Uniformity, Scale-Invariance) so we could argue about them separately:

(1) The objection, stated carefully: panpsychism holds that the ultimates of fundamental physics have experiences, each had by its own micro-subject. But a human being at a time has a single unified experience — one subject for the whole sensory field. So panpsychism owes us a relation R such that many micro-experiences, each belonging to a distinct micro-subject, come to constitute one macro-experience belonging to one macro-subject. Summing subjects yields a collection of subjects, not a subject; causal and functional relations among experiences yield at most coordination, not unity. No candidate R is available, and so panpsychism cannot account for the very datum — the unity of ordinary experience — it was supposed to secure. Call this the subject-summing version of the problem.
(2) Methodological premise: an objection that every theory of mind equally faces cannot be a reason to prefer one theory of mind over another. So whether (1) is a genuine cost to panpsychism depends on whether the problem is distinctive to it.
(3) The bridge case: my experience right now has experiential parts — the visual experience of the left half of my visual field, of the right half, the auditory stream, the proprioceptive one, and so on. Each of these is, apparently, an experience. So experience-to-experience composition is not merely something we demand of the panpsychist; we have an actual instance of it in front of us, in the ordinary case, before any panpsychist helps themselves to it.
(4) Uniformity and Scale-Invariance: whatever relation does the composing in the bridge case cannot be sensitive to what the parts are made of or to how large they are. If experiences compose an experience when the parts are neural events, then experiences compose an experience when the parts are the more basic events physics posits. Composition, unlike causation, does not care about the intrinsic character of its relata, and the size and neural-versus-fundamental status of the parts is exactly such an intrinsic matter.
(5) So either experiences can compose an experience — in which case the panpsychist is entitled to help herself to the very relation already at work in the bridge case, and (1) is answered — or they cannot, in which case my own experience, supposedly a composite of experiential parts, is impossible, and (1) is a problem for the physicalist, the neutral monist and everyone else too.
(6) Either way (1) fails as an objection to panpsychism. The combination problem is either solved by an instance we already accept, or it is everyone's problem and therefore nobody's objection.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I'd rather keep (1) and (2) than any of the rest, so price the alternatives accordingly, and be explicit about what a panpsychist who gives up (3) is left with: if macro-experience is not literally composed of experiential parts, she seems forced back toward something like phenomenal bonding or brute macro-emergence, which was supposed to be her advantage over the emergentist. I also want to know whether the argument, if it is valid, proves too much in a second way — whether the same bridge-case move would equally dissolve the palette (quality-space) and grain versions of the combination problem, and indeed any complaint of the form "the panpsychist cannot account for this macro-feature of consciousness," since on the face of it no theory could then be faulted for failing to explain unity, and that seems wrong. Finally: in your diagnosis, does the argument need a distinction I haven't drawn — between being a proper part of an experience and being co-conscious with another experience, say, or between composition of experiences and composition of subjects — and where, exactly, would that distinction have to be inserted to stop (4) from doing its work? For what it's worth, I'd also like to know how the argument fares if you replace the synchronic bridge case with a diachronic one: if the subjects of my temporal stages compose the subject of my life, the panpsychist has the same bridge; if they don't, no view of personal identity does either.
