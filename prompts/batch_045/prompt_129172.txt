Last night I got into a three-hour argument with my friend, who's an environmental historian, about whether germs, climate and volcanoes can be, in her word, actors in history. She kept saying things like that the famine was the volcano's doing, or that disease conquered the continent. I kept saying no, a volcano is a condition and the famine is what you get when a food system with no slack meets a shock, and those are different claims with different consequences. We didn't resolve it, partly because I think we were using actor in two different senses and partly because she reads history and I read incident writeups.

So: I'm an SRE at a mid-size infrastructure company. I write and review maybe fifty or sixty postmortems a year and have for eight years, so I know that vocabulary cold — root cause and contributing factor, trigger versus latent condition, blameless writeups, five whys, action items and owners, blast radius, time to detect, error budgets. I also have working causal-inference and DAG vocabulary. Don't explain any of that to me. On the other side I've read no philosophy of history and exactly one popular book about the Columbian exchange, so assume nothing there.

What I want: restate her position as faithfully as you can in my vocabulary, then give me the residue report — what translated cleanly, what only translated with something shoved under the rug, and what has no counterpart at all in how we talk about incidents.

Things I want actually dealt with rather than gestured at:

Do germs, climate and volcanoes even translate the same way? I don't see how they can. Germs replicate and evolve under selection presure, and they can evolve in response to what we do, which looks closer to what an agent does than anything a volcano does. Climate looks to me like a slowly drifting parameter that changes base rates. A volcano is a one-off exogenous shock. If the three come apart, say how, and say what that does to the general claim about non-human actors.

In the argument I invented a three-way distinction and I want to know whether it does any work or is just relabeling. Trigger: a difference-maker inside the actual sequence, something you can put at a timestamp. Terrain: it sets background rates and the shape of the counterfactual landscape, and there's no moment at which it acted. Unlocker: it removes or disables a barrier, after which what happens is mostly shaped by things that were already sitting there. My claim was that most accusations of environmental determinism are really accusations of mis-slotting — calling terrain a trigger, so the volcano did it and the question of why the system had no slack never gets asked, or calling an unlocker an author. Tell me whether that holds up, where it quietly imports my framework's assumptions, and what it can't classify.

What the determinism worry looks like from my side: a writeup that names a cause nobody can act on, so the action items come out empty, or worse, all get handed to the human layer even though the narrative says the non-human thing did it. Is her worry the same shape as that, or is it doing something different?

And the residue I most want: my whole vocabulary presupposes a system with a designed normal behaviour, a baseline it deviates from, an owner who can file the action item, and a future incident rate I'm trying to push down. History has none of those. So tell me which of her claims can't even be stated once that presupposition is in place, and whether that shows my vocabulary is too narrow or that her talk of agency was only ever difference-making with better PR.

Run the translation on three cases, at least one of which has to come out in a way I won't like: disease moving through the Americas after 1492, the 1815 eruption and the harvests and unrest that followed, and the 1918 flu during the last year of the war.
