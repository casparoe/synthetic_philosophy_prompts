I'm putting together a one-page handout for the medical ethics seminar I'm TAing this term, and I want a taxonomy of the positions on assisted dying — but not the standard one. Every handout I find sorts people by whether they accept or reject 'euthanasia' full stop, and that doesn't sort anyone in my class: they all think it's obviously fine in one case and obviously not in another, and the cases they actually split over aren't the ones the literature is organised around. So build it from the questions rather than the labels: each branch should be a question a student can answer without already knowing which view it leads to, and each leaf gets the conventional name (or 'unnamed') plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Constraints, because otherwise I'll get the generic version:

- Don't start from permissibility. Start from the easy case — competent patient, metastatic cancer, months to live, pain not controlled, repeated request — assume everyone in the room grants that something is owed to her, and split on what it is that makes her case easy: that her suffering is the problem, that she asked, or that she is dying anyway. Grow the rest of the tree out of that.
- The cases it has to sort: an advance request written years earlier by someone now moderately demented who seems content and no longer repeats it; a 29-year-old with treatment-refractory depression and no physical illness; an 84-year-old with a pile of chronic, non-terminal conditions who says she has completed her life and is tired of it; someone in severe suffering who is refusing the one effective palliative treatment because of its side effects; and a request driven mostly by not wanting to be a burden on family.
- Required leaves, wherever they land: the terminal-illness restriction, the autonomy-only view (competent request is sufficient), the 'medicine has fixed ends' view about professional integrity, the terminal-sedation/double-effect-only position, the disability-rights objection to the suffering-based rationale, and sanctity-of-life absolutism.
- Flag any pair of leaves your questions can't actually seperate, and for combinations of answers nobody seems to occupy, say whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unclaimed.

The bit I've been chewing on, which I want the tree to handle explicitly: most of the disagreement in my seminar wasn't about whether suffering matters but about when you're allowed to count it — whether the relevant unit is the person's trajectory as a whole or the person at the moment of the act, and whether an 'unbearable suffering' claim can be undercut by showing the suffering was avoidable. Call it the snapshot/trajectory problem if you want. Aim for about six branch questions and eight or nine leaves, and use your own cases rather than ones from the literature — I can't check references here and don't want them.
