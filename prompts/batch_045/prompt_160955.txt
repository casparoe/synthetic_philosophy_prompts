hey, ive been going back and forth with a friend about money pump arguments and I realised I dont actually get what they're supposed to establish. Can you walk me through the messy middle of it?

The setup I have in mind: someone has cyclic preferences over three objects — A better than B, B better than C, C better than A — and a trader offers them a series of swaps, each for a small fee, until they're back holding A and poorer than when they started. The textbook move is: so cyclic preferences are irrational. I want more than the slogan. Concretely:

1. What is the argument actually meant to show — that cyclic preferences are irrational, that they're merely imprudent, that they're incoherent with some other commitment, or just that they leave you exploitable? And how is this different in structure from a Dutch book, which is about degrees of belief and (as I understand it) can be scaled up arbitrarily by raising the stakes? People lazily run the two together and I want the difference sharp.

2. Here's a case I invented that I can't diagnose. Call her the tourist. Suppose she doesn't evaluate a sequence of trades by totalling up the money and looking at where she ends up. She has preferences over whole histories of trades: every swap that moves her to something she prefers gives her a little kick, and getting all the way around the cycle is especially satisfying. So for her, A → C → B → A minus three pennies is strictly better than sitting on A. Is the pump argument quietly relying on a separability or additivity assumption about how outcomes and money combine into a value for the sequence? And is dropping that assumption a legitimate defence, or is it cheating — does the tourist have some other incoherence that a pump could still be built around?

3. Related worry about stakes. Money has diminishing marginal utility for most people, and there's a smallest unit of currency, so once the fee has to shrink below a cent the pump stalls and the total loss is bounded — unlike a Dutch book, where you just multiply the stake. Does that asymmetry matter for the normative conclusion? Can pumps be made scalable anyway (a longer cycle with many objects, pumping in some divisible good instead of money, running it repeatedly)? Should a requirement of rationality care about losses that are bounded and small?

4. And the foresight thing: a cyclic agent could simply decline one of the swaps, or resolve in advance not to go around. Does that kill the argument? If not, why not — and is the argument then really a claim about how to choose over time (diachronic rationality) rather than about the shape of your preferences at a time?

One last thing: is there any such thing as a reverse money pump — a case where intransitivity lets the agent extract money from someone else? Being movable from anything to anything feels like a kind of flexibility, but I can't turn it into an actual money fountain, and maybe it genuinely can't be done. If it can't, explain why not.

700-900 words, informal is fine, and please dont bother with citations or paper titles — I want the reasoning, not a reading list.
