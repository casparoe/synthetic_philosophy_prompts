You are a philosopher of mind who works on the metaphysics of consciousness and the epistemology of introspection, and you are unusually good at reconstructing the implicit principle behind a set of case verdicts. Please work carefully and at length, and show the reconstruction rather than only the bottom line. Concretely: first brainstorm several candidate principles that fit the track record below, including at least one you think is wrong together with the reason it is wrong; then check each surviving principle against the new case; then commit to a prediction about what Marlowe will say, with the reasons for preferring it and an honest confidence level; then name the single further case that would best separate the principles that made the same prediction about the new case. Do not survey the literature on epiphenomenalism — I have read it. I want the rule she is actually running.

My supervisor, Professor Ines Marlowe, is a property-dualist epiphenomenalist about phenomenal character. She holds that phenomenal properties are not identical with physical properties, that every physical event has a sufficient physical cause in which no phenomenal property figures, and that the tie between a phenomenal property and the physical state it accompanies is contingent in the sense that the physical world would run exactly as it does if the phenomenal properties were absent. She also holds, and has said so several times in seminar, that epiphenomenalism is not self-stultifying and that the paradox of phenomenal judgment has a solution; she has never said which one, and the manuscript of hers I have been reading stops short of giving it. So I am trying to recover it from her verdicts.

These are the verdicts I have, from the manuscript and from seminar discussion:

1. Ordinary pain report. A normal adult in pain says, sincerely and competently, that she is in pain. Verdict: she knows that she is in pain and knows what it is like; the fact that the pain itself caused nothing is no obstacle to knowledge.

2. The zombie duplicate. A molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a pain-reporting human, physically identical and behaviorally identical, in which no phenomenal property is instantiated at all, emits the same utterance. Verdict: the utterance expresses a belief that is not knowledge and is not even true; there is nothing there for it to be about. She explicitly rejects the line that the duplicate's belief is true because it plays the right functional role.

3. The phenomenally mute subject. A human whose nociceptive system and whose pains are intact but who entirely lacks the mechanisms for forming or expressing any judgment about her own phenomenal states; she is awake, responsive, and behaviorally normal apart from having nothing to say or think about how things seem to her. Verdict: she has pains; she does not know that she does; she has no phenomenal beliefs at all. Experience and phenomenal judgment come apart in both directions, and knowledge requires the judgment.

4. The crossed wire. A subject whose pain-report mechanism is triggered, reliably and only, by a neural state that is in fact accompanied by a phenomenal property of a completely different kind — the one you and I get from cool air moving across the skin. Verdict: not knowledge, and the report is false, even though the mechanism is a perfectly reliable physical instrument, the subject is sincere, and her phenomenal concepts are in good order.

5. The delayed judgment. A subject has a stabbing pain, forms no judgment about it at the time because her attention is elsewhere, and two minutes later, when prompted, judges that it was a stabbing pain on the basis of a memory trace. Verdict: knowledge. The experience is gone and did not cause the judgment, and she does not treat that as a problem.

6. The inverted community. A population physically and functionally exactly like us whose pain states are accompanied by phenomenal properties qualitatively different from ours, and whose reports are produced by those states in the ordinary way. Verdict: their reports are true and are knowledge; their word picks out their own phenomenal property, not ours.

Now the new case, which she has agreed to rule on next week. Patient R. has had a complete callosotomy, with no remaining forebrain commissure. Nociceptive input from her left hand travels up two routes that diverge below the cortex. One route terminates in the right hemisphere's thalamocortical pain matrix; the state produced there is, on Marlowe's own view, the state that is accompanied by the pain experience, and it has no causal route to the left hemisphere. The other route branches off subcortically, before that cortical state, and projects to the left hemisphere's language and self-report system, which is what produces both the utterance and the occurrent thought that she is in severe pain in her left hand. Nothing about the second route is sensitive to whether the right hemisphere's state is accompanied by any phenomenal property: remove the phenomenal properties and the utterance would be produced in exactly the same way at exactly the same time. In the actual world, however, the firing of the left hemisphere's report system is perfectly correlated with the presence of the pain experience, because both are downstream of the same nociceptive input. R. is one subject, not two. She is sincere, conceptually competent, and has no other access to her right hemisphere's state; when pressed she insists she feels it and cannot say anything further.

So: will Marlowe say that R. knows that she is in pain? Separately, will she say that R.'s judgment is true? I want both answers, since I suspect she may want to separate them here in a way that makes this a Gettier-shaped case for introspection.

Please give me: the candidate principles that fit verdicts 1 through 6, stated sharply enough that they make different predictions somewhere; what each predicts about knowledge and about truth in R.'s case; your best prediction with the reasoning that selects it; the further case that would discriminate the principles that agreed about R.; and, if you find that no single principle fits all six verdicts, which verdict she would have to give up and what that would cost her. Also tell me which of the six verdicts is doing the most work in narrowing the options and which of them is compatible with nearly everything and therefore carries little weight.
