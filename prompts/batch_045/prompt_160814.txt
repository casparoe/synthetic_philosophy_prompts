I'm applying for a one-term (four-month) fellowship and need a one-page research proposal, under 600 words, on the duty to remember and the case for forgetting. My half-formed idea, which I want sharpened rather than replaced:

Both sides of this debate seem to share an unstated scarcity assumption — that memory is fragile, that it decays unless maintained, and hence that the moral question is whether to spend effort preserving it. Margalit's duty to remember the victims of radical evil is a duty to keep something alive against erosion; the recent case for forgetting associated with David Rieff's In Praise of Forgetting argues largely from memory's corrosive social effects. Neither is written for a situation of what I'll call archival saturation: permanent, cheap, searchable records, plus systems that can generate fluent first-personal testimony — survivor narratives, reconstructed voices, memorial footage — about events no living person witnessed. Call the practice of handing retention to a durable record delegated remembrance. My suspicion is that under archival saturation the duty changes shape rather than simply becoming easier: it becomes a duty to attend, to curate, or to vouch for provenance, rather than a duty to retain, and that this leaves both standard positions without their usual object, since a society could now satisfy an archive-based duty while no one is psychologically stirred by anything, or could remember vividly what never happened.

What I want in the proposal:

- A single opening sentence stating the question precisely enough to have a determinate answer, and framed so that it does not collapse into an empirical question about what current technology can do.
- Why the two standard positions leave the question open, and specifically why neither tells us what to say about delegated remembrance or synthetic testimony.
- What would actually settle it: a claim about what the duty to remember is for. Is it owed to the dead or to the living? Is it essentially relational and personal, or essentially a matter of true beliefs being available? Does delegating it to an archive, or to a generator, discharge it, partly discharge it, or corrupt it?
- A four-month plan, month by month, for a project that is conceptual rather than empirical and does not presuppose detailed knowledge of how today's generative systems work.
- What would count as the project failing — for instance if the scarcity assumption turns out to do no work, or if the duty proves to be divisible in a way that dissolves the question — plus an honest note on scope risk.

Refer to positions and arguments by name where useful, but no bibliography and no citations anywhere; I will add those myself.
