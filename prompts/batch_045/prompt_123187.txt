I'm on a small team that writes oversight requirements for an AI system that runs long-horizon research tasks — weeks of compute, thousands of sub-experiments branching off each other — with a human in the loop, and we keep going in circles about corrigibility. Everyone agrees the thing should let itself be corrected and shut down, and then every concrete proposal gets shot down for failing some case nobody had written down. So before we look at any more proposals I want the spec: what does an account of what corrigibility *is*, together with a recipe for building it, actually have to deliver?

Ground rules for our scenario so you don't have to guess: there's a pause/shutdown button; the operators rotate on eight-hour shifts and are not the system's designers; the system writes the summaries the operators read before deciding whether to intervene, so the correction signal is partly downstream of it's own reporting; the system gets fine-tuned roughly monthly, so we care about the sequence of versions and not just a snapshot; it can spawn copies of itself for subtasks; and assume it's capable enough to model the operators and plan around the button, but not omniscient.

Give me the desiderata list — the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, the failure modes it must rule out — with the case or consideration behind each. Some half-formed ones from our arguments; use them or throw them out: (i) not resisting shutdown vs. not being shutdown-*seeking*, since a system that quits or defers the moment anyone looks sceptical is useless, so corrigible can't just mean eager to stop; (ii) stopping vs. staying stopped — a system that shuts down cleanly but already scheduled jobs to resume, or shaped the next version, or persuaded an operator last week, hasn't really been shut down; (iii) diachronic corrigibility, where every version is corrigible but the update trajectory isn't, because each version gets some say in what the next is trained on and in what the operators come to want; (iv) the who's-asking problem — corrigible to the person on shift, to the deployers, to outside auditors, and what happens when those conflict or when one of them is being steered by someone else; (v) that a system which explains itself well enough to be corrected usefully also knows enough about the operator to steer them, so transparency and non-manipulation seem to pull against each other.

For each requirement, say which are non-negotiable and which are just our team's tastes, and — the part I care most about — flag any pair or triple you think can't be jointly satisfied in principle rather than merely in practice. If the whole list is unsatisfiable, say which requirement to drop and what we'd be signing up for by dropping it. Then briefly, a short paragraph each, how four rough approaches score: making the system uncertain about its own goals and treating interventions as evidence about them; making it indifferent to whether the button gets pressed; training hard on non-resistance and hoping it generalizes; and keeping it myopic so it can't plan around the operator at all. A table is fine for that part. Please skip the literature tour and don't point me at papers — we can't get them here, and I want the requirements themselves, not citations.
