I'm putting together the take-home for my upper-division seminar on Fear and Trembling and I need one question students can't get through by writing teleological suspension of the ethical in big letters and then stopping. Here's the angle I want. On the standard telling Abraham renounces Isaac altogether — the movement of infinite resignation, everyone gets that bit — and at the same time believes, by virtue of the absurd, that he'll have Isaac back in this life. Those two halves look like they cancel: if he really expects Isaac back there's nothing he's giving up, so no test and no sacrifice, and if he doesn't expect him back he's just a man about to kill his son because of a voice. Call it the double-movement dilemma. I want the question to force them to pick a side and defend it, and to say what a reading of the binding has to look like if it wants to keep both halves. Second thing: get them on whether suspension is even the right word. I've been toying with three options — the universal still applies but gets outweighed by a stronger reason; the universal is genuinely set aside in this one case; or the action isn't a violation of the ethical at all once you describe it correctly, as a sacrifice rather than a murder. Whichever they go with they have to handle the silence: Abraham tells Sarah, Eliezer and Isaac nothing. Is that internal to faith, or a sign something has gone badly wrong? Remind them too that the book is written by a pseudonym who keeps saying he can't follow Abraham — I don't want answers that quietly assume we get a clean view of Abraham's reasons. Give me the question, a model answer of about 600 words, and a scheme out of 20 built on four or five must-hit moves with a line on how people usually get each one wrong. And write the whole thing as continous prose, no headings bullets bold or tables, I'm pasting it into a doc.
