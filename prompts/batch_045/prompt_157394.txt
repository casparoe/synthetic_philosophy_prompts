The thing that makes this hard: last week's session was on observation versus theory in science, and observation got presented more or less as the bedrock, so they're going to walk in as instinctive foundationalists and think the answer is obvious. Most of them are econ or CS majors and are comfortable with proof trees and citation graphs, which I suspect is a mixed blessing. What I want by the end is that they can say one actually strong thing in favor of each of the three views, and can state the best objection to whichever one they like — the usual outcome of this session is foundationalism winning by default and infinitism getting laughed out of the room, and I want to avoid that without turning infinitism into a straw man either.

My half-formed organizing idea is to frame the disagreement as a disagreement about the *shape* of justificatory structure: a tree with a root, a web with no edges, a road with no end. I like that it makes the three views look parallel instead of ranked. But I've started to worry the shape framing is precisely what makes infinitism sound like a lunatic claim about psychology (nobody has an endless road inside their head) rather than a claim about what justification consists in. Tell me if I should keep it, and if I keep it, at what point in the sequence I have to block that slide. Relatedly: I wasn't planning to give them the fourth option, the skeptical one where nothing terminates and so nothing is justified at all — should that be in the room from the start, named and set aside, or does it just derail a fifty-minute session?

I'm now suspicious of it — I think it might smuggle in foundationalism, since students will just take "the primary source" as the foundation and declare the question settled. Is that right? If it's rot, what should the carrying example be, and what would it have to have and lack to do the work?
