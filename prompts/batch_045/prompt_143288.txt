I've been arguing with the same person about whether language models understand anything for about six years now. We met in the comments section of a blog neither of us reads any more, and the argument has survived every release since. She has no patience for the literature and no interest in labels; what she has is very firm verdicts on cases. I've kept a running list, and I've become convinced that the object-level disagreement is a proxy for something else: we keep offering each other new cases, and the cases keep not biting, because they're all cases where the plausible rules agree. I'd like to find a case where they don't. Answering well here means telling me which rule she is actually running and which further case would expose it, not telling me who is right about language models.

Her verdicts so far, condensed, with the question in each case being whether the thing in question understands the relevant domain:

1. An imagined complete lookup table from chess positions to best moves — no, even granting it infinite size and perfect play.
2. A traditional chess program: hand-written evaluation function, alpha-beta search, an opening book, playing far better than she does — no. Her gloss is that the programmers understand chess and the program is where their understanding ends up, not a place where understanding happens.
3. A neural network trained by self-play from random initialization, given no human chess knowledge, playing superhumanly — yes, and more than most human players.
4. A statistical phrase-based machine translation system — no.
5. A large language model of the 2022 generation: next-token prediction, no tools, no chain of thought — no, and this is the verdict she is most confident of.
6. A market, or an ant colony — no, though she grants they do something she doesn't have a word for.
7. A thermostat — no.
8. A crow that works out the water-tube puzzle — yes, a thin, local, real understanding of tubes and water.
9. A medical student who has memorized the textbook, passes every exam, and cannot handle a variant problem — no; she has a line for this one, that he understood the exam and not the medicine.
10. A man with severe expressive aphasia who can neither speak nor write but reliably solves new logic puzzles by pointing at symbols — yes, and I think this case is doing more work in her intuition than she admits.
11. An expert driver who handles situations she can't and cannot explain what she is doing — yes.
12. A rule-based expert system for diagnosing bacterial infections, built by interviewing human specialists, performing about as well as they do inside its narrow range and badly outside it — no.

Note the shape of the list: she is unmoved by success alone (2, 9, 12) and unmoved by the absence of articulate success (10, 11). So the crude behaviorist rule and the crude what's-inside rule both look wrong. I can think of three rules that fit all twelve, which is exactly the problem:

- A structural rule: understanding requires a model of the domain's structure that the system built for itself and reuses across tasks — learned rather than hand-loaded, a model rather than a cache.
- A robustness rule: understanding is a matter of the shape of the system's success and failure across novel variations, including variations in how the task is framed, not of what is inside it. The chess program, the exam-memorizer and the expert system all collapse under reframing; the driver and the crow do not, within their domains.
- A credit rule: understanding is an achievement that has to be attributable to the system's own cognitive activity, not borrowed from a designer, a corpus, or a textbook.

I've also toyed with a fourth — that what matters is metacognition, the capacity to notice and repair its own errors — but case 3 looks like a counterexample, so I set it aside. Tell me if I was too quick.

The case I want to bring next. A lab takes the very model from case 5, the one she ruled out, and changes nothing in the weights. They wrap it in an outer loop written by humans that lets it write and execute code, keep an arbitrarily long scratchpad, generate many independent attempts at each problem, check each attempt with an independently written verifier, and discard the failures. On a set of genuinely novel chemistry problems composed after the model's training data ended and graded against a key the model never sees, the composite goes from roughly twelve percent to roughly seventy-eight percent, and its residual errors are the errors of a rushed but competent graduate student — a mis-set unit, one wrong step in an otherwise sound derivation — rather than the alien failures she objects to in case 5. Nothing is learned at deployment time; nothing is written back into the weights.

What I want:

1. Predict her verdict, and be explicit about which thing you take her to be assessing — the weights, the composite system, the model plus its scaffold, or something else. If the case is phrased so that she can dodge by picking a referent, say how to close that off.
2. Set out the candidate rules that fit all twelve earlier verdicts, including ones I haven't listed, and say what each predicts about this case. Where a rule gives no clean verdict and splits into variants, say so; I suspect the credit rule is the messiest here, since the checking and the search were designed by humans and the candidate solutions were not.
3. Give me your single best guess with your reasons, and state plainly which rules my case does discriminate between and which it leaves conflated.
4. Name the further case, ideally one I haven't thought of, that separates the rules mine doesn't, and explain why it is sharper.

My own candidate for that further case, which you should feel free to dismiss: same lab, same pipeline, but they now train on the successful traces, so that the bare model with no wrapper and no scratchpad reproduces most of the composite's accuracy. If she flips on that one but not on the wrapper case, I think that tells us something quite specific, but I'd rather hear what you think it tells us and whether there is a cleaner probe.

One more thing I want addressed: if she says yes, I expect her to want to say that what the system understands is the artefact — the problem distribution — rather than chemistry. Tell me whether that move is a real distinction or a way of never saying yes, and whether the case as I've built it blocks it.

Don't give me a literature review, a reading list, or citations; I can supply those and they will not move her, and I want the structure of the disagreement rather than a survey of who has said what.
