I am writing a seminar paper whose claim is that the standard presentation of the Frege–Geach problem mislocates its difficulty. The hard part, I want to argue, is not whether an expressivist can underwrite modus ponens with moral premises; that much the higher-order-attitude machinery more or less handles. The hard part is what happens when normative vocabulary sits inside operators that are neither truth-functional nor Boolean. To make that claim stick I need one concrete case, built to specification, rather than a schematic gesture.

The case

One paragraph, set in ordinary institutional life — a hospital ethics committee, a research integrity review, a procurement panel, a workplace compliance check — written in the vocabulary such people would actually use, with no technical terms from the debate and nothing that would look out of place in a meeting minute. Within it, someone reasons, in the ordinary course of business, to a conclusion. The reasoning must have the following properties.

1. Its validity is a matter of form. A careful reader with no philosophical training should be able to check that the conclusion follows, and the following must not rest on the subject matter, on any empirical premise, or on my having stipulated that the argument is valid.

2. Every occurrence of normative vocabulary in the argument — wrong, ought, permissible, required, or whatever you choose — is unasserted. Each sits inside the scope of some operator: the antecedent of a conditional, a negation, a disjunction, a question, an ascription of another person's or an earlier self's attitude, and so on.

3. At least one normative sentence sits inside the scope of a non-Boolean operator — along the lines of probably, it is more likely than not, it is likelier than. And the argument's validity must depend on a formal property of that operator (monotonicity, closure under consequence, or whatever the correct property is), not merely on the fact that some operator wraps a normative sentence.

4. No participant in the case has, is described as having, or is disposed to form any first-order normative attitude toward the actions the normative vocabulary concerns: nobody approves or disapproves, plans or declines to plan, accepts or rejects a norm, for or against those actions. They are suspended, indifferent, or treating the question purely as one of what follows from what. Make this condition realistic rather than contrived — a dry run, a moot, a hypothetical in a training exercise, an auditor's desk check. I will treat any case that needs attitudes attributed to its participants as cheating, since the point is that the verdicts come out of the theories and not out of the characters.

The demonstration

Work with three positions, characterized as follows. If one of my characterizations is unrecognizable, say so in a sentence and proceed with your corrected version.

(a) Simple emotivism: normative sentences have no descriptive content; their assertoric use expresses and arouses attitudes; there is no more to their meaning than that, and no substantive account is available of what a normative sentence contributes where it is not asserted.

(b) Higher-order-attitude quasi-realism: an asserted normative sentence expresses a first-order attitude; in unasserted positions the sentence's role is located by appeal to attitudes toward combinations of attitudes, and the validity of an argument is explained as the incoherence of a package — the higher-order attitude that condemns holding the premise-attitudes together while withholding the conclusion-attitude. Validity is thus explained by a fact about which combinations of attitudes are jointly sustainable, not by a semantic property of propositions.

(c) Hybrid, or ecumenical, expressivism: a normative sentence has full truth-conditional, representational content of the kind a cognitivist would assign, and in addition its assertoric use expresses a distinct non-cognitive attitude; the two components are separable, and the view claims the benefits of both.

For each of the three: say what it says about the argument in your case, whether it can even state what the argument is, whether it can explain why the inference is valid, and what would have to be added to the theory — a new attitude, a treatment of the operator, a further higher-order attitude — to get that explanation. I expect the three verdicts to come apart, and part of the design task is making them come apart for the right reason.

The diagnosis

Which single feature of the case is doing the work? Say what it is, and show that the case collapses without it: specifically, show what happens when the non-Boolean operator in (3) is replaced by a plain conditional or a negation, so that the argument degenerates into the ordinary Frege–Geach modus ponens that (b) is generally credited with handling. If the collapse does not occur, your case has the wrong load-bearing feature.

Then the counterattack. The obvious quasi-realist reply is to refuse to model confidence as a relation to a proposition: treat being more confident that doing A is wrong than that doing B is wrong as a comparative fact about degrees of disapproval, or else run the quasi-realist program one level up and earn the right to talk about probabilities over normative contents in the way the program earns the right to talk about truth. Give that reply in its strongest form, and then tell me whether it restores the case or dissolves it — in particular, whether it leaves (b) distinguishable from (c), or whether it concedes the hybrid's point that representational content is doing the work and the attitudinal component is along for the ride.

Finally, tell me which of the three you think comes out best on your own case, and whether that is a reason to prefer that theory or a reason to suspect the case is rigged. And if my four constraints cannot be jointly satisfied — if, say, every case that silences (a) also collapses (b) for the same reason, or if (3) cannot be met without reintroducing exactly the attitudes (4) forbids — identify the failing constraint, explain why it fails, and propose the nearest specification you can actually meet, rather than quietly relaxing one.

One paragraph for the case; the rest as long as it needs to be.
