I write historical fiction, and I've been invited to apply for a six-month residential fellowship that asks for a two-page project proposal. I need one for a question that has been blocking the novel I'm drafting.

The novel is set in the months before a franchise reform act that every reader will know the outcome of. My characters are a circle of provincial printers, a bankrupt attorney and his sister — none of them anyone history remembers — and in my current draft the act reaches them only as rumour from the capital. My half-formed idea is this. The familiar complaint about whig history is about explanation: reading the past as tending toward the present, treating the winners as having grasped what the losers missed, judging agents by a standard they couldn't have held. But I suspect the deeper problem for a novelist isn't explanatory at all; it's formal. A plot has a shape — complication, crisis, resolution — and the shape itself announces that these events were going somewhere. So a novel can make no whiggish claim about why something happened and still be whiggish in its pacing, in which scenes it lets count as turning points, in the irony with which it shows a character not yet knowing what we know. I want to know whether that kind of formal whiggishness is avoidable, or whether it's simply what narration is.

Three devices I'm toying with and want the proposal to put to the test: writing full 'dead future' scenes, with the same weight as the main plot, of the outcome that didn't happen; filtering everything through characters whose names history dropped, so the famous settlement arrives offstage; and ending mid-crisis so the arc never closes.

What I need from the proposal: the question stated precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; why the obvious positions leave it open (the standard critique of whig history says nothing about form, and the view that emplotment is unavoidable seems to make the question trivial); why a novelist working on chapters and reader response, rather than a philosopher working on examples, is the right person to attempt it; a month-by-month method; and an honest failure condition — if readers can't tell my anti-whig chapter from my whig chapter, or the anti-whig one is merely dull, or the difference turns out to be entirely a function of what the reader already knew before opening the book rather than anything the text does, then the project fails and I should write the whig version and say plainly why. Name positions rather than authors if you need to gesture at the literature; I'll supply the citations. Also tell me honestly whether this is too big for six months, and which part I should cut if it is.
