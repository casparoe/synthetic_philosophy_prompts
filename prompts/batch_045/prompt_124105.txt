I'd like you to write a parody: a client risk-tolerance questionnaire of the kind a brokerage or pension provider sends you before it will let you pick an allocation, except that the allocator issuing it is a longtermist philanthropic fund, and the questionnaire is what determines how the fund applies risk-weighted expected utility to its grantmaking.

Give it a title, a short preamble in compliance-speak, ten to twelve questions with multiple-choice answers, a scoring rubric, and a closing paragraph telling the client what their profile means. Around 700 to 900 words, formatted as a real questionnaire, deadpan throughout: the document should believe, without a trace of irony, that it has solved the fanaticism problem by making risk attitude an explicit, calibrated dial rather than something smuggled in through the curvature of a utility function.

The jokes have to come from getting the decision theory right. Some material I want in there, though use your judgment about how it lands:

- The distinction between a genuinely global risk attitude and local curvature of a utility function: the questionnaire is asking about one thing while presenting itself as asking about another, and the thing it is actually setting is a single parameter that no possible sequence of small-stakes choices could pin down.
- The calibration-style argument against explaining modest risk aversion over small stakes with expected utility at all: if you turn down a favorable coin flip at every level of wealth, you are committed to refusing gambles with enormous upside at large stakes. The fund's questionnaire should run straight into this while insisting it hasn't.
- The fact that re-weighting probabilities does essentially nothing about Pascalian setups unless something has already bounded the value of the outcome: the questionnaire keeps offering the client options that look like they address the mugging and in fact just assume the conclusion.
- The sequential/ergodic objection: in a world where you get to bet again and again and some states are absorbing, the object worth maximizing is something like a long-run growth rate, not any static re-weighting of a one-shot distribution — and a curve fitted to single-case intuitions is the wrong-shaped instrument. I'd like the questionnaire to have a clause that gestures at this and then quietly neutralizes it.
- Model uncertainty rather than risk: with heavy-tailed payoffs, the shape of the tail is not estimable from the data anyone has, so what the client is really being asked to sign off on is a guess about a tail nobody can see. The document should treat this as a solved problem because the guess has been written down.

Feel free to invent the apparatus freely: a named risk function attributed to fictional authors, a calibration that was performed once in a particular year and has never been revisited, a Continuity Clause that automatically defunds anything with more than some small probability of lowering the fund's long-run growth rate, a worked example showing two clients with nearly identical answers getting wildly different portfolios.

The target is the practice, not any person: treating risk attitude as one tunable parameter, and the broader habit of thinking that a portfolio-level formalism settles what to do with a civilization-sized budget. No real names, and no winking — the piece should be legible as satire to someone who knows the literature while never breaking character itself.

Close with a short afterword, 100 to 150 words, out of character, in which you commit: say which two of the jokes above you think are genuinely fair hits on risk-weighted expected utility as its defenders would describe it, which one depends on a reading they would reject as a strawman, and then pick the single objection you think actually survives the defender's reply and give your reason in a sentence or two. I'd rather have a definite verdict with a stated justification than a survey of which objections are available.
