Hey — I'm in a tiny reading group (me, a philosopher friend, and an ML engineer who mostly just sighs at us) and we keep going in circles on the whole "LLMs are simulators, not agents" thing, so I want to force the disagreement into an actual number.

Here's the setup, which is deliberately not the usual one. Forget today's chatbots. Imagine we take a plain next-token predictor — no RLHF, no new training objective, just ordinary autoregressive training on text — and wrap it in a scaffold: a persistent memory it reads from and writes to, tools it can call, and a loop that runs for weeks, where the effects of its own actions get transcribed back into text and fed into its context. No new training signal at all. Call that a scaffolded predictor. Now a second variant: same architecture, trained from scratch on a corpus made only of first-person material from a single fictional protagonist — diaries, letters, dialogue, interior monologue — so that, arguably, there is exactly one character available for it to simulate instead of the usual patchwork of thousands. Then give it the same scaffold. Call that single-protagonist training. The thing I keep wanting someone to actually measure I've been calling the persona-collapse index: over a long episode, how much of the model's next-token distribution is explained by one stable character-conditional distribution rather than by a mixture over several whose weights drift — and, separately, whether that single character's motivations keep tracking the operator's goal even when the surface framing changes in ways that should break any simulated character's reasons.

The proposition I want your credence on: for at least one of those two systems, the best explanation of its behavior over a long horizon will attribute to it persistent goals and beliefs that it acts in order to achieve — i.e. it will be more agent than simulator, and not merely convenient for us to describe that way. Read "will" as within the next ten years, and say what would count as settling it: a predictive or intervention test one framing wins and the other doesn't, or your better idea. My credence is about 0.6; my philosopher friend is at about 0.15, mostly on the grounds that the scaffold adds no agency and the agent/simulator distinction has no empirical content anyway.

So: give me a number, decomposed — with a rough probability and one line of justification each for terms like whether the distinction is in-principle testable, whether stable goal-directedness can emerge from a scaffold without any training objective, whether single-protagonist training would boost agent-like coherence under both framings (and so fail to discriminate), whether attributing beliefs and desires to such a system can ever be more than instrumentally useful, and whatever else you think belongs — then show how they combine, name the term you're least confident about, tell me which hypothetical experimental result would move you most, say where you think the gap between 0.6 and 0.15 actually lives, and don't just tell me it depends on what we mean by agent (pick the reading you think is load-bearing and say which one you picked). End with your final credence on its own last line in the form \answer{0.42}.
