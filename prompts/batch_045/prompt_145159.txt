I am writing a novel set for the most part on a female orthopaedic ward in an English district general hospital, and there is a scene at a bedside that I cannot make work. An eighty-two-year-old woman with moderate-to-severe dementia has fractured her hip. She cannot understand why she is hurting, she cannot weigh one future against another, there is no advance statement, and the family has never agreed about what she would have wanted. Two clinical paths are open to her. I need you to construct the choice between those two paths so that it isolates a single variable and so that the disagreement among the three people at the bedside falls out of the three things they believe, rather than out of anything I have simply decreed.

The three positions, as the novel has them:

- Dr Ashworth, the geriatrician: well-being is additive and tradeable. Sum everyone's happiness minus everyone's suffering, with no special weight on either, and take the larger total.
- Staff nurse Pryce: only suffering counts, but it all has to be added up. No amount of happiness compensates for pain, yet a great deal of mild pain can still outweigh a little severe pain, because quantities of suffering are commensurable and aggregate across time and across people.
- Marianne, the daughter: the worst thing that happens to anyone is lexically prior. You may never bring about a worse worst-thing for someone, however much milder suffering doing so would spare, and however much happiness would follow afterwards. The good hours that come later do not cancel the bad hour that came first.

The verdicts I need: Ashworth and Pryce both take the harder clinical path, because a shorter, sharper suffering buys off a far larger quantity of milder suffering and also restores a good deal of ordinary happiness. Marianne alone refuses it, and she must be the character my reader cannot dismiss.

Constraints on the construction:

1. Realistic throughout — an ordinary ward, ordinary medicine, ordinary numbers. No science fiction, no philosophy-seminar apparatus, no vast populations, no tiny probabilities, no infinite utilities.
2. Hold the length of her life fixed across the two paths. The case must not be won by anything turning on death, on letting her die, on shortening her life, or on people who do not yet exist.
3. Hold fixed any question about whether pain that is not understood or not remembered is worse or less bad. The case must not rest on claims of that kind either way.
4. Consent must not settle the matter: she lacks capacity, there is no usable prior statement, and the family's view is itself one of the contending positions.
5. State the quantities you are using — an intensity scale, hours per day, weeks or years — and show the arithmetic by which each of the two aggregating positions reaches its verdict, so that I can see the agreement is real and not an artefact of numbers chosen to suit me.
6. Flag any clinical assumption that is doing philosophical work, so that I know which parts of the medicine I am not free to change.
7. The whole choice has to be sayable in about a hundred words of bedside dialogue, because that is all the scene allows me.

What I would like back, in this order: the scenario in a single paragraph; what each of the three says and why, given only what that character believes; which single feature of the case is doing the work; what happens if that feature is removed — flatten the worst suffering in the harder path to the level of the worst suffering in the gentler path and tell me whether the disagreement collapses; the strongest attack one of the two aggregating characters can make on Marianne's position, and her strongest reply; the line or two of dialogue that would carry the disagreement in the scene; and an honest note on where, if anywhere, my specifications could only be met by rigging a number or a clinical fact, together with the nearest version of the case that works without rigging.

Please write in British English, and do not refer to any published literature or name any philosopher: I need the scene, not the sources.
