Hi — I'm giving a twenty-minute talk at my local U3A philosophy group in a fortnight and I could really do with some help prepping the question-and-answer bit. Bit of background: I'm 63, retired from IT, no academic background at all, and I've been chewing on this on my own since I read about Libet's experiments in a Sunday paper years ago and never quite let it go. The talk defends one claim: that the famous gap — electrical activity building up in the motor areas something like half a second before a volunteer flexes their wrist, while the volunteer says they first felt the urge only a couple of hundred milliseconds beforehand — doesn't tell us anything very alarming about free will. My first reason is that I think it's a finding about a very narrow kind of action. In that set-up nobody has any reason to press now rather than in five seconds' time; nothing is at stake, there's no deliberating, and nobody could give you a reason for picking that particular moment. I've started calling these 'drift actions', and my rough test for one is what I call the cup-of-tea test: if, when you ask someone afterwards why they did it at that moment rather than another, the honest answer is 'no reason, I just did', it was a drift action. So the gap may well be real for drift actions and only for drift actions — and drift actions are precisely the ones we don't hold anyone responsible for anyway. My second reason is a methodological worry I haven't come across anyone dealing with, though I'm sure someone has and I just wouldnt know: the whole result depends on volunteers watching a spot going round a clock face and telling you where it was when the urge showed up, but as far as I can tell nobody ever checked whether that person is any good at locating anything on that clock. Call it 'dot lag'. My proposal is a calibration run first: play them a tone they didn't cause, or flash a light, get them to report where the spot was, and subtract their personal error from the urge reports afterwards. Maybe the gap survives, maybe it halves, maybe some people are three hundred milliseconds out and the whole thing is mush.

The audience is about twenty-five people, nearly all retired, and I can more or less predict who will talk. There's a retired GP who will want to know about evidence quality, sample sizes and whether any of it has been replicated; a former probation officer who is interested almost entirely in whether any of this touches criminal responsibility and blame; a retired structural engineer who's read a lot of popular neuroscience, thinks we're all meat robots, and will be irritated by my talk; a mindfulness teacher who runs the local meditation group and reckons that with training people can catch an intention forming much earlier, so Libet's volunteers simply weren't paying attention properly; and one woman who finished a psychology degree a few years back and actually ran a version of the task in a lab module — she's the one I'm most worried about, because she'll know the set-up in detail.

What I'd like from you is a prep sheet, not a defence of my view. Give me the eight or so objections I'm actually going to get from these people, in roughly the order they'll arrive, each one phrased the way that person would phrase it rather than the way a philosopher would, with how much damage it does to what I'm arguing, a short reply I could give in about two minutes, and — this is the bit I most want — an honest note on where my reply is weak or where I should just concede the point and move on. Include the one question I'm hoping nobody asks and what I'd say if they do. And at the end, tell me which of my replies is genuinely load-bearing for the whole talk, and what single finding would make me scrap the drift-actions idea rather than patch it up — I'd rather know now where I'm probably wrong.
