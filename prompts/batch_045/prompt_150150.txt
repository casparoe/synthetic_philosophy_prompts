I am putting the finishing touches on the last session of a graduate seminar on practical rationality, and I would like to close it with something more dialectically alive than a survey of the standard arguments. The topic is money pumps and the rationality of preferences extended over time. My colleague in the economics department keeps insisting that the money pump is only a measuring instrument — a dramatic way of showing that an agent with cyclic or otherwise dynamically inconsistent preferences can be made to part with money — and that where no profitable pump is available there is no rational criticism left to make. I want to push back: it seems to me that the pump dramatizes a defect in the agent's own temporally extended agency, and that the defect would be there even in a world with no one inclined to exploit it. But I have to admit that some of the cases my colleague produces make the instrumentalist story look better than I expected, and I would like to see the clash staged properly before I decide what to tell my students.

Would you write a dialogue of roughly 900 to 1,200 words between two philosophers, Dr. Ilse Vandermeer and Dr. Rasheed Adeyemi, who are equally competent and equally well-read, and who disagree about exactly this? Vandermeer holds that diachronic rationality is a coherence condition on the agent's preferences and plans over time, so that vulnerability to a money pump is at best evidence of a defect that is already complete in the merely possible pump; Adeyemi holds that the only intelligible rational complaint is exploitation itself, so that a preference structure no one can profitably pump is, ipse facto, rationally beyond reproach. I would like them to work through three cases rather than declaim at each other. First, the toll: suppose every trade an exploiter induces costs the exploiter some small transaction cost, and the agent's intransitivity is correspondingly slight, so that cycling her around the triangle leaves the exploiter out of pocket; relatedly, suppose the cycle takes long enough that time discounting erases the agent's loss in present-value terms. Is this agent rational? If not, Vandermeer must explain why; if so, Adeyemi must explain why her rationality appears to depend on facts about other people's cost structures and on her own rate of impatience. Second, the dividend: an agent who can credibly commit to a stopping point, or whose resoluteness is known, can charge would-be exploiters an entry fee for the privilege of setting up a sequence she will then refuse to complete, so that being cyclic turns out to be financially advantageous. Adeyemi needs to say whether his view now recommends intransitivity, and Vandermeer needs to say whether the availability of a profit is any better news for her than the availability of a loss was bad news. Third, the strangers: the three trades in the cycle are offered by three unconnected people, in different cities, years apart, and the agent never faces the sequence as a single deliberative unit; after the first round trip she has learned something and never cycles again. I would like the dialogue to also cover the choice between myopic, sophisticated, and resolute ways of handling a sequential problem, and the charge that an agent with no maximal element in her menu cannot settle deliberation at all. Please let each of them raise at least one objection that genuinely lands and that the other has to answer rather than brush aside; let each concede something real at a point where the concession is honest; and let them end by locating where their residual disagreement actually lives — whether it is a substantive dispute about what rationality is for, or a verbal dispute conducted with the same word doing two jobs.

Two further requests. Please argue the positions from the structure of the cases and from first-order philosophical reasoning rather than by attributing views to particular authors or citing the literature; I want something my students can follow without a reading list. And when you have finished the dialogue, add a short out-of-character postscript of three or four sentences in which you state how confident you are that you have given each position its strongest genuine form, and single out the one move in the exchange you are least confident about — the claim you would most expect a specialist in this area to reject outright — together with a sentence on why that move is the fragile one.
