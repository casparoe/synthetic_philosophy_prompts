For the past few months I have been earning part of my income as a paid subject in a motor-neuroscience lab: EEG cap, a dot sweeping around a clock face, instructions to flex my wrist whenever the urge arises and then to report where the dot was when I first felt that urge, repeated many times over while I try not to count or plan ahead. The consent paperwork I signed covers all of this in about a page of flat bureaucratic prose and says nothing whatsoever about what any of it is supposed to show. I have been reading up on the literature since, and the thing I keep returning to is the paperwork itself: the way a single document can treat an introspective timing report as a measurement, a wrist flex as a decision, and a slow negative drift in averaged voltage as the moment at which that decision was made, without ever pausing to say so.

So: write the participant information sheet and informed consent form for the next iteration of this kind of study — Protocol 14-B, or whatever you prefer — as a parody. 700 to 900 words, deadpan institutional voice, numbered clauses, laid out like genuine ethics-committee paperwork: purpose of the study, eligibility, procedure, risks and side effects, data handling, right to withdraw, indemnity, signature block.

The jokes should come from getting the actual methodological disputes right rather than from invented absurdity. In particular, I want the form to quietly commit itself on: the arbitrariness of readiness-potential onset criteria and on what averaging across trials does to the onset; the worry that a slow drift toward threshold in a spontaneous-movement task is accumulation noise rather than a decision being formed; the instruction to keep the urge spontaneous while nevertheless producing one every few seconds; the reliability of clock-based reports of the time of conscious intention; the gap between finding a neural precursor and concluding that the conscious intention does no causal work; the veto carve-out, and whether vetoing is itself something the brain has to prepare for; the claim that an upcoming choice can be decoded from brain activity seconds before it is made; and a clause in which the form draws the metaphysical conclusion on the participant's behalf and invites them to tick a box conceding it, with an opt-out for compatibilists.

Aim the satire at the practice of operationalizing conscious intention and at the slide from method to metaphysics, not at neuroscience as such, and keep it legible to a reader who has never seen an EEG. No citations and no author names — the humour has to work from the ideas alone.

After the document, add a note of roughly 150 words on where the parody takes liberties with the science or leans on a contested reading, and single out the point you are least confident about.
