You are a philosopher of social science and public policy who is equally at home in survey methodology and in welfare economics — someone who has argued with both happiness researchers and their critics and found each side half right. Context. I direct a small wellbeing evidence unit inside the finance ministry of a middle-income country of roughly 40 million people. I have about two years and the equivalent of six analyst-years, the ability to bolt one new module onto an existing national household survey, access to administrative data, and essentially no capacity to run large field experiments. Our minister has said he wants the country to top the annual global happiness rankings within a decade. My job is to decide what my unit should actually spend its scarce effort on.

I can distinguish three goals his slogan might mean: (i) improve our rank; (ii) raise whatever the rankings actually measure; (iii) raise how well our citizens' lives go, whether or not any index captures it. Which of these come apart, and how much, is itself something I want your agenda to tell me how to find out.

Here are a few distinctions my team has been using informally; adopt them, refine them, or explain why they are confused:

- Ladder credit versus affect capital. A government can plausibly raise citizens' answers to the standard 0-to-10 life-evaluation question by improving the story people can tell about their lives — status, national prestige, perceived upward mobility — while depleting the mundane conditions that generate positive daily experience: sleep, unhurried sociability, time not spent commuting. Call the difference ladder credit minus affect capital. The open question is whether the flagship rankings are sensitive to something that can diverge from lived experience in this way, and in which direction and magnitude for a country like ours.
- Compositional versus causal readings of rank. A country's score can rise because its people are doing better, or because the unhappy have emigrated, or because the surviving population has adapted its expectations downward. These have identical signatures in a cross-sectional ranking.
- The publication-lag problem. Rankings grade a government on data collected years earlier, so a government that tops them is largely being graded on its predecessor's economy — which matters a great deal for whether rank is even an actionable target.
- Rank-conditional budgeting. Suppose the treasury must publish a written justification whenever a policy is projected to lower the national rank. That rule turns the index into an explicit constraint rather than a report card.
