I do moderation for a fairly large hobbyist forum, and I also sit on a hiring committee at work, and between the two I keep bumping into the same shape of thing. I've been jotting examples down in a notes app for maybe a year now and I still don't have a word for it. Three of them: a reference letter from a well-known professor that says the candidate is unfailingly punctual, always on time, and keeps immaculate lab notebooks — everyone who reads it concludes the candidate isn't much good, and the writer plainly knew they would; someone at a faculty meeting who opens with "I want to be clear up front that I have no objection whatever to this proposal," after which nobody in the room believes they have no objection; and a regular on my forum who everytime writes "I'm not saying X, I'm just asking questions about X," with the result that X is now treated as settled in the thread.

What I want a name for is the mechanism rather than the content. In each case the person said something true, or at least not false, conveyed something further, and built into their own sentence the exact move they'll use if anyone challenges them. I did one pragmatics module years ago and remember roughly two things: that there's a distinction between what you literally say and what you convey by saying it, and that the conveyed part is supposed to be cancelable, as in you can tack on "but I don't mean to suggest..." and it evaporates. But in these cases the cancelling move is already inside the utterance and it cancels nothing — if anything the disclaimer makes people more certain of the thing being disclaimed. So either I've misremembered how the cancelling is supposed to work, or this is a different animal. There's a second thing that feels morally backwards and I can't tell if I'm imagining it: normally I'd assume that hinting at something false is less bad than flatly asserting it, but when someone picked the hinting route specifically so they could disown it later, it strikes me as worse than just saying it. And then there's an edge I can't place — half the time people hint out of politeness rather than to keep an escape hatch, saying "a few guests had already left" instead of "most of your guests hated your party," and that seems completely fine rather than sneaky. So deniability can't be the whole story.

So: does this pattern have a name, or is it several named things that I'm lumping together into one? I'm after the carving, not the label — what exactly is going on, which nearby things it is not (hinting to spare someone's feelings, outright lying, saying something literally false while communicating something true the way irony does), and where the boundary falls. My partner thinks I'm overthinking a thing that just has a boring ordinary name, and maybe she's right, but if so I'd like to know why the polite version and the forum version feel so different. And if there genuinely isn't an established term for it, coin one and define it tightly enough that I could look at a borderline case and say whether it's happening.
