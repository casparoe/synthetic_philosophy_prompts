The topic is Harsanyi's aggregation theorem and whether it really delivers utilitarianism. Please have Meg state the theorem properly and not vaguely: if the social ranking over lotteries satisfies the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms and respects Pareto in the ex ante sense (if everyone prefers prospect P to Q by their own expected utility, society ranks P above Q), then social welfare is a weighted sum of individuals' expected utilities, and the impartial-observer move gets you equal weights. Be explicit that the Pareto in play is ex ante — that has to be on the table from early on, because it's where the real fight is.

The case they keep coming back to, which you should build into the argument rather than have them recite: 10,000 people, all at the same baseline, fixed population, nobody's existence at stake. Option Flat: everyone gets 1 unit of welfare for sure. Option Spread: an independent fair coin per person, heads 3, tails 0. Option Big Flip: one fair coin for the whole population, heads everyone gets 3, tails everyone gets 0. Stipulate that everyone is risk-neutral about their own welfare, so nobody wins by appealing to people's own risk aversion. Note that Spread and Big Flip are ex ante identical for every individual and have the same expected total (15,000 vs 10,000 for Flat).

Things I want them to actually work through, in their own way:

1. The comparability problem. Avi should press that the weights and the zero points are artifacts of how you normalize each person's utility function (u → a·u + b, a > 0), so the theorem's "weighted sum" isn't a substantive moral conclusion until you've fixed an interpersonal comparability convention. Have him lay out the informational difference: a weighted sum needs comparisons of gains (unit comparability) but not of levels; a concave sum Σf(u) needs full comparability including levels; leximin/maximin needs levels only. Meg gets a real reply here — the impartial-observer device (imagine yourself equally likely to be anyone, with one utility function over being-person-i-at-welfare-x) is supposed to supply both units and levels. Then Avi gets to say why that device doesn't close the gap on its own: it needs the observer's preferences to track each person's own attitudes under uncertainty, which is ex ante Pareto again, so it's a consistency argument rather than a foundation.

2. Why the three options matter. Get them to sort out which case is doing which work. In Spread, once the coins land there's basically no uncertainty left — with 10,000 people the realized split is 5,000/5,000 up to a rounding error, total welfare is 15,000, and the disagreement with Flat is purely about distribution. In Big Flip the uncertainty is aggregate and never washes out. So: is Harsanyi's theorem one argument here or two? Meg gets to argue it's one uniform theorem; Avi gets to argue that in Spread the whole risk-and-uncertainty framing is idle and what's really being tested is ex ante unanimity against a near-certain distribution, and that a principle that recommends Spread over Flat while there's no uncertainty left to appeal to isn't Pareto in any ordinary sense.
