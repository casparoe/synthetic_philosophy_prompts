I work mainly in moral philosophy, but for the past two years I have sat on a university committee that hears complaints, and the experience has pushed me into a corner of epistemology I was never trained in. I keep encountering the same configuration and I have no word for it. Three instances. First: I form a confident view about what happened in a case from the four or five people who were willing to speak to me; only later do I learn that the people with the opposite story had every reason to stay silent, so that the pool of testimony I drew on was filtered by the world rather than by me. Nothing I was told has been shown false, and I do not think I reasoned badly about what I heard — yet I now take myself to be required to be substantially less confident. Second: in my other life I work on a 1930s philosopher's unpublished correspondence. For years the letters I could read were the ones a single executor chose to deposit, and I formed views about her religious commitments on that basis. When the remaining boxes surfaced my confidence collapsed, but not because any letter turned out to be forged or because I had misread them: it collapsed because I learned that what I had was a truncated sample of a larger set I could not see, and truncated in a direction. Third, the mirror image: a senior colleague I trust has silently pre-filtered a literature for me and passed on only what she judged methodologically sound. Here I do not take myself to be permitted to be *more* confident than the papers themselves warrant, even though her filtering is, structurally, just as much a fact about the provenance of my evidence as in the first case.

What I want is not a label to drop but a distinction I could use in print, so please carve rather than name. The thing I am gesturing at sits near several established notions without, as far as I can tell, coinciding with any of them. It is not a rebutting defeater: I acquire no evidence that the proposition is false. It does not look like an undercutting defeater either: each letter still supports the claim about the philosopher's beliefs exactly as strongly as it did before. It is not epistemic akrasia as I have seen that discussed, since I am not left believing that p while believing that my evidence fails to support p — I fully accept that my evidence supports p; what I have learned is that my evidence is probably not the whole of the evidence there is. And it is not confirmation bias or selective exposure, since in my cases the truncation is not something I did. The nearest candidates I can think of are the requirement of total evidence (Carnap's, I believe — correct me if the attribution is wrong), which seems to state a norm rather than name the failure mode; the statistician's notion of a biased sample, which is about estimators rather than about what I should believe; and the epistemologists' category of higher-order evidence, which in my reading of it is mostly about defects in one's own processing or about disagreement with others, whereas my cases concern defects in what the world handed me — and which seem to generate none of the puzzles that literature trades in, precisely because the new fact can apparently be treated as ordinary first-order evidence about the likely existence of unobserved counter-evidence.

So: does this configuration have an established name of its own, and is it classified as a species of higher-order evidence, as a form of defeat, or as something else entirely? If it has no name, coin one, define it, and give me its boundary cases. In particular I need answers to three question I cannot settle myself: whether the phenomenon requires me to know the direction of the bias, or whether merely knowing that my sample is unrepresentative suffices; whether the rational mandate lands on my first-order credence at all, or only on some higher-order attitude about the completeness of my evidence base; and why, if at all, the negative and positive cases come apart. I am not after a reading list or references — I want the distinction itself, sharpened to the point where I could state it in a paragraph and defend its edges in a footnote.
