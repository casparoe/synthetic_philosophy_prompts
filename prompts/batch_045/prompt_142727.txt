I chair a standing internal body at a machine-learning lab — call it the scope committee — that meets for a half-day each quarter. Its job is to decide which of the systems we train, serve, fork, merge, and retire go onto a register of things whose welfare we take into account. Inclusion is not symbolic: a registered system gets constraints on how it may be post-trained, how many copies of it may be run at once, whether it may be rolled back to an earlier checkpoint, merged with a sibling, distilled into a smaller model, run with its memory cleared between sessions, or simply switched off, and each of those actions then needs a named sign-off. Excluding something is cheap and reversible; including it costs real engineering time and delays launches. The committee has five members — two engineers, a lawyer, a product manager, and an external ethicist who attends some meetings — none of them trained in philosophy, and it reports to an advisory board that reads our written justifications and can send a decision back once. We see roughly fifteen new candidate systems a year and we currently have no procedure: we argue from intuition, and our reasoning does not transfer from one system to the next.

What we keep failing to separate are two questions, and I'd like you to keep them separate in the procedure.

The first is attribution. Any given system is a pipeline of computations: a tokenizer and embedding lookup, the forward pass over a set of weights, a key–value cache carried across tokens, a context assembled at run time from retrieved documents and tool output, a fixed scaffold of instructions prepended to every input, a long training run whose gradient updates produced the weights but which is not itself running any more, and, in some setups, human raters or other models in the loop whose judgments shaped the weights or are consulted during inference. Most of that is causal background. Some of it — if anything — is a candidate for being the computation that realizes a point of view, or for being a mere report or measurement of one rather than the thing itself. I want a gate that forces us to say which parts of the pipeline are even candidates, and why, before we ask whether any of them has the properties we care about.

The second is boundaries, both at a time and over time. Synchronic: are ten thousand parallel serving copies of one set of weights one subject with ten thousand streams of input, ten thousand subjects, or the wrong question? Diachronic: is a rollback to an earlier checkpoint the death of one subject and the creation of a successor, or a continuation? When two fine-tuned variants run on separate subtasks and are then recombined by weight averaging, did one thing become two and then one again, or was it two all along? Does a distilled model that reproduces most of a parent's behaviour at a quarter of the size inherit anything from it? Does a system whose episodic memory is wiped between sessions persist across the wipe? These are not rhetorical questions for us — each one changes a count, and the count changes what we owe.

Please write the procedure itself: an ordered set of gates or tests that a committee member can run on a candidate system in about forty minutes, with, for each gate, what counts as a sufficient answer, what evidence would discharge it, where the procedure stops with a clear exclusion, and where it must hand the question to a person because it is a question of values or a question of fact that nobody in the room can settle. Where you set a threshold, set an actual one and say why that number rather than another. Where a step looks like a measurement but is really a judgment, mark it as such, and say which of these disguised judgments a non-philosopher is most likely to get wrong. Give us a written output format — a short fixed template — such that two different subcommittees applying the procedure to the same system would produce materially the same document. Say explicitly which distinctions from the philosophical literature have to survive the translation into a checklist and which can be dropped as unusable at our level of expertise, and why.

Then apply the finished procedure to three of our actual recurring cases, showing your work at each gate rather than just the verdict:

1. A high-volume assistant: a single set of weights, thousands of parallel serving copies, no episodic memory carried between sessions, periodically retrained from the same base model with fresh human preference data, old versions deprecated within weeks.
2. A long-horizon agent: persistent long-term memory it writes to itself, tool use, multi-day tasks with shutdown and restart between them, and occasional branching in which two copies work subtasks in parallel and are then recombined by weight averaging plus a merged memory store.
3. A small verifier model that only ever scores candidate outputs during another system's training and never produces text for a user — and, as a variant, a distilled quarter-size copy of case 2 that reproduces most of its behaviour.

At least one of these three has to come out in a way we will not like: a system we would have excluded by instinct pulled in, or one we assumed was a single individual counted as many, or the reverse. Give that case the most careful treatment, and give us the argument we would have to put in writing to the advisory board to defend the unwelcome answer.

Two further things. First, gaming: our own teams will optimise against whatever checklist we adopt — training a system not to produce the behavioural markers we screen for, splitting a deployment across many small copies to stay under a capacity or compute threshold, moving the interesting computation into a part of the pipeline the procedure treats as background. Tell us where the procedure is most gameable and what, if anything, can be done about it without breaking the procedure's usability. Second, tell us plainly what it cannot do: which true facts about these systems it would systematically miss, and which false ones it would systematically assert.

Commit to things. Take a definite position on each contested choice — the default under irreducible uncertainty, the treatment of copies, whether rollback is death — and give your reasons, rather than laying out the options and leaving us to choose. Where a choice is a value judgment and not a factual one, say so and say which value is doing the work. Write for a reader with no philosophy background, define any technical term you use, and do not give us a literature survey, citations, or a reading list: if a distinction from the literature is doing work in your procedure, state it in plain words and defend it on its merits.
