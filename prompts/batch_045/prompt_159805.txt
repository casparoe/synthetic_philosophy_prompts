After forty years in experimental physical chemistry and a late turn to philosophy of science, I now moderate a small colloquium series for scientifically literate non-specialists, and next month's guest is a philosopher who defends a convergentist answer to the historical argument from unconceived alternatives. Her position, as I understand it, is that underdetermination is genuine but transient: she calls the gap "evidential slack," meaning the space of theories compatible with the evidence available at a given time, and she holds that this slack shrinks as measurement resolution improves, at a rate that she treats as an empirical and discoverable feature of a field, faster in some fields than in others, which is what she then uses to say where realism is and is not warranted. She also has a habit of answering objections with "that is really an empirical question about the history of inquiry, not an objection."

Please draft eight interview questions, each with the reply she is most likely to give and a follow-up keyed to that reply, and make at least one of them a question to which that habitual move is not available. Cover: how evidential slack can be measured without already knowing which alternatives are true; whether her own decay-rate estimate is exposed to the very historical induction she turns against realists, since it is a prediction about present inquiry drawn from past inquiry; how her picture differs from the older holist claim that any theory can be shielded by adjusting auxiliaries; and two candidate counterexamples of my own, each with a question about how she would rebut or absorb it — first, cosmology, where, as I understand it, the data have tightened enormously in recent decades while the space of live models has if anything widened, the cosmological constant remaining observationally indistinguishable from dynamical and modified-gravity alternatives; second, quantum foundations, where empirically equivalent interpretations have proved stubborn across comparable gains in precision. Close with one question about what result in either field would lead her to give the view up. Keep the questions answerable with no citations, named authors, or reading lists, and write in American English.
