I teach a bridge course in reading and writing proofs for first-year philosophy and mathematics students. Over the past two years I have also been building, mostly as a teaching aid, a small proof checker for the course: students write a derivation in a Hilbert-style calculus and the program says whether each line is licensed. The design work is what revived an old worry for me. I found that I could add as many axiom schemas as I liked to the object language, including schemas that seemed to state detachment itself, and the kernel still had to contain a rule of detachment to make any use of them. Then a student who had read Carroll's dialogue about Achilles and the tortoise used it to refuse a step in a proof, and told me that my checker had the same defect as Achilles: it does not apply its rule, it merely contains it.

I tried to answer him, and the answer I built up seems to me to prove far more than I am willing to believe. I will set it out with the steps numbered.

(1) To be entitled to a conclusion C on the basis of premises P and P ⊃ C, a reasoner must be entitled to P, must be entitled to P ⊃ C, and must also stand in some relation to the rule of detachment. That much is the tortoise's own point, and I think it is right: someone can accept all the premises of a valid argument, understand them, and still not move.

(2) Suppose that standing in that relation to the rule is a matter of accepting a content, namely the content that from P and P ⊃ C one may infer C. Call this the premise-model of standing to a rule.

(3) On the premise-model, the tortoise is unanswerable. At every stage he can grant the content and withhold the transition, since accepting a content is one thing and moving is another. No finite list of accepted contents, however long, and however many of them look like statements of the rule, yields the transition by itself. So no additional premise can stop the regress.

(4) Therefore what distinguishes accepting the rule from applying it cannot be a matter of which contents the reasoner accepts. It must be a matter of what she does.

(5) But what she does also has to be characterised. She moves from P to C in accordance with the rule. And for her movement to be in accordance with the rule, we must be able to attribute to her a state or disposition whose content is the rule; if we cannot, her movement is a bare happening, and its accordance with the rule does no work in the account of her.

(6) At this point we appear to be back where we began. Either that state's content is what makes the movement hers and rule-governed, in which case the state is doing exactly the work of a further premise she accepts, and by (3) she can accept it without moving; or it is not doing that work, in which case (5) has given us nothing and we have no account of what following the rule consists in.

(7) So one of two things must hold. Either nothing about the reasoner, no state with content, makes her a follower of the rule; or whatever does make her a follower of the rule is not a state with content at all.

(8) On the first option, rule-following is not something she does for a reason; it is something that happens in her. On the second option, rule-following is a causal disposition, and whether a given disposition is a following of this rule rather than of some other rule that agrees with it on all the cases so far is not settled by anything in her, and is not something she can be correct or incorrect about, since any finite run of behaviour is compatible with indefinitely many incompatible rules.

(9) Either way, inference is not a rational transition. It is not a case of believing one thing because of another. It is a habit produced in her, which we may describe as conforming to a rule or as failing to, but which is not answerable to a rule.

(10) But if (9) is true there is no difference in kind between a student who is persuaded by a proof and one who has been conditioned to write the last line, and no difference in kind between my checker, which its kernel causes to accept a step, and a student who accepts it because she sees that it follows. I take this to be unacceptable, and it would make nonsense of the course I am teaching.

What I want from you is a diagnosis of this argument, not a summary of the topic. I already accept the usual moral, that a rule cannot be replaced by a premise, and I have built that into (1) through (4); the interesting part is downstream. Specifically:

Tell me which step you reject and why that step looked compulsory when I wrote it. I have a preference about where the weight should fall: I want to keep (1), I want to keep (3), and I want to keep the denial of (9) and (10). So I would rather give up something in (5), (6) or (7) than any of those, if that is coherent. If it is not coherent, say so and tell me which of my preferred claims is the cheapest to surrender and what surrendering it costs me.

Then price the alternatives. As I understand the field there are several places one might land: the distinction between stating a rule and using it, taken as a distinction between kinds of thing rather than between kinds of content; the view that circularity in the use of a rule is benign where circularity in the premises is not; the view that inference essentially involves the reasoner taking her premises to support her conclusion; the view that a mental act or activity can have correctness conditions fixed by its aim without those conditions being represented by the agent; and the quietist view that the demand lying behind the whole argument, the demand for a justification of deduction, is simply misfounded and that the right response to the tortoise is to stop rather than to ascend. Say, for each, which numbered step it rejects, what it thereby has to explain that its rivals do not, and what it has to pay.

Finally, bring it back to the two cases I began with. On your diagnosis, what should I say to the student, in one paragraph, that neither concedes his point nor merely asserts the rule at him? And does my proof checker infer, or only compute? If it only computes, I would like to know what would have to be true of it for the question to have a different answer, and whether anything in your diagnosis suggests that the question has no answer at all.
