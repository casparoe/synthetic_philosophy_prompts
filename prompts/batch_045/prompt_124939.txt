Galen Strawson has defended what he calls the Basic Argument against moral responsibility. In outline: you can be responsible for what you do only if you are responsible for the way you are, since what you do flows from your character, desires, and deliberative dispositions; but to be responsible for the way you are, you would have had to bring it about, and bringing it about would itself have had to flow from dispositions you already had, for which you would in turn have to be responsible; so the demand can never be met, and the kind of responsibility that would ground genuine desert — call it ultimate responsibility — is impossible. Strawson adds that indeterminism offers no escape: to the extent that an action is not settled by the way you are, it is a matter of luck, and luck is no better a basis for desert than determinism is.

Now construct the parallel argument for the epistemic case. Call it the Doxastic Basic Argument. To be creditable for a belief — to have it reflect well on you as a knower, rather than merely to have it reliably produced in you — you must have formed it in light of reasons you took yourself to have; what you take yourself to have reason to believe is a function of your prior beliefs and inferential habits; so you would have to be creditable for those prior states; and so on, without terminus. And indeterministic belief formation would not help, since a belief not settled by your evidence-responsive dispositions would be doxastic luck, not insight. So no one is ultimately creditable for anything they believe.

Compare these two arguments. Assume for the sake of the discussion that they share the same regress structure, and address whether sharing a structure means sharing a fate.

In your comparison, engage with at least the following:

1. The obvious asymmetry of escape routes. In epistemology, externalism about justification and knowledge enjoys wide support: one can hold that a belief counts as knowledge in virtue of the reliability of the process that produced it, with no requirement that the subject be creditable for possessing that process. Call the corresponding strategy in ethics the no-credit move: an agent is morally responsible just in case the mechanism issuing in the action is appropriately responsive to reasons, with no requirement that the agent be responsible for having that mechanism. Is this move genuinely available in the ethical case, or does the move that looks like externalism there turn out to be deflationary — a redescription of responsible agency rather than an account of the desert that Strawson's argument targets? Is the same charge available against epistemic externalism?

2. Whether the asymmetry is principled. Argue either that the two domains differ in a way that justifies treating the arguments differently — for example, that knowledge is a success term whose value need not be credit-based, whereas responsibility is essentially an accountability relation that seems to require authorship — or that the differing fortunes of the two arguments are explained by something non-principled, such as what each field is trying to theorize about.

3. Reflexive regress-stoppers. Suppose an agent's dispositions include a higher-order component that takes its own operation as an object: a belief-forming method that endorses only outputs it also judges to be produced reliably, or a deliberative disposition to act only on desires one endorses having. Call this the reflexivity gambit. Does reflexivity stop the regress in either argument, or does it merely relocate it? If it differs between the two cases, say why.

Conclude with a verdict: which of the two arguments is in better dialectical shape, and what does that verdict show about whether the difficulty in the moral case is really about responsibility at all.
