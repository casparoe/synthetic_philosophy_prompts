I am trying to get clear on what the formula of universal law is actually doing — not on whether it is true, but on what shape a workable version of it would have to have. I have stopped trusting the stock examples from the Groundwork, because I can recite their verdicts without being able to apply the test to anything new. So I have invented my own cases, none of which I have seen discussed, and I wrote down my verdicts before I thought about them. I have arranged them as three pairs: in each pair the two maxims look the same to the test I am using, but I judge them differently, and I do not want to give up either verdict unless I am shown why I should.

Here is the test I have been working with — call it U. To test a maxim M, imagine the world W(M) in which every agent has adopted M and acts on it whenever M's triggering conditions obtain, with everything else about the world left as it is. M is impermissible if and only if either

(a) breakdown: in W(M) the action M directs would no longer be able to serve the purpose for which the agent performs it, because some practice, institution, or expectation on which that purpose depends would no longer exist or be relied on; or

(b) will: no rational agent could will W(M) to obtain, because it would systematically frustrate an end that any agent must have.

Otherwise M is permissible.

Now the cases. In each, the maxim is the thing to be tested; nothing should turn on its exact wording.

Pair one: self-defeat without free-riding.

1. There is an honor-system farm stand near me: you take what you want and drop cash in a box. My maxim: when I take produce from the stand and I am short of cash that week, I take it and skip the box. Verdict: wrong, and not a close call.
2. My maxim for Saturday hikes: I take whichever trail is least crowded, because I want solitude. Verdict: not wrong at all — at most mildly foolish, and even that is doubtful.

U handles 1 easily: universal skipping closes the stand, and my getting produce by paying depends on the stand. But I cannot see why U does not also condemn 2: universal trail-choice frustrates the purpose of trail-choice just as thoroughly as universal box-skipping frustrates the purpose of paying. A friend says the difference is that the trail maxim is not about a practice or institution at all, so nothing that its purpose depends on is destroyed. That sounds to me like it might be a way of protecting the test rather than an answer to the question, and I want to know whether there is a principled version of it — roughly, that the maxim must succeed only by relying on others continuing to comply while I exempt myself. Is that the right clause? And if it is, notice that it leaves U silent about a large class of counter-coordination maxims; tell me whether that silence is a feature or a cost.

Pair two: how narrow may an exception be?

3. My maxim about promising: I keep my promises, except on Tuesdays, when I break one if I would rather not keep it. Verdict: wrong, and wrong in the same way as 1.
4. My maxim about promising again: I keep my promises, except when keeping a trivial one would mean failing to get a stranger to a hospital. Verdict: permissible, and obviously so.

U looks as though it must acquit 3: promising survives in W(3), it just does not operate on Tuesdays, and a world in which everyone breaks Tuesday promises is perfectly willable. So U undergenerates badly here. The repair I have been playing with is that a maxim must be tested as an instance of the broader policy it expresses rather than in its own letter, so that 3 is tested as something like break a promise whenever I would rather, and collapses like 1. But 4 is also an instance of a broader policy of breaking promises when it suits me, and I do not want that result. So I need a principled distinction between an exception class I may write into my maxim and one I may not, and everything I try collapses into: exceptions that are arbitrary with respect to the point of the practice. Is that the best available clause, and what exactly does arbitrary mean here?

Pair three: waiting for someone else to go first.

5. My maxim when someone collapses in a public place: I call an ambulance only if nobody else has already called one. Verdict: wrong, and seriously wrong.
6. My maxim in faculty meetings: I raise an objection only if no one else has already raised the same objection. Verdict: I go back and forth. Mostly I think it is not wrong, or wrong only in the weak sense of being lazy; but it has, as far as I can tell, exactly the structure of 5 — universal adoption means nobody ever acts, and the purpose of each maxim is frustrated by its own universalization — and I have no verdict-preserving difference to offer.

Here I cannot even tell which clause of U is supposed to do the work. There is no institution of calling ambulances that my maxim feeds on; if 5 fails it seems to fail under (b), because I cannot will a world in which nobody ever calls for me. But then (b) is carrying case 5 and the familiar refusal-to-aid example on its own, and I worry that the universalization machinery has become decoration while something like beneficence does all the work. And 5 and 6 differ, if they differ, in what looks like stakes — a life versus a tedious meeting — for which U has no room. Should it have room?
