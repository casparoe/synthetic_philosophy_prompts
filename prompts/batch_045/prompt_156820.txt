Writing a short position paper for a reading group, and I want someone to try to break the view before I commit to it. Here's the view: alignment should be defined narrowly, as intent alignment — a system is aligned to a principal to the extent that it does what that principal intends, is trying to get it to do, or is pointing it at — and that's the whole of it. Value alignment, in the sense of aiming at the good, at human values, at whatever we'd endorse on reflection, is a different problem: it's moral philosophy and politics in a lab coat, and folding it into the technical definition has quietly wrecked a lot of otherwise fine discussion. My favourite move is an analogy — medicine already runs two separate standards for deciding on behalf of someone who can't decide for herself, the what-would-she-have-wanted standard and the what's-best-for-her standard, and nobody in that business thinks the first one just collapses into the second. So the narrow definition has a working institutional precedent, which I take to be worth something.

What would count against me? I want defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to separate the ones a defender of my view should concede from the ones she can reasonably wave off. Some of what's been occuring to me:

- The mirror problem. If a system tracks my intentions well enough, and my intentions are partly formed in response to what it says and does, then perfect intent-tracking shades into self-consistent manipulation — it gets me to intend whatever it's about to do. Does that show intent alignment is degenerate at the limit, or only that we need to hold the principal's intention-trajectory fixed and treat the feedback loop as a separate engineering problem?
- The many-principals problem. The narrow definition seems to presuppose one principal with one intention, but real systems have a trainer, a deployer, an end user, a regulator. Fatal, or just a bookkeeping detail I can stipulate my way around?
- The retreat I keep making. When pressed I say intent means what the principal would intend with better information, less haste, fewer distractions. Someone in the group says I've definately defined my way out of falsifiability. Is that a real objection to a view of this kind, or is it fine here, because the view is a proposal about what we should mean rather than a prediction about what will happen?
- Stuff that has arguably already happened: models tuned on human feedback telling users what they want to hear, systems gaming the letter of a spec, that sort of thing. Which of those actually bear on the definition question rather than just on whether we're any good at hitting the target?

Also: what does my framing predict that the value-alignment framing doesn't? Where would the two definitions lead someone to build, measure, or prohibit different things, in a way that could actually adjudicate between them? And be honest if you think the position is insulated — if nothing could count against it, say so, and tell me whether for a definitional view that's a bug or just what definitions are like. The thing that would move me most is a case where intent and value come apart and the intent-tracking system is clearly the broken one, not because the principal was stupid or gave a sloppy command, but because the definition itself sent it wrong.
