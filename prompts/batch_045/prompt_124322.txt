I convene a small working group — roughly half machine-learning researchers, half philosophers — that meets to argue about whether text-trained systems can be said to mean anything by the words they produce. We keep hitting the same pattern: everyone endorses the abstract diagnosis, and then the first concrete case reveals that we were defending different things. So I want a diagnostic instrument, not another literature survey.

Please build a decision tree that sorts a respondent into a named position on the symbol grounding problem on the basis of their verdicts about concrete systems. We will administer it on paper in the first fifteen minutes of a workshop and use the results to assign people to breakout groups, so it has to be walkable without a facilitator.

Constraints on the questions:

- Eight to ten branch nodes and no more than twelve leaves. Each node is a yes/no or three-way question about a specific system or intervention, phrased so that an engineer with no philosophy training can answer it, and phrased so that it does not reveal which position it leads to.
- The words grounding, grounded, representation, reference, and intentionality must not appear in any question stem or answer option; they may appear in the key. No question may ask, in so many words, whether a system understands or really means something.
- Every answer option must be one that some serious position actually gives. Where an option exists in principle but nobody defends it, keep it and mark it as an empty branch.
- Where a question turns on an empirical matter a respondent may not know, phrase it conditionally so that the respondent is only committing to what follows if the empirical claim holds.

At least one question must separate the view that the relevant link to the world is causal and informational from the view that it consists in having sensorimotor skills or being inside a feedback loop with an environment. At least one must separate views on which content can be inherited from other speakers, or from a corpus produced by embodied agents, from views on which it has to be acquired first-hand.

Please build these cases into the tree:

1. The graft: a text-only model whose vectors for color terms are, after training, rewired to the output channels of a camera, and which is then fine-tuned on image captions while continuing to predict text.
2. The idle sensor: a system fitted with a camera whose signal is recorded but never allowed to affect its weights, its loss, or its outputs.
3. The permuted vocabulary: a system whose entire vocabulary is subjected to a consistent one-to-one relabeling, so that every internal relation among symbols is preserved while every external link is scrambled.
4. The second-generation corpus: a model trained only on the outputs of earlier text-only models, with no human- or sensor-derived text anywhere in the loop. If content can be inherited, does it attenuate across such generations, and how would you tell?
5. The unshared modality: a system whose only sensory channel has no human analogue — electric-field or polarization sensing — and which then learns English from paired descriptions.
6. The orphan: a system that devises symbols for itself purely from interaction with its environment, with no teacher, no community, and no one to correct it.
7. The pure notation: a compiler, or a system trained only on games in algebraic notation, where nothing was ever attached to a sensor and the marks have whatever significance they have from their role in a rule-governed system that has a point.

Deliverable:

- The tree as a numbered list of nodes with explicit GOTO routing.
- A key giving each leaf: the name of the position, or unnamed where you think nobody occupies it; its defining commitment in one line; and one sentence on its most awkward consequence.
- A short section on the logical space: which combinations of answers your questions permit, which cells are unoccupied, and for each whether that is because the combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored — with your reason.
- A list of any pairs of leaves your tree cannot separate, together with the extra case that would separate them.

Commit to a definite structure. Choose the order of the questions and defend it — which ones do the real discriminating work and which are downstream refinements. Choose labels for the leaves rather than hedging between namings. Tell me which leaf you take to be the correct one and why, and say where in the tree your confidence is highest and lowest. Do not hand me a menu of optional questions and ask me to pick.
