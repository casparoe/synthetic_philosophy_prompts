I need a case built to order, for a paper I'm working on about why HCH is a bad stand-in for "what a person would conclude if they could deliberate perfectly."

Setup so we mean the same thing by our words. HCH is the scheme where one person gets to break a hard question into smaller questions and hand each piece to a fresh copy of themselves; those copies can do the same, with as many levels as they want and no time limit; the answers get summarized and passed back up the tree. Factored cognition is the bet that a hard question really can be split into easier ones whose answers jointly settle the original. What makes it tempting as a picture of ideal deliberation is that the tree has no time pressure, no memory limit, and nothing gets skipped — it looks like you, but able to think as long as you like.

Here's what I want: a case where running a person as HCH produces a confident, stable answer that is not the answer the same person reaches by deliberating, in the ordinary way, about the whole question — same information, unlimited time. And the gap has to come from the factoring itself, not from anything boring.

Constraints:

- Realistic. One decision a real adult might actually face: job, money, family, medical, moving, whatever. No AIs in the story, no trolleys, no sci-fi, no gadgets. The leaf questions have to be things you could hand to a sharp friend and get a decent answer out of in a couple of minutes, and the person has to genuinely be competent at them, so this can't be an ignorance failure.
- Not a mistake case. Nobody in the tree gets a fact wrong, misreads their own subquestion, runs out of patience, or anchors on a number for no reason. Every node behaves exactly as you'd want it to.
- Not a gerrymander. Each decomposition you use has to be one a thoughtful person would call a natural way to break the question down. No "subquestion 4: what do I secretly hope the answer is," no decomposition that quietly drops a consideration anyone would count.
- Both answers have to be stable, not noise. Run the same tree twice and you get the same thing; the same goes for the person's own deliberated verdict.
- The obvious repair has to fail too. Someone will say: fine, have a hundred copies factor it a hundred different ways and take the majority. In your case, the majority of natural decompositions has to land on the HCH answer, not on the deliberated one. That's the part I think is hardest and it's the part I actually care about.

After the scenario I want:

1. The scenario, one paragraph, in ordinary language.
2. The answer HCH gives, and roughly how the tree gets there, node by node.
3. The answer ordinary unfactored deliberation gives, and why.
4. The one structural feature doing the work, named, plus what happens to the case if you remove it — does the divergence just disappear?
5. What happens if you let the tree deliberate about how to decompose: add a node upstream that chooses the factoring. Does the case survive or does it collapse? Be honest either way.
6. The strongest thing a defender of HCH-as-ideal-deliberation could say. Not a strawman. I especially want you to deal with the objection that real human deliberation is sequential and order-dependent too, so your case shows nothing special about factoring — tell me why that doesn't just dispatch it, or concede if it does.

What it's for, since that fixes what counts as cheating: I want to argue that the factoring structure isn't neutral packaging around a person's judgment, that it distorts the judgment systematically in a way unbounded deliberation doesn't. So anything that gets the divergence by stipulating it, or by making somebody in the tree irrational, or by loading in an assumption that only the tree has to live with, is cheating and I'll have to throw the case out.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — wether the case works and what's carrying it — then give me the rest. No preamble, and don't restate my question back at me. American English please.
