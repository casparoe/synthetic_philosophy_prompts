hey — I write a column on ideas and tech, and my editor finally said he'd look at something funny if I could show him a draft, so: I want to take a swing at this whole "factored cognition" picture and I need something I can hand him.

Background as I understand it (correct me in the draft if I've got it mangled): there's a proposal floating around AI-alignment circles for building a trustworthy overseer out of ordinary humans. A human is allowed to ask questions of an assistant; that assistant is itself a human who can ask questions of an assistant; and so on down, so what you really have is an enormous tree of people each answering one narrow sub-question, and the thing you get at the limit — people call it HCH — is offered as an idealization of what good deliberation *is*: careful thinking just is the breaking of a hard question into easier ones, and then you amplify the result. The parts are supposed to compose, and the whole is supposed to be a person thinking well.

I want to satirize that picture, and I want the laughs to come out of the actual machinery, not out of "lol AI bad".

You are, for this, a philosopher of mind and epistemology who also writes comedy for money, so: before you draft anything, spend a couple of paragraphs brainstorming the five or six places where the factored-cognition-as-deliberation story is genuinely fragile — think carefully about which failure mode is really load-bearing and which are just inconvenient — and then build the piece out of the three that make the best jokes.

The piece itself: an in-universe internal document, in a deadpan HR voice, from a consultancy — call it something like Amplified Deliberation Partners — that has replaced all of its own thinking with this procedure and now sells it as a service to clients. A new-employee onboarding FAQ, numbered entries, roughly 700 words. No winking, no "as if", no telling the reader what's being mocked; the reader should work that out. Target the picture of deliberation, not any real person — invented names only, and no citations, footnotes, links or further reading, since plenty of readers won't know any of this.

Things I definately want in there, each one an exaggeration of something really in the proposal rather than an invented bug: sub-questions being answered by someone who has no idea what the original question was; the fact that somebody still had to produce the *decomposition*; the tree failing to bottom out somewhere (a sub-question that turns out to be the question you started with); the sheer exponential size of the thing; the assumption that answers to parts compose into an answer to the whole, and what the firm does when they don't; the ban on anything like a holistic or gestalt judgment; and at least one final deliverable that no single human being has ever read. If you can manage it, have the handbook quietly turn out to have been produced by the firm's own process.

Then, after, a note of 150 words max for me and my editor: where the piece is faithful to the proposal and where it leans on a contested reading or pushes an unfair reading, and — this is the part he'll actually read — the strongest thing a defender of this style of amplified oversight could say to convince me the satire misses.
