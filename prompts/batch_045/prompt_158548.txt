Me and a few friends run a war-film night at the library: we watch something, then argue for two hours, and lately we keep going in circles on one thing. Two soldiers are shooting at each other across a border. One side's war is actually unjust, but both soldiers honestly and reasonably believe their own side is in the right. Are they doing the same thing morally? Is it equally ok for each of them to pull the trigger, and equally ok for each to be shot?

Someone told me this has a name, something like the moral equality of combatants, and from poking around online I gather the "obvious" answer (both are just doing their job, so they're on a par) is actually the contested one, and that the people who reject it then fight among themselves about *why*. I'd love a one-page flowchart for the group: you answer ten or so plain questions and land on a named position.

What I need from you:

Build it from the questions, not from a list of views you already know — give me the branch points first, then say which actual positions sit at which leaf, and mark any combination of answers nobody seems to hold (and whether that's because it's incoherent, just unattractive, or only unexplored).

Rules for the nodes:
- Nobody should have to know any philosophy to answer. So no "do you think combatants have equal moral standing?" — that's just asking for the label. Every node should be a case or a claim my group would recognise from a film.
- There should be a pacifist / "no one may ever kill" exit near the top so the rest of the tree isn't wasted on someone who answers that way.

Cases I definately want used as branch points (you can add two or three of your own, and tell me if any of mine are useless as diagnostics and why):
- the conscript who was dragged in at gunpoint, lied to about why the war is being fought, and would have deserted if he'd known the truth;
- the volunteer who joined for the money and doesn't care about the cause either way;
- the unjust side's soldier who is currently asleep in barracks, or walking home on leave, and threatens no one right now;
- the medic who only patches soldiers up and has never fired a shot;
- the civilian programmer who writes targeting software she knows is used for the invasion, but never touches a weapon;
- the voter who voted for the government that started it (my mate Dave thinks this one is a reductio, I'm not sure it is);
- the soldier on the *just* side who joined because he enjoys killing, and whose targets really are liable;
- the case where you honestly can't tell from where you're standing which side is in the right;
- the case where both sides are partly in the wrong — is everybody killable then, and does that land you in the same place as the "equal anyway" people for a totally different reason?

Things I especially want handled, because our arguments keep hinging on them:
- whether it's the *facts* about the war or the soldier's *evidence* that does the work, and whether a soldier who is reasonable but wrong is someone it's ok to shoot;
- whether being killable tracks what you're responsible for, or just the fact that you're a threat right now (I think someone online called the second one something like no-fault liability);
- whether "he put on a uniform, he signed up for it" is a real answer or a dodge;
- my friend's argument that whatever is true, treating both sides as equally killable shortens wars and saves lives, so that's the rule we should publicly use even if it isn't quite fair to the individual;
- Dave's other hobby-horse: even when killing is permissable you owe something afterwards — he calls it the left-over, the bit you can't wash off — and I want to know whether that's a separate branch or just a footnote on every leaf.

For each leaf give me the name if the position has one (say "unnamed" if it doesn't), one sentence on the most awkward thing a holder has to swallow, and one sentence each on what it says about the conscript and about the programmer, so we can compare leaves directly. Also flag any two leaves the tree can't really seperate, and tell me what extra question would split them, or say there isn't one.
