I work at a small philanthropic brokerage that arranges moral trades: we introduce two funders who disagree about what is worth funding, and each directs money to what the other believes matters, on the understanding that both can end up better off by their own lights even though neither has persuaded the other of anything. We vet around twenty proposed trades a year with three staff and roughly half a day per proposal, and anything above a modest size has to be signed off by our board. What we do not have is a written procedure. We argue case by case, and the arguments keep collapsing into two slogans: that both sides gain by their own lights, so there is nothing left to object to, and that we are helping people do indirectly what they would not do themselves, which my colleagues treat as conclusive and I do not. I would like something better than either slogan, and better than a list of considerations with no order to them.

Please write the decision procedure we could actually run. It should be a short ordered sequence of questions, with, for each one, what counts as a sufficient answer, what evidence we should demand and from whom, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a named person because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. It has to be usable by someone with no philosophy training, in a few hours, with counterparties who often cannot articulate a general value theory and can only tell us what they would fund. The conceptual work is in what the procedure preserves and what it drops, so it should handle at least these: the difference between a trade and mere cooperation on a project both parties already value, and between a trade and offsetting, where one party does something the other takes to be harmful and then compensates for it; cases where the disagreement is about a matter of fact rather than a matter of value, where what looks like a trade may really be a bet about which intervention is more effective; whether helping the counterparty do what we ourselves take to be wrong can ever be licensed by the fact that they gain by their own lights; whether we should discount the counterparty's gain by our own credence that their view is correct, and what, if anything, agreeing to broker says about that credence; parties whose values contain thresholds, satiation points, or protected and incommensurable goods, so that more money in their favoured cause eventually buys them nothing; trades where one party is a trustee acting for beneficiaries who cannot now consent, and trades whose performance is deferred for decades, where the party that must later perform is not the party that agreed; enforcement, meaning sequencing, escrow, reputation, defection after receipt, and what we owe each side when the other defaults; and our own conflict of interest, since our fee scales with the size of the trade we approve.

Then run it on three proposals. First, a global-health funder who pays for insecticide-treated nets and deworming, matched with a funder who pays for arts education in her own city and makes no apology for favouring it; each commits two hundred and fifty thousand dollars to the other's chosen charity. I expect this one to pass, and I want to see where the procedure still demands judgement, including why equal dollar amounts are the default ratio and when the ratio should differ. Second, an animal-welfare foundation gives a million dollars now to a fund oriented towards the long-term future, and the fund's trustees undertake to disburse an inflation-adjusted equivalent to animal-welfare work in thirty years, across at least two changes of trustee and a governing document that can be amended: I want the procedure's answer on whether current trustees can bind their successors, whether beneficiaries who do not yet exist can be represented in a trade, and what security we should require. Third, a trade that both parties gain from by their own lights and that would be the largest we have ever brokered, in which one side's favoured intervention rests on an empirical claim our staff believe to be false and the other side accepts the trade largely because it defers to that judgement: my co-founder says it is not our business to police anyone's factual beliefs, I think we are being asked to sell our credibility, and I want the procedure to tell us which of us is right, or to tell us what extra question would have to be added to settle it and what adopting that question would cost us. If the procedure also needs a different treatment for trades involving an individual donor rather than an institution, say so. Two or three pages, no citations or bibliography, with a short preamble a new analyst could read to understand what a moral trade is and why the both-gain test is not enough on its own, and an honest closing section on what the procedure cannot do and where it will misfire.
