I've been reading rationalist blogs and forum threads for roughly fifteen years, and one of the recurring arguments in those corners is about whether there are any genuinely general moral rules, or whether the verdict in a case is fixed only by the whole configuration of that case, with nothing general carrying over. I think I have noticed something in my own thinking that I cannot locate in that argument, and I do not have a word for it.

Two instances. First: I judge a case — a doctor who gives a patient a hopeful half-truth about a prognosis — and I settle on 'wrong, but mildly.' Later in the same conversation someone raises a much worse variant: a doctor who lies to enrol a patient in a paid trial. No new fact about the first case has been supplied, but my verdict on it moves. I now want to call it barely wrong, or fine. Second: when I read a post that walks through a numbered sequence of variants of the same scenario, my verdict on variant three depends on whether variant four turned out to be worse, and it shifts again if I re-read the sequence in a different order.

The pattern, as best I can state it: my verdict on a fixed case is a function of which other cases are under discussion in the same sitting, where those other cases are not options available to anyone, not counterfactuals about the agent, and not part of the case's own description. Some features of it that may matter: the effect looks asymmetric, in that a much worse neighbour pulls me toward leniency far more than a much better neighbour pulls me toward severity; it is local to the conversation and seems to reset between sittings; noticing it does not straightaway undo it; and it moves severity much more than it moves direction, since I rarely flip between 'permissible' and 'wrong' but travel a long way along 'how wrong.'

It is not, I think, the familiar point that one and the same feature can count in favour in one case and against in another, since that point requires the cases to differ, whereas here the case is held fixed and only its surroundings change. I also have a foggy recollection of a view on which reasons relate a consideration to a menu of alternatives rather than to a single act, but as I understand it the menu there is the agent's own options, and the cases doing the work in my examples are nobody's options.

What I want is the carving, not the label. Specifically: does this have an established name, and if the nearest candidates are things like the context-sensitivity of gradable terms, contrastive accounts of reasons, the holism claim about moral reasons, or ordinary anchoring and contrast effects from psychology, then which fits best and exactly where each one breaks against the four features I listed? What is shifting — my psychology, the semantics of the moral predicate, the truth of the matter, or the scale on which weights are even defined? Give me one or two edge cases or probes that would discriminate between those explanations rather than merely restate them. If nothing established fits, coin a term, define it, and give its boundary cases. And tell me whether the phenomenon, if real, cuts for or against the 'no general moral rules' side: it seems to me to cut both ways, since weights that need a comparison class are bad news for principles with invariant weights, but a verdict that moves for reasons not present in the case looks equally bad for anyone who wants the case itself to determine the verdict.

If the right answer is that this is a known bias and I should debias and move on, argue for that rather than asserting it, because the persistence under reflection is part of what puzzles me. Please keep the whole reply under 550 words, and if it helps, use a small table with one row per candidate explanation, a column for what it predicts about the four features, and a column for where it fails.
