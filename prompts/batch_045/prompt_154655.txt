Last night I had a long argument with a friend who works on AI policy, and I have not been able to shake it. The topic was whose values a widely deployed AI assistant should serve. My position was that the question is unavoidably substantive: someone has to decide what the system optimises for, and any such decision encodes a contested view of what is worth wanting. Hers was that the question has a procedural solution, and she leaned on one analogy throughout: an aligned AI should be like a liberal state.

She drew the mapping out on a napkin, roughly like this. The state must be neutral among the comprehensive doctrines of its citizens, so the AI must be neutral among the values of its users. Citizens become the set of people affected by the system. Constitutional essentials become the small set of hard constraints written into the system that no user may override. Elections become the aggregation mechanism, whether an explicit social-choice rule or the distribution of feedback used to tune the system. Public reason becomes the style of justification the system gives for what it does. Constitutional review becomes the oversight body. And the payoff she drew was fourfold: do not hardcode contested substantive values; aggregate instead, taking people's values as you find them; treat procedural fairness as the whole of legitimacy, so that whoever ends up on the losing side has no complaint; and conclude that the question of whose values dissolves into a problem of mechanism design rather than a problem of moral judgement.

I pushed back with three wrinkles, and she answered each by saying the analogy was only ever a regulative ideal for the procedure, not a description of the target, so descriptive disanalogies do not bite. First: a liberal state governs a people whose values it did not author, whereas a general-purpose assistant that becomes everyone's default interlocutor helps produce the values it will later poll. Suppose two firms ship assistants with slightly different defaults about, say, how much weight to give personal achievement versus care for dependents, and that after some years the two user populations genuinely disagree. Aggregating either population's revealed values then ratifies an earlier design choice rather than registering something independent of it, and the analogous worry for states is muted only because a state inherits a background culture it mostly did not create and which it can be contested against. Second: the analogy presupposes a demos. A state has membership rules, borders, exit, elections, and a sovereign able to enforce a settlement; the constituency of an assistant has no membership rule, no exit if the system is infrastructure, no vote, and unclear inclusion of future users and of people affected without using it. Third: the supposedly neutral part is where the substance hides. Choosing how to weigh intensity, whether to count stated or revealed or idealised preferences, how to handle values that are inchoate or mutually incomparable, and who counts as affected is the first-order moral dispute, not a way of stepping outside it, and there is no aggregation rule that satisfies even a short list of minimal-looking fairness conditions.

So I would like an audit rather than a verdict on the underlying politics. Please give me the correspondence table as it should properly be drawn, including the cells my napkin version leaves empty. Then separate the disanalogies that carry weight for the four conclusions she wanted from the ones that are merely cosmetic, and say which features of a liberal state the analogy imports into the target without argument: a demos, a settled plurality of doctrines to be neutral between, preferences stable across the aggregation window, exit and voice, a sovereign that can settle disputes, a background culture it did not author. Tell me whether each of her four conclusions survives once the load-bearing disanalogies are on the table, and in particular whether an analogy offered purely as a regulative ideal can be exempted from descriptive fit while still doing argumentative work. Finally, if the state is the wrong source, what is the better one: something like a public broadcaster, a curriculum committee for a school nobody can leave, or a constitutional convention writing for a people not yet born and whose identity the constitution will partly determine, or something else entirely, and what would each of those make salient that the state makes invisible?
