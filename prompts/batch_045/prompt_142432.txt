My mum has been in intensive care for about a week and I've noticed the hospital cant make up its mind who the patient actually is. When she was still awake they wouldn't tell my brother and me anything without her say-so — she was the only person in the room who counted. Now that she's sedated they ask us what she would have wanted, even though she signed a living will years ago, they've asked us twice whether she should even be told how bad things are, and the transplant people came to talk to us about donation and I got the feeling that if we'd said no theyd have dropped it, donor card or no donor card. At the same time the nurses still talk to her and about her as a full person while she's completely out of it.

Over the last few months I've been reading a bit about ubuntu — my dad's family is South African and I got into a stupid argument about it at a funeral — and this idea that you become a person through other people, that personhood is something you're brought into and can partly lose or be denied. So: what does the way hospitals handle families actually presuppose about what a person is? Can a hard-nosed individualist explain all of it — family as mind-reader, as affected party, as lawsuit risk — or does it only really make sense on a relational view? What would a ward run on the relational view do differently from one run on the patient is an autonomous individual, full stop? And where's the risk: my mate says these views basically license the family to steamroll the person in the bed, and I cant tell if that's a real objection or a scare story.
