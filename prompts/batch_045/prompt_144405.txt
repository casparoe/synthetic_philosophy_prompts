Been lurking since the Overcoming Bias days and reading the blogs on and off ever since, so I already know the standard shtick: shut up and multiply, EU is what you get from the axioms, Allais is a cute puzzle, and Pascal's mugging is where it all gets embarassing. What I can't get a grip on is the family of views where you're allowed to fiddle with the probabilities rather than with the utility function — the risk-weighted / rank-dependent stuff, where you sort outcomes worst-to-best, form cumulative probabilities, push them through a monotone weight curve and then difference, so a curve sitting above the diagonal loads extra weight onto the bad tail. Apparently there's a literature arguing this is rationally permissible, not merely a description of how people happen to be risk-averse, and that giving up independence / the sure-thing principle is a feature rather than a bug. I'd like to actually understand that argument, but every writeup I find is in philosophy-ese and I bounce off it within a paragraph.

So: translate it into the vocabulary I actually use, and then tell me what didn't make it across.

Context. I'm an RL engineer, seven-odd years, mostly distributional methods (QR-DQN, IQN, that whole quantile family) applied to inventory and logistics control, where tail outcomes genuinely matter — one blown stockout is much worse than a slightly worse average. And here's the hook: for about a year we've been training on an in-house objective that I now suspect is this thing in disguise. At each update we take the batch of finished episodes, sort them by realised return, apply a fixed monotone spectrum to the ranks — ours sits above the diagonal, so it upweights the bottom decile — and use the weighted mean as the training signal. We arrived at it purely pragmatically: it stopped the policy cheerfully trading a 5% chance of catastrophe for a better mean. We never thought of it as normative. Two hacks were needed to make it train at all. (1) It isn't decomposable, so you can't write it as a per-step Bellman backup; we got round that by conditioning policy and critic on an extra scalar, a running budget-to-go / quantile-threshold variable, which makes it roughly Bellman-shaped again. (2) Because the weighting is non-linear in the probabilities, our off-policy importance-sampling corrections stopped being unbiased, and we just live with it.

Please translate, as faithfully as you can:

- the claim that a rational agent may evaluate prospects by rank-weighting distorted cumulative probabilities instead of by probabilities, and that this is a matter of rational permissibility — not prudence, not taste;
- the case against it: the independence / sure-thing style arguments, and the strongest version of the "this isn't rationality, it's risk aversion smuggled into the wrong slot" objection;
- whatever gets said about an agent facing a sequence of such decisions rather than one.

...into: return distributions and quantile functions, spectral risk measures and CVaR, training objectives versus evaluation metrics, policy gradients and their bias, importance sampling and off-policy correction, Bellman backups and state augmentation for objectives that won't decompose, and the difference between a criterion for a single decision and a signal you backprop through for a few million steps.

Then the residue report, which is the bit I actually care about:

My guess is that "rationally permissible for a single agent" is the big one: in my world a risk spectrum is either imposed from outside — a mandate, a regulator, a client's utility curve — or adopted as a design decision, and there's no notion of the agent itself being rationally entitled to one. Also possibly this: I have to choose whether the distortion is applied per-step within an episode, per-episode within a batch, or over the whole run, and those give me different objectives with different gradients. Does the philosophical version say anything about what fills the "outcome" and "probability" slots, or is that left as a free parameter?
