I run a two-hour onboarding session for new programme officers at a mid-sized grantmaking foundation — usually about 12 of them, four tables of three, mixed backgrounds, most have done a bit of quantified cause-prioritisation reading but none of them are economists. By the time they get to me they can all recite importance × tractability × neglectedness in thier sleep, and I've watched two different cohorts come out of the standard version of this session able to multiply three numbers and still having no instinct for where the product is lying to them. So I don't need another explanation of the framework. I need a game that makes them feel it breaking, played before anyone in the room says the words "marginal", "funging", "absorption capacity", "crowding in", "field building" or "option value" — that vocabulary should be what the debrief hands them for something they already did, not a lecture they sit through first.

Some half-formed mechanics I've been turning over and can't get to work together, so steal or discard freely: (1) a shared "almanac" on the whiteboard showing where the other tables put their money, but updated only after a one-round lag, so anyone who chases neglectedness is reacting to stale information and the neglected cause has stopped being neglected by the time they arrive; (2) at least one cause with a genuinely lumpy funding gap — it needs a big chunk of money inside a window or the whole spend is wasted — so the smooth multiplication of three factors visibly gives the wrong answer and the real problem becomes getting four tables to coordinate on a threshold none of them can see; (3) an expensive-now, diffuse-later cause whose payoff is to permanently raise the returns on the other causes, which I expect everyone to under-invest in for predictable reasons; (4) something that forces the timing question into the open without me announcing it — my pet idea is a scoring rule that silently only counts impact realised by the end of the session, so the one cause that pays off far in the future scores a fat zero, and it only emerges afterwards that the rulebook imposed a time horizon and a discount rate on them and nobody thought to ask; (5) an "auditor's note" handed out partway through revealing that one cause's headline impact figures include work that would have been funded anyway. Each table gets the same budget across maybe six rounds, they're scored table-by-table during play, and the total across all four tables only gets revealed at the end.

What I want back from you: the rules, invented cause cards with the numbers worked out (please invent the causes — I dont want anyone arguing about actual bed nets or actual AI timelines, and I don't want the figures smuggling in real empirical claims), the round-by-round timeline in minutes, exactly what goes on the whiteboard and when, and a scoring rule simple enough that I can compute a round on the back of an envelope with twelve people waiting.
