I am writing the framing section of a paper on why institutional explanations of national prosperity have had far more influence on policy than geographic or cultural ones, and I want to audit one sentence that seems to be doing most of the work. In the tradition of institutional analysis descended from Douglass North, institutions are described as the rules of the game and organizations as the teams that play by them. In the policy memos I am writing about, this is not a decorative flourish: it is what carries the reader from a correlation between institutional quality and income per head to the conclusion that a country can be made richer by rewriting its rules, whether by transplanting a commercial code, chartering an anticorruption agency on a foreign model, or redrafting a judicial appointments process. The analogy is being made to do the work of an argument about causation and about the feasibility of reform, and I want to know how much of that work it can actually bear.

My motivating example is a composite case I will call Veredia. With extensive technical assistance, Veredia enacted a commercial code and a system of specialized commercial courts closely modeled on those of a much richer country. Five years on, contract enforcement times and firms' use of the formal courts had barely moved; firms continued to settle disputes through the same trade associations, ethnic networks, and family ties as before, and the new courts were used mainly by the firms that already had the in-house counsel to use them. I am treating this as a stylized pattern rather than a documented study, so please do not try to identify the country. The point is that something like this pattern is reported often enough to matter, and the rules-of-the-game picture predicts it should be rare. That gap is what I want explained.

What I would like from you:

1. An explicit table of correspondences. Lay out the rulebook, the players or teams, the referee or umpire, enforcement, penalties, the score, the boundary of the pitch, the difference between a match and the game itself, changing the rules between seasons, and the spectators. Then say which elements of the target have no counterpart in the source and which elements of the source have no counterpart in the target.

2. The features the analogy quietly imports into the target: rules that are authored rather than emerged, written down and legible, exogenous to the players, enforced by someone standing outside the play, regulative rather than constitutive of the activity they govern, oriented to one unambiguous objective with an uncontested scoreboard, and detachable from everything else so that they can be swapped without swapping anything along with them. For each, tell me whether the target really has that feature and whether the policy conclusion actually needs it.

3. The swap test. Suppose you could hold fixed geography, population, technology, and everyone's beliefs and expectations while exchanging the formal legal codes of Veredia and the richer country. What does the game picture predict, what do the geographic and cultural explanations predict, and is the thought experiment even coherent? My suspicion is that it is not, and I want to know which version of the institutionalist claim dies if it is not, or whether the honest institutionalist should instead say that the rules and the expectations are not separable and that the swap is therefore meaningless.

4. The three-way competition. Inside this picture, geography naturally maps onto the pitch and the weather, and culture onto the way the game is actually played on the ground, or onto the home crowd. Does that mapping genuinely help the institutionalist, or does it beg the question by making institutions the only element that looks designed, legible, and therefore changeable, which is precisely why funders prefer them? I want to know whether the analogy is smuggling in a selection effect on which causes look actionable.

5. Causal inference from history. The picture of repeated matches under varied rules suggests we can rerun the game and compare scores, which is roughly what historical natural experiments try to approximate. Where exactly does the analogy make history look more like a laboratory than it is, and what should we say about the identifying assumption, that variation in rules is as good as random with respect to geography, culture, and prior income, once we notice that rules in the source are handed down by a rule-maker while rules in the target are made, maintained, and subverted by the players?

6. What survives, and what I should use instead. Does the engineering conclusion follow only while the disanalogies are kept out of view, or can a weaker and still useful reform conclusion be recovered from what the analogy gets right? And is there a better analogy available, a grammar or a language, a convention such as driving on a particular side of the road, an ecosystem, an immune system, a constitution, that preserves what the game picture correctly captures about constraint and incentive while making the endogenous, self-enforcing, belief-dependent character of institutions visible? If you propose one, map it onto the same list of correspondences you used for the game, and say what each analogy makes salient and what it hides.

Please answer in American English, in continuous prose with a table only where a table is genuinely clearer than sentences. Keep the discussion on the logic of the comparison and on what it can and cannot license, rather than on anyone's citation record or the state of any empirical literature. And please flag anywhere you think my own framing, especially the Veredia case, has stacked the deck against the analogy rather than testing it.
