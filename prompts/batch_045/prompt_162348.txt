hey — act as a professional philosopher here, somebody who works on reasons and fittingness, and please think it through properly before you commit to anything: sketch out to yourself what each of them is actually committed to, then give me the verdict.

Two friends got into it at game night on saturday and they asked me to referee and i honestly couldnt tell who was winning, so im passing it to you. They started from the standard setup: a demon offers you a million dollars to believe it exists, and that payoff is supposed to be the wrong kind of reason to believe — evidence is the right kind, the money is only a reason to end up *having* the belief, not a reason the belief is a response to. Then Dev made up his own version, which he called the conditional patron: a collector offers a painter 50k on the condition that by the end of the year she genuinely admires his collection *for the merits of the works*, with an independent panel checking, and no money at all if she fakes it or just talks herself into it. She takes the deal, spends the year in the gallery learning how to look at that kind of thing, and ends up really admiring the work. Here's roughly how it went:

1. Marta: the cash is still the wrong kind of reason. Her admiration is perfectly in order, but what makes it fitting are the merits of the paintings, and the merits are what her admiration answers to. The money is a reason to get herself into a position where she can see the merits. The condition on the offer doesn't change anything — it just forces the causal route from the money to the admiration to pass through the merits, and a route isn't a reason.

2. Dev: if that's all the distinction is, it stops doing the work people want it to do. The point of calling a reason wrong-kind was that payoffs are external to the attitude — they don't speak to whether the object deserves it. But here the money does speak to that, in two ways. First, it's a reason to bring it about that she is merit-responsive, and her actual basis for admiring is the merits. Second, and more interesting: the collector staking 50k on what a panel will find is itself evidence that the works really do reward sustained attention — so the money is indirect evidence *about the object*, not just a payoff for a state, which makes it object-given after all.

3. Marta: the evidence move backfires on you, because run the same structure for belief. A demon pays you 50k conditional on your believing p *for good evidence*, panel-verified. You go investigate, you find the evidence, you believe for it. Now the money is equally "evidence that there is evidence," and you still want to say the money isn't your reason for believing p — the evidence is, and the money was a reason to go get it. So the conditional structure doesn't collapse the distinction at all, it only shows a wrong-kind reason can be a reason to acquire right-kind ones. And if you want to insist admiration and belief come apart here, fine — here's my diagnosis: admiration, unlike belief, is partly constituted by attention, and attention can be guided, so money can be a reason to attend and attending is internally related to admiring; inquiry has no such internal relation to believing. The asymmetry is about the structure of the attitude, not about the kind of reason.

4. Dev: that asymmetry is doing no work unless you can say what it explains, and I think you can't, because of the reverse case. The collector instead threatens to burn the paintings unless she stops admiring them. On your view that's a wrong-kind reason against admiring — but everyone happily says a threat gives you a reason to *fear* the threatener, since danger is what fear is fittingness-conditioned on. So "wrong kind" turns out to be nothing more than a restatement of what each attitude is a response to, attitude by attitude. And once you say it that way, the money in my case is wrong-kind only if a payoff can't figure in admiration's correctness condition — which is exactly what I denied in move 2.

5. Marta: I'll concede the fear point, but it supports me. For each attitude the right kind of reason is fixed by what that attitude purports to be a response to — danger for fear, evidence for belief, merit for admiration. What my case actually reveals is a category people haven't described properly: the money is a reason to *become the sort of person who has the attitude for the right reasons*. It's a reason for the attitude in the "because of this" sense and not in the "answers to" sense, and we only have one word for that, which is why the whole thing feels like a paradox. So the taxonomy is too coarse — "reasons for the attitude" and "reasons to have the attitude" need a third slot — and that's a discovery, not a counterexample.
