You are a decision theorist who has published on the value of information. Think carefully and, before settling on an answer, lay out the candidate mechanisms you can think of and test each one against the cases below.

In health economics we repeat a platitude: more information can never leave a chooser worse off, because someone who has it can always do whatever she would have done without it. Being informed adds options; it never removes them. This is offered as the reason a diagnostic test is at worst worth nothing.

I keep meeting situations where it looks false, and I cannot tell whether I have found one phenomenon or several.

One: a woman declines a predictive genetic test. She is not confused about the probabilities. She expects that if she learns she carries the variant she will be unable to stop herself from choosing a prophylaxis she currently regards as worse than waiting. Paying not to be told is how she secures the choice she wants.

Two: a patient would rather hear nothing until the result is conclusive, because the interval of not knowing is itself miserable and the test lengthens it. Here the message does not merely guide action; receiving it is part of what happens to him.

Three: a firm decides whether to commission a market study, where the commissioning itself is visible to a competitor, who infers the firm's plans from it and moves first.

A colleague says the positive half has a classical name — he thought something like Good's theorem, perhaps from a short paper in the 1960s — but he could not state its assumptions, and I suspect it is really a bundle: something about ranking prospects by averaging outcomes against their probabilities, something about being free to disregard the message, something about no one else reacting.

So I am not fishing for a label. Tell me whether there is an established concept, or a settled taxonomy, for the ways information becomes harmful. For each of my three cases, identify which assumption does the work and say whether the case is a genuine counterexample to the platitude or merely a case where a hypothesis quietly fails. One and three resemble each other in that someone later reacts — the chooser's own future self in one, a rival in the other — and differ in another respect; tell me which resemblance is the theoretically important one. Then take up a fourth suspicion I cannot turn into a case: that for a chooser who does not rank prospects by probability-weighted averaging at all, but by the worst case over a range of probability assignments, resolving a question can destroy a hedge and so hurt even when the message is free and could in principle be ignored. If that is real, give me a minimal example; if it is impossible, say exactly what rules it out.

There is also a behavioural-finance label for investors who avoid opening their statements in a falling market. My sense is that it concerns where attention goes rather than the structure of the choice, but say whether the two collapse into one. If nothing established covers what I am after, coin a term, define it, and give me the boundary cases that settle membership — including what should not count.
