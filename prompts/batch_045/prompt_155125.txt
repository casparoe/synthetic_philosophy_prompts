I'm teaching a seminar on the symbol grounding problem and I want to open the last session with a poem instead of a recap. So: write me a sestina.

The case I've been using all semester I made up myself, and I like it better then the usual sealed-room rule-following stuff: a fictitious language, call it Vantu, attested in exactly one manuscript — a word list where every Vantu entry is glossed using other Vantu words. No translation into any known language, no drawings, no field notes, no surviving speakers. A philologist could still reconstruct a fair amount: near-synonyms, antonyms, the hypernym tree, a few inference patterns of the if-X-then-probably-Y sort. But not one Vantu word is ever tied to anything outside the list. Seminar question: is that meaning of a thin, purely inferential kind, or is it not meaning at all yet?

Sestina because it's the same trap: six end words permuted over and over, never escaping their own set. Pick six concrete sensory words — salt, knee, ash, that kind of thing — and let them mean only by their places in the poem. Hard rule: no image, simile, or small narrative move that smuggles the missing ground in from outside. If the reader finds out what ash means by picturing a hearth, you've cheated, and you've also settled the question by sleight of hand.

Commit to the thin side: by the three-line tornada I want the reader to feel that the six words have earned something like meaning just from keeping each other company for thirty-six lines, and I want the real retrogradatio order (6-1-5-2-4-3 and so on — I'll check).

Then a paragraph, committed, no hedging: is that feeling evidence in the debate or just a rhetorical trick, where does the poem lean on a contested reading, and is my Vantu case doing anything the standard grounding setup isn't, or is it the same problem in a costume?
