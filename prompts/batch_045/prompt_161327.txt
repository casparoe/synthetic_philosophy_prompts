I'm a graduate student in philosophy, mostly working in philosophy of mind, and I'm sitting in on a philosophy of physics seminar this term. Last week's session was on the quantum suicide apparatus and the immortality argument that usually gets attached to it, and I came away unsure whether the argument is really about quantum mechanics at all, or whether the quantum setting is just a vivid way of staging a puzzle about branching and self-locating credence that could be staged just as well with nothing more exotic than a copying machine. So what I want from you is a comparison rather than a verdict on the physics.

Here is the pair I want compared. Version A is the Everettian one: an idealised device performs one measurement per round; on one outcome a trigger kills the subject instantaneously and painlessly, on the other it does nothing and the experiment proceeds. Assume perfect branching, no collapse, no decoherence complications, and no escapism about whether a minimal brain state still supports experience. After n rounds there are 2^n continuations, exactly one of which has survived every round, and that continuation carries Born weight 2^-n while the total measure of surviving branches is 2^-n and the number of surviving branches stays at one. The argument then says, roughly, that dead branches contain no experiences at all, so every experience you will ever have belongs to a survivor, and so from the inside you should anticipate surviving indefinitely. Version B is what I've started calling the duplication machine, which I take to be the classical shadow of the same argument: at each round the experimenter makes a perfect physical duplicate of the subject, and then an ordinary fair coin decides which of the two copies is painlessly destroyed and which one goes on to the next round. There is no branching, no amplitude, no Born rule; the surviving population stays at one while the number of people who have ever existed grows without bound, and the survivor's history has classical chance 2^-n. Please say what, if anything, is specifically quantum in version A. I'd like the structural features pulled apart: branching versus duplication, conservation of total measure versus growth in the number of people, Born weights versus copy-counts or equal-weights-among-continuations, and the move of conditioning on the existence of an experience. Which of these is doing the work, and is A simply an instance of the general argument B exhibits, or does A have resources — or liabilities — that B lacks? Please be explicit about which rule for self-locating credence you are applying at each step instead of sliding between them, since that seems to be where the disagreement actually lives.

Two probes, because I suspect they separate the readings better than the standard setup does. First, replace the fair quantum gun with an unequal one: at round n the death branch has weight 1/n, so the per-round risk shrinks toward zero while the total measure of surviving branches is 1/n and therefore still goes to zero; then compare that with a version where the death branch has weight 1/n^2, so the total measure of surviving branches converges to a positive value (one half, if my arithmetic is right) and never gets thin in aggregate even though infinitely many rounds have some death branch of positive measure. What does each of A and B predict in each case, and does the disagreement between the continuation-only reading and the measure-weighted reading persist once per-round risk is negligible, or once total surviving measure stops vanishing? Second, a diagnostic rather than a variant: replace death with a permanent dreamless coma, so that the bad outcome involves no death but also no subsequent experiences. If the argument survives that substitution, I take it to show that the argument was never about mortality but only about which branches contain experiences, and I'd like you to say how much that undermines it. Finish by telling me which of the two versions is in better shape and why, stating the criteria you are using, and by telling me whether either version gives anyone a practical reason to run such an experiment — or whether both only ever deliver a claim about the structure of anticipation. I'm less interested in who has defended what than in where the argument's force actually comes from, so please argue it out rather than reporting a consensus.
