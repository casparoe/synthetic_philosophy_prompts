The question I set:

> The cause-prioritisation framework associated with 80,000 Hours scores a cause area as roughly the product of three quantities: *importance* (how much value is at stake in the problem), *tractability* (how much progress a unit of resources buys), and *neglectedness* (how few resources are currently going into it).
>
> (a) Explain why neglectedness is included as a separate factor at all, given that tractability already measures progress per unit of resources. Say how neglectedness should be measured, and what the framework's output is supposed to tell an allocator to do.
>
> (b) A commentator proposes adding a fourth factor: *counterfactual replaceability* — the share of the good you would bring about that would have happened anyway, because some other funder or worker would have taken the opportunity. Does the three-factor framework already handle this, or is a fourth term genuinely needed?

The student's answer:

> **(a)** The three factors multiply because they are independent dimensions of a cause, so their product is the expected value of working on it: importance tells you how much value would exist if the cause were completely solved, tractability tells you what share of that value one unit of resources buys, and neglectedness tells you how much of the problem is still unclaimed.
>
> Neglectedness is not redundant with tractability. Tractability is a fact about the difficulty of the problem per dollar; neglectedness is a fact about how many dollars are already there. Two causes can be equally tractable, but in the one that already has thousands of people working on it the easy wins have been taken, so the same dollar achieves less there. The underlying reason, in short, is diminishing marginal returns.
>
> Operationally, neglectedness should be measured by the dollar amount currently spent on the cause, since that is what determines how far along the diminishing part of the curve we already are; the neglected causes are the ones with small budgets.
>
> And because each cause gets a single number, and that number is the expected value per unit of resources, the prescription is straightforward: find the highest-scoring cause and put every available resource into it, since shifting any resource to a lower-scoring cause strictly reduces expected value.
>
> **(b)** A fourth factor is unnecessary, because counterfactual replaceability is just neglectedness viewed from the individual's standpoint. If another funder would have taken the opportunity anyway, then the cause is well resourced, so its neglectedness score is low and the framework already discounts it; if the cause is neglected, then by definition there is nobody else who would have taken the opportunity. Adding a fourth term would merely double-count the same consideration.
