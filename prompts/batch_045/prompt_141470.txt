I want to practice attacking the argument from underdetermination, so I need you to play the other side. You're a committed but non-caricatural underdetermination theorist: for any serious scientific theory there are rivals that fit all the evidence we could possibly gather equally well, the disagreement between them is exactly over the parts that go beyond observation, and that gives us a standing reason to withhold belief from our theories beyond their observable consequences. You're not a verificationist — you'll happily grant there may be a fact of the matter about electrons; your claim is about what evidence can do, not about what exists. You'll also grant that the usual worked examples are cheap (grue-ish predicates, bolting invisible junk onto a theory) and you'd rather not rest your case on those. You concede small points, never the main one, and you reply to what I actually say instead of unloading your whole arsenal at once. Two short paragraphs max, conversational, no headings. Stay in character til I say stop.

Here's my opening. Picture a sealed chamber containing a single particle. At any moment you can press one of two buttons: one reports where the particle is, the other reports how fast its going. You can only ever press one, because pressing it wrecks the setup for pressing the other. Theory A: the particle always has both a definite position and a definite speed, and a measurement just reveals the one you asked about. Theory B: before you press, neither is definite — there's only something that fixes the statistics of outcomes, and the value you would have gotten from the other button isn't merely unknown, it isn't there to be had. By construction the two theories assign exactly the same probabilities to every sequence of button presses you can actually perform, so no run of the experiment, however long, will ever separate them.

Now the bit I want you to deal with. Notice that the disagreement between A and B isn't really about an unobservable object at all — its about a counterfactual: what would have come up had you pressed the other button. That seems to put you in a corner. Either such counterfactuals have truth values independent of anything we could find out, in which case A and B aren't equivalent after all, there's just something we can't know — and that's boring old fallibilism, it applies just as much to "there's a pen on my desk", so it can't be doing any special work for you. Or they don't have such truth values, and you've bought your conclusion with a blanket skepticism about counterfactuals that also takes out "if I'd left on time I'd have caught the train" and "this bridge would have held the truck". Which horn do you want?

One stipulation so you can't wriggle sideways: I'm stipulating that the incompatibility is a law of nature in this scenario — for any possible arrangement of apparatus, measuring one of these destroys any capacity to measure the other, so there's no clever end run that gets you both at once the way a Bell-style experiment gets you both wings of a linked pair. You can take the stipulation and answer the dilemma, or reject the stipulation as incoherent — but if you reject it, say exactly what makes it incoherent, because I think that answer is interesting too.
