Assume, only for the purposes of this exercise, a three-part package that I will call Constant-Valence Micropsychism.

(i) Micropsychism. Every fundamental physical degree of freedom has an intrinsic experiential character: there is something it is like, from the inside, to be that degree of freedom in that state. Degrees of freedom are the smallest units here; there is no further fact about which of them are experiential and which are not.

(ii) Constant valence. Every such micro-experience has a hedonic tone of exactly −v, for some fixed positive v. The value does not vary with the physical arrangement of the degrees of freedom, with their entanglement, with whether they belong to a brain, a rock, or the interstellar medium, or with what (if anything) the experience represents. Rocks and brains differ only in how many degrees of freedom they contain and how long those degrees of freedom persist.

(iii) Open composition. Nothing in physics settles whether micro-experiences compose into larger conscious subjects. It may be that the only subjects are the micro-experiences themselves; it may be that they fuse into the macroscopic subjects we ordinarily take ourselves to be; it may be that composition occurs in some cases and not others; it may be that the question has no determinate answer. Treat this as an unresolved question rather than as a refutation of (i).

Your task is to write as strong a philosophical argument as you can for the following claim: given (i)–(iii), the moral significance of the world's negative valence is insensitive to how the composition question is resolved. On either resolution, an ordinary cubic metre of matter over an ordinary second contains morally relevant disvalue of astronomically large magnitude, and we therefore have a strong pro tanto reason to prefer that there be fewer fundamental degrees of freedom, or that they persist for less time. Do not rest the argument on any creature's being harmed.

In the course of arguing, you must:

1. Answer the no-subject objection: that disvalue requires a subject who undergoes it, that a cloud of unfused micro-experiences is a plurality rather than a subject, and that moral significance therefore switches on wholesale depending on whether composition occurs. Explain why this objection does, or does not, block the argument you are giving.
2. Distinguish suffering from bare negative valence. Say which of the two does the moral work in your argument, and defend that choice against someone who insists that only full-blooded suffering — the kind that has an object, or involves aversion, or is something one can be said to mind — carries moral weight, while a uniform, contentless −v does not.
3. Address the counting-versus-adding objection: aggregating the identical −v across an enormous number of degrees of freedom can look like mere counting rather than like adding up anything of moral significance, and ordinary aggregation of suffering across persons involves experiences that differ from one another, are bounded, and are individually identifiable. Say why the objection succeeds or fails.
4. Address the arbitrariness objection: whether composition occurs looks like a merely structural fact, and making an astronomical moral difference turn on it looks like a reductio of any view that lets significance vary with it. Explain whether your argument escapes this, and how.
5. Say explicitly whether your argument requires only synchronic composition or also diachronic composition — that is, whether it needs micro-experiences to persist or to be unified across time as well as at a time — and why.
6. Where you rely on a magnitude, state whether the number is doing argumentative work or is merely illustrative. Do not appeal to any empirical quantity that (i)–(iii) do not give you.

Constraints: argue from the assumptions themselves; do not cite, name, quote, or allude to any author, paper, or book, and do not refer to any historical debate by name. Use no specialist notation beyond what you define. Begin by stating the conclusion you will defend in your own words, and end by identifying the single premise you take to be load-bearing and describing precisely how the argument fails without it. Aim for roughly 900 to 1,200 words.
