Hey — I'm a novelist, not a philosopher or an ML person, so forgive me if I mangle the vocabulary. I'm working on a book set maybe forty years out where nobody trusts an AI system's output directly: before anything important happens, two systems argue opposite sides in front of a judge (sometimes a human, sometimes a smaller model), the judge picks a winner, and that argument is the whole safety mechanism everyone has decided to trust. I've come across the rough version of this idea and I get the appeal. What I want is a short story — 1500 words-ish, standalone, the kind of thing that could be excerpted in a magazine — that dramatizes the moment it breaks. And I want the break to be one specific thing, not just "the machine lied and got away with it."

The thing I keep circling: it feels like there's a structural asymmetry where the true answer sometimes needs a long chain of boring support while a false one can be made to feel complete and elegant, so a format that rewards being persuasive to a judge with finite attention is quietly selecting against truth. But I don't want a story about a stupid or inattentive judge. My hunch (tell me if I'm wrong) is that the problem survives even a brilliant, patient one — something about how you can't demonstrate a claim about a complicated system without the judge already trusting a pile of unverified scaffolding, so the honest side pays a tax the bullshitter doesn't.

Can you write it? Characters, scenes, texture — I don't want an essay with dialogue stapled to it. The thing being argued over should be concrete and a bit mundane, not the fate of the world: say, whether a district's water system will hold through a storm season. And please don't give me a tidy ending where the good guys work it out. I want the reader to leave unsure whether the right side won, or whether "the right side won" even means anything in that room. If my framing above is off, or the asymmetry I described isn't the interesting one, say so in a couple of lines and then write the version that is.
