I've been reading the rationalist blogosphere since the original sequences, and a comment-thread argument about how much weight to give scientific consensus in fields where I can't evaluate the primary evidence myself pushed me toward the epistemology of testimony. Someone recommended a chapter in a recent general-audience book on social epistemology, and I came away unsure how much of its summary of the reductionism/anti-reductionism debate to trust. The passage that stuck with me runs roughly like this: "Reductionists hold that you should never take another person's word for anything you haven't independently checked: testimony carries no evidential weight of its own, and whenever you come to believe something on the basis of what someone told you, the real justificatory work is being done by your own perception, memory and inference. Anti-reductionists, following Reid, hold the opposite — testimony is a basic source of justification on a par with perception and memory, so a hearer needs no reasons at all and is entitled to believe whatever anyone tells her. The standard objection to reductionism is decisive: since almost everything we know about whether a given speaker is trustworthy reaches us through further testimony, reductionism would make testimonial knowledge impossible and would leave children unable to learn from their parents, so reductionism is refuted, except perhaps in a weakened 'global' version that only asks for general evidence that people are usually reliable. Fortunately the dispute turns out to be largely verbal: both sides now agree that we should accept testimony by default and withdraw belief once defeaters appear, so nothing of practical importance depends on which label you prefer." My own sympathies are reductionist, partly because I tend to model evidence as likelihood ratios and it seems obvious that the force of a report depends on your model of the reporter — but then I noticed that nearly everything in my model of the reporter is itself testimony-derived, which is exactly the objection the chapter calls decisive, and I don't know whether to be embarrassed by that.

What I'd really like is an accuracy audit I can act on. Please go through the passage sentence by sentence and sort it into: what a careful reductionist would accept, what a careful anti-reductionist would accept, what is a defensible simplification for a general reader, and what is actually a misrepresentation — and where a misrepresentation is a conflation, say which two claims are being run together. Three things I'm especially suspicious of: the idea that reductionism is a norm of inquiry about independently checking content rather than a thesis about the structure of justification; the claim that anti-reductionism treats testimony as a basic, generative source, since I gather that at least some anti-reductionists in the Burgean content-preservation tradition explicitly deny that and think of testimony as transmitting rather than generating warrant; and the description of the circularity/regress objection as decisive, plus the implication that the 'global' variant is the weakened one, which I suspect may be backwards relative to the global/local distinction.

There is also a reason this matters beyond the summary. I ran into a short unpublished paper — I have the PDF but no author, I think it was linked from a forum thread — that tries to settle the debate with a case type it calls a *closed chain*: a hearer H believes p only on the basis of an assertion by Sₙ, each Sᵢ's assertion is itself based solely on the assertion of Sᵢ₋₁, and no non-testimonial evidence about any link's reliability enters anywhere. The author argues that reductionism, applied consistently, entails that H is never justified however short or well-functioning the chain is; that anti-reductionism in content-preservation form entails that H is justified just in case the originating assertion was, however long the chain is; that both verdicts are wrong, since our practice treats a long game of telephone or a citation-of-a-citation as transmitting little while treating a two-link chain as transmitting plenty; and therefore that the truth is a third view, which he calls the decay view — default entitlement attenuates with distance from the originating justification. So, having corrected the chapter: what do the two positions actually predict about the closed chain, and are the paper's first two entailments real? In particular, does content preservation really predict transmission across a chain whose intermediate speakers merely relay what they heard without themselves having the relevant justification? Is the decay view a genuine third position, or does it collapse into one side — say, anti-reductionism plus a chain-length defeater, or local reductionism in disguise? And concretely: once everything is straightened out, does any of it change what I should do about deferring to expert consensus in a field where I cannot check the primary evidence myself, or is the chapter right that nothing practical turns on the label even if it is wrong about why?
