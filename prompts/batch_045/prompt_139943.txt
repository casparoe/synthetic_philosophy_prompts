I keep losing an argument in my graduate seminar on comparative collapse, and I need to know which step to refuse before I have to answer it in writing. The argument is a friend's, and it runs like this:

(1) A society is not an organism: it has no good of its own and no end it can fail to reach. Only persons and their practices can fare badly.
(2) So collapse-talk can only be talk of measurable change along dimensions — population, number and size of settlements, administrative reach, literacy, craft specialization, monumental building, caloric intake, life expectancy.
(3) Every one of those is continuous, so between any two adjacent periods there is some nonzero decline.
(4) Whether a period counts as a collapse therefore depends on where a threshold is set.
(5) No threshold is principled. The best candidate — the floor below which a practice cannot be maintained at all, because too few people per generation carry it (fewer than the specialists needed to keep a script, a smelting chain or an irrigation system going) — only relocates the arbitrariness: that floor varies with the practice, with a society's capacity to substitute one for another, and with whom you count as carrying it.
(6) Nor is there a principled relatum. In the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean the palatial and redistributive layer disappears and literacy collapses, while the region continues as small village cultures that look no worse for it; in the Maya lowlands and on Easter Island monumental and elite activity stops while subsistence farming carries on. 'Collapse' always describes a subsystem, and which subsystem you pick is a choice.
(7) If the dimensions, the threshold and the relatum are all chosen, then whether a society collapsed is not settled by the evidence: it is a perspective, not a finding — no more a matter of fact than whether the grains still make a heap.
(8) So it is not a discovery that any of these societies collapsed; and by the same reasoning the claim that our own civilization is at risk of collapse is not a forecast but a mood.

I cannot accept (8), and I do not want to buy my way out of it by conceding that 'collapse' names a natural kind or that a society has a good of its own — I would rather keep (1), (2) and (6). Which step fails, why did it look sound, and what does each of the alternatives cost me? Please be concrete where it matters: if the diagnosis is that some thresholds or some relata are principled after all, say how one could in principle tell from the evidence whether a given society had crossed one, and what should be said about a case that crosses it on some dimensions and not on others — literacy gone, say, while caloric intake rises.

Please write your answer in British English.
