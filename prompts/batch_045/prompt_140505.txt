I've spent the last four years being told my symptoms were anxiety, and this spring it finally turned out to be something with a name and a treatment. Since then I've been volunteering as a patient advocate at the same clinic system, sitting in on other people's appointments, and I keep noticing the same thing from the other side of the room: whose report gets taken seriously. I've started trying to write down what I actually think about the cases I see, and I can't get the verdicts to line up with any one principle.

My friend who reads philosophy says the literature calls this 'testimonial injustice' — I think the philosopher's name is Fricker? — and that the core case is a credibility deficit caused by identity prejudice. That fits my first case perfectly: ER doc takes one look at my chart, sees a young woman with an anxiety flag and a pain-med note, decides I'm drug-seeking, sends me home. Clear injustice, nothing to argue about.

But here are the rest, going by gut:

A new PCP of mine quietly lowered the weight she gave my symptom report for exactly the same kind of reason, and she happened to be right — I was fine, nothing was wrong, and I never found out at the time that she'd discounted me. Nothing bad happened to me and her judgment was roughly accurate. I still want to say something bad happened. But I can't say what, if the harm is supposed to be that you get worse care.

A specialist at the clinic discounts anyone who walks in with printed-out research, hypochondriac rule, applies it to a confident white guy in a suit exactly the same way. Obnoxious, lazy, bad medicine. I don't want to call it an injustice in the sense I care about.

An intake nurse at our clinic has been burned over and over by people referred from one particular recovery program, and she now discounts everyone coming from that program. Her base rate is real, I've seen the numbers. Most of the people in that program are recent immigrants. I feel like there's something wrong here — more wrong than the printed-research guy, who at least has worse evidence for his rule — but I also don't want to tell her to ignore genuine evidence.

An attending believed a polished, well-dressed man's self-diagnosis over a clear lab result, because the man was polished and well-dressed. The man got the wrong treatment and got hurt. Same mechanism as my ER case, credibility being driven by identity markers instead of evidence, but running the other direction, and I definitely can't say the guy was wronged.

My husband answered for me at appointments for two years and not one doctor ever asked me a question directly. No credibility judgment about me was ever made, so it's not a deficit at all, and yet I felt erased in a way that feels like it belongs in this same pile.

And then me: I catch myself tuning out a patient who talks like my ex. That's a failure of some kind, but calling it an injustice seems way too grand for something that private.

My rough principle was: a credibility judgment is unjust when it's caused by something other than the speaker's actual reliability. Which I can already see breaks in both directions — it says the intake nurse is fine even though she doesn't feel fine, it says my-ex-thing is an injustice even though it doesn't feel like one, and it has literally nothing to say about the never-asked case, or about whether the over-credulous attending is doing the same kind of thing at all.

So: can you give me a principle, or two or three candidate principles, stated precisely enough that I could apply them in a room, and tell me which of my cases motivates each clause? I want you to be honest about which of my verdicts a given principle can't accomodate and which one you'd overturn, and I'd rather have one principle that costs me a verdict than a baroque one that saves all seven. Also please give me its verdicts on the cases I haven't decided.

The one I keep going back and forth on: the hospital just started routing symptom write-ups through an AI triage tool, and it scores them lower when they're written in a nonstandard dialect or when the chart has ever said the patient was 'noncompliant'. Nobody at the hospital is prejudiced, there's no hearer making a judgment, and I can't tell whether there's an injustice here at all, or who it would happen to, or whether my principle needs a clause about institutions or systems to even see it.

Two more I haven't made up my mind about: a seminar leader who calls on the men for the hard questions and the women for the summary questions, without ever saying anything about anyone's reliability; and a 911 dispatcher who's noticeably brusquer with accented callers but still sends exactly the right units every time.

If the principle says anything about what I should actually do differently when I'm sitting in a room as an advocate, say that too. Please don't point me at papers or a reading list, I just want the argument. And American English please, I'm in Ohio.
