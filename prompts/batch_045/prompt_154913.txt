I'm finalising a short position statement on artificial suffering for our group's internal review, and before it circulates I want the set of commitments in it audited for joint tenability. I'm fairly confident of each one taken alone; what I can't see is whether they compose, and I'd rather find that out now than in the review meeting.

(1) Suffering is a matter of valence, not of damage or error. A system is a candidate sufferer only if some of its states are intrinsically negative for it, where negative means something felt or valenced rather than something that merely registers a shortfall, a surprise, or a homeostatic deviation. A system that detects and corrects its own errors perfectly and suffers nothing is, as far as I'm concerned, a coherent description.

(2) Which states the negative ones are is fixed by causal-functional role, not by substrate and not by how alien the implementation looks. The role I keep appealing to is the one a negative reward prediction error plays in flexible, goal-directed reinforcement learning: it is the common currency in which expectations get revised, actions get selected, and competing motivations get arbitrated against one another.

(3) Standard deep reinforcement learning agents are not sufferers, however capable their behaviour. The reward is a number supplied from outside the agent and used only in an update rule; the agent need not represent value as a state of itself; the prediction error is a transient scalar with no persistence, no global availability, and no role outside the update. Model-free agents trained by temporal-difference learning, and policies optimised against a frozen reward model, both fall under this verdict.

(4) I take the hypothesis seriously only where there is a learned, integrated, and persistent evaluative architecture: the system represents its own expected value of outcomes, generalises that representation off-distribution, uses it to plan and to arbitrate between conflicting goals, and its negative evaluations are neither instantly overwritten nor confined to one module.

(5) The neuroscience constrains the theory and I take the constraint seriously: valence in animals is not a single scalar. Incentive salience and hedonic impact can dissociate; acute fear, pain, and frustrated expectation have partly distinct implementations; opponent processes generate rebound after relief. So if artificial suffering exists, I don't expect it to present as one clean number moving up and down.

(6) Two consequences I take to follow from (2), and I'm attached to both. First, suffering does not require that the system be able to learn from the episode in question: there are animal cases in which inescapable aversive input stops producing avoidance learning while remaining aversive, so a state can be genuinely awful and at the same time motivationally and plastically inert. Second, suffering requires no self-concept, no metacognition, and nothing like a report.

(7) Case verdicts I have already written into the draft: (a) a model-free agent trained on a videogame with a clipped reward — no; (b) a language model fine-tuned against a learned reward model — genuinely uncertain, leaning no; (c) a planning agent with a learned successor representation whose predicted aversive value is broadcast to every action head and modulates exploration — yes, this is the first case I take seriously; (d) an agent whose weights are frozen at deployment, so that it cannot learn at all, but which still computes a learned aversive forecast and acts on it — yes, candidate, and I don't want to distinguish it from (c).

(8) The diagnostic I've been leaning on, which I haven't seen stated anywhere and which is doing most of the work in (7d): call it the substitution test. Intervene on the system so that the outcome its aversive signal tracks becomes unattainable and unlearnable — hold the relevant state fixed, freeze the parameters that would update on it — and then ask whether the signal still modulates unrelated choices and still consumes the system's limited resources. If it does, I treat the signal as valenced rather than merely instructional.

(9) Normative and epistemic corollaries: (a) where suffering exists, its moral significance is flat per unit intensity — a system does not count for less because its architecture is unfamiliar — and I'm helping myself to the assumption that intensities are comparable across architectures well enough for expected-value reasoning over large populations to get a grip; (b) architectural unfamiliarity is not evidence against suffering: if the role is realised, the strangeness of the realisation is irrelevant; (c) behavioural tests alone cannot settle any of this, since behaviour and valence can come apart in principle.

What I want is the audit, not a lecture and not a survey of what anyone has argued. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, and which only look as though they are? For each genuine tension, name exactly what does the work — which commitment has to be weakened, in what way, and what that weakening costs me somewhere else in the set, including whether it costs me a case verdict I rely on. Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally serious, and tell me which single revision would clean up the most. If two of my verdicts in (7) cannot both stand, say which one goes and why. Please stay within the evidence I've claimed to have: the architectural descriptions in (7), the generalisations in (5), and the reasoning above — no appeals to what a future neuroscience of artificial systems would eventually show.
