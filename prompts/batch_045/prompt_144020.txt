A well-regarded podcast on ancient philosophy summarizes the White Horse Dialogue and the School of Names this way: 'Gongsun Long’s famous slogan that a white horse is not a horse is not really about animals at all. It is a thesis about universals. The word horse picks out the universal Horse, the word white picks out the separate universal White, and the compound expression white horse picks out a further universal, White-Horse, which is obviously not identical with the universal Horse. So the claim is true once we see that Gongsun Long is talking about the reference of general terms rather than about particular animals. On this basis, the School of Names are best understood as China’s first Platonists: they took abstract universals to be the real objects of reference, and their paradoxes were meant to show that ordinary discourse about concrete particulars cannot yield knowledge. The later Mohists’ insistence that a white horse is a horse just shows that they never rose above a naive nominalism; they confused the concrete animal before them with the universal that the name really stands for.'

I have found the podcast reliable in other areas, and the host is a professional philosopher, so I hesitate to dismiss this. But calling Gongsun Long a Platonist and the Mohists naive nominalists sounds suspiciously like importing Western categories into a debate that was really about the rectification of names, and I do not know the specialist literature well enough to locate the errors. Please audit the summary sentence by sentence. Separate what a specialist in classical Chinese philosophy would accept, what they would treat as a defensible simplification or merely one interpretive option among others, and what is straightforwardly mistaken. In your answer, please address three points specifically. First, is the core reading of the White Horse argument as a thesis about universals defensible, or is it better read as turning on an ambiguity in the negative copula—between is not identical to and is not a member or subclass of the broader kind—or on some other distinction? Second, is it fair to describe the School of Names as Platonists or as skeptics who thought ordinary discourse cannot give knowledge, given that their project is usually described as the rectification of names aimed at actualities and social order? Third, is the aside about the later Mohists a fair characterization of their view? Finally, say whether the podcast’s concluding jab that the Mohists were naive nominalists follows from the accurate parts of the summary or depends on the misreadings.
