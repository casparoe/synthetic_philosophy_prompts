I am a journalist writing a feature on a proposed regional agreement for handling asylum claims, and I want to understand the moral and political assumptions it builds in.

The agreement—call it the Regional Protection and Contribution Compact—works like this. A group of neighboring states agrees that anyone who reaches one of their territories without prior authorization, or who overstays a visa, cannot apply for asylum there. Instead, the person is transferred to a processing centre in a cooperating transit country. If officials decide the person meets the standard legal definition of a refugee, the person is not sent back to danger; their name goes into a shared pool of people recognized as needing protection. The states then take people from the pool in numbers set by a formula based on population, gross domestic product, and unemployment. A state may also take extra people by sponsoring them, and sponsors are allowed to say which languages or occupational backgrounds they prefer. Someone who applies from abroad through the regular resettlement program, before starting any irregular journey, is processed under the older, more familiar rules and can be resettled directly.

What puzzles me is the combination. The compact says it is trying to protect people fleeing danger and to share responsibility fairly. Yet it treats two people who fled identical dangers completely differently depending only on how they traveled, and it lets receiving states choose some refugees partly on the basis of their economic usefulness or cultural fit.

What I need from you is the implicit theory. Reconstruct the view of who counts as a refugee and of what makes someone eligible for protection that the compact presupposes. Distinguish what the rules strictly require—what would have to be false for the compact to make no sense—from what merely fits comfortably with one familiar theory or another. In particular, tell me what the compact assumes about whether the right to asylum is a right against the particular state a person has reached, or a right to be protected by the international community that any state may help discharge; about whether the way someone arrives—lawfully through a resettlement program versus irregularly by crossing a border—can change the content or strength of their claim, or only change which institution processes it; and about whether admissions should track need alone, or may also track the contributions refugees are expected to make and the preferences of admitting states. Then say whether the different parts of the compact presuppose the same view or whether they are philosophically underdetermined, such that several rival theories would license this mixed set of rules. Finally, tell me what would have to change in the compact if we rejected the idea that mode of arrival is relevant to a protection claim, or if we accepted that the duty to admit falls on whichever states have the capacity to help rather than only on states with a prior connection to the person or the conflict.

I am interested in the structure of the argument and the practical implications of each presupposed view, not in a survey of who has said what about it.
