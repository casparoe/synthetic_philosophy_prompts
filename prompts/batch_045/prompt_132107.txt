Rank these replies a virtue ethicist can make to the situationist critique into tiers, strongest to weakest, with one sentence per reply saying why it lands where it does. If you had to actually put one into a paper, say which one.

1. Lab artfiact: the Milgram obedience results and the seminary helping study are products of weird one-shot set ups with an authority figure hovering, so they don't license any conclusion about character in ordinary life.
2. Go local: virtues are only ever reliable within a restricted band of situation-types — honest with colleagues, honest with family — and the experiments don't even test for consistency across situations, since each subject is only ever observed in one.
3. Character is a property of a life, not a moment: a virtue should show up as a pattern across decades, the sort of thing a biography or an obituary tracks, and a single twenty-minute lab session is too small a sample of a life to count as evidence about that.
4. The regress point: to say "it was the situation" you already have to pick out which features of the scene were the operative ones, and doing that well is just the practical wisdom the situationist says we don't have.
5. Fix the situations: if behavior really tracks situational detail that tightly, then designing better situations becomes the main moral lever, so the critique pushes you toward an environmentalist virtue ethics rather than away from virtue ethics.
6. It's an ideal, not a census: virtue ethics describes what a well-developed agent is like, it doesn't predict how many people are like that, so data about ordinary subjects is beside the point.

Feel free to add a seventh reply of your own and slot it into the tier list if you think something important is missing.
