At the hospital ethics committee I sit on, two members argued past each other for most of a session and I genuinely could not tell who had the better of it. The case that started it was a young man declared dead by neurological criteria whose family asked that he stay on the ventilator for a few days so that relatives could travel in and say goodbye.

(1) Miriam, an intensivist, said brain death simply is death: the organism has irreversibly lost the capacity to function as an integrated whole. The ventilator only moves air, the heart beats only because we keep it oxygenated and pressor-supported, and the body can no longer regulate its own temperature or blood pressure; stop support and every system disintegrates within minutes. That loss, not the flat line, is what death consists in.

(2) David, a philosopher, said the integration claim is empirical and partly false: maintained bodies heal wounds and mount immune responses, and there are documented cases of brain-dead pregnant women kept on support for weeks and delivering a live infant. Miriam's disintegration test also proves too much, since a dialysis patient disintegrates when support is withdrawn. The brain, he said, is being built into the definition rather than doing independent work.

(3) Miriam replied that these are functions of parts, not integration: a set of subsystems each running on its own is not an organism acting as a whole. And the argument cuts against David: under the circulatory criterion we declare death after a few minutes without a pulse, while the liver and kidneys are still viable enough to transplant and the cells are still metabolizing. If treating some living parts as parts of a corpse makes brain death a convention, it makes circulatory death one too.

(4) David said that was exactly his point. The two criteria pick out different events, jurisdictions do not even test for the same thing (some require irreversible loss of brainstem function, others loss of whole-brain function), and what we really have is a decision about when a warm, perfused body may be treated as a corpse — a decision made partly because organ procurement and the withdrawal of treatment need a bright line.

(5) Miriam said that is a dodge with a dangerous corollary: the rule that organ procurement must not kill the donor collapses if dead just means we have decided to treat someone as dead, and no family would accept being told their son is dead for some purposes.

(6) David then said the only way to arbitrate between the criteria is to say which failure death consists in, and offered a case: a patient whose brainstem is destroyed but whose cortex is kept oxygenated by a device that does the brainstem's work of regulating breathing and blood pressure — awake, conscious, able to converse. By the whole-brain criterion as written she is dead; by any criterion that tracks what we care about she plainly is not.

(7) Miriam said that a patient with a working cortex on unusual support is simply a patient, that the criterion would be revised if such cases arose, and that what the case really shows is that what families are grieving in brain death is the irreversible loss of the capacity for consciousness, which the criterion tracks well enough.

Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the seven moves, say what is right, what is mistaken or rests on a confusion, and whether it actually answers the move before it. Then say what both of them are missing, and who is more right overall.
