I am a novelist, I have no philosophical training, and the closing chapter of the book I am finishing turns on a-choice that a philosopher friend tells me is structurally the same as Kavka's toxin puzzle. I have been reading a widely syndicated essay about that puzzle — a piece written for general readers by someone with a philosophy doctorate, and it reads as though it knows what it is about. Before I build a scene on it I should like to know how much of it to trust. I have kept the essay's wording but numbered its sentences:

1. An eccentric billionaire offers you a million pounds: if, by midnight tonight, you have formed the genuine intention of drinking, tomorrow, a vial of toxin that will make you wretchedly ill for a day and leave no lasting harm, the money is yours, and nothing requires you actually to drink it.

2. The billionaire is an infallible judge of intentions, so there is no question of deceiving him, and the puzzle is that the money seems unobtainable: by the time tomorrow comes the only reason you ever had for drinking, namely the prize, will have lapsed, and you cannot simply decide now to intend to drink, any more than you can simply decide to believe something you know to be false.

3. The lesson is that intentions are not, in the relevant sense, up to us: an intention is a state that befalls one, closer to a mood or a headache than to an action, and this is why our everyday vocabulary of making up one's mind and holding oneself to a resolution is, strictly, only a manner of speaking.

4. It is really Newcomb's problem in another costume: the evidential decision theorist has to tell you to cultivate the intention, since having it would be excellent news about the million pounds, and then has to tell you not to drink, which is incoherent; the causal theorist gets it right, because by tomorrow the intention can do no causal work.

5. The puzzle is sometimes said to dissolve, since people plainly do intend things they know perfectly well they will not do, the resolution to rise at six, the dieter's intention to refuse pudding, and once we grant that intending to do something is compatible with a low expectation of doing it, nothing prevents you from forming the intention tonight and collecting the money.

6. The upshot is that no one can ever collect such a prize, and the same holds of every case in which someone resolves now to do later what she will then have no reason to do, which is at bottom why promises bind us far less than we pretend and why resolutions are worth nothing.

What I want, if you would be so good, is an audit I can act on. Tell me sentence by sentence which of these a philosopher working on intention and rational choice would accept as stated, which are right only with a qualification or a missing distinction, and which are wrong or would make a specialist wince — with the correction, and with any distinction the essay blurs stated plainly. Then tell me whether the closing moral in sentence 6 actually follows from the parts of the summary that survive correction, or whether it adds something the earlier sentences do not support. I am inclined to distrust sentence 4, it feels to me like a category mistake, but I cannot say why; sentence 5 sounds like the sort of move that would be too easy, though I cannot see what is wrong with it either.

Then there is my own case, which differs from the philosopher's in four respects, and I need to know whether any of them changes the verdict. My character is offered not money but a bequest, a dead rival's papers, which she wants for reasons of her own. The thing she must intend to do is burn the only manuscript of her finished novel, and unlike the man with the toxin she will still have a residual reason to do it: she has come to despise the book and part of her wants it destroyed, though she judges that part insufficient on its own. The intention is to be assessed by a panel of readers from the diaries she keeps, and it occurs to her that she might simply write tonight that she intends to burn the manuscript tomorrow, and that the written line might do more than record the intention. And the offer recurs on seven successive nights, so that she can try again. So: does the puzzle depend on her having no reason at all later, or merely an insufficient one, and if the latter, can a residual desire be leaned on to form the intention? Can an intention be constituted by an inscription rather than merely expressed in one, and does it matter that the panel's judgement of her diary is itself an interpretation, so that they may be constructing an intention rather than detecting one? Does repetition help her or hurt her?

I am not asking you to tell me what the character decides; I want to know which of these claims I can let a character rely on in an argument without a philosopher in the audience groaning. Please do not give me reading lists, references, or citations — I have no way of chasing them up. And please write your answer in British English.
