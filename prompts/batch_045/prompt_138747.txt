Roughly, as I remember it: (1) Ines: the whole thing is hopeless in principle — for any finite set of demonstrations there are infinitely many reward functions that would produce exactly that behavior, so there's no such thing as "the" reward the engineer was optimizing and we'd just be fitting noise and then shipping it. (2) Owen: the degeneracy is real but it isn't "anything goes" — once you fix the environment dynamics and treat the demonstrations as optimal, the rewards consistent with that behavior form a strongly constrained set, and you can break the remaining ties by picking the explanation that makes the observed trajectories least surprising / most random (maximum entropy over trajectories), or by assuming a noisily-rational demonstrator. (3) Ines: that tie-breaker is a prior you picked, not a discovery about me, and the "how noisy is the human" parameter is a knob you set by hand; also all of it assumes the demonstrator is near-optimal, which anyone who has watched me at 4am knows is false. (4) Owen: we don't need the true reward, we need behavior that matches where it matters — the reward is just a compact encoding, and we can validate on held-out weeks. (5) Ines: we explicitly do want it to generalize to new services and alert types we've never seen, and on top of that the assistant's action space isn't ours — it can page three teams at once, reopen a closed ticket, work all night without getting tired — so "match what a human would do" isn't even defined for the actions we most want it to take. (6) Owen: fine, then let's not infer from raw logs at all — show the engineer two candidate triage plans and record which one they pick, like pairwise preference data, which is a much more direct signal. (7) Ines: clicks are behavior too, produced under time pressure by someone who knows their picks get reviewed and who will choose whichever plan looks defensible in a postmortem, and who — once she realises the data is training the thing that may replace her — will act differently; and no pattern of clicks separates "escalating this alert is low-value to me" from "I believe escalation is currently broken / it's not my call," because a false belief and a genuine preference generate identical behaviour. (8) Owen: then model the human as part of the system and treat her actions as evidence about the objective the assistant should be optimizing, rather than as something to copy — the assistant serves the human's reward instead of imitating her policy. (9) Ines: that just relocates the assumption, because you've now built in that the human's reward is the target and that her behavior is informative about it, which is exactly what's in question here — our on-call behavior is produced under a contract (we're measured on time-to-acknowledge, we're watched in postmortems) and that contract disappears the moment the assistant is deployed, so the reward you recover describes preferences in a situation that no longer exists; and separately, whole families of reward functions — scaling everything up, adding a constant, or adding a term that just reweights future states in a way that cancels out along trajectories — leave an optimal agent's behavior completely unchanged, so no amount of observation in principle distinguishes them, even though they'd behave very differently once the environment shifts.
