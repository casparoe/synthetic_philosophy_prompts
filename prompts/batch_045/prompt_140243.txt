I am a novelist, and I am writing a long essay — part criticism, part workshop talk — on why goodness is nearly impossible to put on the page. I want to build the argument around Prince Myshkin in Dostoevsky's *The Idiot*, and I want it to be a genuine encounter with the philosophical debate about moral saints rather than a decorative use of it. The question the debate asks is whether maximal moral goodness is even desirable: whether a life wholly given over to improving the welfare of others would be a life we have reason to want, given what it costs in other excellences — knowledge, appetite, play, friendship in its partial and preferential forms. I have Wolf's 1982 essay in mind, with its two models of the saint, one organized around love and one around duty, and the claim that neither would be an attractive or well-rounded life; I also have in mind the older integrity complaint that impartial moral deliberation asks the rescuing husband for "one thought too many" when he saves his wife. Please don't give me a reading list or footnotes — I know the literature; I want the reading.

Two things I would like you to test rather than assume. The first is my own craft claim, which I have been calling the problem of narrative availability: a protagonist needs projects, and projects require that one sometimes be unavailable, whereas someone whose entire life is given over to the good of whoever is nearest can always be interrupted and therefore can never initiate. On this view Myshkin is remarkable because he is the one major figure in the novel with no project of his own — he arrives with an inheritance claim and a purpose he can barely articulate — and that is why he functions as a catalyst for everyone else's plot rather than the driver of his own. Does that hold up against the actual book?

The second is a thesis I want to defend about the people *around* the saint, which I think the debate undercounts because it is framed almost entirely around the saint's own well-roundedness. Call it the coercion of unconditional love: an offer of unconditional regard is one that cannot be refused without self-indictment, because refusing it confirms exactly the worthlessness the offer was meant to deny. If that is right, sainthood's heaviest cost is externalized onto its beneficiaries — and it lands hardest on those who most need to be able to say no. I think Dostoevsky dramatizes this better than any argument could, and I think it is a distinct objection from the ones usually canvassed: it is not that the saint loses out on non-moral goods, but that the saint's goodness is a kind of force.

Please structure your response in the following sections, at roughly these lengths:

1. "The thesis, stated plainly" — about 150 words. Restate, in your own words and as sharply as you can, the claim you take me to be making, including where you think I am wrong about it.

2. "The scenes I should build on" — about 400 words. Give me three or four scenes from the novel that carry the argument: the name-day party where the money goes into the fire, Myshkin's instantaneous forgiveness of the man who strikes him, the confrontation between the two women whose claims on him are incommensurable, and the vigil beside the murdered woman — but substitute or add as you think best. For each, say what it shows about saintly goodness and where it complicates rather than confirms my thesis. I have no text in front of me and do not want quotation; describe. If you are unsure of a detail, flag it rather than inventing one, since I will be fact-checked.

3. "Where the philosophical frame holds" — about 250 words. Where does Myshkin genuinely instantiate what the debate is worried about, and on which of the two models, if either?

4. "Where the novel resists the reading" — about 300 words. Be adversarial here. Is Myshkin's goodness even separable from his illness and his social helplessness, or does the novel make his compassion *depend* on the very deficits the debate treats as incidental? Is the catastrophe around him evidence against sainthood, or against a world unreceptive to grace? Say plainly if you think a novelist reaching for this reading is conscripting Dostoevsky into an argument he would have despised.

5. "What the novel adds that argument cannot" — about 300 words. My candidate answer is the externalized cost and the coercion of unconditional love; develop it, correct it, or replace it with something better the book supports.

6. "The hostile question from the floor" — about 200 words. Give me the single strongest objection a philosopher in the audience would raise against my essay, and my best available answer to it.
