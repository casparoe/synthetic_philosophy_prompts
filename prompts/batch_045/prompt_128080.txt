I am forty-six, and three months ago I had to choose between two treatments for a condition that is not going to kill me quickly enough to make the decision easy. For the standard protocol my specialist could quote a response rate from a large trial. For the newer one she said, more or less, that the honest answer is that nobody has a number yet. I took the first. My brother, who does quantitative work in a field I will not name because he would enjoy it, has been insufferable ever since: he tells me I committed the Ellsberg fallacy in a hospital corridor, that no single probability I held about the unknown option could have generated the pair of preferences I displayed, and that this makes me, technically, incoherent.

Having read a little since then, I no longer think the insult lands where he thinks it does. What looked to me like a fact about my situation — that the number was not known, by anyone — gets reformulated as a defect in me, because the only machinery he has for an unknown probability is a number that he is entitled to demand and then audit for consistency. So I would like to laugh at the machinery rather than at myself, and I would like the laughter to be technically competent.

What I want is a parody of a government white paper: a draft code of practice, issued by an invented agency, requiring that any probability which would otherwise be reported as unknown must instead be replaced by a single precise figure, filed and binding. Perhaps nine hundred words, in numbered sections — preamble, definitions, the core stipulation requirement, three worked examples, enforcement and audit, consultation questions, and an appendix containing an extract from a dissenting response by an invented professional body, at least one line of which I can quote to my brother.

The substance has to be right, and it has to be the source of the jokes. I want the worked examples to include Ellsberg's three-colour urn — thirty red balls and sixty that are black or yellow in some unstated proportion — with the department supplying the missing split, and the pair of preferences that cannot be generated by any single probability over it: a bet on red preferred to a bet on black, but a bet on black-or-yellow preferred to a bet on red-or-yellow, the two comparisons differing only by the addition of the same colour to both sides. I want the audit section to enforce that preferences line up across every bet, so that the sure-thing principle becomes a compliance test. I want a clause forbidding revision of a filed figure once decisions have been taken on the strength of it, since revision would expose inconsistency. I want a hospital consent form in which the words no reliable estimate is available are struck out and replaced. And I want the department to notice, without alarm, that persons who choose the unknown proportion after seeing the filed number will exploit it, and to answer this by requiring certified randomisers — thereby conceding, in the driest possible prose, that the objection was never about the agent's consistency but about who was filling the urn.

The dissent should be a real position, not a punchline: a respondent who argues that an act should be scored by the worst case over the range of figures it could take, so that the correct deliverable is a floor and a range rather than a value, and the department's deadpan reply that a range cannot be filed, cannot be audited, and yields caution rather than a decision. If there is room, I would also like the department to handle the observation that people dislike ignorance most when someone else is better informed, and to propose registering and surcharging that informational gap.

Constraints: stay deadpan throughout, no winking, no puns, no jokes at the expense of accuracy, and no closing moral — the piece should end inside its own voice. Nothing should be attributed to any real person, living or dead, and no real institution should be named. The target is a practice: the conversion of an unknown probability into a stipulated one so that the model can keep running, and the habit of diagnosing the resulting discomfort as incoherence in the agent rather than as a limitation of the model. A decision theorist should recognise each move, described correctly, and should find the satire fair; my brother, who is a structural engineer with no formal exposure to any of this, should understand it without footnotes. And please do not let the department be simply wrong — let the proposal follow the logic of the position it parodies to a conclusion the reader recoils from, while leaving visible the genuine discomfort that an honest I don't know carries information that a single number destroys, and that a procedure which cannot accept it as an input fails exactly where it matters most.
