So here's where I'm at. I did about thirty-five years as an atmospheric physicist — lots of data assimilation, lots of arguing about what a model's error structure actually is — and I retired a few years ago. For the last two years I've been poking my nose into philosophy proper, mostly decision theory, because that's the part that feels like it might reward the habits I already have. I came in through the Newcomb family of puzzles and I've now got stuck on two of them.

Counterfactual mugging: a very good predictor flips a coin; if it comes up heads it hands you a large sum provided it predicted you'd hand over a small sum on tails; it came up tails and it's now asking for the small sum. Nothing you do now causally changes anything. And XOR blackmail: you get a letter from someone who has sent it iff exactly one of two things holds — you have a rare nasty disease, or you're the sort who'd pay on getting the letter. Since the disease is rare, the letter is strong evidence you don't have it; paying buys you nothing causally either, but it's evidence-linked to the good news.

I've got maybe a year of spare-brain time, call it ten hours a week, and I'd rather get properly clear about one thing than skim all of it. So: which question in this neighbourhood deserves the year, and why that one ahead of the others? I want the ordering defended, not a survey. I'm less interested in which theory you like than in what makes a question worth the time: how much of the rest depends on its answer, whether it's tractable for someone with probability, statistics and a bit of game theory but essentially no philosophy training and only patchy acess to the literature, and what kind of argument would actually move it up or down your list — since obviously nobody can run an experiment here, what would count as progress?

Things I keep circling, which you should feel free to reorder, replace, or tell me are pseudo-problems:

1. Whether these two puzzles are one puzzle. Both look like "do the causally useless thing because of what your doing it says about your decision procedure." But in the mugging the branch where paying matters is counterfactual and you know it didn't happen; in the blackmail the letter genuinely arrived and the branch is actual. Is that difference doing real work, or is it decoration? And is "updatelessness" even the right frame, or is the better move to keep updating and instead change which counterfactuals you use?

2. The noisy mugger, which is the one that actually bugs me. Every version I've seen has a perfect predictor, and in my old life nothing was ever perfect. So: suppose the predictor works by running a bounded simulation of you — it can afford some fixed number of steps and defaults to "won't pay" if the sim hasn't halted — so its accuracy isn't a single number but a function of how long you deliberate and of how your algorithm degrades under truncation. Does the case for paying survive once you put in a realistic error structure, or does it quietly depend on assumptions about how errors correlate that nobody has motivated? And related: what's the right grain for "the counterfactual where I pay" when the predictor's model of me is only approximate? I have a suspicion that most of the interesting content is hiding in that granularity question rather than in the yes/no verdict.

3. A common-cause version of the blackmail. Keep the numbers identical but make the correlation come from a common cause instead of from a prediction of you: the mailing list was compiled from a registry that's correlated with the disease, so there's no dependence of the letter on your decision procedure at all, only a correlation. My instinct is that the case for paying collapses and the whole thing becomes a smoking-lesion case — but if it does, then "logical or subjunctive dependence on your output" versus "mere correlation" is doing all the work, and I want to know whether that distinction can be cashed out without assuming the agent is logically omniscient, which no real agent is.

4. Whether any of this composes. One coin shared across many agents, or many independent muggers in a row: does acquiescence add up, does anything like superrationality or acausal trade show up, or is that a red herring bolted on by people who liked Hofstadter too much?

And then the bit I'm most suspicious of: the claimed implications. People say this stuff shows that functional-style theories are exploitable and would get blackmailed in real bargaining; that it matters for how you'd want an AI system to reason about agents who model it; that it bears on ordinary human commitment, reputation and credible threats; that it underwrites acausal trade between civilisations. Which of those actually follow, which are overrated, and which don't follow at all? Give me the ranking and the reason for the ranking — that's the part I'll use.

Please don't send me a reading list or point me at papers; I want the questions and the arguments, and I want to be able to argue back.
