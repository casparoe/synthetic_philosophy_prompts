A friend and I got into an argument about the centipede game last week and I keep going in circles, so I'm outsourcing it to you.

The setup, so we're on the same page: two players, 100 nodes, alternating turns. On your turn you can either TAKE, which ends the game and gives you the bigger share of the pot, or PASS, which grows the pot and hands the turn to the other person. The game is arranged so that every later ending is better for *both* players than every earlier one -- if we both pass to node 70 and you take there, I'm still better off than if I'd taken at node 1. If nobody takes by the final node the pot is split almost evenly and we both walk away rich.

Here's his argument, which I've written out because I want the steps separate:

(1) At the final node there's nothing after me, so TAKE strictly beats PASS. A rational player takes at the last node.

(2) So at the second-to-last node, if I pass, my opponent hits the final node and takes, and I get the smaller share. That's worse for me than taking now. So I take at the second-to-last node.

(3) Same move 98 more times. Take at node 1.

(4) Nothing in (1)-(3) depended on how many nodes there were. A 2-leg centipede and a 100-leg centipede come out exactly the same: take immediately, tiny payoffs for both.

(5) But that means all 98 extra rounds are worth precisely zero, even though both of us like the payoffs at every one of them better than the payoffs at node 1. Adding a huge pile of mutually profitable future moves to an interaction changes nothing about what rational people do in it.

(6) And the reasoning isn't really about centipedes. It's the same shape as the surprise-exam thing (it can't be on the last day, so it can't be on the second-to-last day, so no exam is ever a surprise -- and yet surprise exams happen), and the same shape as the finitely repeated prisoner's dilemma (defect in the last round, so defect in the round before, so defect from the start). So: in any interaction with a known expiry date, trust, reputation, deterrence and cooperation are all impossible between people who know each other to be rational.

(7) Which means the only thing holding cooperation together, anywhere, is somebody not knowing when the thing ends. He's happy with this and pushes it: a rational agent should pay to be kept in the dark about the end date, should prefer a deal that might dissolve on any given day to one guaranteed to run twice as long, and should treat the entire future of any relationship with a definite end as worth nothing. Since our lemonade stand has a fixed last Saturday, we should both quit today.

Step (7) is where I got suspicious, but I can't locate the damage. I built a variant to test it, which I'm calling the Blind Centipede: identical game, except you're never told how many legs there are, and after every pass there's some fixed chance the game just stops and you're not told whether it stopped because that was the end. He says this doesn't help, because the number of legs is still finite so the argument "goes through in expectation." I say it can't even start, since there's no last node to begin the induction at -- but then I can't tell whether I've shown that the culprit is the *known* horizon or the *induction* itself, and those feel like very different diagnoses.

The bit I keep poking at is (2). By the time we're at the last node, my opponent has passed 98 times, which is exactly what somebody who takes at the last node wouldn't have done. So am I allowed to use (1) there, or is (1) only licensed somewhere I can actually get to? He says optimality is node-by-node and history is irrelevant, which sounds right and also sounds like it's doing all the work.

So: which step do I give up? I'd like to keep (1) if I possibly can -- at a real last node with nothing after it, taking really does look better -- and I'd rather not swallow the claim that rationality is a liability. But tell me why the step you're rejecting looked innocent, what each of the other options would cost me, and, if your answer is "bite the bullet, rational players really do take at node 1," what I'm then committed to saying about the actual humans in the actual experiments who mostly don't.
