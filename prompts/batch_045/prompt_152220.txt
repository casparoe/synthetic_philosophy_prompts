Hi — I chair the "Beyond the Call" panel at a fairly big district general hospital, and I need something we can actually run. Every month we get 10-15 nominations from staff for colleagues who supposedly went above and beyond, we have 90 minutes, six people on the panel (two nurses, a doctor, an HR rep, a patient rep, and me — I'm in facilities), nobody has any philosophy training, and the nomination forms are two pages of narrative with almost never a clear statement of what the person's actual job description was or whether they were on duty. Decisions get read out at a staff event, they affect morale, the union watches them, and our comms lead has one hard rule: the citation has to praise the act without implying that everyone who didn't do it did something wrong. That last bit is the one we always screw up.

What I want is a decision procedure, not an essay. Give me:

1. The questions in the order we ask them, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each one.
2. The gates where the answer is just "no award" and we stop.
3. The gates where the procedure hands it to us as a values call that we have to make ourselves, flagged as such, because no amount of procedure settles it.
4. What to do when we simply can't establish the facts (the form is vague, accounts disagree, the person's role is contested) — do we defer, default, or drop it.

The distinctions it has to make, roughly: (a) the job done well, or a nice extra that's still just optional-but-ordinary; (b) genuinely beyond what was required of this person and praiseworthy; (c) actually required, i.e. the person was lucky not to be disciplined rather than heroic; (d) something admirable in spirit that we still shouldn't celebrate, because rewarding it signals the wrong thing — reckless self-risk, or papering over a staffing failure by turning it into a story about individual virtue.

Hard cases it has to survive, because we've hit all of these:

- The person's own mistake caused the emergency, and then they stayed fourteen hours fixing it. Is that heroism or just putting right what you broke?
- What HR has started calling "quiet refusals": the act was an omission or a waiving — turning down a bonus so a junior could have it, giving up a promotion, taking their name off a paper they'd partly written. Costly, but is it the same shape as doing something?
- The person profited afterwards: press coverage, a fundraiser, a promotion, a book deal. Does being rewarded retro-actively stop it from counting?
- Mixed motives, off-duty acts, acts by people with no special role at all, and acts where the person was asked by a manager rather than volunteering.
- The "they could have done it at trivial cost" end of things — you know the style of argument, that if you can prevent something terrible at almost no cost to yourself then it isn't optional at all. Some of our nominations look like that: huge benefit to someone else, tiny inconvenience to the agent. I want the procedure to tell us where that tips over from praiseworthy to required, and to be honest that it's drawing a line rather than finding one.
- And the deep one: if the act really was optional, what are we praising, and if it really was the obviously best thing to do, why isn't skipping it blameworthy? I don't need this solved. I need the procedure to mark exactly where it stops being able to answer and just stipulates a convention so we can be consistent.

Also: our HR director has started floating "second-order" nominations — not the person who did the thing, but the person who arranged things so other people could do it (a ward manager who quietly rewrote the rota so staff could sit with dying patients). I have no idea whether that belongs in the same category or breaks it. Deal with it or rule it out, but say which.

Then run the whole thing on three real-ish nominations:

- A night nurse who stayed six hours unpaid past the end of her shift to sit with a dying patient whose family were stuck on a motorway. The ward was two staff short and the manager had put out a general "stay if you can" message.
- A senior pharmacist whose own dispensing error caused a patient's crisis, who then worked through the night to stabilise him and visited the family every day for a week.
- A porter who donated a kidney to a colleague he'd known about three months. The story got picked up locally and raised a chunk of money for the hospital; the transplant team were visibly uneasy about the whole thing.

We've more or less decided informally that the nurse is getting it, so if your procedure says no, say so plainly and tell us why. I also want a short closing section on what this procedure can't do — where it will misfire, what it can't rank or compare, and the cases where we should ignore it. Keep the language usable by people who've never thought about any of this; if you need a term of art, define it in a sentence.
