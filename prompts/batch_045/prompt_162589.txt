so I've somehow ended up on the volunteer committee of a tiny municipal archive — the kind of place that has town council minutes from 1921 to 1974, a shoebox of unlabelled photographs, and a ledger of pauper burials — and we're about to put the council minutes online behind a search box. This has started an argument I can't settle. My friend Priya says the point is to get everything up there, that absence only looks like silence because nobody has looked properly yet, and that digitisation plus a decent search index fixes it. I keep saying the stuff that isn't in the minutes isn't in the minutes, and no amount of OCR recovers a person who was never written down at all — but I also don't want to end up writing sad speculative paragraphs about people I have zero evidence about, which feels like its own exercise of power, just a nicer-looking one.

So before we write the policy I want a spec rather than a reading list. What would an adequate account of silence in the archive have to deliver, given that the thing it's for is a practical one: deciding what this collection owes to the people who are missing from it — what to digitise first, what to annotate, what to refuse to guess at. Give me the requirements with the concrete case behind each, and please don't hang them on named theorists or books; I want the cases doing the work. Some distinctions I've been using and would like you to either use or argue with: (i) never-created vs. created-then-destroyed vs. created-but-unfindable, where the last one means there's no finding aid, or it's catalogued under a term nobody today would think to search; (ii) present-but-distorted, where somebody appears only as the object of someone else's act — a fine, a removal order, a line in a missionary's diary — so the record is full of them and tells you nothing about them; (iii) abundance that functions like absence, where there's so much material that nothing in particular can be found, or so much of it concerns the same handful of officials that the rest of the town is technically documented and practically gone; (iv) silence somebody chose, or would have chosen if asked, which makes "more records" not obviously the right goal. Tell me which of these an account has to handle and which are optional extras, and what case I'm leaving out.

Then the part I actually care about: mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as a preference of one camp; find me any pair that probably can't be satisfied together — my guess is that "explain the mechanism that produced the gap" and "don't invent voices for the people inside the gap" pull against each other, but tell me if the conflict is somewhere worse; and say which single requirement does the most work in ruling out the obvious candidate approaches, like "preserve and catalogue everything", "read the sources against the grain", "plug the holes with oral testimony", or "stick to original order and provenance". Also tell me what would count as cheating in an answer to this: I have a feeling that requirements like "be reflexive about your own position" or "centre marginalised voices" sound good and constrain nothing, so if you include anything like that, give me the test that makes it bite. And assume you've got no internet — argue from the cases, not from anything you'd have to look up.
