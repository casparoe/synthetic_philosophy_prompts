I lead bird walks and give the occasional evening talk at the nature center where I volunteer, and this spring I have been asked to do forty-five minutes on "animal minds" for a mixed audience: mostly adult birders and hikers, a few teenagers, nobody with any philosophy or biology training, and a handful who are firmly convinced their dog understands every word they say. I have been reading about Dennett's four kinds of minds — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian and Gregorian creatures — and I want to build the talk around that taxonomy, because it seems to me to cut across the "instinct versus intelligence" split the audience will walk in with, which is exactly the split that makes people think a crow is either a robot or a furry person. I don't need the four kinds explained to me. I need a plan for explaining them, and I have one example of my own that I want ruled on. Please don't send me off to further reading — what I have is what I have, and I'd rather you committed to a plan than hedged.

The thing I have invented, and which I suspect is either the best or the worst part of my outline, is what I call the blue-bucket test. A bird has been reinforced for getting food out of a red bucket whose latch is on the left. I then describe presenting it with an otherwise identical blue bucket whose latch is on the right, and separately with a bucket where the latch is hidden under a flap the bird has to nudge aside before it can see anything at all. My idea is that the Skinnerian creature has to be re-shaped by trial and error on the new bucket, while the Popperian one transfers on the first try, because something in it is trying out "latch on the other side" before it acts and letting the bad option die in its place. A friend who read my draft says the middle of the talk still collapses into "animals that learn versus animals that think," and I think she may be right. So: does the blue-bucket test actually isolate the Skinnerian-to-Popperian transition, or does it just read as smarter-versus-dumber? What must a carrying example have, and what must it lack, at each of the three transitions? And is it better to run one thread I re-describe four times, or four animals? I lean toward one thread; tell me if that's the wrong call.

What I want from you is the architecture. First, the order the pieces have to go in and why — I assume the notion of design without a designer has to be on the whiteboard before any of the four kinds is named, by which I mean the idea that there can be a reason for a feature without anyone, not the animal and not a maker, ever having that reason in mind (I have seen this called a "free-floating rationale"), and I assume the Gregorian move cannot land until the audience has genuinely taken on board that selection can accumulate design over many lives and not just within one. But I want that dependency structure argued, not listed: tell me what breaks if a piece is moved. Second, the misconceptions, where they sit in the sequence, and which ones have to be defused before the example rather than after it. The ones I expect: that this is an evolutionary timeline or a ladder with us at the top; that a human being is a Gregorian creature rather than all four stacked at once; that "Skinnerian" means the creature is a mindless reflex machine with nothing going on inside; that "Popperian" means the animal is conscious, or has a self that knows what it is doing; and that tool use or social learning automatically makes something Gregorian, so the last category is really just "us, with language." Give me the ones I haven't thought of too, including the ones my audience will form from my own example. Third, what I should refuse to explain at this level and say out loud that I am refusing — I am thinking the hard problem of consciousness and anything about whether the animal feels anything, but I am genuinely unsure whether to disown those questions at the start or hold them to the end. Fourth, one question I can put to the room in the last five minutes that would show me they had the taxonomy and not just four new words. Commit to a single order, a single carrying example, and a single placement for each misconception, and give your reasons; and where your plan rests on a contested reading of the scheme — whether tool-making or tradition-bearing birds already count as Gregorian, say — tell me in a line which reading you are betting on and what would convince you it was the wrong one.
