I'm a backend engineer — no philosophy training beyond whatever seeps in from HN threads — and I keep bumping into two things people call the lottery paradox and the preface paradox. I'd like them restated in the vocabulary I actually use, and then I want the honest residue report.

The vocabulary I'm fluent in: CI pipelines and build gating, blocking vs non-blocking checks, flaky tests and quarantine lists, SLOs and error budgets, per-request reliability vs fleet-level reliability, Bloom filters and the gap between "this element is probably in the set" at 1% and "the filter has no false positives at all", type systems vs tests (sound, complete, pick one), code review sign-off, canary deploys. If anything in these paradoxes has a home somewhere in that list, put it there instead of hand-waving.

How I'd set it up, and tell me if I'm mangling it: a lottery with 10,000 tickets — I'm about 99.99% on "ticket #4371 loses", and the same for every other ticket, but nowhere near 99.99% on the AND of all ten thousand. And the preface: an author who's written 400 claims, is confident of each one individually, and also says in the preface that the book surely contains at least one mistake. My instinct is that both are just the move from a per-item guarantee to an aggregate one, which in my world is error-budget arithmetic and isn't spooky at all. I suspect I'm flattening something.

Three wrinkles I want you to actually deal with rather than smooth over:

1. Independent vs correlated failure. Lottery tickets are independent, which is what makes the conjunction fall apart cleanly. The author's errors aren't — if she's wrong about one thing she's probably wrong about a cluster of related things, the way one bad shared library takes out a dozen services at once. Does my vocabulary have anything for both shapes, or does it really only have the independent case and have to fudge the correlated one?

2. A test that asserts the suite is wrong. I've been messing with a convention at work I'll call shadow assertions: they run, they log, they page someone, but they never turn the build red — as opposed to blocking assertions, which do. Is the author's preface move just "every claim is a shadow assertion, plus one blocking meta-assertion that at least one of them is wrong"? Or does it break? Can a suite coherently contain, as a blocking test, the claim that some other blocking test in the same suite is wrong? Walk me through what my CI would actually do with that, because I think this is where the analogy either cashes out or dies.

3. Stakes. Apparently it matters to the lottery case how much is riding on being wrong — I'd act on "my ticket loses" for the price of a pint, but you couldn't pay me to act on "this plane won't crash" at the same confidence level. My vocabulary handles that as different thresholds in different environments: dev vs prod, P0 vs P3. Is that the distinction the philosophers are making, or a different one wearing the same clothes?

Then the residue report, which is the bit I actually care about:

- What mapped cleanly onto build gating and reliability math?
- What did you have to bend to fit? Say explicitly when you were bending rather than translating — I'd rather see the seam than a tidy answer.
- What has no counterpart at all in my vocabulary? My guesses, and argue with them: "rationally permitted to believe" (my pipeline has no notion of rational, only of configured); the difference between believing something and being willing to act on it; and whatever it is that makes the preface case feel like it's about the author's own fallibility rather than about statistics.
- Finally, the straight verdict: is the leftover pointing at something my engineering vocabulary genuinely can't say, or is it pointing at the philosophers having conflated per-item confidence with confidence in a conjunction and then being surprised by arithmetic? I can't tell which, and a definite answer either way is worth more to me than a balanced one.
