You are a philosopher of economics and decision theory — someone equally at home with the representation theorems behind choice-based welfare measurement and with the normative literature on well-being, advantage, and adaptive preference. I need you to adjudicate a disagreement carefully rather than quickly: before you rate anything, spend a few sentences laying out what you take to be the distinct questions the two sides are conflating (utility as a representation of choice versus utility as a standard of well-being; measurement versus aggregation; counterfactual versus actual preference; the evidential versus the constitutive role of choice). Use that map to structure your verdict. Take your time and be precise about where a move is valid but under-described, and where it quietly shifts the question.

I am a postdoc in public policy, and I was the bystander at a dinner where two senior colleagues went at it for an hour. They asked me to settle it and I could not, so I am passing it to you. I have tried to reconstruct both sides in their strongest form; if anything I have made the economist's case stronger, simply because I followed it more easily, so please do not read my phrasing as a hint about who wins.

The case that started it: a city housing authority relocated about 900 households from a dense central district to newer units in a peripheral one, with no individual opt-out. Eighteen months later the authority ran what it calls a revealed-welfare audit: households were offered the chance to move back to their original buildings, which had been refurbished and were offered at the original rents. Fifty-eight percent declined. On that basis the authority recorded a net welfare improvement, and separately reported a money-metric figure — computed from the households' observed housing demand — of about $2,400 per household per year as the cash equivalent of the gain. A substantial minority did move back, and they were disproportionately long-tenured elderly residents; the decliners were disproportionately younger households with children.

Nadia (the economist) opened: observed choice is the only evidence about someone's good that isn't question-begging. "Utility" in her discipline just names a numerical index that ranks options consistently with choices; it was never a mental quantity or a happiness reading. If we want to know whether people are better off, we should ask what they take when the alternatives are genuinely available, and nothing else is measurable. On that view the audit is a strict improvement over officials guessing about what makes a good life, and it treats residents as authorities on their own good instead of overriding them.

Tobias (the philosopher) replied that the audit measures ex post preference, formed after eighteen months of accommodation to a policy the residents did not choose; preferences migrate toward what is attainable, so their declining the return does not by itself show they are better off. Second, the "old option" on offer is not the old option: neighbors dispersed, so the bundle being declined is not the bundle that was taken away, and the choice therefore does not reveal a comparison between the two policies. Third, a 58/42 split is being reported as one net gain, which is aggregation presented as measurement.

Nadia answered that the adaptation worry proves too much, because any welfare criterion — including Tobias's — has to take preferences as given at some point, and ex ante preferences have their own distortions (status quo bias, loss aversion) that make them no cleaner than ex post ones. She granted the "not the same bundle" point as a caution about specifying the counterfactual, not as a principled objection, since the restored option can be made as close to the original as feasible. And she said the split is not being hidden: the incidence — who gains, who loses — can be reported separately.

Tobias conceded that no criterion is immune to endogenous preference, but pressed an asymmetry: the choice-based approach advertises itself as positive and neutral while in fact making a normative decision about which preferences count (measured when, held under which beliefs, over which menu of options). And if both ex ante and ex post preference are contaminated, then revealed preference has no privileged tie to welfare at all — which was his deeper charge: if utility merely represents choice, then no policy can ever be criticized on welfare grounds, because any pattern of choice is consistent with some utility representation, and "they chose it, so it maximizes their utility" is empty.

Nadia's response was that emptiness is avoided by the consistency conditions on choice across budget sets: those conditions are testable, they sometimes fail, and when they hold they let us predict counterfactual demand. That predictive content is what makes the money-metric measure — how many dollars at the new prices would leave a household as well off as it was before, derived from observed demand — more than a redescription of behavior. She insisted this needs no cardinal, interpersonally comparable utility and no claim that utility is happiness.

Tobias said the money metric smuggles back exactly what the ordinalist position had ruled out. Demand curves are budget-constrained, so willingness to pay tracks ability to pay, and the metric systematically underweights the poor. And expressing everyone's gain in a single dollar unit presupposes that a dollar carries the same welfare significance for each person — which just is an interpersonal comparison, reintroduced through the back door while insisting the framework never left choice behavior.

Nadia's closing move was to separate measurement from normative judgment: the economist's deliverable is the incidence table plus the compensation test — whether the gainers could in principle compensate the losers — and elected officials set distributional weights. That, she said, is a division of labor, not smuggling; actual payment is unnecessary for the criterion to be informative. She added that for small changes, or when demand is insensitive to income, the compensating and equivalent measures coincide, so the criterion is well defined in precisely the cases policy usually involves.

Tobias's closing move had two parts. First, the incidence table is already denominated in dollars, units whose welfare significance varies across people, so the supposedly neutral deliverable embeds a cardinal and interpersonally comparable metric: neutrality is simply unavailable at that step. Second, the compensation test is not even an ordering: because it is applied at different price and income configurations in the two directions, there are ordinary preference configurations in which the move from policy A to policy B passes the test and the move from B back to A also passes it. A criterion that can bless both directions cannot underwrite the word "improvement." At that point they began repeating themselves.

Where a move is valid only on one reading of a term, say so and say which reading is doing the work. Then give an overall verdict: who is more right about what, which of their claims are compatible once the conflations are separated, and what both of them are missing — including anything the case itself (the 42 percent who went back, and who they were) should have made them address and didn't.
