You are a political philosopher with a decade of experience turning distributive principles into rules that non-philosophers can actually operate. Work carefully and in stages: sketch the reasoning you will need before you begin writing, and be willing to flag any step that is really a value judgment wearing the costume of a test.

I direct the allocation office of a regional public funder, a community investment board. Roughly forty times a year, with ten working days and one analyst who holds a master's degree in public policy and no philosophy, I have to decide which of two or three competing programs gets a fixed pot of money. Every decision is reviewed by an elected board, audited against the previous year's decisions for consistency, and exposed to public comment. Our recurring failure is currency drift: each applicant writes its case in whichever measure makes it look strongest — gains in self-reported well-being, resources delivered per dollar, what people are newly able to do and be — and my office then compares those numbers as if they were the same kind of thing.

I want a procedure, not a survey of positions. Build it on the equality-of-what debate: the three families of answer — welfare, resources, capabilities — and the difficulties that divide them, namely adaptive preferences, expensive tastes and expensive conversion needs, the gap between what people report and what they can do, the levelling-down objection, the fact that capabilities are plural and not obviously commensurable, and the question of whether the relevant unit is a moment, a year, or a whole life. Assume my analyst has read none of this.

My office already maintains four in-house instruments, and the procedure must say what to do with each of them:

- Conversion ledger. Three columns per beneficiary group: the bundle delivered, the functioning achieved, the experience reported. The resource-to-functioning gap is conversion loss; the functioning-to-experience gap is the adaptation gap, and a large positive one is evidence of adaptive preference rather than evidence that a group is doing fine.
- Repeat probe. If the same rule were run for five consecutive funding cycles, would the same households be served every time, or would the queue rotate? Equalizing a state now and equalizing a share across a life come apart here.
- Standstill test. If the worst-off group improves and nobody else changes, does the headline inequality figure move in the right direction? A metric that registers only relative gaps, or that can be satisfied by making the better-off worse off, fails this.
- Swap test. Replace each intended beneficiary at short notice with a randomly drawn member of the same eligible population. If the chosen currency no longer selects the same priority group, the currency is tracking particular individuals rather than tracking disadvantage.

The deliverable:

1. A numbered procedure. For each step give the question to ask; what counts as a sufficient answer at my analyst's level of skill and inside my time budget; the point at which the procedure stops with a no and the proposal is rejected or sent back for redesign; and the point at which it escalates to the board because what remains is a disagreement about values rather than facts. State explicitly, step by step, which of those it is.

2. A half-page glossary an analyst with no philosophy can keep open while working, defining only the terms the procedure actually uses, in ordinary language.

3. Worked applications to the three pairings below, run through the steps in order, with the ledger, the probes, and the tests genuinely applied rather than gestured at, and with the filled audit template shown for each.

4. Two closing sections: where this procedure will misfire, and what it cannot do at all — including the questions it should never be allowed to settle on its own.

The pairings:

Case 1. A chronic-pain self-management course: eight group sessions, very low cost per person, large and well-measured gains in self-reported well-being and pain bother, small gains in measured functioning, no resource transfer. Against a home insulation and heating retrofit for low-income households: a large resource transfer per person, large gains in functioning and in health-related capability, and a small, statistically fragile change in self-reported well-being.

Case 2. Powered mobility with home assessment and training for people with progressive mobility impairment: high cost per person, large capability gains, and a change in self-reported well-being that is modest overall and negative for some recipients during the first months. Against unconditional cash transfers of equal total cost to the same eligible population: far cheaper per person, reaching several times as many people, strong gains in self-reported well-being, diffuse and hard-to-attribute capability effects.

Case 3. Our board's flagship: subsidized instrumental music instruction for children across the region, genuinely transforming for the children who take it up, overwhelmingly popular with the board, and serving recipients concentrated in the better-off half of the distribution on every currency my office tracks. Against extending a legal-aid service that represents tenants facing eviction. I expect the procedure to give me an answer here that I do not want and that I will have to defend to the board; I want it anyway. Tell me which step forces that conclusion and which premise I would have to reject in order to escape it.

Constraints: procedure and glossary together must fit on two printed pages and be followable unaided by someone with no training in philosophy; each application must leave a reusable audit trail. Do not cite or recommend any reading, since I have no way to follow it up. Write in American English.
