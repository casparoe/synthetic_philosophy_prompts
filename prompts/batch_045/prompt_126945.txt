I am the only philosophy student in a graduate metascience seminar, and next week I present my term paper to that room. The other participants are working psychologists who have become preregistration advocates, two applied Bayesians who use hierarchical models in their own research, and one committed error statistician who thinks in terms of severity and long-run error control. They are sympathetic to reform and hostile to armchair philosophy of science, so I need to meet them on methodological ground.

My thesis is that the replication crisis is standardly framed as a verdict on null-hypothesis significance testing, with Bayesian methods offered as the repair, and that this framing mislocates the problem. The real fight, I argue, is not about the updating rule but about who owns the prior. I propose treating the prior the way we already treat the choice of an estimator: as a public, registered, auditable part of the model, fixed before data collection, whose required output is not a single number but what I call a prior-sensitivity band, the range of posterior conclusions that a family of registered, audited priors would license from these data. When the band straddles the null, the study is reported as uninformative rather than as a finding in either direction. My second claim is that on this proposal a good deal of the frequentist-versus-Bayesian dispute in the replication literature turns out to be verbal, and dissolves once one separates the question of what an individual should believe from the question of what a journal should let a field assert. I suspect my soft spot is that an audited prior family does no real work until someone specifies who audits and by what standard.
