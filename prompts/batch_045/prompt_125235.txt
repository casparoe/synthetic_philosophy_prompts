I host a podcast on science and ideas, and next week I'm recording an episode with a philosopher of mind who has spent about a decade defending a revised version of the extended mind thesis. Her version runs roughly like this: the parity principle from the original notebook case was never an argument, only a heuristic for spotting candidate cases; what actually does the work is a set of conditions about the resource being trustworthy, reliably available, easy to consult and automatically endorsed, so that a subject's standing use of it resembles the way the rest of us use biological memory; and, finally, she holds that the boundary of a mind comes in degrees rather than falling at a sharp line. In conversation she is very good at absorbing objections by saying that the live question is empirical — how cognition is in fact distributed across brain, body and world — and that metaphysical worries about what "constitutes" a cognitive system are a distraction.

Draft me eight to ten interview questions that keep her from restating that package. For each question, tell me the answer she is most likely to give, and then a follow-up that presses on that specific answer. I need at least one question on each of the following:

1. What argument carries the burden once the parity principle is demoted to a heuristic — and whether "functional equivalence" between the notebook and biological memory can be stated without making the thesis trivially true of any reliably consulted instrument, a thermometer for instance.
2. The objection that the thesis trades on confusing mere causal coupling with constitution, and what she thinks a non-question-begging account of the difference would look like.
3. The worry about cognitive overflow: pocket calculators, abacuses, and the sort of case where a system's "memory" is spread across a device so large or so exotic that almost any physical process turns out to realize cognition.
4. Whether making trust and automatic endorsement the test hands the thesis over to the subject's own psychology — what happens with someone who consults a notebook they half-distrust, or a horoscope they trust completely.
5. A case she will not have a stock answer for: two researchers who have jointly maintained a single external store for years, each able to retrieve entries the other authored and each unable to say, of a given entry, who produced it. If the store is part of a mind, whose mind is it in, and are we then committed to minds that overlap rather than to one mind per person?
6. A related modern case: a subject who has relied for years on a cloud-hosted assistant that she consults automatically and trusts completely, whose internals she cannot inspect or reconstruct offline, and which her provider can silently update overnight, changing what the system will "tell" her the next morning. Does that count as part of her mind, and if so, what does her view say about a subject whose beliefs can be rewritten by a third party while she sleeps?
7. Whether the thesis extends to consciousness at all, and — if she denies that it does — whether the parity rationale that got the view off the ground can be abandoned halfway without cost.
8. What would settle the dispute empirically, given that she wants to call it empirical: what observable difference is there between a resource's being part of a subject's memory system and its merely being coupled to one, and who would be in a position to detect it.
9. Whether the thesis has any consequences outside the seminar room — for instance, whether confiscating a subject's long-standing external store is more like an assault than a theft, or whether an hour without a smartphone is a temporary cognitive impairment.

Keep the questions in language an educated listener with no philosophy background can follow, and avoid anything that could be answered by a definition alone.
