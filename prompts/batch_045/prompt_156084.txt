I'm a journalist working on a long feature about a question I originally thought was a joke: what makes every 'a' an 'a'? I've spent the past month interviewing type designers, a handwriting teacher, and a couple of cognitive scientists in the orbit of Douglas Hofstadter's Letter Spirit project — the one that tried to get a computer to generate stylistically coherent alphabets built on a coarse grid, on the theory that what the machine has to capture is not a shape but something more like a spirit that gets realized differently in different styles and sizes. Everyone I talk to agrees that the double-storey 'a' of a book face and the single-storey 'a' of a geometric sans are the same letter, and no one can tell me what that sameness consists in. My editor thinks readers will glaze over the moment the words "Platonic letterform" appear, so I want to open the piece with a short story — roughly 1,000 to 1,200 words, third person, present tense — that dramatizes the puzzle instead of arguing it.

Here is the seed I have in mind, which you should feel free to rebuild if you can see something better: a type designer, let's call her Núria Ferran-Vidal, has published a family called Peristyle whose lowercase 'a' is a single-storey form so reduced and so tight at its display grade that, in isolation, readers cannot distinguish it from the 'o'; in running text they read it perfectly well, because the neighbouring letters do the work. She has petitioned a fictional standards body — I'm imagining something like a Script Registry that assigns code points to graphemes — to encode her glyph as a character distinct from the ordinary Latin small letter a, and the story is set at the hearing, though I'd rather it were made of scenes and small physical details than of people taking turns to hold forth. My art desk will kill any diagram, so the shape itself has to be conjured in prose, in a way that makes the reader feel the unrecognizability before anyone explains it. Please do not stage it as a tidy Socratic dialogue in which the wisest speaker wins; give the characters something at stake — a contract, a reputation, a pupil, a railway signage tender — and let the argument come out of that.

The philosophical material I want the fiction to carry is the menu of answers my reporting keeps circling: that the 'a' is defined by its parts and their topology (bowl, stem, aperture, how they connect); that it is defined negatively, by contrast with the other letters in the same alphabet — an 'a' is whatever in this script is not an 'o', not a 'd', not a 'g', not a 'q'; that it is defined genealogically, by descent from particular Roman and uncial ancestors, so that a shape with the wrong ancestry is a different letter no matter how it looks; or that it is defined perceptually, as a flexible schema with roles to be filled — the "spirit" idea — so that there is no list of necessary and sufficient features at all, only a family of realizations held together by something like style. If your story can make one of these feel true in the gut and then make the reader notice, by the last page, the case that it leaves out, that is exactly what I'm after. And if the fiction ends up resisting the whole framing — if it suggests the question is badly posed, or that the answer is different for a chisel, a nib, a pixel grid and a child's pencil — I would rather have that than a fable with a moral I could have printed as a sidebar.
