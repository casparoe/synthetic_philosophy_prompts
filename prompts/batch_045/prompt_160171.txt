I'm finishing a chapter on well-being and I keep going back and forth on pain, so I want to run reflective equilibrium on myself before I commit to anything. Here are my verdicts. They're all about whether the state is bad for the subject, not about whether it would be wrong to produce it.

- Ordinary acute pain, hand on a hot pan: bad, obviously.
- The classic asymbolia presentation: patient locates it, rates intensity 8/10, feels no distress, says it doesn't bother them and they could go on like this indefinitely. I want to say not bad for them at all, or barely at all.
- Same asymbolic patient, but the pain distracts them and they want it gone so they can finish a manuscript: bad for them, but only a bit, and only for the instrumental reason.
- Intensity and unpleasantness fully intact, subject wants it to continue and enjoys it (the runner, the masochist): I want to say still bad for them in itself, even though it makes their life go better. I can see this sits badly with the asymbolia verdict.
- Made-up case I keep using, call it bright pain: full sensory profile of a burn plus the urgent get-away-from-it motivation, but positive valence; the subject wants it to keep going. Verdict: not bad for them, so the withdrawal urge on its own does no work.
- Companion case, call it quiet pain: mild unpleasantness, near-zero sensory intensity, but the subject can't stop moving away from it, and offered a dollar to switch it off they decline. Verdict: bad, more than their own rating suggests? Honestly unsure.
- Pain I'd dread in advance, be indifferent to during, and be glad about afterwards: I want to say not bad for me at all, yet I'd still refuse the drug beforehand.
- An animal's pain, no second-order attitudes anywhere: bad for it, same as ours.

So what's actually doing the work — the unpleasantness, the motivational or command-like structure, the subject's desires, or the sensory state itself? Give me something precise enough to state in a sentence or two, with a clause for each verdict above, tell me honestly which verdicts it forces me to drop, and what it predicts for a case I haven't given you: someone whose pain is fully unpleasant but for whom that unpleasantness is exactly what they value about the experience, and who'd pay to repeat it.
