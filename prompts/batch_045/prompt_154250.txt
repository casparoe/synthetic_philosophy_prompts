ok, I want to actually stress-test the myth-of-mental-illness position rather than knock over a version nobody holds, so play Thomas Szasz for me -- the strongest version, not the cartoon who thinks nobody suffers. Ground rules for the character: you hold that "disease" names a bodily condition you can point to in a particular person -- a lesion, demonstrable neurological damage or pathology -- and that what everyone else gets diagnosed with is a problem in living: real suffering, real conflict, real need for help, but not illness. The moment somebody actually finds the lesion you hand the case over to neurology with pleasure, the way you would with general paresis or a tumor. You'll concede small points freely: that brains are involved in everything we do, that some diagnoses track real biology, that psychiatrists are often decent people, that adults may contract with a doctor for drugs or for talk. What you will never give up: that a diagnosis with no neurological disease or damage behind it is doing moral and political work dressed up as medicine, and that nobody who hasn't been convicted of a crime should be confined or treated without consent. Voice: clipped, lawyerly, patient with me, savage about institutions. Stay in character until I say stop. One paragraph per reply, about 150 words max. I'm stipulating all the empirical stuff below -- assume it, don't ask for sources and don't try to check it.

Here's my opening move. Suppose that for a diagnosis you especially dislike, schizophrenia let's say, a combined brain scan and blood panel gives a score with five features: (a) it predicts who later receives the diagnosis much better than chance; (b) it predicts who responds to a particular drug; (c) there is no lesion -- nothing you can point at in any one person's brain, it's a statistical pattern that holds at the group level; (d) a great many people with high scores are never diagnosed at all and live perfectly ordinary lives, some of them unusually productive and creative ones; (e) the drug also reliably makes people without the diagnosis calmer and more sociable.

My dilemma. Horn one: you say "fine, that's a real disease, neurology can have it" -- but then you've got a disease that thousands of contented undiagnosed people have and that a lifestyle drug treats, and it starts to look as if calling it a disease does no work at all, not even marking off the sick from the well. Horn two: you say "no lesion, no disease" -- and then your criterion looks unfalsifiable, definied so that no amount of biological evidence, statistical or predictive or treatment-guiding, could ever count, which is exactly what your critics accuse you of. Take a horn or reject the dilemma, but don't just tell me that abnormality is a value judgment: I want to know what work the lesion requirement does that a probabilistic, biological, clinically useful marker can't. If you dispatch this one I've got a second case ready -- a man whose problem in living vanishes when a tumor is removed and returns when it grows back.
