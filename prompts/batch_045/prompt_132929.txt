So I'm a novelist and I've made the mistake of building a book around a retired refrigeration engineer who is obsessed with Maxwell's demon — the whole middle section is him trying to explain to his granddaughter why you can't get free work out of sorting fast molecules from slow ones, and it's load-bearing for the plot that he's right and that the reason has to do with information rather than with clumsiness. My writing group has kindly agreed to let me inflict this on them: eight novelists in a pub back room, fourty minutes, one of those whiteboards that never quite erases, and nobody in the room has done any physics since they were fifteen. One of them has definitely read a pop-science book and will tell me entropy means disorder within the first four minutes.

What I want from you isn't the explanation, it's the plan for the explanation. In order, the pieces, and for each one why it has to come before the next one — what depends on what. I have my own guesses about the sequence (something like: what the second law actually claims and why it's statistical rather than cosmic; then the demon; then shrink the whole thing down to one molecule and a partition so that a single yes-or-no is the only thing the demon ever knows; then the notebook the demon has to write in; then whatever it costs to wipe it) but I dont trust my sense of what has to be in place before the demon even looks like a threat.

The carrying example: tell me what the one scenario needs to have, and more usefully what it must not have. I think mine has to let you count the work you get out in the same units as the bit you wrote down, and must not quietly smuggle in a power supply or a hidden cost of looking — but I bet there's a third thing it has to lack and I can't see what it is.

Then the misconceptions, in the order they'll actually come up, and for each one the exact point in the sequence where I should kill it rather than later. The ones I can predict: entropy is mess; the fix is that the demon just isn't clever or quick enough; the fix is that looking costs energy — I have a feeling that one is the old answer rather than the current one, and I'm not sure whether it's simply wrong or a different fix that solves a different version of the problem; and the one where someone notices that I seem to be saving the second law by making the demon pay a fine at the very end, and concludes the whole thing is bookkeeping.

And the bit I most want your judgement on, because it decides whether my engineer sounds like he knows something: there's a worry I only half understand, that the principle doing the work here — that erasing a bit has a thermodynamic floor — is often argued for by running a demon argument in reverse, assuming the second law in order to prove the thing you then use to rescue the second law. If that's right, the exorcism might be circular, or might be an identity that shows the demon can't win without saying why. Should I put that in front of eight novelists at all, and if yes where in the fourty minutes, and what does it do to the punchline if I do?

After that: what I should flat-out refuse to explain at this level and say out loud that I'm refusing, and the single question I can ask at the end that tells me whether they've understood the thing or just enjoyed it — something they'd get wrong if they'd only got the gist.

Last thing, since I have to actually write the scene: my engineer wants to use the demon as a metaphor for an editor separating good sentences from bad ones. Tell me where that metaphor breaks, because I suspect that wherever it breaks is exactly where the real idea is.

No reading lists, please — I already have too many tabs open.
