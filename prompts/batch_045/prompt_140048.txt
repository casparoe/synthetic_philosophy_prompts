I am advising the governing board of a scholarly society that has to adopt, this year, one standing rule for choosing a single option from a field of three or more — conference site, journal editor, constitutional amendment, all decided by the same procedure. The board has no formal training but will answer concrete questions honestly, and the deliverable is a decision tree they can walk through in a single meeting: branch nodes phrased as questions, leaves phrased as rules they could actually write into the bylaws, plus a short note at each leaf about what has been given up to stand there.

Build the instrument from the logical space rather than from a list of names. Assume the reader knows only the informal content of the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem: that a rule cannot simultaneously be deterministic, single-valued, defined on all possible profiles of rankings, able to select at least three different outcomes, free of any voter whose top choice always wins, and such that no voter ever does better by reporting a ranking that is not their own. Start by fixing the questions that determine which of those properties is relaxed, then say which combinations of answers are inhabited, then place the actual rules in the inhabited cells. Six to eight branch questions and eight to ten leaves is the right size.

The questions must be answerable by someone who does not know the labels, and no question may simply ask whether the board is willing to appoint a dictator — that leaf has to be reached. Sort instead on things like these: whether the rule must always return one option or may return a shortlist, a lottery, or a set with a later tie-break; whether the options can be laid on a single published ordering before ballots are cast, and who publishes it; whether voters may record intensities, and whether some transferable numeraire is available that may be paid or burned; whether the board cares about voters who calculate well or about voters who cannot reason through what others will do, so that honesty should be optimal without contingent reasoning; whether a trusted source of randomness is available; and whether the board decides once or takes a sequence of related decisions over time, in which case a voter might misreport early in order to move the precedent for later rounds.

Two devices the board is actively arguing about must appear as leaves, and I want them diagnosed rather than filed. The first is an enforced domain restriction: the society already maintains a public policy scale on which every candidate has a recorded position, and the proposal is to void any ballot that is not single-peaked with respect to that scale and then run the rule on the surviving ballots. I want to know whether this reaches the usual escape route at all, given that the set of admissible profiles is then fixed by what people report rather than fixed in advance, and I want the leaf's awkward commitment to include the incentive to submit a deliberately non-single-peaked ballot in order to be struck from the count. The second is a formally dictatorial rule with a supermajoritarian override and an annual confidence vote on the chair; place it, and say plainly whether your tree can distinguish it from the bare dictatorial leaf or only from the point of view of someone modelling one decision in isolation.

Distinguish, throughout, escape routes the theorem itself licenses from sociological work-arounds it says nothing about — norms of sincerity, deliberation that makes preferences common knowledge, reputational audits after ballots are unsealed — and from leaves that work only by quietly redefining what counts as an outcome, for instance by adding a reversion option such as none of the above to the range. Mark each leaf accordingly.

Finally, give me a short table of the combinations of answers that nobody occupies, with one line each on whether the cell is empty because it is impossible, because it is coherent but unattractive, or because it has simply not been explored; and a collision log naming any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, with the reason. Where a leaf depends on a technical choice I may not have anticipated — for example, on how voters are assumed to rank sets of options rather than options — say so at that branch rather than resolving it silently. I do not want references, citations, or a reading list; the names of the positions and the reasoning that places them are all I need. Present the tree as a nested list, followed by a numbered walkthrough of a board that answers yes to the published-scale question and no to everything else, then the collision log and the unoccupied-cells table.
