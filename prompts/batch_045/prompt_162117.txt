I'm retired now — 30-odd years in experimental particle physics, most of it on calorimeter calibration and unfolding detector response, so I'm completely at home with likelihoods, priors and posteriors, response and migration matrices, nuisance parameters and marginalizing over them, KL divergence and maximum-entropy fits, toy Monte Carlo, coverage and closure tests, sufficient and ancillary statistics. Please don't explain Bayes' theorem to me. In the last couple of years I've started reading philosophy properly for the first time, and I've got stuck on a small case the formal epistemology people apparently argue about a lot: the Judy Benjamin problem. As I remember it (I may well garble the details): a soldier parachutes into a region her map divides into four quadrants — friendly vs enemy territory crossed with something like company-A and company-B sectors, with different prior probabilities — radios for help, and gets back a garbled message which is standardly read as telling her something like: the probability that she's in friendly territory given some condition about the sectors has gone to three quarters, or maybe that the odds on her being in the northern half are even. Something along those lines. The point being that this is evidence she isn't certain of, and it isnt a straight likelihood either — it's a constraint on her probabilities over a partition — and different update rules give different answers.

From what I can gather there are roughly three families of answer, and I want them translated, not explained:

1. probability kinematics — this is the Richard Jeffrey one, and please keep him separate in your head from Harold Jeffreys of the prior, I mix those two up constantly: hold fixed the conditional probabilities of everything given each cell of the partition the message is about, and just redistribute the weights across the cells to match what the message says.
2. imaging: don't reweight cells at all, move each possibility to the most similar possibility in which the message comes out true. I think this is the one associated with Lewis, though I may have that wrong.
3. something like: find a proposition you really are certain of afterwards — e.g. that the message said what it said — set up a likelihood for it, and conditionalize straight.

I've also read somewhere that the case was originally raised as a problem for people who identify updating with minimizing relative information (KL divergence) subject to whatever constraint the new evidence gives you, so treat that as a possible fourth framing if it's genuinely distinct.

What I want from you: restate all of them in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, and then give me the residue report — what translates cleanly, what you had to distort to make it fit, and what has no counterpart at all in how an experimentalist handles evidence. A compact table for the translation part would be fine, prose for the residue.

My own guesses, which I want you to check rather than humour: it looks to me like (1) is exactly what we do when we reweight Monte Carlo events to change bin normalizations while assuming the shape inside each bin doesn't migrate — so rigidity is a no-migration assumption, empirical and checkable, not a principle of rationality. And (2) looks like a hard-constraint projection, move to the nearest point in the feasible region, which in my world needs a metric or a loss function, and I have no idea what the philosophers' similarity-between-worlds is supposed to correspond to. But I want you to tell me where those identifications break.

Three things I especially want you to chew on:

(a) My understanding is that what makes this a problem for the KL people is that projecting onto one constraint and then the other doesn't give the same answer as the reverse order — correct me if that's wrong. That non-commutativity smells to me exactly like successive projections onto non-orthogonal subspaces. So: what happens if you just keep iterating the projections until they settle down, alternating-minimization style, the way you'd run I-projections or Dykstra? Does it converge, does it land on anything sensible, and is there a reason the philosophers don't just say that and go home? If it does converge but the fixed point depends on the order, or on which constraint you treat as the hard one, say so and explain why that matters.

(b) Suppose we change the story slightly: the operator's garbling depends on where Judy actually is — reception is worse deep in enemy territory — and she knows the garbling rates. Then as far as I can see she has an ordinary likelihood P(message | location), plain Bayes settles it, and there is nothing left to argue about: no kinematics, no imaging, no similarity orderings. Is that right? And if so, is the whole debate an artifact of being handed a distribution over the message-partition instead of a measurement model — i.e. is the philosophers' disagreement less substantive than it looks — or is there a real case where no likelihood is available even in principle?

(c) A variant I made up because I couldn't find it discussed anywhere: the message is about a partition that cuts across the one Judy cares about rather than refining it — the operator says: the probability that you are north of the river is 0.8, where north/south has nothing to do with friendly/enemy. Say which of the three rules even has anything to say here, and whether the gap between them gets bigger or smaller.

Finally, and this is the bit I actually care about most: in my working life an update rule is judged by whether it's calibrated over repeated trials — coverage, pull distributions, closure tests. Judy updates once. Is there any way of testing these rules, even in simulation, that would tell us which one is right, or is which update rule is rationally required here a question my whole vocabulary has no grip on? If it's the latter, tell me plainly whether that's a gap in the experimentalist's toolkit or a sign that the philosophers are asking something under-specified.
