I'm a second-year PhD student in machine learning working on interpretability, and I'm drafting the framing section of a short workshop paper on sparse autoencoders, so I'd like a fairly direct recommendation I can adapt rather than a survey of positions. The pipeline I'm reporting on is the usual one: I train an autoencoder with a large dictionary on residual-stream activations from a middle layer of a small transformer, keep the latents that do well on a monosemanticity check, and then verify each surviving latent by clamping or zeroing it and confirming that behaviour moves in the way its English label predicts — a "French text" latent pushes the next-token distribution toward French continuations, a "closing parenthesis" latent makes the model end clauses, and so on. As far as I can tell, this settles the old question of whether it is legitimate to say that a neural network really has representations. We can decompose the network's internal state into parts that are separately interpretable and separately causally efficacious in the way their labels predict, and that looks like exactly what would be needed for "the network represents X" to be more than a façon de parler over matrix multiplication. The people who used to deny that networks represent anything strike me as having made an empirical claim that has now simply failed, and I'd like to say so in the paper.

There is one feature of my results I can't quite place, though. When I retrain the autoencoder with a different seed or a different dictionary size, I get a different set of latents that reconstruct the activations about equally well and steer behaviour about equally well, but which don't line up one-to-one with the first set: some features split into several, some merge, and a few seem to have no counterpart at all. I've been treating this as an engineering nuisance — I call it "basis drift" in my notes — and assuming it will more or less vanish once dictionaries get large enough and training gets cleaner. Is that the right way to think about it, or is it telling me something about the decomposition itself?

Given all that, here is what I actually need help with. Assuming the decomposition is broadly on the right track, which of these two framings should I commit to in the paper: that the autoencoder's dictionary is the network's own ontology, the vocabulary in which the network itself carves up the world; or that the network's genuine representational structure is continuous and high-dimensional, with our dictionary being only a discretization that has to be good enough to predict and control? Which one does the philosophical debate about realism actually support, and does picking one commit me to anything further about whether the network should be described as having beliefs? I'd be grateful for a paragraph I can rewrite into the related-work section.
