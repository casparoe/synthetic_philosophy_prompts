I convene a fortnightly reading group — half philosophy graduate students, half public-policy economists — on welfare economics and decision theory, and in a few weeks we're doing a session on whether interpersonal comparisons of wellbeing are possible and what turns on the answer. The economists keep saying, in effect, that they do this every day so of course it's possible; the philosophers keep saying that this is exactly what needs to be shown; and I've come to think the standoff persists because several quite different disagreements are stacked on top of each other. I tried starting a previous session with a quiz I wrote myself and everyone landed in the same box, which I suspect is because my questions tipped their hand about which view they were testing for. So I'd like a better instrument: a ten-question sorting quiz that classifies people by what they're willing to say about cases, not by which label they'd pick for themselves.

What I need:

- Ten items, three options each, each a concrete case or a claim someone could agree or disagree with, phrased so that a thoughtful non-specialist could answer without knowing any view's name. No item may ask directly whether interpersonal comparisons are possible, whether utility is measurable, or whom the respondent agrees with.
- The cases have to do real diagnostic work, and I have opinions about which ones. Please include at least: (i) an adaptive-preference case, where someone's desires have shrunk to fit a restricted life; (ii) a case where two people describe their own states in incommensurable vocabularies, so the difficulty is translation rather than magnitude; (iii) a case where both parties agree on the ranking but disagree only about the *gap* — one says the difference between the first person's two states is about twice the difference between the second person's, the other says it is more like ten times — since I want to know whether respondents think the ratio questions have answers even when the ordinal ones do; (iv) a control item on intrapersonal comparison across a single person's life, including a variant with substantial memory loss or value change, so I can see whether people who are relaxed about comparing one person to herself at two times are also relaxed interpersonally; (v) a case where the parties themselves disagree about which currency of comparison is the right one, so the comparison has to be made against second-order disagreement; (vi) an aggregation case in the utility-monster family, but with the twist that the "monster" is a large dispersed group of people each gaining a little, summing to the same total — I want that to separate an objection to aggregation itself from an objection to concentration; and (vii) a variable-population case, where a life that is only barely worth living is added to an otherwise fixed population and respondents must say whether this improves things, worsens them, or is simply indeterminate.
- Then the structure: build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names. Give me the questions and the answer-patterns they permit first, and locate the actual positions in that space afterward. I'd like roughly six leaves, labeled by their commitments, and I'd like to be able to reach at least these: full cardinal interpersonally comparable utilitarianism (sum- and average-flavored variants); an ordinal-only view that still gets a social ordering out of unanimity plus some tie-breaking rule; a partial-comparability view on which some interpersonal claims are determinate and others are not; a weighted or prioritarian view on which comparisons are possible but not equally weighted; a skeptical view on which comparison fails and aggregation is therefore off the table; and a conventionalist view on which no scale is correct but we must pick one for decision-making purposes anyway. If you think a different partition is better, argue for it rather than forcing mine.
- A short appendix with three things: (a) the combinations of answers your questions allow that nobody really occupies, with a one-line diagnosis of each — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; (b) any pair of leaves your questions cannot pry apart, flagged rather than papered over; (c) the one question you think is least diagnostic, and why.
- Practical constraints: the quiz has to be usable live, by show of hands, so give me a quick scoring rule rather than a long rubric. No citations, no reading list, no suggested papers — the group reads together and I don't want names to carry any argumentative weight. Where a leaf is conventionally associated with a particular figure you may note the name in parentheses, but only where you're reasonably confident of it, and the label itself should be a description of the commitment.
- Finally, the thing I most want to know: how confident are you that this instrument sorts people where you say it does, and which single point — a question, a mapping from answers to leaves, or a leaf's most awkward commitment — are you least sure about?
