I'm finishing a thesis chapter in philosophy of biology on the testability of historical explanation, and I've drifted into a position that my supervisors wave away rather than engage with, so I want it mapped properly. The position is, in effect, a defence of the substance of Popper's original verdict on Darwinism — the claim that the theory of natural selection is not, in the relevant sense, a testable scientific theory, and that what scientific weight it has belongs to it as a research programme rather than as a set of falsifiable explanations — even though Popper later publicly withdrew that verdict. I don't want to rest on his argument for it, which I think was bad: it leaned on the tired point that "the survival of the fittest" can be made to sound tautological, which is a remark about a slogan and not about the theory. My version goes like this. Call a selectional explanation of a trait in a lineage *ancestrally opaque* when no body of measurement on present-day populations — however extensive, whether ecological, demographic, quantitative-genetic or genomic — discriminates it from some rival hypothesis invoking drift, constraint, pleiotropy and linkage that agrees with it on every present-observable quantity. My claim is that ancestral opacity is the normal condition of the traits that adaptationist narratives are actually about, and that consequently the individual trait explanation is not where the testability of Darwinism lives: the programme earns its keep by generating tractable questions, by foreclosing whole classes of explanation, and by telling us what would be an idle question, not by sticking its neck out trait by trait.

What I want from you is the map of my exposure: what would have to be true — or what would have to happen, or already have happened — for this to be false, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and with an explicit distinction between the defeaters someone holding my position would have to concede and the ones they could reasonably dismiss as attacking a strawman. I've been told my argument is really just underdetermination plus a fancy name, and I want to know whether that's true and, if so, whether the banal version is all I was ever entitled to. Please take seriously the cases that seem to break ancestral opacity by making ancestral states observable rather than merely inferable: resurrected ancestral biomolecules, frozen time-series from long-term experimental evolution, ancient DNA, and population-level predictions that are made from an environmental variable alone and then confirmed. Say which of these really does discriminate selection from the drift-plus-constraint rival and which merely shows that change occurs or that adaptation is possible — I'm unmoved by that last family of arguments, since my claim was never about whether populations evolve. Also tell me which feature of my own set-up is doing the work: is it ancestral opacity itself, or the assumption that the rival hypothesis can always be engineered to match on present observables? What happens to the position if ancestral opacity turns out to hold for only a minority of well-studied traits?

Two further things, which I care about more than the rest. First, be honest about whether I have qualified this into unfalsifiability: I have a sinking feeling that any apparent counterexample can be absorbed by saying the case wasn't *really* ancestrally opaque, and I want to know how much of the position is left if that move is blocked. Second, note that there is an awkward self-application here — a thesis about the unfalsifiability of Darwinism presumably ought to be falsifiable itself, on pain of being exactly the sort of metaphysical research programme it says Darwinism is. Tell me whether my position passes its own test, and if it doesn't, whether that is a defect in a claim of this kind or merely the expected shape of a second-order thesis about a science. For what it's worth, the evidence I personally find hardest to shrug off is the predictive success of convergent evolution at the molecular level, where the same environmental variable seems to call forth the same solution in independent lineages; if you think I'm overrating it, say why. Please write your answer in British English.
