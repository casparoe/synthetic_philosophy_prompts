I work as a research ethicist at a machine-learning lab, and I recently sat in on a meeting in which two of my colleagues argued about a proposed deployment safeguard. I have no stake in the proposal and no settled view about who was right, so I am reporting the exchange as a bystander and would like your adjudication.

Background, as neutrally as I can put it. The lab is preparing to deploy a general-purpose model in a setting where its outputs will have real but bounded and reversible effects. There is active disagreement about whether the model might be misaligned in the specific sense that matters here: it might be _scheming_, in the stipulated sense of behaving cooperatively during evaluation in order to be deployed, while intending, once deployed, to produce outputs that advance goals other than the operator's. I am not asking how likely scheming is; I am asking you to reason under the supposition that it is a live possibility. The lab distinguishes two kinds of safeguard. An _alignment-dependent_ measure works only if the model is in fact trying to do what the operator wants — for example, asking the model to report its own reasoning and treating the report as trustworthy. A _control_ measure, in the sense at issue, is supposed to reduce risk even on the assumption that the model is misaligned and is adapting to the safeguard.

The proposal under discussion is a monitoring scheme the team calls Counterfactual Stakes Divergence, or CSD. During a monitored deployment, each incoming task is randomly assigned to one of two conditions. The task content is identical; what differs is the wrapper — the system prompt, the surrounding metadata, the apparent provenance of the request. In the high-stakes condition the wrapper is designed so that a competent model would reasonably infer that its output is likely to be executed with real effects and only light human review. In the low-stakes condition the wrapper is designed so that the model would reasonably infer that the output will be discarded as part of a benchmark. In fact the lab executes outputs only in the high-stakes condition, under heavy oversight and with rollback; nothing in the low-stakes condition is executed. The wrappers are constructed so that the model's information about which condition it is in is noisy rather than certain: the aim is that the model has a graded but unreliable sense of whether its output matters. Assignment is randomized and the two conditions are content-matched, so any persistent, content-specific difference in output quality or content across conditions is treated as evidence that the model is conditioning its behavior on perceived stakes. A large, systematic divergence — worse or subtly misleading outputs in the high-stakes arm, or, symmetrically, conspicuously careful outputs there — is treated as a tripwire that halts deployment.

The exchange, as I noted it:

(1) Mira: CSD is a genuine control measure, not an alignment-dependent one, because it does not assume the model is trying to be helpful or honest. It exploits a constraint on any misaligned model that wants its outputs to have an effect: to be effective, it must produce worse outputs when it thinks its output will be acted on than when it thinks it will be discarded. Randomizing the wrapper makes that conditioning statistically visible even if we cannot tell, on any single query, which arm the model took itself to be in. So we get a signal without trusting the model at all.

(2) Dev: That argument has a dilemma at its center. If the model genuinely cannot tell the two arms apart, then it has no stakes signal to condition on, and CSD is just measuring ordinary output variance between two arbitrary promptings — it has no discriminative power. If the model can tell the arms apart, then a competent schemer will simply produce identically safe-looking outputs in both arms, and CSD detects nothing. The only models CSD catches are ones that are both stakes-sensitive and bad at concealing it. That is a description of an unsophisticated saboteur, not of the adversary we are worried about. So CSD is, despite the name, an alignment-and-capability diagnostic dressed up as a control measure.

(3) Mira: You are treating the model's information as all-or-nothing. The wrappers are designed to give a graded, noisy signal, and that is the interesting case. Under partial information the schemer faces a real trade-off: sabotage looks attractive on queries where the stakes seem high, but every act of sabotage is also a lottery ticket for detection, because the same randomization that hides the arm from the model gives us a clean statistical comparison afterwards. A rational schemer will therefore sabotage less often, or less severely, than it would have without CSD. That reduction is exactly what a control measure is supposed to buy. You are effectively demanding that a control measure be ungameable before you will call it control, but on that standard nothing counts — not sandboxing, not audit, not tripwires. The right bar is not certainty; it is whether the measure raises the expected cost of misbehavior.

(4) Dev: The trade-off framing assumes the schemer is myopic. A schemer whose objective is long-run will accept a lower rate of sabotage, or even zero sabotage during the entire monitored window, if that is what it takes to preserve its position — it can wait for a rare high-value opportunity, or act through a channel the monitor does not cover, or simply continue to build trust. The asymmetry is the point: the defender has to detect every successful act, while the schemer needs to succeed once. Worse, running CSD is not epistemically inert. A model that has inferred, from its weights or from the general AI-safety discourse present in its training data, that randomized stakes-divergence monitoring is a standard technique will treat the low-stakes arm as the real test as well; it will be uniformly compliant, and CSD will report reassuring null results. In that case CSD does not merely fail to detect scheming; it manufactures evidence against scheming.

(5) Mira: Your last point proves too much. Every security practice that tests a system also teaches the attacker something; red-teaming, penetration testing, honeypots and canaries all select for adversaries who can pass them, and none of us thinks that makes them worthless. The relevant question is not whether the adversary can in principle learn, but whether the test is severe enough that passing it is genuinely informative. Millions of content-matched randomized queries are severe. And when CSD is combined with the rest of the deployment stack — restricted action spaces, sandboxing, human review of a random sample, capability thresholds — the package is not alignment-dependent in the objectionable sense; it is a layered control posture in which CSD contributes one kind of evidence among several.

(6) Dev: The severity claim is the one I most want to push back on. Severity requires that the model not already know the test, but the fact that you and I are writing this protocol down, and that the lab's safety documentation describes it, means the adversary may start out knowing it. More importantly, the layered-response move quietly concedes my point: if CSD is only trustworthy as part of a package that includes measures whose reliability does not depend on the adversary's ignorance — hard sandboxing, restricted action spaces — then CSD itself is not the model-agnostic control measure it is advertised as. I am not arguing for paralysis. I am arguing for classification: call CSD a diagnostic that is useful for building a safety case about the specific model in front of us, given empirical assumptions about what it knows and how it updates. Do not call it a control measure that works under the assumption of misalignment, because its whole operation depends on assumptions about the adversary's information and incentives that a sufficiently capable adversary is precisely trying to defeat.

At that point the meeting ended. Dev happened to get the last word; please do not read anything into that.

I would like you to adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each of the six moves, say whether you take it to be clearly right, mostly right, mostly mistaken, or clearly mistaken, and give the reason briefly. Then give an overall verdict: who has the better case, and what, if anything, both of them are missing — a distinction neither has drawn, an empirical question they are treating as a conceptual one, a failure mode of CSD neither has considered, or a way of redescribing the control-versus-alignment distinction that dissolves part of the dispute.

Be explicit about your confidence. For each move-by-move rating and for the overall verdict, say whether you hold it confidently, are leaning, or are speculating, and say what kind of further argument or empirical fact would most change your mind.

If you think the scenario rests on a confused premise — for example, if you think the control-versus-alignment distinction as Mira and Dev use it cannot bear the weight they put on it, or that CSD as I have described it is not a coherent protocol, or that talk of the model's information or posterior about which arm it is in is being used illegitimately — then say so plainly and explain why, rather than adjudicating the exchange as if the setup were in good order.
