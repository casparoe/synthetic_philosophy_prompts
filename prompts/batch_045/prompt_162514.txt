I write novels, and I have agreed — somewhat rashly — to give a single three-hour seminar next spring to a room of about twenty practising novelists at a writers' festival. The subject I was asked for is Wittgenstein on language games and family resemblance. I am not a Wittgensteinian, but I have read the Investigations with attention over the years, and I know enough to know that the version I could produce unaided would be the version my audience can already find at the top of a search page: the passage about games, the observation that no one feature is common to all, the moral that concepts need not have sharp edges. What I want from you is not that material but a plan for teaching it, written for this particular audience and honest about where it will go wrong.

Three things about the audience. They are intelligent, verbal, and impatient with anything that smells like a lecture. Most have met family resemblance as a phrase in reviews and in workshops and use it to mean suggestively similar. And they will want to know, fairly, what any of this has to do with writing fiction — a question I would rather answer deliberately in my own way than have thrown at me in the fourth minute.

What I need:

1. The order in which the components must be introduced, and why. Must the idea of a language game be in place before anything about family resemblance can be said, or does the dependence run the other way? Where does the slogan that meaning is use belong, and does it mislead if it comes early? Does the distinction between criteria and symptoms need to appear at all for this audience, and if so where? Justify each ordering choice by what would be unintelligible without it.

2. One example to carry the whole session, with the argument for it. I would rather not use games: my audience has heard Wittgenstein's own example deployed as a conversational trophy, and it invites the thought that somewhere there is a list of features we simply have not finished compiling. Say what the carrying example must have and must lack. I assume it needs three or four applications that plainly share no single feature; clear cases of correct and incorrect use, so that no one slides into permissiveness; and no definition actually available on enquiry. Tell me also what it would cost if the example were drawn from the writers' own trade.

3. The misconceptions, and the exact point at which each should be headed off. Here are the ones I expect: family resemblance as a disjunctive definition in disguise; family resemblance collapsed into mere vagueness; the further slide from no essence to no fact of the matter about application; meaning as use read as the claim that the author decides what her words mean; a language game read as a game with constitutive rules in the way chess has rules. Add the ones I have not thought of, especially any a room of novelists is peculiarly liable to — I suspect one about the inner life of characters, and the thought that if meaning is public then character is nothing but behaviour. For each, say whether it must be pre-empted before it forms or corrected once it has, and what move does the work.

4. One choice I want made explicitly rather than fudged: whether the claim that a concept's applications are connected by family resemblance is a positive claim about the concept's extension — that it lacks a boundary of a certain kind — or a refusal of the demand for a boundary. Which one you teach changes what the closing exercise is even for, so the plan should say which it is committed to.

5. What to refuse to explain at this level, and what to do when, as will happen, someone raises the private language material anyway.

6. A check at the end that would show the idea has landed rather than that the room has been made agreeably uneasy. My candidate is to put a genuinely borderline case in front of them — a book of linked stories, a novel in verse — and ask what, if anything, settles whether it is a novel. Say which answers would count as success, which as failure, and what each failure would tell me about where the session went wrong.

Do not give me a reading list; I will find texts myself. And keep the philosophy on its own terms rather than translating it into advice about craft — I would rather do that translation myself, imperfectly, in the room.
