Hi — I run the escalation queue for a studio that operates a persistent online world: player-built housing, guilds with their own territories, tradeable rare items, player-run events, the works. About forty or fifty tickets a week come past first-line support to me and two juniors, roughly ten minutes each, and anything involving a permanent ban or any kind of compensation has to be signed off by our legal lead and finance. The same argument breaks out every single week. Half the team says it's only pixels so nobody was harmed; the other half says that house had four hundred hours in it so of course it's a harm; and we end up deciding by gut feel and then writing a reply to the player afterwards that tries to sound like we had a reason.

So: I want a decision procedure my juniors can actually run, in order, in ten minutes, with no philosophy training. For each step, what counts as a sufficient answer; where the procedure stops with a no; and where it has to hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts, and it should say so explicitly rather than pretending to be mechanical.

I don't want a survey of positions, but I do want the distinctions that are actually doing the work to survive the simplification. The ones I think we need: whether a virtual thing is real in the sense of having causal effects under the game's rules versus real in the sense of mattering to someone; whether a harm requires a loss or whether being seriously set back in something you were building is enough; whether agreeing to play by rules that permit losing things turns a harm into a fair loss — that one is the crux for us and I want you to take a definite position on it, not lay out both sides; whether a harm is undone by restoring an item byte-identically when what's really gone is its history; and whether wrecking something shared can harm people who only ever watched it. There's also a wrinkle I want handled: our engine can roll back almost anything, and I keep suspecting the availability of a technical fix is quietly changing how we classify the harm rather than just what we do about it.

Two crude tests we already half-use, and I want you to tell me to keep, fix or bin each: would anyone pay real money for it, and would someone who has never played understand why the player is upset. My sense is both are broken but in different directions.

Then run the procedure on three real shapes of ticket. One: an account taken over by social engineering, a rare weapon and about nine hundred hours of progression deleted; we can restore the items but not the record of what was done with them. Two: a guild holds a memorial in-game for a member who died, a rival group crashes it, kills the characters, loots the corpses and streams it with taunts; everything was recoverable and we gave it all back inside a day, no lasting loss, and the reporting player wants permanent bans. Three: our own economy patch made a rare crafting line worthless overnight and the players who'd spent months on it want compensation. I would really rather the procedure didn't come out against the company on that last one, but if it does, tell me plainly. Include at least one case where the answer is one I won't like, and say what the procedure can't do — the cases it will get wrong or that it shouldn't be used for at all.

Commit to something, please: take a line on whether these are real harms and where exactly the cut-off falls, give your reasons, and then tell me which step of your own procedure you're least confident about and what would shift you. And write it as continuous prose, like an email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
