hey — ive been going round in circles on this one and I want someone to actually do the mapping carefully instead of just having an opinion about it.

The episode: the My Lai killings in March 1968, and then the Calley trial in 1971, and then the aftermath, where a lot of the country decided he was either a murderer or a scapegoat and his sentence got commuted and he served a few years under house arrest. Take the following as given, I don't want to argue about contested details: several hundred unarmed villagers, mostly old men, women and children, were killed over a few hours in and around the hamlet; the company took no fire that morning, though it had been losing men to mines and snipers for weeks before; the men were told to expect an enemy unit in the area and there wasn't one; Calley was the only person convicted; one helicopter crew flying overhead realised what was happening, put their aircraft down between the soldiers and a group of villagers and got some of them out. Also take as given the counterinsurgency context: the Viet Cong did fight among the civilian population, often without uniform or with only part-time status, and a large part of what those soldiers were angry and frightened about was that they couldnt tell who the enemy was.

The theory: the moral equality of combatants. The thesis that soldiers on either side have the same permission to kill and the same liability to be killed, not because their causes are equally just but because their status is symmetric; that what licenses the killing is that theyre dangerous, not that theyre in the wrong; and that this is meant to be one piece with noncombatant immunity rather than a separate doctrine. Ive been reading around Walzer's version of it, roughly, and i'm paraphrasing from memory, so please don't turn this into citations or a reading list — i don't want either.

What I want:

1. The mapping, done properly. Who are the "players" as far as the thesis is concerned, what were the options, and what would have counted as evidence that a given person in that hamlet was killable? My own read is that the thesis is basically empty at My Lai itself, because there were no combatants there at all, so there was nobody for anybody to be equal to — and that the real test case is the nearby one: the hamlet where some of the men genuinely are part-time guerrillas who farm by day. Do the analysis for that case as much as for the actual one.

2. The wrinkle i keep coming back to. The permission to kill looks like it's indexed to an empirical condition — that combatants are identifiable as combatants, uniforms or open carry or a front or whatever — and that condition is both a matter of degree and largely under the control of whichever side chooses to blur it. So the enemy can partly switch off your permission just by hiding. Is that genuinely built into the thesis, or am i smuggling it in? And if it is built in, it looks like there are only three things to say: the permission holds until identifiability drops below some threshold and then it disappears; or it scales, so you may kill only when the expected value is positive given how often you're wrong; or the loss doesn't shrink your permission at all but gets transferred — the side that blurred the line forfeits protection for the people it hides among. Which of those does the thesis actually support, and is the third one just the human-shield argument smuggled in at the level of a whole population?

3. Take apart a fix ive been toying with. Call it an identification budget: a soldier's status-based permission to kill is only good for as many killings as the prevailing identification conditions can actually underwrite, and in a counterinsurgency the budget gets quietly overspent, and the overspend is absorbed by exactly the people least likely to be combatants. Does that do any work, or does it collapse? My worry is that the moment the permission depends on anything like this you've let just cause back in through the side door, and then the whole point of saying the equality is about status and not about the justice of the war is gone.

4. What the case does to the theory, going the other direction. Does My Lai just show that the thesis was never written for counterinsurgency and goes silent there? Does the scapegoat reaction — the feeling many people had that it was unfair for one junior officer to carry it when plenty of men did the same thing — actually fall out of the thesis plus the fact that the permission is bounded? Does the thesis have anything at all to say about the psychology of soldiers who've been taking losses from an enemy they can't see, or is that simply outside what it's trying to do?

Be disciplined about it: where the thesis has nothing to say, say it has nothing to say rather than squeezing an answer out, and please don't give me a summary of the war or a pile of thinks i should go read. A thousand words or so.
