In a campus debate about removing a statue of a nineteenth-century imperialist, a speaker invoked Nietzsche's early essay on history and said that 'history has to serve life, not the other way around,' and then treated that as settling the question: because the statue only encourages pious veneration of a dead past, it must come down. I think something is being smuggled in. Nietzsche distinguishes three kinds of history—monumental, antiquarian, and critical—and each is supposed to serve or endanger 'life' in a different way. Using that distinction, lay out as a menu the precise claims the slogan could be making, from the trivial to the radical. At minimum, separate: (1) the banal claim that history or commemoration should have some human point; (2) the Nietzschean claim about cultural or psychological health, that a person or people need usable models, a rooted identity, and also the power to forget; (3) the critical-history reading, on which life is served by putting the past on trial and destroying what oppresses it; and (4) a strong consequentialist reading, on which any monument that does not directly increase present flourishing should be removed. For each, say what 'life' would have to mean (mere survival, cultural health, greatness, or the flourishing of a particular group), whether the claim is plausible, whether Nietzsche would accept it, and whether it actually gets the speaker his conclusion. Which reading is he really relying on, and is that reading defensible as an interpretation of the essay?
