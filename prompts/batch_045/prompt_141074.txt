I'm in a small reading group on classical Indian epistemology — we have just worked through the pramāṇa chapters — and I want to test the framework on a real case rather than keep discussing it in the abstract. Please take the Nyāya set of four, if you are happy with that (perception, pratyakṣa; inference, anumāna; comparison, upamāna; testimony, śabda), together with the inference machinery that goes with it: the subject or locus (pakṣa), the property to be established (sādhya), the reason (hetu), the pervasion (vyāpti) that is supposed to tie the reason to what is to be established, its positive and negative formulations (anvaya, vyatireka), the ways a reason goes wrong, and in particular its being counterbalanced by an equally good reason pointing the other way (satpratipakṣa) and its being vitiated by an adventitious condition (upādhi) that makes the concomitance hold only conditionally; plus tarka, the suppositional or reductio-style reasoning that Nyāya declines to rank as a pramāṇa in its own right but relies on to firm up a pervasion suggested by repeated observation (bhūyodarśana). Say which version you are using if you would rather work with another school's, but do not stall on that question.

The case is John Snow and cholera in London in the 1850s. The facts I stipulate, so that we need not argue about them: since 1849 Snow had argued that cholera is swallowed and multiplies in the gut and is spread through water, against the prevailing view that it is carried by foul air, which was the view of most medical opinion and of the official inquiries; in the 1854 Soho outbreak he mapped the cases around the Broad Street pump and persuaded the authorities to remove the handle, the famous gesture, though the epidemic was probably already past its peak by then; his strongest evidence was what he called the grand experiment, a comparison of households in south London supplied by two different water companies serving much the same streets, one drawing from the sewage-polluted tidal Thames and one from a cleaner stretch upriver, whose customers' cholera mortality differed by a large factor, with the wrinkle that from the outside he often could not tell which company supplied a given house; his view was not accepted in his lifetime, he was eminent as an anaesthetist rather than as an authority on epidemics, and the organism was convincingly identified only in the 1880s, long after his death. What I do not want is a paragraph saying that Snow used inference while his opponents relied on testimony. I want the mapping done properly. Reconstruct Snow's central claim as an inference: what exactly is the pakṣa, the sādhya, the hetu, and what is the vyāpti supposed to be? How would a Nyāya say he established that pervasion — by repeated observation, by tarka, or by neither? My own candidate reading, which I want you to take up and either defend or break, is that the grand experiment is not evidence for the pervasion at all but a device for killing a particular upādhi: the miasmatists could say that what travels together with foul water is foul air, low elevation, crowding and poverty, so that Snow's reason is connected to his conclusion only conditionally, and the point of the two-company comparison is to hold those things roughly fixed and let the water vary. If that is right, then a single well-chosen contrast has done the work that the framework expects many co-observations to do, and I want to know whether Nyāya has any resources for certifying a contrastive, quasi-experimental design, or whether this is the first place the account strains. And tell me where the disagreement actually sits: is the miasma theory best modelled as satpratipakṣa, a rival probans in equipoise with Snow's, or as śabda, the word of the relevant reliable experts (āptas) against one man's inference, or as both at once — and if both, does the framework say which one loses? Conclude the forward direction by saying what Snow was and was not entitled to, in the framework's terms: genuine cognition (pramā), mere removal of doubt, or neither.

Then the direction I actually care about: whether this episode is a vindication of the pramāṇa framework, an embarrassment to it, or a case it never anticipated. Three things push me toward embarrassment, and I would like you to take them in turn. First, the framework looks designed to grade a finished cognition as knowledge or not, and to be close to silent about what to do while the pramāṇas conflict; Snow acted in exactly the condition that satpratipakṣa describes, and he was right to, so is there Nyāya machinery for warranted action under doubt (saṃśaya), or must that be imported from outside the theory? Second, the evidence that decided the matter was aggregate and statistical, a difference in death rates between two populations, and I do not see how a framework whose stock example is smoke on a hill grades that: the closest thing it has, the upādhi, is recognisably a confounder notion but seems to presuppose that one can inspect instances, whereas what Snow had was rates whose instances he could not individually resolve. Third, there is the retroactive question: the agent became visible decades later and the concomitance stopped being inferential. Does that turn Snow's cognition into pramā, does it make his pervasion genuine after the fact, or is the framework's account of the episode indifferent to how it eventually came out — and if it is indifferent, is that a feature or a cost? Please argue rather than catalogue, and do not pad the answer with citations or further reading; I want the analysis.
