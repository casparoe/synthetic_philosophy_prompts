Hey — I'm a third-year and I'm running one session of my philosophy of mind / cog sci cross-listed seminar (about 16 people, mostly phil majors with maybe four CS kids who will be insufferable, one 75-minute slot, 9am, which is its own problem). The session is on that "competence without comprehension" cluster of ideas — the strange inversion thing where all the designed-looking stuff in biology gets explained without a designer ever having had the design in mind, and the parallel Turing-style move where apparently intelligent performance gets explained without anyone understanding anything. We did the reading (the Dennett-ish framing of it, but I really don't care about the exegesis) and it went exactly as you'd expect: everyone nodded, nobody felt anything. I got into an argument with my flatmate afterwards — she does ML and thinks the whole move is a rhetorical trick that only works because "comprehension" is doing undefined work — and I decided I'd rather the group run into the thing themselves than read another paper about it. So: design me the activity.

What I want, concretely: rules, materials a TA can prep in half an hour, a timeline with rough minute marks, and real choices or small stakes for people (points or sweets, no money). No lecture up front, and I'd rather not say the phrase "competence without comprehension" until the last ten minutes or so. Two things I care about most. First, the inversion should happen to them rather than be asserted by me: they should start out naturally giving the mind-first explanation of whatever they're doing — someone must have designed this, I must know what rule I'm following, there has to be a reason I can state — and then hit something that makes the mind-first order look like the wrong direction of explanation. Ideally their own first-person reports get collected as data and then visibly fail to track what they can actually do, so the mismatch is theirs and not something I announce. Second, I want both halves in there if you can manage it — the "I can do it but I can't say what I know" half, and the "this looks designed and was produced by a dumb iterated process" half — and tell me which of the two is easier to stage in one room in one session, and why. Please don't just hand me the Chinese Room again, or a straight Wason 2-4-6 demo; everyone has seen those and they read as philosophy-of-mind theatre. If you want to use something in that family, twist it so the trick isn't visible from the first two minutes.

Then the debrief: map specific moments in the activity onto specific parts of the idea, minute-by-minute-ish, and note where the mapping is loose. Be frank about what a 75-minute game cannot reproduce and that I'll have to supply in so many words — my guess is that it can show people performing well while being unable to report what they know, but can't really show that there's no comprehension anywhere in the system, and that the Darwinian half is doing something over generational time that an afternoon can only gesture at. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: especially the person who says "I do know the rule, I just can't put it into words", and the CS students who spot the generating process immediately and then sit there smug while everyone else is still confused. I'd like both of those handled inside the design somewhere, not just shrugged at in the debrief.
