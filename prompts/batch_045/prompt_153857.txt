So I'm writing a short piece arguing that Hofstadter-style superrationality is best seen as the rho = 1 special case of a more general how-much-does-my-choice-predict-yours view, and I want a case that shows the general view is not just a blend of the two familiar endpoints. Build me one.

Setup: eight to ten people facing the same decision at the same time with the same information, no private tips, and three genuine options: go all in on some joint project, sit it out, and contribute something moderate but real. Ground it somewhere ordinary, a workplace or a tenants association or a group of colleagues, with stakes a normal person cares about (credit, money, hassle). No clones, no telepathy, no predictors, no perfect copies: the imperfect correlation between what you pick and what each of them picks has to come from something recognizable, like shared training or shared professional norms or everyone having read the same argument before deciding.

Specs: ordinary game-theoretic reasoning, understood as best response to the unique symmetric equilibrium, or equivalently expected-utility maximization with your own choice telling you nothing about theirs, says sit it out. Full superrationality, in the sense of picking the act you would want every similar reasoner to pick on the grounds that they will all land on it, says all in. And partial superrationality at some middling correlation, somewhere between 0.3 and 0.6, says contribute moderately, with a margin that is not knife-edge. The middle option has to be a distinct action with its own payoff structure, not a coin flip between the other two, I know a mixture cannot beat the best pure act in a linear model like this one, that is exactly why it would be cheating.

Then show that it works: give the payoffs, work out the correlation thresholds where the verdict flips, and say which structural feature of the case is doing the work. Also tell me what happens if you delete the middle option, does the whole thing collapse into an ordinary threshold prisoners dilemma where the answer flips once at some single cutoff, and how a defender of whichever view comes out worst would attack the case. My guess is the superrationalist says partial correlation is not their theory's subject at all and that the argument only ever bound people who had fully taken it in, and I want to know whether that dodge definately survives or whether it costs them something.
