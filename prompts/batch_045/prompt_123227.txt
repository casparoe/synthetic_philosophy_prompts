I've recently been handed responsibility for defending my employer's knowledge-management budget, and I keep losing the same argument with our finance team: if the system already hands us correct answers, why are we paying a premium for the layer that records provenance, checks whether an answer was reached for the right reasons, and flags the ones that came out right by luck? I've since realized this is the Meno problem in a lanyard, and I'd like to turn it into a piece.

Write a ~600-word satirical business case: a vendor's pitch for the premium tier of an epistemic product line, sold to a company that already owns true belief. Structure it as a short memo with a headline claim, three or four numbered value propositions, and a brief FAQ. The voice is upbeat management-consultant prose; the piece never winks at the reader and never names a philosopher.

The joke is that the value propositions fail in recognizable philosophical ways. Each should be a real answer to the value question — higher reliability across a range of cases, credit attributable to the knower rather than to circumstance, robustness of a true belief across nearby counterfactual situations, anti-luck conditions — and each should visibly collapse into either no value beyond the accuracy the client already has, or a mere relabelling of true belief. Work in at least one item that passed quality assurance for the wrong reason. Let the FAQ defend the premium by refusing the question instead: knowledge is not a composite of belief plus extras, so there is nothing here to cost-justify. Close with a straight-faced recommendation to buy the upgrade anyway.
