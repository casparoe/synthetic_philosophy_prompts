I host a small podcast for my school's philosophy club — about forty minutes per episode, one guest, an audience of classmates and two teachers who keep me honest — and next week's guest is a philosopher who defends what I've been calling the transparency explanation of Moore's paradox. As I understand his version of it: because belief is constitutively aimed at the truth, a subject cannot, in the first person and present tense, treat the question whether p as settled by anything other than whether p, so what needs explaining about "it's raining but I don't believe that it is" is not that the sentence is self-contradictory — he concedes it can be true, and perhaps even known, in cases of self-deception — but that nobody can coherently judge or believe it. My worry is that I'll repeat the mistake of our last episode, where I let the guest restate his own view for half an hour, because he has a very smooth way out of awkward cases: whenever I produce a case where a Moorean sentence sounds perfectly fine, he says the subject in that case isn't really in the state of full belief at all — it's avowal, or alief, or merely standing and dispositional belief — or, failing that, that the subject simply isn't fully rational.

So I'd like eight to ten questions, in the order I should ask them, that make that escape route progressively harder to use, each one paired with the follow-up I should have ready given the answer he is most likely to give, plus a short bracketed note after each pair on why the follow-up bites rather than letting him re-describe the case. Three things in particular need a question each. First, whether the absurdity belongs to the act of asserting or to the state of believing: "either it's raining or I don't believe that it is" and "if it's raining, then I don't believe that it is" seem to embed without any absurdity at all, and there is something odd about being asked to suppose, in the course of an argument, that p and that I don't believe p — so I want him pinned down on what exactly is supposed to be impossible, and why embedding dissolves it. Second, the asymmetries: "it isn't raining but I believe that it is" strikes me as much less strange, closer to confessing a mistake, than "it's raining but I don't believe that it is"; "Jones believes that it's raining but it isn't" and "it was raining and I didn't believe that it was" are both completely fine; and swapping the attitude changes things — "it's raining but I don't want it to be" and "it's raining but I don't know that it is" are fine, while "I'm going to do it but I don't intend to" feels strange but not strange in the same way. I want to know which of these his account predicts in advance and which it has to bolt on. Third, the first-person present-tense restriction itself, and whether a genuine explanation should make it fall out of the account rather than be stipulated.

The cases I want saved for the back half, once he has committed himself, are these: a mourner who says "she's dead but I don't believe it," which sounds natural rather than incoherent; a subject who says "Jones is plainly the strongest candidate but I don't believe that she is — I can see my own reaction tracking something irrelevant," which sounds not merely acceptable but epistemically admirable; and, hardest, a subject whose introspection has been scrambled but who has a reliable neural readout of their own confidence and who says, on that basis, "the deck is stacked but I don't believe that it is" — a case where the "that's not full belief" reply seems to have nothing to grip, since the subject has better evidence about their own belief than introspection could ever supply. Please close with one question about what would count as evidence against the view: what we would have to observe about speakers, or about what people can and cannot bring themselves to judge, that would make him abandon it. Assume a bright audience that has seen propositional logic but no philosophy of mind, and please flag anywhere you think I've misdescribed his position — I'd rather not spend the first ten minutes being corrected on air.
