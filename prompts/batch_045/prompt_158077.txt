I've been reading the rationalist end of the internet for the better part of two decades, and there is one move I have watched happen hundreds of times: someone notices that a practice, slogan, or technique became more common, says something about selection on memes, and then cannot say what the replicating unit was, what its fitness depended on, or what observation would have made them stop saying it. I now teach an upper-level seminar on Darwinian theories of culture, and I want the final exam to make that move expensive.

The course is a third/fourth-year seminar, cross-listed between philosophy of biology and cognitive science; the assessment is a take-home final with a single essay question, nominally 90 minutes, target length 1,000 words. I need three things from you: the question itself, a model answer of about 900 words, and a marking scheme out of 20.

Here is the crux I want the question to turn on. I don't want students to be able to pass by reciting the gene/meme analogy, or by reciting the standard objection that cultural transmission is reconstruction rather than replication and therefore not Darwinian; both of those are moves a well-read student can execute without thinking. I want them to have to say what would discriminate selection on heritable cultural variation from population-level convergence produced by learners independently re-deriving the same solution, and then to confront the wrinkle that these are not cleanly opposed: if novices reliably converge on a form because of biases in how they reconstruct what they see, those biases may themselves be the product of selection operating somewhere else, on some other timescale. That raises the question of whether the convergence explanation is an alternative to a Darwinian explanation at all, or just a Darwinian explanation with the selection relocated.

I also want the question to press on fidelity. Cumulative adaptation is usually said to require that improvements be retained with enough fidelity to build on; but a population that reliably re-converges on an endpoint from many different starting points can ratchet without any high-fidelity copying at all. Students should have to say whether reconvergence can substitute for replication fidelity, and what we would have to measure to tell.

Here is the empirical vignette I've been drafting, which you should feel free to tighten or replace:

Fieldworkers document sixty distinct procedures for making waterproof containers from the same locally available materials, across thirty neighbouring communities. Three findings. (1) Over the sampling window, the population frequency of procedures shifts toward ones that score better on a standardised leak test. (2) Experimenters recruit novices who have never seen the corpus, tell them only the goal and hand them the same raw materials, allow each a fixed number of attempts in isolation, and forbid contact with one another and with any informant; the same handful of procedures recurs across novices, and they are the ones that were winning in the field. (3) There is no lineage data: nothing in the record reveals who learned from whom, so nobody can reconstruct the transmission tree.

Two constraints on what you write. First, the question and the model answer must not depend on names, dates, or knowledge of the literature: I want something a student could answer from first principles plus what was said in seminar, and that I could reuse next year without the reading list changing. Second, the marking scheme should be built around five or six must-hit points with marks attached, it should name explicitly what an A answer contains that a B answer does not, and it should give a one-line note per point on how students typically get it wrong. Please also say what should cost marks: treating mere spread as evidence of selection; treating convergence as automatically fatal to a selection story; treating the re-derivation result as an explanation rather than as a description that itself needs explaining; sliding between the level at which selection acts and the mechanism by which variants are transmitted.

I would rather the question admit a sharply wrong answer than be vague enough that every response earns half credit.
