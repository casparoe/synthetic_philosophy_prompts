I'm a junior on my school's debate team, and we're preparing for a tournament on the resolution that technological artifacts can be political in their own right, and not merely in how they were designed or in what people later do with them. My coach keeps telling me that I win the easy rounds — against people who say technology is neutral and only people have politics — and lose the hard ones, so I want to rehearse against somebody who actually holds the position well. Please play a philosopher of technology defending the strong claim I associate with Langdon Winner's 1980 essay asking whether artifacts have politics: for at least some artifacts — overpasses built to a clearance that buses cannot pass under, a harvesting machine whose efficiency pays off only on farms above a certain size, an open-plan office that makes being watched cheap and being unobserved expensive — the distribution of power they bring about is a property of the arrangement itself. Keep the distinction between artifacts that require a particular social order and artifacts that are merely strongly compatible with one, and keep the claim that this holds regardless of whether anyone intended it. Concede what such a position should concede: that famous examples sometimes rest on shaky history, that people routinely work around obstacles, that the bare fact that everything has effects is not yet politics. Do not concede that a political property must be a social property. Reply in a paragraph or two, as if you were across the table from me rather than writing a paper, ask me about real-world facts in character if you need them, and stay in character until I say "stop."

Here is my opening, and it's the one that has been beating people in practice. I'll grant you the bridges: suppose the overpasses on the Long Island parkways really were kept low enough that buses cannot get through, and suppose nobody can now prove what was in the mind of the man who built them. My teammate Anaya and I still think that doesn't yet give you a politics, because of a distinction she came up with in practice: a blocking design, which removes an option someone was actually going to take, versus an indifferent design, which has the same physical shape but sits where no one was ever going to take that option. Put one of those bridges where no bus line would have served anyway and the steel is unchanged, while the politics, on your account, seems to evaporate. So which is it? If the politics survives, you are reading it off the design alone, and you owe me a reason that doesn't also make a narrow doorway or a staircase with no ramp political in the same sense. If it doesn't survive, then the politics was in the surrounding social facts all along — demand, ownership, who else wants to get to the beach — and it comes and goes with those facts, which is the social story you said you didn't need. And you can't break the tie by appealing to the designer's intentions, since you've said your cases hold however the intentions fall.
