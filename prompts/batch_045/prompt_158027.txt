I'm in a final-year undergraduate epistemology seminar, and our last unit is on the epistemology of testimony. The assessment is a short position statement on whether testimonial warrant is reducible — reductionism versus anti-reductionism — which another student will then be handed and asked to attack. I've assembled the set of commitments below over the course of the term, and I'd like it audited before I write it up, because I can't tell which items are load-bearing and which are only decoration. I've stated them, roughly, in the terms I intend to defend.

(1) Reductionism in what I take to be its structural sense: there is no primitive, sui generis source of warrant for belief. Whatever warrant a hearer acquires from being told that p is ultimately inherited from perception, memory, and inductive reasoning over the track record of testimony as a kind of source. Testimony passes warrant along; it does not manufacture it.

(2) I also want to hold something like the assurance picture for the distinctive case of being told: when a speaker tells me that p, she takes responsibility for its truth, and my belief is answerable to her in a way in which my reliance on a barometer reading or a set of footprints is not. I want this to be doing real epistemological work, not merely describing the phenomenology of conversation or the etiquette of trust.

(3) A verdict on a case: a three-year-old who comes to believe, on her mother's say-so, that there are penguins is warranted — not merely excusable, not merely usefully credulous — and her warrant is the same in kind as an adult's, only thinner. I don't want a separate story for children, and I don't want to say that what she has is some different, developmental substitute for warrant.

(4) A second verdict: if I later discover that a colleague's confident assertion rested on nothing — she guessed, or half-remembered a headline — then I lose my warrant, or never had it, even though I had no inkling at the time and even if what she said was in fact true. No warrant upstream, none downstream.

(5) A third verdict, on a case I invented and am least sure about: a clinician who does not herself understand the diagnostic system she uses reads its output to a patient as her own professional judgment, telling her that the growth is benign. Assume the system is in fact highly reliable, and assume that what makes the patient's trust reasonable is the clinician's credentials rather than any track record the patient has observed. I want to say two things at once: the patient is warranted in believing what she is told, and there is something specifically epistemic — not merely unprofessional or impolite — about what is wrong with the clinician's telling. I can't tell whether the rest of my set entitles me to both halves.

(6) A claim about the default: trust is the standing position. I am warranted in accepting what I'm told absent positive reasons for doubt, I need to have nothing available to produce if challenged, and the bare possibility that my interlocutor is lying or careless is not a defeater. I mean this as a claim about what I must be able to offer in the way of reasons, not merely as a claim about how comprehension works.

(7) A methodological conviction: the dispute between reductionists and anti-reductionists is substantive rather than verbal, and I'd be dissatisfied with an account of my own position that reconciles it only by showing the two camps to be describing one phenomenon in different vocabulary.

What I want is an audit, not a survey. Work through these in pairs — or in threes where an interaction is genuinely three-way — and tell me which combinations are actually in tension and how severely; which pairs only look as though they conflict, and what dissolves the appearance; and for each real tension, what the cheapest revision would be and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set. Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad. Where you think whether two items conflict depends on how one of them is read, say what the reading would have to be and what turns on it. Finish by telling me which single revision, if I were willing to make only one, would clean up the most. Please don't tell me which commitment I ought to care most about keeping: I'm genuinely undecided between them and would rather the argument decide for me. And please don't spend the answer explaining what reductionism and anti-reductionism are — assume I have the standard arguments on both sides in front of me, and invoke them only where they bear directly on whether my set is jointly tenable. Citations and reading lists are unnecessary; I'll handle that part myself.
