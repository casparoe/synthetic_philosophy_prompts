I'm working on a novel and I've painted myself into a philosophical corner, hoping you can help me out. The setup: my narrator's sister has died, the family has fed years of her messages into one of those companion-bot services, and the narrator — who started a philosophy PhD at Berkeley back in the day and never quite got over it — keeps arguing with the sister's widower about whether the thing that types back actually means anything. There's a scene where the widower says, look, it says I love you, and it says it in the right places and in the right tone, and it even corrects itself when I push back, so how is that different from meaning it. My narrator has to answer that without sounding like either an idiot or a textbook, and I want the answer to be Davidsonian, because Davidson is basically the only philosopher he ever quotes.

Problem is of course that Davidson died in 2003, so he never saw any of this stuff. But that's half the fun — I'd rather have the narrator rebuild the answer out of the machinery than report an opinion. So: what would a committed Davidsonian say about whether a text-trained model has beliefs and means what it says? The bits I half-remember, roughly: radical interpretation is supposed to be what meaning consists in, i.e. in principle a competent interpreter could work out a truth-theory for your utterances from evidence about your behavior and circumstances; you do that by making the speaker come out mostly right about the world, on the grounds that massive error is unintepretable; and the whole thing only gets off the ground if interpreter and speaker are jointly responding to a shared world and to each other, his triangulation idea. Then there's the late anti-conventionalism, where he says no set of shared rules or conventions does the work people think it does and that what carries a conversation is ad hoc and made up on the spot. And there's the swamp case, the guy struck by lightning who ends up molecule-for-molecule identical to a real person but supposedly has no thoughts at all, because of how he came to exist.

Here's where I can't decide which way he'd go, and I'd love your read. (1) If the complaint is really about triangulation, is the fix just hardware — bolt a camera and a mic and some kind of body onto the thing, park it in a room with a person for a couple of years so both of them are responding to the same objects and to each other, and now it's a candidate for interpretation? That feels way too easy, and my narrator says so. (2) A text-only model does have a kind of environment: the conversation so far, the corpus, what word is likely to come next. Could two creatures triangulate on a text, on jointly attending to what comes next, or does Davidson's requirement quietly smuggle in the real physical world and the real causal chains? (3) Charity seems to break in a funny way here: when a bot confabulates we can't assume its mostly right, and yet we understand it fine — we know perfectly well what it means when it invents a court case that doesn't exist. Does that show massive error is interpretable after all, or is he protected, because the error has to be local against a background of mostly-true belief? (4) Or maybe the honest move is the parasitic one: the sentences mean whatever they'd mean in the mouth of a person, the model itself believes nothing, and so the narrator's answer to the widower is something like, you're not talking to her, you're talking to a very good echo of a great many sentences. But I'm not sure that last bit is Davidson and not just me.

Which of these should the scene actually hang on, and why? And if you were writing the narrator's reply — a few sentences, in his voice, not a lecture — what would you put in his mouth?
