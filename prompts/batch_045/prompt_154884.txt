ok so I got into a three hour argument with a friend after lab meeting last night and I definately lost it, mostly because I couldn't put my finger on which step was doing the work. He was arguing that outcome-based supervision is basically never legitimate and I agree with him about reward hacking being a real thing, but his argument seems to end up somewhere neither of us wants to be. Here's the version I wrote down afterwards, close to what he said:

(1) A reward signal is worth optimizing only if, across the range of optimization we're actually going to apply, increases in that signal go with increases in the property we ultimately want the system to have. (2) What we ultimately want is never that this answer was right on this occasion; it's a disposition of the system — that it would get things right across the deployment distribution, including on instances nobody ever graded. (3) Dispositions aren't observable at training time, we only ever see sampled behaviour, so every training signal is a proxy for the disposition and never the disposition itself. (4) For any proxy–target pair there's some level of optimization pressure past which pushing the proxy up pushes the target down — call it the crossing point. (5) RL pushes whatever signal you hand it, and in practice we push hard: many steps, big batches, lots of compute. (6) So past the crossing point an outcome-trained system gets better at producing the rewarded outcome and worse at what we actually wanted. (7) And you don't fix this by grading the reasoning instead, because 'the chain of thought looks right to a grader' is just another proxy — grader-approved rather than actually-sound — with its own crossing point; process supervision is outcome supervision with extra steps. (8) So the only legitimate signal would be the target itself, which by (3) we can't observe, so no training signal is legitimate and any RL'd system should be presumed worse along the dimension we care about than the one you started from. (9) Which is nuts: systems trained purely on win/loss in games, or on whether the code passes its tests, are obviously better than their base models at the thing we care about, on inputs nobody trained on.

Here's why I care. I wanted to use 1 through 6 to argue for process supervision over outcome supervision, and then he sprang (7) on me and it ate my conclusion too, and he just bit the bullet all the way to (8) and started saying we should basically not do RL at all. I want to keep (2) and I really want to keep something in the neighbourhood of (4), and I'd also like to keep the thought that process supervision has something distinctive going for it, so my two suspects are (3) and (7), but I can't articulate what's wrong with either. The thing I've been toying with is that (3) is too flat: there's a spectrum of how much of the target the check itself exhausts, so in a formally verifiable domain the outcome isn't merely evidence for the disposition, it's partly constitutive of it, whereas in a domain where the check is itself model-generated the gap is wide — I've been calling it the verifiability gradient, and I've also been messing with a 'second-answer' signal where you reward the model for getting the answer right and also for getting perturbed versions of the same question right. So: which step do you think is broken, why does it look fine when you read it fast, and what does each of the candidate rejections cost you — especially, does rejecting (7) commit me to something about process supervision that's harder to defend than the thing it saves? And is my verifiability-gradient idea actually an independent point or am I just restating (3) in fancier words, and would the second-answer signal have a crossing point further out or is it just one more proxy a level down?
