I'm a 4th-year PhD student in philosophy — I work on markets and commodification, my dissertation is mostly about repugnant-markets stuff and about how far the standard objections to organ sales actually reach — and on Thursday I'm giving a 20-minute talk plus about 25 minutes of questions to the Living Donor Working Group at the hospital attached to my university. It's six people: a transplant surgeon who chairs it and is basically on my side but is terrified of how anything like this looks from outside; a nephrologist who runs the waitlist and thinks allocation should track medical urgency and match quality and nothing else; a clinical ethicist who is allergic to incentive-talk and speaks entirely in the idiom of the gift; a health economist who will want magnitudes, selection effects and crowd-out before she'll grant me anything; a patient advocate who is herself a recipient (deceased donor, four years on dialysis) and who I expect to be the most hostile person in the room; and one health-policy guy who will ask what the statute actually permits. There are no philosophers there, so nothing I say will land if it sounds like a journal article.

The proposal — I've been calling it a "reciprocal priority credit," open to better names — is this: a living kidney donor gets no cash, no tax break, no voucher redeemable for anything general. What she gets is a non-transferable, non-sellable credit, expiring after ten years, that lets one person she names at the time of donation (named then, never changed, no brokers, no secondary market in credits) be moved up within her own match-and-urgency band on the deceased-donor waiting list if she ever needs a kidney. My three moves are: (1) the return is denominated in the same good rather than in money, so there is no price, no exchange rate, nothing to arbitrage or resell, and the specific mechanism that makes cash markets ugly — recruiting poor people to bear surgical risk for money they need right now — never gets going; (2) it's framed and administered as reciprocity, a return in kind by the community she gave to, so the motivational crowding-out worry is supposed to be muted rather than triggered; and (3) because the credit can't be cashed out or handed to anyone else, it can't drift into being a market even informally, which is what I think dooms most "in-kind reward" proposals.

What I want is a Q&A prep sheet for that specific room, not a defense of the view in the abstract: the seven or eight objections I'll actually get, ordered by how much damage they'd do coming from these people, each phrased the way that person would actually say it (I want to hear the nephrologist's version and the advocate's version as different sentences, not as one generic objection), with a 60–90 second reply for each and an honest note on where the reply is soft or where I'm just asserting something. Tell me what I should concede in the first two slides so I'm not defending it later, which single reply has to land if the proposal is going to survive the meeting at all, how to handle the economist when she asks for effect sizes I definately don't have, and — most useful — the question I'm hoping nobody asks. Please don't pad it with a reading list or citations, I know that literature, and don't just restate my three moves back at me as if that were an objection.
