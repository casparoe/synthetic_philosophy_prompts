I am a novelist, and I am several drafts into a book whose central situation I can no longer think about without the help of someone who knows population ethics. My narrator, Mara, has a degenerative condition caused by a fertility drug her mother took — a drug without which Mara would not have been conceived at all, and almost certainly not the same child. Mara now wants a child herself and is offered the same drug. The knot I keep tying: the child she would have by taking it would not be the child she would otherwise have had, and that child's life would plainly be worth living, so the argument says she wrongs nobody; yet if she declines, there may be no child at all. What interests me more than the decision is the asymmetry inside Mara. She cannot bring herself to say she was wronged by her mother — she will not say she wishes she had never existed, and I don't want her to, since I think that move is a cheat — and yet she is entirely certain she will not do what her mother did. Her refusal looks impersonal, and she has no vocabulary for it that doesn't sound like a wish to have been erased.

Before I commit to a structure, I would like a reading of Kazuo Ishiguro's *Never Let Me Go* through the nonidentity problem: the students exist only because the donation programme exists, their lives are worth living, and no individual donor seems made worse off by anything done to her. I want the reading taken seriously in both directions, not settled with the observation that cloning people to harvest them is obviously wrong.

Specifically: does the novel find a *form* for the grievance that the argument cannot state? What work is Kathy's narration doing — I read it as equable, unindicting, almost collusive — and is that calmness a dramatization of the trap (a voice that cannot say "I was wronged" because she would not be here to say it) or a refusal of it? What should I make of the deferral strand, in which the characters can ask for time but never for exemption: is Ishiguro showing that nonidentity reasoning leaves them only the vocabulary of postponement? And where does the frame distort the book — where does it turn the novel into an illustration and miss what the Hailsham experiment, the gallery, and Miss Emily's recollections are actually about?

Finally, the part I care about as a writer rather than a reader: what does the novel add to the philosophical debate that argument alone doesn't, and what should I steal from it — and refuse — in writing Mara? I would be grateful to be told plainly if my premise rests on a confusion between the same-number and different-number cases, or if the asymmetry I find so promising is just the impersonal view smuggled in under a character's feelings.
