I retired from a membrane biophysics lab two years ago and have been auditing a philosophy of biology reading group, and a grad student there has me cornered. I can't find a step to reject.

(1) If "alive" names a real property, its extension is fixed by nature: every object either has it or lacks it, and where we can't tell, that's our ignorance, not the world's vagueness.
(2) Every criterion anyone offers — metabolism, reproduction with heredity, evolution by natural selection, self-production, any conjuncton of them — either gives an answer nobody wants or gives no answer at all. A mule is alive and evolves nothing, its line ends with it. A forest fire metabolizes and grows. A virus particle in a dish does nothing whatsoever. A self-replicating RNA in a test tube evolves beautifully as long as somebody keeps feeding it nucleotides and polymerase. The cell with the computer-written genome can't be kept going outside a lab.
(3) And it isn't that we haven't hit on the right criterion yet. There's a graded series — a fatty acid vesicle that grows and splits but evolves nothing, a virus, a plasmid, an obligate intracellular bacterium, a free-living cell — and for any criterion you like, some neighbour on that series differs from a clear case by less than the clear cases differ from each other.
(4) So no criterion fixes where the line goes. What fixes it is us: our interests, and which cases we happened to argue about first.
(5) A predicate whose extension is fixed by us rather than by nature doesn't name something that is there anyway. So nothing is strictly alive, and "alive" is on a par with "weed".
(6) He then runs the same argument for "cell", for "organism" (siphonophore colony, slime mould fruiting body, ant colony), for "gene" (a mobile element, an enhancer that works in one tissue, a prion with no nucleic acid at all) and for "species" in bacteria. Same structure, same conclusion.
(7) But molecular biology is engineering now: people write a genome on a machine, put it into a cell with its own DNA removed, and the cell does what the design says. You can't do that with a vocabulary that tracks nothing in nature.

My own shot at it was that life is not one axis but two: how much of the specification of the system is carried inside the system (virus: none, it borrows the host's ribosomes; cell: all of it) and whether its variants are subject to selection. Mule: high on the first, zero on the second. Test-tube RNA: the other way round. I thought that was progress. He says it makes things worse, because now there are two continuous axes and I have to draw a curve through the plane, which is even more obviously our choice and not nature's.

He says the weight is all on (1). I think it's the move from (3) to (4). A third person in the group says keep (1), accept (5), and conclude that biology was engineering all the way down and never had kinds. Which step do you actually reject, what does rejecting it cost you, and is there anything salvageable in the two-axes thing?
