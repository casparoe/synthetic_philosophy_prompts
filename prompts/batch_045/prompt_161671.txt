I'm rewriting my lecture notes on dispositions for my metaphysics course and want to stop hand-waving at the counterexamples to the simple conditional analysis. Here's the patch I've been teaching: x is fragile iff (i) x would break if struck, and (ii) nothing would prevent x from breaking. On this reading the fink case and the mask case are really one phenomenon — in both, something blocks the manifestation — so in both the glass simply isn't fragile, and the conditional analysis survives once you bolt on a ceteris paribus clause.

Two things are nagging me though. A colleague raised what she calls an antidote case: a glass that isn't fragile at all, but wired to a device such that the instant it's struck the device alters its microstructure so that it shatters. My test counts it as fragile — it breaks, and nothing prevents the breaking — which seems wrong. And the glass packed in sawdust comes out non-fragile on my test, which also seems wrong: surely how you ship a glass doesn't determine whether it's fragile.

So: is the antidote a genuinely seperate kind of counterexample, or can I absorb it into the fink/mask pair? Is my second clause actually doing any work, or have I just restated the original conditional in more words? And if the patch collapses, am I stuck saying a disposition is nothing but its categorical basis?
