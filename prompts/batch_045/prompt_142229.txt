So I got moved onto the model-risk team at work a few months ago, which means I now have to sign sentences like 'the model does not represent or use the applicant's ethnicity' in audit reports. The trouble is I don't think I could say what 'represents' means here to save my life, even though I use the word constantly and my gut is surprisingly firm about cases. So: here are my verdicts, and I want the principle behind them. And please don't send me to the interpretability literature — I've tried, there's too much of it and half of it seems to assume the answer.

Say we are talking about a toy credit model that scores loan applications, and a toy clinical model that reads admission notes.

1. The credit model was trained with ethnicity scrubbed from the data, but a linear probe on its last hidden layer recovers it at 88% where the base rate is 60%, and if I patch that direction up or down the scores move the way you'd expect. Verdict: obviously yes, it represents ethnicity and uses it.

2. Same model. A probe recovers the first digit of the applicant's ZIP code perfectly from layer 3 — but zeroing that direction out changes nothing to six decimal places, and nothing downstream is correlated with it. Verdict: no. That's information sitting in the activations, not a representation.

3. For 'applicant is self-employed', no linear probe on any layer beats chance on real data. But a steering vector found by gradient descent shifts the score in exactly the pattern self-employment would produce, and doesn't move anything unrelated. Verdict: yes, it represents self-employment. (My colleague says this flatly contradicts 2, and I can't quite tell her she's wrong.)

4. The clinical model: a probe reads out 'will be readmitted within 30 days' at 92% from the notes, and it's genuinely useful. Then we find the real thing the relevant units track is whether the note was written on a weekend, which happened to correlate with readmission in training. Verdict: no, it represents weekend-documentation, not readmission risk — even though the probe is real and the number was right.

5. A model trained only to output legal moves in a board game. A linear read-out recovers the whole board state from its activations, and editing that read-out changes which moves come out, in the right way. Verdict: yes, and this is my strongest case — nothing in the training objective ever said anything about representing a board.

6. After fine-tuning on a synthetic task, the model grows a direction that behaves like a clean Boolean variable under intervention, but nothing in the real input distribution ever makes it fire. It's a knob with no natural input that turns it. Verdict: I lean no — a control knob isn't a representation of anything. Which again seems to fight with 3.

7. And if you hand me a second credit model with identical behaviour to the first but nothing linearly decodable anywhere and no steerable direction, I don't want to say it has no representations. I want to say I can't see them, and that the question might not have an answer for that model yet.

The principle I keep trying out on myself is three clauses: the network represents X iff (a) some function of its internal state carries information about X, (b) downstream computation actually reads that function — it's used, not just present — and (c) it's about X rather than about something that merely travelled with X, in the sense that it would still fire if X were varied with the correlates held fixed. My problem is operationalising (c): I can't intervene on ethnicity in a model, only on strings, and I'm not sure 'vary X holding the correlates fixed' means anything when X is a social category.

So: does that three-clause thing survive my cases, and where does it break? Which single verdict should I drop if dropping it buys me a clean principle? What does the repaired version say about the case I actually have to decide next week — a debiased credit model where the linear probe is now at chance but an MLP probe recovers ethnicity at 78%, patching that nonlinear feature does move scores, and the errors still correlate with ethnicity? Do I write 'represents' there, or is 'uses' the only word my report can defend? And give me one case I haven't listed that would tell apart the two or three candidate principles you end up with, plus the version of the principle I could say out loud to a regulator without getting laughed at.
