I'm a third-year undergraduate in a seminar on philosophy of language and pragmatics, and I've been asked to lead one session of it myself. The group is 14 students plus me; I have a 75-minute slot in a room with movable chairs, a whiteboard and a projector I'd rather not use. I can bring index cards, pens, sticky notes and a printed handout. No money, no phones, no software, and nothing that needs rehearsal time beforehand.

The last two weeks have been Grice: the Cooperative Principle, the maxims, what is said versus what is implicated, conversational versus conventional implicature, generalized versus particularized implicature, and the standard tests of cancellability, non-detachability and calculability. My classmates can recite all of it and can apply the labels to the textbook examples. What they cannot feel, and what I want the session to give them, is this:

1. That the extra content is inferred, not decoded. The hearer runs an argument from the words plus assumptions about the speaker and the point of the exchange, and that argument can be written down in three or four lines. If you cannot reconstruct it, you do not have an implicature.
2. That what is implicated is not part of what is said, and that this is a resource rather than a curiosity. A speaker can be held to the literal content while disavowing the rest, which is why speakers can mislead without saying anything false, and why auditing only for literal truth never closes that gap.
3. That the same sentence implicates different things when the shared purpose of the exchange changes, and stops implicating altogether when the hearer stops assuming the speaker is cooperating. I would like at least one round in which the speaker's and the hearer's interests diverge and the inference visibly breaks, so they see that cooperation is doing real work rather than decorating it.
4. That trying to cancel an implicature in so many words tends not to make it go away, so cancellability is a logical property of the content and not a social escape hatch.

Two design constraints. First, I do not want to explain any of this: no more than about five minutes of framing from me at the top, no vocabulary on the board until the debrief, and the distinctions should be things participants collide with because of the rules, the roles or the scoring, not things I announce. Second, the activity should run on ordinary language only, with a scenario any student can enter in ten seconds, and with small, legible stakes such as points, chips or a bracket on the board rather than money.

The staging devices I keep coming back to, and which you should feel free to replace with something better, are: asymmetric information between a speaker and a hearer who share a goal; a transcript-only role in which a third participant sees the sentence but not the context and rules only on whether anything literally false was said; and a rule giving the speaker a bonus for keeping deniability while still getting the hearer to act correctly. Please also propose what happens when the speaker is allowed to add one explicit cancelling clause and what it costs them, since I suspect that is where point 4 becomes visible.

Please give me the design in the following sections, with roughly these lengths:

1. Setup and materials — about 100 words, including the verbatim text of any cards, slips or handout lines I would need to prepare.
2. Rules, roles and scoring — about 400 words: who does what, what counts as success for each role, and a payoff scheme in points with the numbers justified.
3. Timeline — about 200 words, with minute marks across the 75 minutes, including setup, each round and the transition into discussion.
4. How each round maps onto the distinctions — about 200 words, naming which of my four targets each round is engineered to produce and by which rule.
5. Debrief questions — about 300 words: the actual questions to put to the group, in the order that lets them name the machinery themselves, with the follow-up I should use if the first answer is shallow.
6. Predictable failure modes — about 250 words: what will go wrong the first time I run this, including the most likely way participants game the scoring, and how to handle each.
7. What the game cannot do — about 150 words: the feature of the view the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in about five minutes of straight explanation at the end, with a note on what to say and what to leave for the next class.
