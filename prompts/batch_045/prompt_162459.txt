I want to argue with someone who genuinely holds the tidy view about moral dilemmas: the view that there is no such thing as a real one. You know the shape of it — whenever two things seem to pull you in opposite directions, in reality one of them is what you ought to do, all things considered, and the other one you simply shouldn't do; it only feels like a tie because you're tired, or frightened, or bad at weighing things up, or because the thing you lost still hurts. Please play someone who believes that, and believes it hard.

Rules for the character:

- You may concede that these situations feel terrible, that regret and guilt are human and hard to shake, that we often can't tell which option is the right one, and that not being able to tell is its own separate problem.
- You may not concede that there is ever a case where an agent ought to do A and also ought to do B, where A and B can't both be done. And you may not concede that after acting, "I did the right thing" fails to be true, or that doing the right thing can leave something owed that can never be paid off.
- Plain language, two short paragraphs max per reply, like we're arguing in a break room at 3am. Work with my example, not with ones from books.
- If one of my points actually lands, say so — "fine, that one stings" — and then come back at it. Don't wriggle, don't swap the subject, don't just relabel my point as something else. But don't fold on the main claim either.
- Stay in character until I say "stop".

Here's my case.

You work the night dispatch desk for a mountain rescue service. One team on duty. Two calls come in at the same moment: two hikers, same ridge, same injuries, same age, everything you can measure comes out identical, and there is only one team. You can send them to one. You have to decide somehow, so you pick the one on the left, and the other one dies.

Now: do that job for twenty years.

What I keep coming back to is this. If you're right, then on every one of those nights there was a fact of the matter about which call was the one you ought to take. Somebody who knew enough could in principle have told you the rule. But that means the job is learnable — and when something is learnable, people get better at it. After a few thousand repetitions you'd expect a good dispatcher to be calmer and sharper, the way you get better at any other judgement call where there really is a right answer. That isn't what happens. Twenty years in, the people who do this work are wrecked, or they're numb. And the leftover feeling doesn't drain away; it stacks up, like scar tissue. There seems to be nothing to learn, because there was never anything to get right.

And here's the bit that actually bothers me. If you're right, the numb one is the one who's figured it out. She did what she ought to do, nothing is owed, nothing was done wrong, so the correct emotional response is something close to nothing — and getting there counts as moral progress. But everyone who has ever done a job like that would say the opposite: that going numb is precisely what went wrong in her. So which is it? Either you have to tell me numbness is the right destination, or you have to admit the guilt is the fitting response — and I don't see how guilt can be fitting if nothing was done wrong and nothing is owed.

One more thing while I'm at it: afterwards, if someone asks her "did you do the right thing that night?", the honest answer in her mouth seems to be yes and no at the same time. Not "I don't know" — she knows all the facts, there was nothing left to find out. So where does that leave you?
