I chair the Commitments and Defaults Review at a consumer-finance app, and I want our working distinction between helping users keep their own plans and profiting from their changing their minds turned into a procedure we can actually run.

The setting: every quarter my board reviews roughly twenty features that constrain, reverse, or automatically renew a user's future choices — auto-renewing subscriptions whose renewal price rises after the first term, savings locks with early-withdrawal penalties, automatic escalation of a contribution rate on every pay raise, gamified savings streaks that reset if a week is missed, cooling-off windows before a large withdrawal. Reviewers are product managers and one compliance officer; none of them has any training in decision theory. Each feature gets ninety minutes. The output is a one-page verdict — permitted as designed, permitted with specified changes, or not permitted — that the general counsel's office signs off on, so the reasoning has to be legible to a lawyer who will ask what the test was and whether we applied it consistently.

Please write the procedure my reviewers will follow: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the gates where the procedure stops with a no, and the gates where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Two constraints on what the reviewers have to work with: they can use product telemetry (reversal rates, how often users re-enrol, how much money moves and to whom) but must not rely on surveying users about their own preferences, and they frequently will not have the number a step asks for, so every step needs an instruction for what to do when the data is missing.

The conceptual core is the money-pump argument, and I need the procedure to keep the distinctions that carry the argument's weight rather than smoothing them away. Specifically, it must be able to tell apart: a cycle in preferences held at one time from a change of preference over time; a reversal that is information-bearing (the user learned something, her circumstances changed, the product revealed itself to be different from what was advertised) from one that is gratuitous drift; a design that works by users mispredicting their own future behaviour from one that a user who fully anticipated her own future defection would still have chosen at the outset; a mechanism that recreates the original choice situation and can extract again from one that merely charges once for a change of mind; and extraction that leaves the user worse off to a third party's benefit from a pure transfer among the user's own time-slices, where the earlier self gains exactly what the later self loses. My reviewers will know none of this vocabulary, so define each test in operational terms and say what telemetry pattern would satisfy it.

Two questions I want the procedure to take an explicit stance on rather than dodge. First, pump closure: does a working exploitation argument require that the mechanism be able to run indefinitely, or is a single completed cycle enough to condemn the design, given that a one-off fee for changing one's mind may be a perfectly rational price for keeping options open? Second, what I have started calling autonomy pumps — cycles that extract no money at all but leave the user with no stable plan she can act on, because the mechanism undoes her commitments each time she forms one. If the argument from money pumps does not extend to these, say so plainly and tell us what, if anything, we should do with them instead.

Then run the finished procedure on four features, showing the work at each gate: the auto-renewing subscription with the rising renewal price; the auto-escalating contribution rate with an easy opt-out; the savings streak that resets after a missed week; and our flagship Lockbox, a savings lock with an early-withdrawal penalty where the funds remain the user's and earn interest. Lockbox is the feature my team is most confident is simply helping people, so run it honestly and do not soften the result: if the verdict under your own procedure is that it exploits diachronic inconsistency, say what we would have to change about it, and say what we should conclude if the change is not commercially viable.

Close with the procedure's limits: state plainly which questions it can settle on the evidence a reviewer has, which it can only re-describe, and which — whether preference change over time is ever irrational in itself, how much paternalism toward a user's later self is licensed — it has to leave to someone else.
