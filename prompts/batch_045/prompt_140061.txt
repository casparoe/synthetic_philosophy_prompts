I'm retired — thirty-odd years as an experimental physicist, mostly instrumentation, so I'm used to following a formalism somewhere my gut doesn't want to go — and I've started sitting in on a philosophy reading group. I built the argument below to defend the claim that a good enough simulation of a person would be a person, and I've managed to convince myself of something I definitely don't believe. I'd like to know which step to throw out.

(1) What matters morally about a system is what it does with what it gets: how its states depend on its inputs and on its earlier states, and how what comes out depends on all that. Not what it's made of. Call that its organization.

(2) If two systems have the same organization, they have the same moral status, whatever they're made of.

(3) A simulation detailed enough to match a human brain state for state has the same organization as that brain. So switching the simulation off is the same kind of moral act as killing the person, and running it in pain is as bad as torturing them.

(4) Organization is abstract. The simulation is just a set of state-types plus a rule saying which state-type follows which, given which input. Any physical setup whose states can be labelled so that the labels obey that rule has that organization.

(5) Now spread the simulation out in space instead of time. Instead of one machine stepping through a billion states, build a long row of small gadgets, gadget n wired to gadget n+1, where each gadget does nothing but take a description of state n, emit that state's output, and produce a description of state n+1. No gadget is aware of anything; the row never "runs" in any intuitive sense, it just sits there wired up. But under the labelling from (4) its states obey the rule. So by (2) and (3) it has the moral status of the person.

(6) Now relabel something else. Take a physical system with plenty of distinguishable states and plenty of change over time — the ivy on my shed, a rock face, a cup of water cooling — and allow myself freedom in how fine my labelling is and in what counts as an input. I can find a labelling under which its successive states also obey that same rule. Someone in the group says there's an old theorem to this effect, something about every ordinary open system realizing every finite automaton (I think that's Putnam's). Then (2) and (3) give me: the ivy is a person. And so is the rock. And each of them is every person at once.

(7) That's false. So something above is wrong, or one of the moves between steps doesn't hold.

If I have to choose what to keep: I most want to keep (3), that was the whole point. I have a soft spot for (1) too, because substrate-independence looks like the only view consistent with what we actually know about brains. And I notice I'd rather keep (5)'s verdict about the row of gadgets than (6)'s about the ivy, but I cant say why and that bothers me.

Which step do you reject, why does it look innocent, and what does rejecting it cost me elsewhere in the argument? Please don't just tell me the conclusion is mad — I know that already. I want the step and the price. Under 400 words, and no reading lists, I've got enough on my pile.
