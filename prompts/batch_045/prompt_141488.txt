I am a journalist covering science and ideas, working on a feature about whether we will be able to tell when a system that knows more than we do is telling us something false. I keep running into the same institution everywhere I report, and I want to know what it implicitly believes.

Here is the practice. AI labs increasingly grade one model's output with another model. In the weak version, which is now close to industry standard, a judge model reads a single answer to a question and assigns it a score, and that score becomes a reward signal or an evaluation result. In the strong version, usually called debate, two systems are given the same question and assigned opposing answers; a judge reads both transcripts, declares a winner, and the winner's answer is treated as the target for training and as the output at deployment. In both versions the judge is deliberately weaker than the systems being judged, in exactly the domains being judged, and that is the point: the whole procedure exists so that supervision can be scaled past human ability. Debate adds a structural claim on top of this, namely that an adversarial format is what makes the weaker judge's verdict trustworthy, because the opponent is incentivised to find and expose whatever is wrong with the other side's case.

I have built some working vocabulary for what I think the weak point is, and I would like you to use it or tell me it is misleading. Call the fixed length, time, and attention within which a claim has to be made good to the judge the dialectical budget. Call the depth at which a chain of reasoning bottoms out in something the judge can check without taking anyone's word for it the judge's verification horizon. Call an argument obfuscated when whatever makes it unsound sits below the judge's verification horizon while its surface features are the ones the judge rewards: length, confident structure, apparatus that looks like citation, technical vocabulary, and agreement with what the judge already believes. Call the gap between how cheap it is to produce such an argument and how expensive it is to expose it the refutation asymmetry. And separately, call it muddying when the point of the obfuscation is not to make the judge believe the false answer but to make the judge unable to distinguish, so that the protocol's tie-break rule decides the outcome instead.

My question is what this practice presupposes. What is the implicit epistemology of a pipeline in which truth is to be extracted by pitting speakers against each other in front of a judge who could not have produced the correct answer alone? Does it presuppose that truth has a dialectical advantage, in the spirit of the old Millian claim that a belief is held well only when it has to survive collision with error, or does it presuppose only a reliabilist story, that this procedure happens to track truth in the domains we run it in? Does it presuppose that every false claim has a short refutation, or only that most do? Does it presuppose that the judge's residual errors are scattered rather than aimed, since obfuscation is aimed by construction? Does it presuppose that the judge's tie-break default, the state in which it has no discriminating evidence, is itself not exploitable? Does it presuppose that the questions being judged come with certificates, so that the regress of who-checks-the-checker bottoms out somewhere?
