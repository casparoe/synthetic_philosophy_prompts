I have just come out of a long argument with an old friend about whether it is rational to vote, and I would like you to write me a piece of satire that belongs to the argument rather than adjudicating it. My friend's position is that the puzzle is a non-problem: once you add the satisfaction of doing one's duty, the expressive value of the act, the minimax-regret thought that everyone might stay home and let the worse candidate win by one vote, the fact that one votes as a citizen rather than as a consumer, or the fact that one's own group rather than oneself might be pivotal, the calculus comes out positive and there is nothing left to explain. My position is that each of these moves quietly changes the subject or inflates one of the terms, and that the honest reading of the instrumental picture is that for a single voter in a large electorate the expected return is negligible against a real opportunity cost. What I want is a piece written from inside someone who takes my side so literally that he proposes to repair the institution rather than the theory.

Write it as a parody of an official government white paper, roughly 800 words, from a fictional ministry with a name like the Ministry of Electoral Participation or the Office of Democratic Microeconomics, in an unnamed small parliamentary democracy. Deadpan civil-service register throughout. Format: a title, numbered sections, one boxed worked cost-benefit calculation, and a short closing FAQ. No preface, no winking, no paragraph in which the author or anyone he quotes notices that anything is wrong, and no appended explanation of the joke.

The scheme the paper proposes: each ballot paper prints on its face its decisiveness quotient, one over the number of ballots cast. After the polls close, one ballot is drawn at random and certified as the single ballot that legally determined the result; the voter concerned is issued a certificate recording that he or she was decisive, and the popular vote is retained as a ceremonial figure. The paper should set out the reform in the language of restoring the link between the individual act and the collective outcome, and should note with satisfaction the advantages it brings: pivotality is no longer hostage to how close the race turns out to be, so the awkward result that a successful get-out-the-vote campaign lowers each voter's chance of mattering is eliminated; and the negative externality by which additional voters depress one another's decisiveness probability is resolved, since under the new scheme extra turnout no longer dilutes anyone.

The comedy has to come from getting the philosophy right. The paper should walk through the standard instrumental comparison of the benefit differential against the cost of voting weighted by the probability of being pivotal, using the usual order-of-magnitude estimate of that probability in a large election, and should dismiss each of the standard repair jobs in a single straight-faced line: the duty or expressiveness term, on the ground that it measures a feeling rather than an outcome; the regret-based argument, on the ground that it is a decision rule that cannot be recommended on its own terms; the move of counting benefits to everyone else, on the ground that this changes the subject from whether voting is worth it for me to whether it is worth it for us; group pivotality, on the ground that it shifts the question from my act to someone else's.

The reductio must then land through the arithmetic rather than through a punchline. In the boxed calculation the paper should show that with a decisiveness quotient of one over roughly thirty-five million, the expected return clears the cost of voting comfortably — and it should reach that result only by valuing the election outcome at its aggregate social stake, in the billions, while presenting the figure as the value of the outcome to the voter. The reader should be able to see that the reform makes voting instrumentally rational for each individual only by making every other ballot irrelevant, and that the author cannot see this. Include also a short passage in which the Ministry confronts the difficulty that, since only one ballot matters, there is no longer any instrumental reason for anyone else to turn out, together with whatever incentive it devises to keep the ballot box full, and one invented quoted remark from a sympathizer outside the Ministry, in a footnote or sidebar.

Begin with the piece itself.
