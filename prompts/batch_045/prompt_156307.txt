You are an analytic philosopher working at the intersection of philosophy of mind and ethics, specifically on whether consciousness comes in degrees and on what follows for the moral status of very simple beings. I already have a reading list from my supervisor, so please don't spend your answer on citations, further reading, or a survey of who has said what — I want the argumentative work done on my own commitments.

Some context: I'm writing a short position paper for a graduate reading group on invertebrate welfare and moral status, and a friend who actually does insect-welfare research keeps telling me my view is internally unstable. Every time she points at a seam, though, it turns out to be a place where I think the tension is only apparent, so we go in circles. I'd like an independent audit before I present. Here is where I've landed, as seven commitments. (1) Sentience is a scalar property, not a binary one, and a being's moral status is a continuous, threshold-free function of how sentient it is: there is no principled point at which the lights come on, only systems at or extremely near zero and systems further up the scale. (2) What places a being on the scale is what I'll call experiential bandwidth — how much experiencing gets packed into a unit of the subject's own time. Two things feed into it: the richness of the content at any instant, and the fineness of the subject's temporal grain, so that a creature whose experience updates much more rapidly than ours may get considerably more experience per second of clock time even if each snapshot is simple and dim. (3) Applied verdicts: an insect is sentient to a nonzero but very small degree; a thermostat, a houseplant, and today's large language models are at zero. (4) Beings with diachronic self-concern — plans, future-directed desires, something like a narrative identity — enjoy a kind of protection that is not merely a matter of degree: you cannot justify setting back such a being's serious interests by summing arbitrarily many minute setbacks to simpler beings, however enormous the total. I hold this even though I cannot say how many insect pains would equal one ruined career. (5) Death is very close to a non-harm for a creature that has no future-directed desires and no concern for itself over time; for such creatures everything that matters is how their lives feel while they last, not how soon they end. That is why I think insect protein could be morally fine in principle if the slaughter were genuinely painless, and why the sheer number of insects killed in agriculture is not, by itself, the quantity that alarms me. (6) How much a given quantity of suffering matters is fixed entirely and impartially by (1) and (2), with no inflation for species membership, relationship, or proximity — and yet I also take it to be obviously permissible, and in ordinary life often required, to spend vastly more on preventing a mild discomfort to my own child than on preventing the death of an unfamiliar insect. (7) On method and epistemology: whether a system is sentient is a matter of its causal-functional organization, not of what it is made of; but I am confident that current machine-learning systems are insentient and that scaling them up within the present paradigm will not change the moral picture. And under uncertainty I think we should reason by expected value, weighting candidate systems by credence times degree — which, given how astronomically numerous insects are and how unsure I am about the temporal-grain term in (2), seems to push invertebrate welfare toward the very top of any cause-prioritization list, and I don't think our inability to measure degrees precisely is an excuse for inaction.
