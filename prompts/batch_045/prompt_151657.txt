I chair the serious-incident review committee at a 600-bed teaching hospital, and I want to replace the way we write causal statements with something my committee can actually run.

Context: we review roughly a dozen incidents a month. Every review ends in a written report with (a) a causal statement — what caused the harm to this patient — and (b) the corrective actions we will take. The report goes to the board, and for the serious ones to the regulator and potentially into litigation, so the wording has to be defensible under hostile questioning. The committee is two physicians, a risk manager, a quality-improvement nurse and me; nobody has any training in causal inference beyond a regression module years ago. We have ninety minutes per case, the incident timeline, the chart, and whatever data we already collect routinely. We cannot commission new studies, and we will not adopt a procedure that needs a statistician in the room.

What I want is a decision procedure for the causal statement, built on the two families of account that philosophers actually dispute: counterfactual-dependence accounts in the Lewis line, on which X caused Y roughly because, had X not occurred, Y would not have; and interventionist accounts in the Woodward line, on which X causes Y because there is some intervention that sets X to a different value and changes Y with everything else held fixed. I am not asking you to adjudicate between them. I am asking you to turn what each one gives us into a checklist we can run in about fifteen minutes per claim, and to be explicit at every step about which account is doing the work, because they come apart in ways we keep tripping over.

Design constraints:

- Ordered questions, each with a stated sufficiency condition (what a yes has to consist in) and an explicit stop: either a hard no that kills the claim, or a flag that routes the factor elsewhere in the report.
- The distinctions that have to survive the translation into plain English: the contrast class (caused relative to what — X at one value versus X at another, not X happened); whether the counterfactual comes out true only on a backtracking reading, that is only if we also change something upstream of X, which is the move that makes our risk scores, early-warning scores and abnormal lab values look causal when they are only tracking a common upstream condition; whether the intervention we can imagine is surgical, setting X directly and breaking the other routes into X, or only works by first changing something else that also reaches the outcome by a second route; whether the effect is invariant across the different routes by which we could set X — permanent staffing versus agency cover, automated alert versus staff training — since if the answer depends on the route, X was never one variable; whether we may test the intervention against any admissible background setting of the other factors or only against what actually happened that night, together with a bound on admissible so that we do not end up calling everything a cause; and what to do with factors nobody can set, such as the patient's age, frailty, sex, race or rural address, including the case where the honest intervention is on someone else's perception of that factor rather than on the factor itself.
- A tie-break rule for when the two accounts disagree, and a short ladder of permitted language — cause, contributing factor, risk stratifier, marker — with the condition for each rung, because most of our arguments are really about which rung a factor belongs on.
- Honesty about where judgment cannot be eliminated: which variables belong in the model, how far upstream we trace, how much invariance is enough, whether an intervention counts as available for us (physically possible, legally permitted, fundable by next quarter), and the gap between the general-level evidence we actually have and the single-case attribution we have to write, given that the interventionist test is naturally about what happens in general while our report is about this patient on this night. Mark those as sign-off points for me rather than as questions with answers.

Then run the finished procedure step by step on three shapes of case from our last quarter:

1. An abnormal set of observations on a ward round was documented but not escalated; the patient arrested several hours later. The committee wants to write that the failure to escalate caused the arrest.
2. The patient was eighty-four and frail. The committee wants frailty named in the causal statement, because it is the thing everyone brings up in the meeting.
3. The infusion pump was programmed at ten times the intended rate and, independently, the patient was given a second drug at a dose that would on its own have produced the same toxicity. Either would have done it alone.

At least one of these should come out in a way the committee will dislike — my guess is that the counterfactual reading and the interventionist reading will not both give us what we want in the third case — and I want that result left as it comes out rather than softened.

Finally, tell me plainly what the procedure cannot do: which of our recurring questions it has no purchase on, which kinds of incident it will systematically misgrade (rare events, omissions and things that did not happen, harm avoided by luck), and where it will return confident-sounding answers that are really metaphysical positions in disguise.
