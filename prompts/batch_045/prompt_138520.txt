hey so I run the philosophy club at my school — its a lunch block so 45 minutes max, usually 12 to 15 people, we meet in a normal classroom with desks pushed into a circle, and stuff I have access to is index cards, tape, string, a deck of cards, a whiteboard, my phone timer, and I can bring a box of donuts. Last session we argued about Clifford vs James on the ethics of belief (we only read short paraphrases, not the actual essays, and the club split basically down the middle) and I've been stuck on something Maya, one of the juniors, said afterwards: in Clifford's shipowner case the belief that the ship is fine doesn't make the ship fine, so obviously believing there without evidence is a screw-up. But in the cases that actually show up in our lives — a group project, a team, a campaign, a walkout — the confidence is part of what makes the thing work. The wrinkle I want people to feel is that this kind of confidence doesn't work alone. Me secretly believing we'll pull it off does nothing if everyone else thinks we're doomed, and even everyone believing it individually might not be enough if each person also believes they're the only one who believes it. So the gap I care about is between first-order confidence and the stacked version where everyone knows that everyone knows, and then the weird thing that follows from it: once the evidence for "we'll succeed" partly depends on how confident other people are, and their confidence partly depends on what they think you believe, there's a loop — you can kind of bootstrap a belief into looking justified, and I definately can't tell whether that's vicious circularity (so Clifford is right that you've manufactured fake evidence) or whether it's just the honest structure of any social case like this (so James has a point that lives inside ordinary group stuff, not only in the religious case he's famous for).

Can you design an activity for that? It needs to be something we actually do, not a discussion question — a game or staged challenge or role thing with real rules, roles if that helps, and a small real stake so it matters whether they succeed (donuts, or the losing side has to do something mildly embarrassing, but no money and nothing that would get me in trouble with the teacher who supervises us). Two things I really care about: first, the common-confidence thing has to show up in what happens to them — the group failing or succeeding in a way that makes it obvious that everyone privately believing it wasn't enough, or that the shared-and-known-to-be-shared part was what actually did the work — rather than in me explaining it at the end; second, it can't just collapse into "positive thinking is useful", which is where the club will go in about thirty seconds, because the interesting part is the loop and whether that confidence is cheating on the evidence or is itself part of the evidence.
