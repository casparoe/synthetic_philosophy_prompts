I'm on the board of a small housing nonprofit that just signed its first big government contract, and our group chat keeps using 'mission drift' and 'power-seeking' as if they mean the same thing, so I've been trying to get the difference straight in my own head. Take the East India Company: it starts as a chartered trading venture, and a century and a half later it has a private army, collects land revenue in Bengal, and is basically governing a subcontinent. Run that through the two failure stories people tell about misaligned optimizers — (1) the system keeps its original objective but works out that building power, influence and resources is instrumentally useful, and goes and does that; (2) the objective itself slowly moves, with nobody ever deciding it, until 'trade profitably' has quietly become something else. Which story actually fits the Company, and how much of what we know about it even discriminates between the two?

The bit I really care about, and where I want to be pushed back on if I've got it wrong: I think the test that separates them is how the system responds to correction. A power-seeker that still has the original goal in place should fight back, hide things, or try to capture whoever is correcting it, while a drifted system should be perfectly cooperative and may even agree with the correction, then drift right back, because the thing it keeps returning to is now a different objective. Does that survive contact with what happened when the Company's own directors in London tried to rein in their men in India, and later when Parliament started hauling those men in front of committees? And be honest about where the theory just goes quiet: does either story say anything about the timing, or about whether a fixed founding document like the charter actually does any work in holding an objective in place, or is that just decoration we tell ourselves?
