Been getting into philosophy in the evenings since I retired from the lab (solid-state stuff, nothing glamorous) and I keep going round in circles on this question about time. Could you write me a dialogue between a presentist and an eternalist? Both of them should be good at this — I don't want either one to be a punching bag, and no name-dropping or citations, just arguments.

Have them work through roughly this: the eternalist opens with the relativity-of-simultaneity argument (the version where two people ambling past each other in opposite directions end up committed to wildly different answers about what is happening right now in the Andromeda galaxy). The presentist grants that this kills off a single global "now," but then tries a move I haven't seen discussed: claim instead that the present is thick and fuzzy — a window of something like a hundred milliseconds, on the order of the time a nervous system takes to integrate what it senses — and that whether some far-off event falls inside that window simply has no fact of the matter, vague in the way "bald" is vague. The eternalist should press on whether that vagueness is out in the world or only in our language, and whether a vague thick present still buys the thin ontology the presentist wanted in the first place. Then let the presentist turn it around and ask what the extra timeslices are actually for: do they do any real work in explaining why our memories and instrument records are reliable, or does the block universe just redescribe the problem with bigger furniture?

Actual back-and-forth please, each one answering what the other really said, and let them end up genuinely stuck rather then one of them winning. A page or so is plenty.
