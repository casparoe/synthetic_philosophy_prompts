I'm the student member of my high school's Campus Collections Committee, and I have to circulate a decision memo before Friday's meeting. I'd like your help drafting it.

The case: last spring a senior sculpted a figure called *Anthem* from one lump of the ceramics room's clay. It won a regional award, and the committee voted to give it a permanent spot in the front-hall display case. In August a custodian knocked it off its pedestal and it shattered. A ceramics class gathered every fragment, re-wedged the same clay, and threw forty mugs, now boxed for the winter fundraising sale; they had also laser-scanned the piece before it broke, so an exact replica can be printed. The sculptor asks us to pull the mugs from the sale, saying that what the school promised to keep permanently was destroyed, and that selling it as cups is a second injury. The ceramics teacher answers that nothing of value was lost: all the matter survived, the shape survives in the scan, and reclaiming clay is what a studio does every year. Our policy says only that collection items may not be disposed of without committee approval, which assumes an answer to just the question at issue — whether the statue was ever something other than the clay.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate: memo header, under two pages. It should recommend one concrete course of action with a deadline and a named responsible party, choosing among (A) withdraw the mugs and display the replica, clearly labeled as a replica; (B) sell the mugs and treat the replica as fulfilling the promise; (C) sell the mugs, display nothing, and issue a public correction. It should engage the ceramics teacher's argument on its own terms rather than simply asserting that the statue was a separate thing — in particular, what follows for the mugs and for the replica if the statue was, or was not, the same object as the clay — and it should say whether an exact copy or the continued existence of the matter could count as keeping our promise. Give the strongest consideration on the losing side a fair hearing, and note briefly what ruling commits us to the next time a collection item is made of reusable material.
