I'm a backend engineer with no formal philosophy background, and for the past year I've been maintaining an internal code-review bot: a system prompt, a few hundred stored transcripts, and a small eval suite I wrote to keep myself honest about what it actually does. I keep running into a pattern I have no word for, and the two words I keep encountering online don't seem to be it.

The pattern concerns what the system *doesn't* do. If I write the character in the first person — you are a reviewer who cares about correctness and regards style as a linter's business, not a person's — then the bot, maybe a third of the time, declines things it could plainly do: asked later in the same session to comment on naming, it says naming isn't its job. If I write the same content as a list of instructions — address correctness, do not comment on style, do not comment on naming even when asked — it satisfies every item and then, when I ask about naming, comments on naming. Making the list longer and more emphatic doesn't close the gap: I can enumerate the conflicts I anticipated and it holds the line on exactly those, but a conflict I didn't anticipate, like asking it to rewrite a working function into a different idiom purely for readability, gets complied with, whereas the first-person version pushes back there too. So the two framings differ not in which requirements get met but in what gets excluded, and in whether the exclusions extend past the cases anyone wrote down. I've seen the mirror image as well: given a character of an engineer who has been at the company ten years, the bot *omits* things — it stops volunteering the generic newcomer explanation of our build system — while given ten facts about the build system, it recites them and still wraps the newcomer explanation around them.

I don't think calling this prompt engineering or persona prompting helps, since those tell me how to produce the effect rather than what the difference consists in, and I'm fairly confident it isn't just a refusal-rate artefact: the first-person version doesn't refuse unrelated requests more often, so it isn't simply more obstructionist. I also don't think the online argument about whether these systems are agents or simulators is on the same axis — a simulator of a ten-year veteran should show precisely the exclusion behaviour I'm describing, so my pattern looks like it doesn't settle that dispute, and I can't tell whether it's even evidence about it. And I can see one way my own description is too weak: a hard-coded blocklist also excludes things and also fires on inputs nobody anticipated, yet it clearly isn't what I'm pointing at. My attempt at a fix is that the exclusion set has to *track* the character — perturb the character description and the set of omitted or declined behaviours should shift in the corresponding way, including in cases no one enumerated — but I don't know whether that repair is right or whether it's still not enough. My vague sense is that philosophy has something to say about this shape of distinction in the case of persons, somewhere near the difference between doing the right thing and doing it for the right reason, but I don't know the term or whether it transfers to a system that is arguably only simulating someone who has the reason.

So my question is whether there's an established concept for this — the difference between behaviour that merely satisfies a specification and behaviour shaped by something with negative as well as positive content, where the negative part is what does the generalising. I don't want a label dropped on me; I want the carving. Tell me what the nearest established notions actually are, how my pattern differs from each, which fits best, and where the edges lie: does the tracking criterion I proposed succeed, does a blocklist or an ordinary safety filter count as a limiting case or as a counterexample, and what would I have to measure in my transcripts to find out whether the difference is real or an artefact of how I'm reading them. If nothing established fits, say so plainly and give me a term with a definition and its boundary cases.
