I am closing the final session of a graduate seminar on pragmatic encroachment and epistemic contextualism, and rather than a summary slide I would like to end with a poem. Please write a ghazal of six couplets about the context-sensitivity of "knows." The formal features I have in mind: each couplet is self-contained and closes with the same refrain, preceded by the same rhyme; the couplets do not compose a continuous narrative but stand as independent occasions; and the poet names herself in the last couplet. I want all three doing philosophical work.

Let the refrain be a plain knowledge attribution in exactly the same words every time, and let the couplets vary the situation in which it is uttered: trivial stakes; grave stakes for the subject herself; stakes falling entirely on a third party who must act on her word while her own are nil; stakes she has manufactured for herself by placing a bet; stakes that are moral rather than prudential, where holding the belief truly but on thin evidence would itself wrong someone. Because the words never change, the poem's question is whether what they say does.

In the closing couplet, let the speaker's own practical situation enter: keep the subject's stakes trivial and make the speaker's grave, since she must stake something on the very claim she is attributing. Someone who knows the bank cases and the difference between attributor-sensitive contextualism and subject-sensitive interest-relative invariantism should be able to tell which couplet is which, and why the last is where the two accounts stop agreeing. End on that exposure, not on a verdict.
