I am giving a forty-minute colloquium talk in my own department in three weeks, with about twenty minutes of questions afterwards, and I would like a Q&A preparation sheet for it.

Some background, because it bears on the questions I expect. My father died last spring. In the months after, with no plan and no scholarly purpose, I read a great deal of biography: Plutarch's Parallel Lives mostly, but also some medieval saints' lives and a couple of modern political biographies. It has changed my sense of what one part of moral philosophy is even for, and the talk is my attempt to say why. The personal fact matters for the questions: several people in the room know about my father, and I would rather be ready for that than be surprised by it.

The thesis I am defending: reading lives is not a way of acquiring moral rules and not a way of finding exemplars to imitate; it is a training in noticing, in becoming the sort of person who sees which features of a situation are the morally salient ones. Moreover, the genre has structural features that make it better suited to this than either principles or fiction are, and Plutarch's own practice shows that he understood this even though he is standardly read as an exemplarist.

The argument has three steps.

1. The emulation model is a misreading, including of Plutarch. He opens by saying that he writes lives and not histories; he repeatedly says that a jest, a small kindness, or a look often shows a man's character better than a famous battle does; he pairs an admired life with a flawed one and closes the pair with a comparison rather than a verdict; and several of his subjects are not admirable at all. A genre built that way is not handing you a pattern to copy.

2. What the genre actually exercises is contrastive, case-bound perception. Principles tell you what to do once you have already seen what is relevant, and they cannot tell you what to notice; so if noticing is a skill, it needs its own training, and the paired life is a training device for it — the comparison at the end of a pair is the exercise, not the prize.

3. Why a real life rather than an invented one, given that novels would seem to work the same way: because a life is closed (death fixes it, and the retrospective shape is not the reader's to redesign) and because it resists redescription — the reader cannot adjust the case until the lesson comes out right. Noticing practiced on the actual is answerable to something outside the reader's own imagination; noticing practiced on the invented is not, or not in the same way.

The audience, which I can describe precisely since it is my own department:

- Two senior normative ethicists, one broadly Kantian and one broadly consequentialist, both of whom think moral philosophy's job is to produce action-guiding, extensionally adequate principles, and both of whom regard virtue talk as fine for private life and useless for institutions and policy.
- One philosopher of psychology who is a convinced situationist about character: she thinks the evidence does not support stable, cross-situational traits, and she will hear my talk as resting on exactly the trait attribution her work rejects.
- One historian of ancient philosophy who knows the Parallel Lives well, is fond of Plutarch, and will be annoyed by any anachronism; she will press me on whether I am reading a Platonist moralist of the Roman Empire as if he were a colleague in moral psychology.
- One person who works on narrative identity and is broadly sympathetic, but who will worry that narrative form itself imposes a shape — closure, teleology, the noble ending — that distorts the very perception I say it trains.
- Two graduate students, one of whom is likely to ask why novels or well-designed case studies would not do the job better and more inclusively, and one of whom is likely to press the obvious point about the genre's subject matter: these are lives of elite men, mostly military and political, and what I call noticing may just be a class- and gender-specific set of habits.
- A senior colleague who has known me and my family for years, and who may or may not say something about why I am doing this now.

What I want: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room, in the order they are likely to come, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it and in their vocabulary rather than mine, each with an estimate of how much damage it does, a reply I could give in about ninety seconds, and a note on where that reply is soft. Also: what I should concede in the first five minutes before anyone asks anything; the single reply that has to land if the thesis is to survive the hour; and the objection I am hoping nobody asks. Please do not pad the sheet with objections I would get at a specialist classics or virtue-theory conference — I want the ones from this room.

Please lead with the bottom line in your first sentence: name the one concession or reply on which the talk stands or falls. Then give the preparation sheet. No preamble, and no restating back to me what I have asked for.
