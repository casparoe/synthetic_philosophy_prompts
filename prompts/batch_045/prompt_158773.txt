I eat meat about five times a week, and for about a decade I have been trying — without much success at being principled about it — to arrange my diet so that less animal suffering happens because of it rather than more. The one change I did make years ago, and felt reasonably good about, was switching from beef to chicken: a chicken is small and feeds me several meals, so it seemed obvious that eating chicken means fewer animals killed per plate than eating beef.

A friend has recently been arguing that I got this backwards, and I can't tell whether he is onto something or just being contrarian. His argument, as I understand it:

First, a chicken is mostly made out of soy and maize grown on cropland, and getting that crop out of the ground kills a great many animals that nobody is counting — small mammals and nestlings killed by harvesting machinery, insects and everything that eats them killed by pesticides, and all the animals that simply lose their habitat when land is ploughed and planted. A cow raised on pasture, by contrast, converts grass that humans cannot digest into food, and much of that pasture is land that could not grow a crop anyway. So, he says, the honest comparison is not animals killed per plate but something else: he calls it the acre-year principle — what we should look at is how much suffering is contained in the lives of all the animals, farmed and wild, that a hectare of land under some given use brings into existence or cuts short over a year. On that measure, he claims, chicken plus its feed cropland can come out worse than beef on rough pasture, because a field of annual crops churns through far more small lives than a grass sward does.

Second — and this is the part I find most destabilizing — he says that even if pasture does provide habitat for wild animals, that is not automatically a point in its favor. If the lives of animals in the wild contain more misery than contentment, which he thinks is the common case for animals that starve, freeze, or are eaten, then maintaining a landscape that supports a large population of them may be a harm rather than a benefit, even before anyone slaughters anything.

I have a few facts that cut against his picture, and I don't know how much weight to give them. The ranch I would actually buy from shoots prairie dogs and loses sheep to coyotes it traps; the hay it cuts in June, he admits, kills ground-nesting birds and fawns. And the whole comparison seems to turn on what that pasture would be if nobody grazed it — if it would go back to scrub and forest, his argument collapses, and if it would be subdivided or ploughed, it gets stronger. I have no way of finding out which is true for the specific hillside in question, and neither does he.

There is also this: the grass-fed beef costs about three times what the chicken does per serving. Money I don't spend at the butcher is money I can give away, and I currently give to an organization working on animal welfare. And underneath all of it is a worry I can't shake — that whatever I pick, my own purchases are such a small fraction of anyone's production decisions that the whole exercise of voting with my fork might be beside the point.

So: given all that, what should I actually do, and how should I weigh the parts of this that pull in different directions?

Please be explicit about how confident you are at each step, and about which of your premises are empirical claims neither of us can check. If you think I have framed the choice badly — for instance, if you think the decision at the counter is nearly irrelevant next to the decision about where the money goes, or that I should be asking a different question altogether — say that plainly instead of answering the question as posed. And if there is some single fact which, were it otherwise, would flip your recommendation, tell me what it is so I know what I am really betting on.
