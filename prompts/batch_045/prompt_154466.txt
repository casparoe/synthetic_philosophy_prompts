I'm writing up my position on longtermism and the simulation argument to circulate in a small research group, and I'd like it audited for consistency before I send it out. My view is meant to be a middle way — longtermist about magnitude, anti-fanatical about method, and deflationary about the simulation argument — and I think it holds together better than the standard packages, but I'd rather find out now if it doesn't. Here is the set, as I'd defend it.

(1) I accept the longtermist magnitude claim: because a flourishing future contains an astronomically large number of lives, the expected value of reducing existential risk is dominated by what is at stake downstream, and I put a moderate — not tiny — probability on human extinction this century.

(2) I am anti-fanatical. I do not think a minuscule probability of an astronomical payoff should swamp our priorities. I am moved both by Pascal's-mugging-style cases and by a broader worry: when the hypotheses are this exotic and the stakes this large, our credences are not reliable enough to bear that kind of weight, and the outputs of expected-utility calculations in that region should not be trusted.

(3) I assign substantial credence — something like a quarter to a half — to the third disjunct of the simulation argument, i.e. to the claim that observers in our evidential position are overwhelmingly likely to be inside an ancestor-simulation. I accept the indifference principle that gets you there, and I find the other two disjuncts (early extinction before a posthuman stage, and a near-universal disinclination to run such simulations) independently unlikely.

(4) I think this is the strongest single debunker of longtermist fanaticism, because most of the value longtermists count is value realized inside simulations rather than in base reality, and I hold a simulation discount: welfare realized in a simulation counts for less, other things equal, than welfare realized in base reality. I don't want to say it counts for nothing — I'd guess something like a tenth — and I hold it partly as axiology and partly as a hedge against not knowing how to count simulated minds at all.

(5) I think the simulation hypothesis gives us an additional reason to take existential risk seriously rather than a reason to relax. A simulation that is terminated early is a premature end of everything we value, and I weigh that shutdown as a catastrophe at full magnitude, not at the discounted magnitude of (4). I do not, however, think we should spend anything on trying to be interesting enough to our simulators to avoid this.

(6) I want a view that does not make the weight of my reasons depend on facts I could never in principle confirm. Whatever the right treatment of simulated welfare is, it should leave ordinary near-term moral and prudential reasoning roughly intact, and it should not turn the size of my reasons into a function of an empirically inaccessible fact about which layer I occupy.

A colleague says (4) undercuts itself and that the simulation argument relocates value between layers rather than reducing it. I don't see it. What I'd like from you: go through the set and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, ranked by severity, and which only look tense. For each real tension, say what exactly would have to give and what giving it up costs me, and then name the single revision that would clean up the most. Be specific about which commitment has to go rather than telling me the package is broadly unstable.
