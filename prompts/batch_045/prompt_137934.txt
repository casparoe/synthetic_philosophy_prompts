So a friend and I have been going back and forth for like a month about the American war in Vietnam, and I keep feeling like both of us are reaching for concepts from the decision-theory / alignment side of the blogosphere and using them sloppily. I want somebody to actually do the mapping carefully instead of vibing.

Facts as I'm assuming them, correct me if any are wrong or contested: through the mid-60s the US ran a attrition strategy under Westmoreland — search and destroy, pacification, and progress tracked in enemy killed, kill ratios, hamlet security ratings, that kind of thing; the people generating the numbers had every reason to make them look good; and the numbers kept looking fine right up until the 1968 Tet offensive made it obvious they weren't tracking much. The stated reason for staying in was partly about credibility — the thought that walking away from one commitment would get priced into every other commitment, so the whole containment setup gets more expensive to run.

Two lenses, and I want both of them applied and then interrogated.

Surrogate goals, in the alignment sense: you hand a system a measurable stand-in for what you actually want, it optimizes the stand-in, and the stand-in comes apart from the target. Please lay out what the real goal was supposed to be, what the actual proxies were, who was doing the optimizing, who was doing the gaming, and how much optimization pressure there really was. Then the part I actually care about: I think there are at least three different failure modes that get lumped together as "Goodhart", and I want to know which one this was — (a) the proxy was genuinely correlated with the goal until people started pushing on it hard enough to break the correlation; (b) the proxy was never meaningfully correlated, and nobody ever checked; (c) the proxy was more or less fine as a measurement but was secretly doing a different job, like coordinating a bureaucracy or justifying continued escalation to people back home. My guess is Tet looks like evidence for (a) but the honest answer is (b), and I can't tell. Also: is the interesting failure at the top of the chain or the bottom? "A secure, non-communist South Vietnam" turns into "we control territory" turns into "we control this hill" turns into "enemy bodies on this hill" — is the rot in the last step or the first?

Acausal threats, in the sense where you carry out a costly retaliation not because it causally undoes anything (the bad thing already happened) but because being the sort of agent who retaliates makes you less likely to get targeted in the first place. Is the credibility argument in Vietnam actually that, or is it just plain causal reputation — adversaries watching what you did and updating? This is where I want you to be pedantic: who was the threat even aimed at as an audience (Hanoi, Moscow, Beijing, allies, the US public) and does the argument go through if the audience is running ordinary Bayesian updating on your track record rather than something like modeling your decision algorithm? Does an acausal-threat story require the target to be able to tell what kind of decision procedure you're running, or to be running a similar one itself? And what do the deliberately causal commitment devices do to the analysis — advisers on the ground, tripwire forces, public red lines, agreements that make retreat embarrassing — are those evidence that the acausal framing is unnecessary here, or are they just what you build when you want the acausal thing to be legible?

Then the reverse direction, which is where my friend and I actually part ways: the theory says the agent that can't carry out the costly threat gets targeted. But the side that was visibly paying the costs, burning domestic support, and making the threats is the side that lost, and the side with no exit option at all is the side that won. Does that tell against the acausal-threat framing, or is it consistent with it, or is it just a case where the theory has nothing to say because the two sides' stakes were wildly asymmetric and the theory silently assumes comparable ones? Related: is there a version of this where "credibility" was never aimed at an adversary's decision procedure at all but at internal factions and domestic audiences, in which case the whole acausal framing is a category error dressed up in decision-theory language?

I'd much rather you tell me which of the two framings is doing real explanatory work here and which one is decorative than that you apply both evenly. And say explicitly where each theory goes silent instead of stretching it to cover the case.
