For most of my working life I was an experimental condensed-matter physicist, and in retirement I have drifted into the philosophy department's Tuesday reading group and, somewhat to my own surprise, onto the Science Integrity Committee of the Merrivale Science Museum, where I am the token natural scientist. We have before us the script for a planetarium show, *Deep Time*, which is scheduled to open in September and whose narration is due to be locked on 15 August. One ninety-second segment has split the committee. In it the narrator explains that on a number of otherwise respectable cosmological models the overwhelming majority of observers having exactly the sort of experience you are having now are not organisms produced by evolution in an ordered universe but momentary "Boltzmann brains": brains assembled by an improbable thermal fluctuation out of an equilibrium or near-equilibrium background, complete with apparent memories of a life never lived and apparent perceptions of a world not present. The narrator then invites the audience to notice that they are, right now, having just such an experience, and asks why they should not conclude that they are one. The reassurance is staged as an interactive bit: visitors press a button on their seatback if they think they are a fluctuation, and the near-unanimous result is displayed on the dome, after which the narrator says that the answer lies in the tally itself — a fluctuation far more likely produces a single disembodied brain than an entire auditorium of people enjoying mutually consistent views of the same show, so the shared and orderly character of what you seem to be experiencing is strong evidence against the hypothesis.

Our education director loves the segment and wants it kept as written; a trustee who is a retired cardiologist wants it cut, on the ground that we would be telling schoolchildren that their memories may be fabrications, and in any case that the segment's own reasoning destroys itself, since if the visitors were fluctuations then the auditorium, the seatback buttons, the tally on the dome and the narrator herself would all be ingredients of the illusion, and nothing in the argument could be trusted to deliver the reassuring conclusion. My own instinct, as a physicist, is that the trustee's objection proves too much: the same self-undermining structure turns up in ordinary scepticism about an external world, and we do not on that account forbid planetarium shows about mirages or optical illusions. But I have not been able to shake the feeling that there is a real difference here, and my fourteen-year-old granddaughter, who sat through the preview, asked me in the car whether her memory of the drive over was real — which is precisely the effect the trustee fears. The writer has offered a third option, which we have not yet discussed: keep the scenario but drop the interactive tally and reframe the whole thing as a diagnosis of cosmological models rather than as a live hypothesis about the audience, the narrator saying in effect that a theory which implies that typical observers are fluctuations thereby indicts itself and is to be rejected for that reason, not believed. We meet Thursday and must choose between cutting the segment, keeping it as written, or adopting the reframed version with the writer's revised narration circulated to us before the 15 August lock.

Please help me draft the short decision memo I will circulate. Adopt the stance of a philosopher of science who works on cosmology and has served on museum advisory boards, and before writing, spend a moment brainstorming the distinct ways a visitor could come away from this segment misled, and which of those the museum is actually answerable for. Give me your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no concluding paragraph: (1) the recommendation — which of the three options, with the date by which it must be executed; (2) the central reason, stated so that a physicist and a trustee could both follow it; (3) what, if anything, distinguishes the Boltzmann-brain worry from the familiar external-world scepticism it superficially resembles; (4) the strongest version of the argument for whichever option you are rejecting, in a form its advocates on this committee would recognise as their own; and (5) the one or two sentences of narration the writer should be told to substitute, worded so they could go straight into the script.
