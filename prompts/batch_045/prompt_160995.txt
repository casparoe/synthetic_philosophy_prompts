I'm putting together a one-page handout for the philosophy-of-science reading group I run — a decision tree that takes someone from a commitment we all actually share to a named position on reduction and emergence. I want it printable, ASCII-ish, somthing I can hand out and argue about in one sitting.

The entry commitment, fixed: everybody in the room grants (i) that macro-level descriptions in the special sciences — thermodynamics, population dynamics, whatever — are sometimes genuinely explanatory and not just convenient shorthand, and (ii) that whenever a macro fact holds it's made true by microphysical goings-on, with nothing injected from outside.

Constraints on the tree:

- No more than seven branch questions. Each one has to be phrased so that somebody who has never heard the words "reductionism" or "emergentism" could still answer it. Nothing like "are higher-level properties real?", and none of the leaf names may appear inside a question.
- Anchor every branch question in one running case, and I want it to be the traffic jam: all the cars moving forward, the jam itself propagating backwards through the line at a speed set by car density rather than by any individual car's speed, invisible from the drivers seat, and perfectly reproducible in a simulation built from rules about individual drivers. Cash out each node as a question about that jam.
- One node has to be my "peeking" problem: you can often derive the macro description from the micro one only after you've already used the macro description to choose the right coarse-graining, boundary conditions, or variables. Is that a failure of reduction, or just what reduction looks like when you do it honestly? I want it as a real fork, not a footnote.
- Leaves that must appear: Nagel-style bridge-law reduction; mechanistic/constitutive reduction of the Craver sort (decomposition rather than derivation); token-identity non-reductive physicalism; Bedau-style weak emergence (the 1997 one); the old British-emergentist view where the emergent level brings its own irreducible laws and some downward causation; eliminativism about the macro property; pluralist antireductionism of the Cartwright sort; and the Batterman/renormalization-group style view that macro behaviour is autonomous because of singular limits and the thermodynamic limit rather than because of anything ontologically new.
- Plus a home for my own hobby horse, which nobody in the group likes and which I suspect is one of the above wearing a hat. Call it descent-without-translation: the macro level gets its own dynamical equations; you cannot get them from the microdynamics by any amount of coarsening, averaging, or ignoring; and yet you cannot run the macrodynamics at all unless the micro stuff is sitting there underneath it. Either give it a leaf, or tell me which existing leaf it really collapses into and what I'd have to give up to say so.

Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names: lay out the questions first, say how many answer-combinations they allow, then place the actual positions in the cells. Mark the empty cells and say for each whether nobody lives there because it's incoherent, because it's just unattractive, or because nobody's bothered. And flag any pair of leaves your tree genuinely cannot separate instead of inventing a question to pry them apart — I'd rather have two arrows running into the same box than a fake distinction.

One line per leaf on the most awkward thing that position has to swallow. Leave consciousness out entirely; we did it last term and it derails everything.
