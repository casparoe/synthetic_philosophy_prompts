Please write in American English. I am trying to get clear on a question that is often posed as though it were already well-posed: which computations matter morally — that is, which physical implementations of which computational processes are the ones whose welfare, if any, we owe consideration to. My worry is that the question cannot even be asked until we fix what it is for a system to implement one computation rather than another, and three features of implementation seem to make that hard: glut (under a sufficiently contrived mapping, nearly any physical system can be described as implementing nearly any computation), grain (anything that implements a computation at a fine grain also trivially implements a host of coarser-grained ones, with different state spaces and different transition structures), and approximation (real systems track the intended state transitions only over a restricted class of inputs, and then only mostly).

Explain how each of these three constrains the moral question, and especially how the standard reply to glut — that genuine implementation requires the system's causal organization to mirror the computation's state-transition structure under a mapping that is not gerrymandered — interacts with approximation. Then take this case: two systems are behaviorally identical across every input that either will in fact ever receive, and both track the same target computation over those inputs. They differ only counterfactually: system A would keep tracking the computation across a wide range of inputs it will never receive, while system B, whose organization is tuned to the actual input distribution, would diverge wildly on most of them. Does the requirement of counterfactually robust causal organization force us to say that A implements the computation and B does not, and hence that moral status can turn on how a system would behave in circumstances that never arise? Tell me which answer is most defensible and what it costs — in particular, whether it commits us to moral status being intrinsic to a system's causal structure, or to its depending partly on an interpretation or idealization that we impose.
