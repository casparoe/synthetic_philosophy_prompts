I teach a senior seminar on collective action — philosophy majors, most of whom have never seen a payoff matrix, so I keep the formalism light — and I've ended up defending a line I'm not sure I can actually cash out. My colleague in the econ department says it's all just Prisoner's Dilemmas and externalities with better PR, and I want to know whether I have a real answer or just a vibe. The line is that the "Moloch" / multipolar-trap framing that gets thrown around in rationalist-adjacent corners of the internet is picking out something the standard toolkit doesn't, and that the difference matters because it changes what you'd do about the case in front of you. Three moving parts, and tell me which ones you'd attack. (1) The cases that matter aren't all cases where everyone in the race ends up worse off than if nobody had raced. Often the racers are doing fine and the cost lands on people who aren't at the table — future people, bystanders, the animals, whoever. Call these outsider-bearing races as opposed to insider-losing ones. Mix them up and you reach for the wrong remedy: coordination among the racers in the second kind, and something like liability or a hard external constraint in the first. (2) A lot of what gets called a race to the bottom isn't a bad equilibrium you're stuck in, it's a path. Each step is improving for the people taking it, given where they've ended up, but the endpoint is one the people at the start would have rejected out of hand — credential inflation, or a sector ratcheting up a risky capability because each increment is defensible given what everyone else has already done. (3) The framing earns its keep normatively, not just descriptively: it relocates blame from individuals who had no unilateral escape onto the structure, and I claim that relocation is both true and politically necessary, since exhorting participants to be less greedy has, as far as I can tell, never once worked.

So: what would count against it? Not the standard critic's hits — that it's just a Prisoner's Dilemma with mood lighting, or that it lets participants off the hook — but the things a defender of my own view should concede are evidence against it, sorted into fatal, serious, and merely annoying. Concretely: what observation or argument would show that the distinction in (1) is a distinction without a difference? Is there a test that separates an outsider-bearing race from an insider-losing one that I could actually run on a real case, or does it come down to whose welfare I'm counting, in which case it's a moral disagreement wearing a game-theory costume? For (2): how do I tell a genuine ratchet from a case where people simply changed their minds, or were lied to, or were pushed by whoever stood to gain — i.e. when is "no one wanted this" true and when is it a story the losers tell afterwards? For (3): is there actual evidence that structural explanations demobilize, that hearing "it's the structure, not you" makes people less likely to refuse, and should I weigh that as a cost rather than wave it off as a rhetorical risk? The thing that would move me most personally: a well-documented case where coordination was genuinely available and cheap, the participants knew it, and they raced anyway and said afterwards they'd do it again. I suspect there are several of these and I'm not sure how much damage they do to me. Also say which of the defeaters have arguably already arrived, in rough historical cases you'd expect an educated reader to know, and which are live possibilities rather than merely logically available escape routes.

Two more things I want, and please be blunt about both. First, insulation: I think my view is better protected than it deserves to be, because any apparent counterexample can be absorbed by saying the coordination wasn't really available, or that if the participants wanted to race then it wasn't really a race to the bottom. Tell me whether I've qualified myself into unfalsifiability, and whether that's a fatal problem for a view of this kind or whether a diagnostic concept — one whose job is to redescribe situations and redirect blame rather than to predict — is legitimately allowed that kind of protection. Second, predictions: what does my framing forecast that the plain-vanilla PD-and-externality story doesn't? My own candidates are a willingness-to-exit test (offer the participants costless coordination and see whether they take it) and a claim about phenomenology (participants in a real trap report feeling powerless and say they'd stop if only the others would). Say which of those are weaker than I think, and what a rival would predict instead. Don't bother with citations or a reading list — I know the literature well enough to fill those in myself, and I mostly want to know whether I should keep teaching this or quietly drop the lecture.
