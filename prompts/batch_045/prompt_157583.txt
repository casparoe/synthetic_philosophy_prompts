I chair the collections committee of a mid-sized city museum — roughly 120,000 objects, of which perhaps 4,000 fall within the periods and regions that restitution claims concern. We receive four to six inquiries or claims a year. Our capacity is two registrars, a shared conservator, and no in-house lawyer; outside legal advice is billed by the hour and the board resents paying for it. Triage has to happen within a week of an inquiry arriving, and any recommendation to return has to reach the board's collections subcommittee, which meets quarterly, as a file a non-specialist trustee can read in twenty minutes.

Please write me a decision procedure my committee can actually run: the questions in the order we should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops with a no, and the point at which it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Build it on the following positions, which the committee has argued itself into over the last two years and which I want the procedure to honour rather than reopen:

a) The anti-washing rule: a later good-faith purchase does not cure an originally tainted taking while the dispossession is still live — that is, within the living memory of the community concerned, or before a genuine change in the political relation between that community and the power that took the object. Work out how "live" is to be determined on our records, and what to do when the record is silent.
b) Burden of proof: for anything acquired under a colonial administration with an incomplete chain, the gap counts against us rather than against the claimant. Silence in our own documentation is evidence, not neutrality.
c) Authorisation by an occupying or imperial power does not by itself make a removal legitimate — but tell me what work, if any, a surviving document of permission can still do in the procedure.
d) Capacity: where the receiving institution's storage or conservation weaknesses are traceable to the same historical relation as the taking, they cannot be made a condition of return. Remediation is something we fund, not something we require. Where the weaknesses are not so traceable, decide whether and how much they may count.
e) Display value: any argument from what the object teaches in our galleries has to survive a surrogate test — could we teach the same thing with a replica, a digital surrogate, or the documentation together with the story of the object's removal? Decide what passes and what fails, and say who judges.
f) Role reversal: any condition we attach to a return must be one we would accept if the roles were reversed.
g) Claimants: continuity of grievance matters more than formal state locus. A body that has carried the claim forward and is recognised by the community should count even without state backing. Tell me what to do when two such bodies claim the same object, and what to do when a state claims without them.
h) Sets and parts: a "set" is our curatorial category more often than theirs. Say when a group may be divided and when it may not, and whether we may retain a token piece.

At every fork where reasonable people could go either way, commit to one branch and give your reason. I do not want a menu of options with their trade-offs; I want a procedure with your choices baked in, each with a one-line note saying that a reasonable committee could have gone the other way and why you went yours. Where a step is irreducibly a value judgement, mark it as a place where the procedure stops and a person signs off, and say which person.

Then run it on three live files:

1. A Benin-type brass plaque bequeathed to us in 1938 by the family of a colonial officer, with a documented chain going back to the British punitive expedition that sacked Benin City in the 1890s. The claim comes from Nigeria's federal antiquities authority and from the court of the Oba. Tell me what paperwork is sufficient, what timing the procedure implies, and — this is the part the board will fight about — which conditions we may *not* attach. They will want a long-term loan back, a shared rotating display, and a delay until our digitisation project finishes. Say which of these the procedure blocks and why.
2. A fragment of sculpture from the Parthenon, bought at auction in 1921 from a private collection, with a chain going back to the early nineteenth century but not to Elgin himself. The claim comes from the Greek state. The committee is split on whether the gap in our chain is our problem under (b), and on how much the permission documents from the imperial authority then in place are worth under (c). Commit. Also say how an architectural-reunification argument should be weighed against an encyclopedic-display argument at the display-value step.
3. The one where I expect to dislike the answer. A carved figure bought from a dealer in 1961, whose file shows it was carved in the 1920s and sold by its maker, with nothing in the record connecting it to any taking; the claim comes from a national heritage body on the argument that all such material belongs to the national patrimony. My instinct is that refusing feels wrong and will read as special pleading. Tell me honestly whether the procedure returns a no here, and if it does, what the committee should say out loud when it says no.

Finally, be explicit about what the procedure cannot do: where it will misfire, which claims it cannot adjudicate at all, and what we should do with those. We know the general shape of the restitution literature and do not need citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; what we lack is something we can run. Keep it to something my committee can hold to a few pages.
