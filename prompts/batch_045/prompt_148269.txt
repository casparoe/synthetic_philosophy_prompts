Hi — I co-host a long-ish interview podcast (me and a friend from grad school; we tape for about 50 minutes and usually only get through seven or eight questions) and next month's guest is a political theorist who's just published a book arguing that Carnap's distinction between internal and external questions, together with the principle of tolerance — you pick your linguistic or institutional framework, and you judge it by how well it works rather than by whether it's true — gives us a better grip on economic and institutional planning than any appeal to justice, rights, or the common good. She calls the resulting view something like "framework pluralism about planning" (I may be mangling the label), and her signature addition to Carnap is what she calls the non-regression clause: a revision of a framework is admissible only if it preserves, for every party affected by it, the standing to raise claims inside the framework and have them adjudicated — you can't restructure things so as to put some group outside the reach of internal questions altogether. She also claims this lets her dodge both technocracy and moralism, and in the couple of interviews I've heard she has a reliable escape hatch: the moment something bites she says "that's just moralism in a lab coat" or "that's an external question wearing an internal question's clothes." I definately don't want an episode where she restates the set-up for forty minutes, and no warm-ups about how she got into Carnap or what the Vienna Circle was like.

What I want is eight to ten questions that press on the machinery, each written as something I could actually say out loud, each with a follow-up already keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. At least one question each on: (a) what the currency of "what works" is supposed to be when we compare frameworks, and — when she says that's itself a question internal to some higher-order framework — what stops the regress; (b) whether the non-regression clause is a moral side-constraint smuggled into a view that says external questions are only about expediency, and what happens to her position if it is; (c) the complaint of the losers — workers displaced by a planning decision, say — whether on her view they're merely on the losing side of a practical choice rather than wronged, and whether a framework with no compensation provision therefore wrongs nobody; (d) whether tolerance extends to frameworks that would abolish tolerance, and why the answer isn't just an expedient shrug; (e) what work the "scientific" in scientific planning still does if the framework settles what counts as a reason and evidence is always internal to one; (f) who the "we" is that does the choosing, and what makes the choice legitimate if it isn't truth-apt; (g) whether the internal/external distinction even stays sharp when the framework is a legal-institutional one where competent practitioners genuinely disagree about what the framework says; and (h) one concrete case run through live — I was thinking municipal zoning, whether a city should allow duplexes on lots currently zoned single-family — where the twist is that the people most affected (renters, people who'd move in later, people already priced out) mostly weren't participants in whatever framework choice is supposed to have authorized the rule. End with a question about what would actually count as evidence against her view. And for two or three of the questions, arrange them so that the "moralism in a lab coat" reply gets progressively harder to reach for, instead of being available from her first sentence.

Last thing: put a short aside to me (not for air) under each question marking what's settled among people who actually work on this and what's genuinely contested — I gather there's real disagreement about whether Carnap's principle of tolerance has any political content at all or is purely methodological, and about whether it's even right to read him as having a politics, and I don't want to build an episode around a gotcha that half the literature would reject. Where a question only lands on one contested reading, say so and tell me what I'd be committing to by keeping it. And please don't point me at anything to read or cite anything — I can't chase down references and I don't want her bibliography read out on air either.
