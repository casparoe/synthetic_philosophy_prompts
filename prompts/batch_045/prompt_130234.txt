Hi — I need a proper Q&A prep sheet for a paper I'm giving, not a list of twelve generic objections I could have thought of myself.

The setup: it's a one-day workshop on contingency and agency in early modern political thought, I've got 40 minutes plus a whole hour of discussion because the convenor couldn't fill the last slot. The room is about twenty people, mostly faculty. Specifically: three intellectual historians in the contextualist mould who'll want to know what a quattrocento humanist could possibly have meant by fortuna and who treat any whiff of anachronism as fatal; two Renaissance republicanism people who read the Discourses as the real Machiavelli and the Prince as an extended job application; one historian of astrology and humoral medicine who will insist that fortuna was a live technical discourse and not a colourful metaphor for "stuff that happens"; a classicist who'll want to know what's left of my reading once you subtract Polybius, Sallust and Livy; one analytically trained philosopher who'll press hard on anything that looks like a capacity/disposition distinction; and an emeritus at the back who reads everything as esoteric writing. Assume they've all read chapter 25 of the Prince recently and that at least two of them are bored of it.

My thesis, as I'll present it: fortuna isn't luck or chance in general, it's the portion of what happens to a prince that comes from the timing of other people's actions and that his own settled way of proceeding can't absorb. And virtù isn't a set of traits, or even a general disposition to act well — it's a second-order capacity to drop your own modo, your habits and methods and temperament, when the occasion demands it, and pick up another one. That's supposed to explain why he can praise impetuousness in one place and caution in another without contradicting himself, and why on my reading success isn't evidence of virtù and failure isn't evidence that it was missing. I've got a diagnostic I'm rather pleased with, which I'm calling the two-tempi test: take any trait he praises, check whether he praises its opposite somewhere in the same work; if he does, virtù can't be a first-order disposition at all. And in the last five minutes I want to float a claim I'm not sure I believe: that this is also why republics fare better against fortune than principalities — not because their citizens are more virtuous one by one, but because a republic holds several incompatible modi at once, so the switch doesn't have to happen inside a single person's head. Call it redundancy.

What I want: the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this specific room, ordered by how much damage they'd do rather than by how clever they are, each attributed to who'll raise it and phrased the way that person would actually say it — I want the astrology person to sound like the astrology person, not like a philosopher with a hobby.
