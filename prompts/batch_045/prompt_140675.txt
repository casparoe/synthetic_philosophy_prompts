Channel a professional moral philosopher who's also served on a grant panel a few times: someone who knows what makes a question tractable and what makes reviewers start checking their phones. Think it through carefully, and before you draft anything, do a few minutes of messy brainstorming about where the real crux of my idea actually lies — I'd rather see the thinking than a tidy abstract. Setup: I'm applying for a solo nine-month fellowship (small grant, no research assistant, no real travel budget). The deliverable they want described is roughly a 7000-word paper plus a one-page "how to compare" protocol aimed at people who genuinely have to make these calls — memorial-funding boards, curriculum committees, reparations or compensation commissions. Two pages of proposal, call it 1000–1200 words. I'll add the citations myself, so please don't include a bibliography or drop in named references; just refer to positions in the abstract ("the uniqueness thesis", "the flat incomparabilist", "the everything-is-on-one-scale consequentialist"). If you reach for a historical illustration, keep it to episodes any educated reader already knows and don't attach numbers, dates, or death tolls to them — I'll fill those in, and I don't want the proposal resting on a half-remembered figure.

The half-formed idea I'm bringing: the literature seems stuck on whether you *can* rank historical atrocities — is it even possible, is it obscene. I suspect that's the wrong fight. My hunch is that "comparison" is doing three or four different jobs at once, and once you pull them apart the metaphysical panic either evaporates or at least moves somewhere more interesting. Concretely, I want to split things along three axes: (1) *what is being compared* — a single event, a policy, a regime, a practice lasting centuries — since total-suffering rankings look hostage to how you cut history into units at all; (2) *along which dimension* — death toll, duration, cruelty, betrayal of a state toward its own citizens, destruction of a culture, how deliberate it all was — where each dimension can yield a different ordering; (3) *what the comparison is for* — deciding which to prevent, which to commemorate, whom to blame, how much to compensate. Guess: comparability is purpose-relative, and the feeling that ranking is obscene is really the absence of any single weighting of the dimensions that would serve every purpose at once.

Two things I really want worked in, because they're the parts I think are actually new. First, the forced-choice test: if an agent can prevent only one of two atrocities, they have to weigh them somehow, so the incomparabilist owes us a story about what deliberation even looks like in that case — and it's an open question whether that's a reductio of the view or just an honest cost of holding it. Second, the witness asymmetry: how offensive it feels to rank seems to shift depending on who is doing the ranking (a descendant of the victims, a descendant of the perpetrators, a detached foreign academic, a government issuing an apology), which suggests that at least part of "you can't compare" is a claim about who is licensed to say certain things rather than a claim about what is true. And there's an ugly tension buried in that: the people in the best epistemic position to compare are exactly the ones we're least comfortable asking.

So write me the proposal. It needs: the question stated precisely enough that it has a determinate answer; why the standard positions — the uniqueness claim, flat incomparabilism, and the everything-is-measurable view — each leave it open; the approach, and why it beats the obvious alternatives (I definately don't want another survey of the uniqueness debates, and pure conceptual analysis of "worse than" feels like it would just spin its wheels); a month-by-month-ish method for nine months; what the possible outcomes are and which considerations would decide between them; something concrete about what the one-page protocol would actually contain; three specific things that would count as the project failing; and an honest paragraph on what I'd have to cut to fit nine months. Don't hedge the whole thing into mush — commit to the version of this question you think is actually worth nine months of somebody's life.
