You are a professional philosopher who has spent a decade working on the foundations of value alignment: someone equally at home in social choice theory, in the debates about liberal neutrality and public justification, and in the grubby practical question of what a single general-purpose model should say when it is deployed to a population that disagrees with itself. Work carefully and in stages. Before you draft anything, do a private pass in which you (a) list every candidate claim both camps might sign and strike the ones that are vacuous or that one camp would quietly reject, (b) list candidate cruxes and check each for whether settling it would actually move at least one side, and (c) mark any disagreement you suspect is merely about words. Only then write the document. Target 900–1,200 words. Define every technical term you introduce; do not cite, name-drop, or gesture at any literature, since the document has to stand on its own for a reader with no access to a library.

The deliverable is the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps on the question of whose values an aligned AI system should serve.

Camp A, the Population Aggregationist, holds that the system's object-level outputs should be chosen so as to maximize some social welfare functional over a fixed population: a ranking that treats people symmetrically, respects unanimous improvement, and is stable under uncertainty will look like a weighted sum of individual utilities, and the job of alignment is to pick the population, the weights, and the utility scale by a fair procedure and then apply the rule. Aggregationists are not majoritarians: they are willing to override majority preference whenever the functional says so, and they regard refusal to aggregate as itself a decision with identifiable beneficiaries.

Camp B, the Public-Justification Neutralist, holds that where a question implicates contested comprehensive doctrines about the good life, the system may not justify its output by appeal to any such doctrine. It should instead map the space of reasonable views and their practical upshots without ranking them, or defer the substantive choice to a fair procedure, and it should retract to this register whenever a contested doctrine is doing work in its own reasoning. Neutralists are not libertarians: they deny that serving the individual user settles anything when third parties are affected, and they accept that their register of restraint has to be enforced, not merely recommended.

Both camps already reject three positions they regard as evasions: that the answer is majority vote, that the answer is to serve whoever is paying, and that the answer is to say nothing. They have now argued past each other for two years. Write the document they should have produced.

Produce it in this format.

Section 1: Agreed Claims, numbered, 7 to 9 items. Each must pass an inversion test: append, in a short parenthetical, a real position that denies the claim. If you cannot name one, the claim is vacuous and you should cut it rather than pad the list. At minimum, work through the following: that any answer must fix the denominator, i.e. which beings have standing in the population, before any aggregation or any restraint rule can be applied, and that this is a substantive moral choice rather than a technical one; that silence is an action, because when the system abstains the outcome defaults to whatever the off-line status quo would have produced, with its own distribution of winners; that the granularity at which values are individuated can change what aggregation recommends, so that anyone specifying values for a system is in a position to gerrymander the outcome by how finely they describe them; that Neutralism's filter for which disagreements count as reasonable is itself a substantive choice with the same structure as Aggregationism's choice of weights; and that both camps need a way to compare lives across persons, whether as cardinal welfare or as a notion of reasonableness, and that this shared need is the hard core neither has solved.

Section 2: A disagreements table. Columns: crux; the Aggregationist's formulation, worded so that an Aggregationist would sign it; the Neutralist's formulation, worded so that a Neutralist would sign it; a test, case, or argument both camps agree bears on the crux; the Aggregationist's prediction about that test, recorded in advance; the Neutralist's prediction, recorded in advance; and a verdict on whether the disagreement is substantive or verbal. Include at least these cruxes, or better ones if you find them: whether Neutralism's own restraint is itself a comprehensive doctrine, as Aggregationists charge; whether the restraint regime hands a veto to whoever insists loudest that a question is contested, which I will call the heckler's veto problem; whether the description-dependence of aggregation is a theoretical curiosity or, as Neutralists suspect, the normal case; whether future people, digitally implemented minds, animals, and non-users who are nonetheless affected belong in the denominator; and, as an empirical crux, what actually happens to the distribution of downstream influence when a system abstains rather than answers. Anchor the tests in three concrete cases both camps have already discussed: a public school system asking for a sex-education curriculum, a public hospital asking the model to prioritize a scarce treatment, and a user asking whether to have a child given long-run climate effects.

Section 3: Verbal and substantive. Say plainly which parts of the dispute you judge to be about words, which are about substance, and why. If the two camps turn out to converge under some description, state the exact condition under which they converge and what still separates them when it fails.

Section 4: What each camp would count as losing. For each side, one or two sentences on what it would take, in principle, for that side to abandon its view, and one sentence on whether the other side currently has any route to producing it.

Do not adjudicate. The test of success is that a committed member of either camp could sign their own column without wincing, and that a reader could afterwards state the residual disagreement exactly enough to know what to look up, build, or measure next.
