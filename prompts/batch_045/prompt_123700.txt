I'm in the final year of a philosophy degree and taking a special subject on Leibniz — we have been through the Discourse on Metaphysics, the Monadology and the Theodicy, and my assessed essay is due in a fortnight. The essay question I want to write is what Leibniz would have said about the non-identity problem, which of course postdates him by roughly two and a half centuries. Here is the case I want him put in front of.

A state adopts an energy policy which, as a foreseeable side effect, changes who gets conceived over the coming century: different parents, different conceptions, an entirely different population. The number of persons is exactly the same as it would have been under the alternative policy, and every resulting person has a life well worth living, on average slightly better than the lives of those who would otherwise have existed. Standard person-affecting intuitions stall here, because no one in the second scenario exists in the first, and yet it feels as though there is something to be said against the policy.

I want you to reason through what Leibniz's own machinery would deliver on that case, and to press on two things in particular. First: his doctrine of the complete individual concept, and the conceivability of substances differing only by origin, looks like an unusually strong origin essentialism, so he should perhaps refuse to say that the resulting people are worse off or benefited — but then where, if anywhere, does the wrongness go? Second: whether his optimism about the actual world, together with his definition of justice as benevolence guided by wisdom, lets him call the policy wrong without anyone's being harmed — for instance as a defect in the selection of a world rather than as an injury to a person — and whether he would treat my same-number variant differently from the ordinary different-number case. One worry I can't settle is whether his optimism sneaks in the conclusion that no actually adopted policy could be wrong at all, given that it belongs to the best of all possible worlds; if you think that worry is misplaced, say why, drawing the line between divine choice and human action as he would.

Do not give me a bibliography, reading list or citations: I already have the reading, and you cannot check library sources anyway. I want the argument, and I want you to mark where you are extrapolating from his system rather than reporting something he commits to. Please write in British English, in continuous prose, and keep the whole answer under 400 words. Close with two sentences: one saying how confident you are in the reconstruction overall, and one singling out the specific claim you are least confident about.
