A friend of mine, Mira, is the person everyone at her hospital network goes to when a comparison table comes out backwards, and I've been trying for a while to figure out what rule she's actually running. I cant pin it down. Her verdicts so far:

(1) Textbook case: the more invasive treatment beats the newer one inside both the small-stone and the large-stone group, but loses overall because it was used much more often on large stones. She says the invasive one is better and the aggregate just tracks who got sent to which treatment.
(2) A reversal that only shows up when you split patients by whether they completed the full course. She says throw it out, you've conditioned on something that happens after treatment starts, and go with the aggregate.
(3) A reversal that appears when you chop a continuous variable into arbitrary bins and disappears when you chop it differently, with no story about why the bins should matter causally. She says aggregate again.
(4) A clean pre-treatment severity marker with a big within-stratum reversal, but the two strata have 9 and 13 patients in them. She says there isn't enough evidence to act on and refuses to switch anything.

The case on her desk next week: two drugs, and the stratifier is a severity grade read off a baseline scan. Within every grade the new drug does better; overall the old drug does better, because the new drug went mostly to the sickest patients. Two catches. The real-time reads of those scans were lost, so the grades in the dataset were assigned afterwards by radiologists who knew which drug each patient got and how the patient did. And our hospital sees way more late-stage patients than the trial population did.
