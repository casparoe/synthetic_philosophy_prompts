**Three things we keep calling one thing** (draft)

Everyone has seen Simpson's paradox: a treatment beats control in every subgroup and loses overall, or the other way around. The moral people take away is "aggregated numbers lie, disaggregate." I think that moral is close to useless and occasionally dangerous, and I want to replace it. I think there are three genuinely different things going on, and they have different fixes.

**Type M: different weights.** Users on mobile and desktop. Treatment beats control by 10 points within each: mobile 20% vs 10%, desktop 90% vs 80%. Overall: treatment 270/1000 = 27%, control 900/2000 = 45%. Treatment is 18 points worse overall. No mystery: 90% of the treatment sample is mobile, 50% of the control sample is, and mobile converts badly. You can split the overall gap into two pieces exactly:

overall gap = (within-stratum gap, weighted by the control's mix) + (mix term)

= [0.5 × (0.20 − 0.10) + 0.5 × (0.90 − 0.80)] + [(0.9 − 0.5) × 0.20 + (0.1 − 0.5) × 0.90]

= +0.10 + (−0.28) = −0.18

So there's a +10 point "real" effect and a −28 point penalty that comes purely from who ended up in which arm. Note the consequence: if the arms have the same mix, the overall difference is a plain weighted average of the stratum differences and has to land between them, so a reversal on the difference scale is arithmetically impossible. Type M requires imbalance. In a randomized experiment, big imbalance is basically always a bug — sample ratio mismatch, a ramp that shifted mid-flight, a bot filter that fired on one arm only. That's why I think Type M is best read as "your assignment system is broken," not as a philosophical puzzle.

**Type S: different scale.** Now a perfectly balanced experiment. Half "easy" users, half "hard," same in both arms. Within easy: control 10%, treatment 25%. Within hard: control 50%, treatment 75%. Both stratum odds ratios are exactly 3.0. Pool it and you get treatment 50% vs control 30%, a marginal odds ratio of 2.33. So the aggregate is *outside* the range of the subgroup numbers despite perfect balance and zero confounding. Look at the other scales for the same data: risk differences are 0.15 and 0.25 in the strata, 0.20 overall — sits right between them. Risk ratios are 2.5 and 1.5, 1.67 overall — also between them. Only the odds ratio does the weird thing, because it doesn't average the way differences and ratios do. My understanding is that the risk difference and risk ratio are "collapsible" and the odds ratio isn't, which is why this can only bite you on the OR (and I think hazard ratios have the same problem). So Type S isn't about aggregation at all: you can be perfectly balanced and still not have any single number that is *the* effect. The number also moves if you deploy to a population with a different easy/hard mix, which nobody ever writes down.

**Type C: stratifying on something the treatment moves.** This is the one I see constantly in product work. We ship something, then compare only the users who actually triggered it — saw the tooltip, opened the modal, whatever. But who triggers is itself an effect of the treatment, so the stratum isn't a fixed group of people; the treatment changes its membership. Same for segmenting by anything the treatment can shift: session count, plan tier, notification opt-in. The within-stratum comparison then isn't a contrast between two possible worlds for the same people, it's a comparison between different people, and reversals here are close to meaningless.

Except — and this is where I think the usual moral is actively harmful — sometimes the shifting mix is the point. If the treatment is a "download the app" prompt and it moves people off mobile web onto desktop, then the aggregate drop is real and is the thing you wanted to know; the stratified numbers give you the effect holding device fixed, which may be the least interesting quantity on the table. It's not "aggregate bad, disaggregate good." It's total effect versus controlled direct effect, and which one you want depends on the decision you're making.

**The bit I actually care about.** My claim: an effect isn't a number, it's a function of three arguments — (a) which population you average over, (b) which scale you compare on, (c) which causal pathways you hold fixed. Simpson's paradox is what it looks like when you silently change one of those arguments and then act surprised the number moved. Every "resolution" of the paradox I've run into is really a decision about the arguments, presented as a discovery about the data. So the paradox isn't a fact about the world and isn't a fact about arithmetic; it's a naming failure. We use one word for a whole family of quantities. As an engineer that's an extremely familiar kind of bug.

**My hot take, which I'm not sure about:** we should stop reporting odds ratios in experiment readouts altogether and just report risk differences. They're collapsible, and they're the ones that map onto "how many extra conversions did this actually buy us."
