I'm giving the closing plenary at a small interdisciplinary meeting on periodization — about forty people: a dozen stratigraphers and Quaternary palaeoecologists, several historians and historical geographers, a scattering of philosophers of science in my own line of work, and two curators from a natural history museum who are already designing an exhibit around whatever the meeting concludes. The organiser asked us not to close with slides, and I let myself be talked into reading a poem instead of summarising my paper; the paper is finished, the poem is not, and I would rather not improvise something sentimental at the podium. What I want is a sestina: six stanzas of six lines, six end-words rotating in the standard pattern, then the three-line envoi — thirty-nine lines, which at reading pace is about three minutes.

The content is an argument I have been making in the paper and would like the form to carry. I distinguish three things the word can be doing when people ask when the Anthropocene began. There is the chronostratigraphic question, where a unit is a body of rock and its base has to be pinned to a marker that is effectively synchronous worldwide, which is why fallout radionuclides and isotopic excursions carry such weight and why the Industrial Revolution is such an awkward candidate: it starts in one coalfield and spreads outward over a century, so it has no single base. There is the event question, in the sense of a geological event with a drawn-out onset whose most legible deposits have not been laid down yet — we are dating something whose clearest layers are still in the future. And there is the historical question, about when it became possible for human beings to understand themselves as a force of this kind, where the interesting candidates are not spikes at all but things like the Columbian exchange and the dispossession whose signature is a change in pollen and charcoal in a lake bed. My thesis is that the golden spike is not a discovery that settles the question but a decision given the grammar of a discovery, and that the argument keeps not converging because each party is answering a different one of those three questions while using the same word.

So: let the six end-words be candidate markers rather than abstractions — I have in mind spike, coal, maize, fallout, plastic, concrete — each doing double duty, with a stratigraphic referent and a plain one, so that the rotation keeps forcing them against new neighbours and prevents any single one from quietly accumulating authority. Put roughly two in the service of each of my three questions, without announcing which is which; someone who knows the literature should be able to tell. Then use the envoi for the real work: the tornada drags the six wandering words back and forces them into three couplings chosen by the permutation rather than by the poem, and I want each of those three lines to pair two markers that do not belong together, each pairing legible as a different verdict about which question governs. End on the question of who gets to decide, not on an image; leave the three registers unreconciled, since that is where the meeting will actually end up. It has to survive being read aloud to a room that includes people who have spent years arguing about isochrony, so it should be accurate enough that the stratigraphers do not wince and free of the usual pieties — no asteroids, no reassurances that the planet will be fine — and it should still work for the curators, who do not know the literature and who mainly need to feel what it is like to be asked to date the thing you are standing inside.
