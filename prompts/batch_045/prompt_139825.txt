I have been reading about moral uncertainty on my own over the past year — I have no academic background in philosophy, just a stack of popular books and a lot of forum threads — and the move I keep running into is that being unsure which moral theory is correct is supposed to work exactly like being unsure about some ordinary matter of fact. You list the possibilities, attach a probability to each, score every option under each possibility, and take the option with the highest expected score. The people who lean on this comparison then use it to license two conclusions that I find quite different in plausibility:

1. that I should hedge my giving — split my donations between, say, a cause that looks best on my own favorite view and a cause that looks best on a view I think is probably wrong but not certainly wrong, so that I do tolerably well either way; and
2. that very small credences in theories that posit astronomically large stakes can legitimately swamp everything else, so that a fraction of a percent of confidence that some huge number of beings matter morally can make that the only thing worth funding.

I have already noticed one place the comparison seems to strain: when I try to score the same option under two different theories, the numbers come from different theories' own scales, and if I am allowed to multiply one theory's scale by a constant, I can change which option wins. I have also read that under ordinary factual uncertainty there is usually some story about repeated similar cases and long-run frequencies that backs up expected-value reasoning, and I cannot see what plays that role here, since many of the choices in question are one-off.

Before I let the analogy do any work for me, I would like an audit of it, written so that a careful non-specialist can follow the technical parts:

- Lay out the mapping explicitly — a correspondence table is welcome. What plays the role of the sample space, of an outcome, of a probability measure, of the value function, of learning from evidence, and of the justification for aggregating by weighted addition? Be explicit about which elements of the factual case have no partner in the moral case.
- Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the two conclusions above from the ones that are merely decorative. I suspect some breaks sink conclusion 2 while leaving conclusion 1 intact, but I would like that argued rather than asserted.
- Tell me whether either conclusion survives once the breaks are on the table, and if one does, what the weakest link in the surviving argument is.
- I have a patch of my own that I would like tested rather than flattered. Instead of adding each theory's normalized score, normalize each theory's scale against one fixed reference pair (the difference between a normal life and a life of unrelenting agony, say) and then multiply the normalized scores rather than add them, on the model of the multiplicative rule used in a standard two-person bargaining solution — the thought being that multiplication is unaffected if one theory's units are rescaled, and that it gives each theory something like a veto, so a single theory positing enormous stakes cannot dominate all by itself. Does this actually solve the scale problem, relocate it, or add new problems of its own (for instance about how the reference pair gets chosen, or about theories that return zeros or infinities)?
- Does the analogy, taken seriously, also recommend spending on moral information in the way it recommends spending on factual information, and if so what could that even consist of?
- If the uncertainty-is-just-uncertainty comparison is the wrong one, what is the closest analogy that does the work people want from it? I have seen being unsure which expert to trust, and being subject to several overlapping legal systems at once, floated as alternatives, but I do not trust my sense of which features each of those imports.

In your answer, please say plainly which parts of what you tell me are things people working on this would mostly agree about, which are contested, and which are your own judgment. I would also like to know what would change your verdict — the one argument or consideration that would move you, and the place in your own reasoning where you think it is most likely to have gone wrong. Please write in American English.
