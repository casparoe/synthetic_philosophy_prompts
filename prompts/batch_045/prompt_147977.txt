He's the one who got me into this stuff. Last year he had me read about the Rietdijk–Putnam argument — the one that uses relativity of simultaneity to argue that events in my future are already present for some other observer, so every event is equally real and the universe is a block. Here's what I can't work out for the speech. I keep coming up with two ways of saying what the block means and I don't know which one is honest:

- The museum reading: if every moment of his life is equally real, then his life is a finished exhibit, retirement isn't an ending it's just a location in the display, and "make the most of the years ahead" is kind of nonsense because whether he does is already part of the block. But the flip side is that the 38 years can't be undone or taken away from him, which might actually be the useful thing to say to someone who is low-key scared retirement erases the work.
- The score reading: the block is real all at once, but that isn't something that happens to the events from outside. A symphony being one object doesn't stop the notes at 8:03 being the ones that happen at 8:03. So a thank-you said tonight at 7:40 is a real thank-you happening at 7:40, and the future is open in whatever sense openness ever meant.

I think the second one is probly what I want for a room full of family, but I also think I may have just invented it so I can keep the nice feelings, and I'd rather know if that's what I'm doing. Also he has said that "closure" is a made-up word that should be banned and I promised him no "the future is yours" lines, so those are out either way.
