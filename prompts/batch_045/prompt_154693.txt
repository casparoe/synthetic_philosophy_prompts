I teach one 90-minute session of an interdisciplinary graduate seminar — 16 participants, roughly half philosophy and half computer science, one room with a whiteboard and a projector, and a budget that runs to a box of chocolates and nothing else. The session sits in a unit on inferring what people want from what they do. Most of the group has met the word reward but has never held the inference problem in their hands, and the philosophers in particular arrive assuming that if you watch someone long enough you will eventually see what they are after.

Design a runnable in-class activity that breaks that assumption by having them try and fail. No participant should need calculus or code.

Constraints on the design:

- No lecture up front and no vocabulary until the final ten minutes. Participants may be told that each agent has a score it is trying to make high. They may not be given the words reward function, policy, optimality, identifiability, or inverse reinforcement learning, and they should not hear shaping or any of its cognates before the debrief.
- The observers' data must be genuinely restricted to behavior. My plan is that each agent writes its entire route on an index card in advance, hands it to a recorder, and the recorder reads the moves aloud one at a time, with the agent silent and unreadable. Improve on this if you can, and say what it still fails to exclude.
- Stakes: points, settled in chocolate. Nothing requiring money.
- Pre-specify everything so I can walk in and run it cold: the exact grid (dimensions, per-cell movement costs, where the bonus tokens sit, which cells are exits), the exact contents of every secret card, and a payoff table computed in advance so nobody does arithmetic under time pressure. Four agents; the rest in teams of three or four.

The spine I want — reorganize it if you have a better one, but keep all four beats:

1. One observed route through a known world. Each team writes down what the agent is after, with a confidence wager.
2. The same route, or a set of routes in the same world, that two visibly different score-cards both make perfectly optimal. Reveal both cards. Someone will have been confident and wrong.
3. A transfer round: the world is altered slightly and each team predicts, for points, where the same agent goes now. Predictions diverge even though everyone saw identical behavior.
4. The punchline I most want them to feel: a pair of score-cards that no amount of further observation, in any rearrangement of the world, could ever tell apart. They differ only by a per-step term that pays the height of the cell you land in and charges the height of the cell you left, which telescopes along any route and so leaves the ranking of routes untouched. If this invariance has a standard name, use it, but derive it in the room rather than asserting it. Be explicit about what you do with the cells where a route may end, since the telescope leaves a remainder there, and say what constraint your card pair has to satisfy in order for the invariance to actually hold.

Two things I want handled honestly rather than smoothly.

First, the noise assumption must be built into the activity, not only into the debrief. Under a hard assumption that the agent always does the best thing, a card that scores every route identically is consistent with everything observed — behavior then says nothing at all. Under a soft assumption that better routes are likelier but not guaranteed, that same card predicts uniform randomness and is refutable. Stage this contrast, and tell me which of your rounds the hard version survives and which it breaks, and why. My own view is that the interesting moral is that the inference is not defeated by scarcity of data but by the structure of the problem, and that the noise assumption is doing philosophical work that normally passes unnoticed — argue with that if you disagree.

Second, the invisibility in beat four holds under the hard assumption and fails under the soft one. Say so, and show where.

Then the debrief: map each moment in the run onto the part of the idea it produced; tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover (I expect leakage from the agents, and I expect someone to say we could simply ask the agent what it wants — give me the answer I should have ready); and be frank about what the activity cannot stage and I will have to state in my own voice. I have three candidates: that real behavior may not be produced by anything like score-maximization in the first place; that real applications parametrize the score by a function approximator whose architecture and regularizer quietly select among the equally good answers; and that a score recovered from behavior is not the same object as what a person would endorse on reflection. Add any I have missed.

Write it as a run sheet with timings, and finish with one sentence on what a participant should be unable to forget by the end.
