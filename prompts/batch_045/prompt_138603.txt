Give me an exmaple of a conceptual issue that shows up when you try to formalize the "presumption of independence" that heuristic arguments lean on — the move where someone says, look, we've got no reason to think A and B are hooked up, so let's just multiply. I want an example where two people agree on all the evidence, both sincerely apply the presumption, and still land on different numbers, because they cashed out "independent" differently.

The wrinkle I keep getting stuck on, and I'd like your example to hit it or something close to it: there seem to be at least three things a person could mean, and they don't line up.

- factorizing: P(A and B) = P(A)P(B) in your actual credence function.
- wire-free: there's no causal path between them and no shared upstream cause — nothing in the wiring of the world connects them.
- filter-proof: the independence survives conditioning on whatever you're already treating as background, including the fact that you came to be thinking about A and B in the first place.

Wire-free is supposed to support factorizing, but it stops supporting it as soon as there's any collider or selection structure nearby: if A and B each raise the chance that a case gets flagged, noticed, or recorded, then conditional on "this one got flagged" they come out anti-correlated even with zero causal connection between them. And filter-proof is the one heuristic arguments actually need, beacuse you're basically never multiplying unconditional probabilities — but it's also the hardest to state as a clean default, since "no reason to think they're linked" sounds like a claim about the world, while filter-proof is a claim about your conditional distribution.
