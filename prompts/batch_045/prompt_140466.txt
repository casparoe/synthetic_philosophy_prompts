I have no formal training in philosophy or physics — I read about this kind of thing in the evenings — and I have got stuck on an analogy that a video essay I watched leans on very heavily.

The essay's case for the block universe runs like this. A film is a physical object: every frame of it is already on the reel, first through last. What we experience as the film's motion is just the projector lamp moving along the reel, lighting up one frame at a time. The universe, the essay says, is like that: all moments — my fifth birthday, this sentence, whatever happens decades from now — are already on the reel, and what I call the passage of time is only my consciousness arriving at each in turn. The essay then uses this to license a conclusion I actually care about: that the future is already written, and that agonizing over whether to change careers is therefore, strictly speaking, pointless, since the outcome exists either way.

What makes me suspicious is that the same essay also uses a second analogy, which seems to pull in the opposite direction. Time, it says, is a fourth dimension, and to see that all moments are equally real you should picture a loaf of bread: the loaf simply exists, and different observers carve it into different nows depending on how they are moving, the way you can slice a loaf at different angles. But if the loaf can be carved any way you like, there is no distinguished frame 4 of 10 — whereas the reel has a fixed frame order and a lamp that visits frames in one canonical direction. As far as I can tell from reading around, relativity is supposed to deny that there is a privileged slicing. So: does the film analogy quietly import exactly the privileged global present that the loaf analogy denies? And if so, can the reel still tell me anything about the future, as opposed to about some observer's future?

A few further things I noticed, which I cannot tell are deep or cosmetic.

The projector lamp's motion seems to need a second time to happen in — twenty-four frames per second, but per what? If the passage of time is supposed to be explained by the lamp, and the lamp itself moves in time, the analogy looks as though it relocates passage rather than eliminating it.

The reel arranges events in a single queue, one after another. My understanding is that the geometry of relativity only orders some pairs of events — the ones that can signal to each other — and leaves others unordered. If that is right, the reel's linear sequence misrepresents the very structure it is meant to depict.

The frames are discrete and the reel is finite. That feels irrelevant, but I would rather have it confirmed than assume it.

A film has a director and a script. If the reel is what makes the fatalist conclusion feel compelling — it has already been shot — some of the force may be coming from that, and not from anything about time.

I also came across an objection I only half understood, aimed at the argument from relativity to the block universe that I have seen called the Rietdijk–Putnam argument. It said the argument has to run through a chain — this event is simultaneous with that one in one frame, that one with another in a second frame, and so on — and that this only works if is real together with is transitive, whereas the relation actually being used, namely that two events are spacelike separated so that some frame makes them simultaneous, is not transitive. Is that a real objection? And if it is, does it sink the block-universe argument outright, or only careless versions of it?

Since the loaf cannot give me a distinguished now and the reel cannot give me one without cheating, I have been trying out an analogy of my own. Picture a database table whose rows have no intrinsic order. Different saved queries play the role of different observers' presents: each returns its own set of rows as current, the queries disagree, and no query is the correct one. The rows themselves are all stored — that is the eternalist part. But here is the bit I find interesting: databases also have views that are not stored at all, only computed when someone runs them. That looks like a way to picture the view on which only the present is real, or only the past and present are, with the future merely derived on demand. Is that a better analogy than the film, or does it break somewhere I have not spotted? My own worry is that a query is written by someone and its rows are derived from stored data, neither of which seems to have any counterpart in spacetime.

What I would like is this: an explicit correspondence table for the film analogy — what plays the reel, the lamp, the frame rate, the final frame, the audience — and then the same for the loaf; a sorting of the disanalogies into those that bear on the fatalist conclusion and those that do not; and a verdict on whether that conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the entire argument. If every moment exists does not by itself give you the future is settled, I would like to know what the extra premise would have to be, and whether anyone could believe it without the film picture in front of them. I have no access to a university library, so please argue it out rather than refer me to things I ought to read.
