Writing a novel at the moment and I've blundered into a bit of a thicket: one of my characters is a violinist who's kept sedated for a very long surgery, and afterwards she insists that during it she had this enormous inner life — streets, conversations, a whole piece of music she'd never heard before — just with no way of saying so at the time. The surgeon character shrugs it off with something like "the global workspace was still broadcasting, it just wasn't reaching anything you could move or speak with," and my philosopher friend, who I was discussing the scene with, says no, that line works only because everyone involved is quietly leaning on the theater picture: a stage of working memory, a spotlight of attention, actors competing for it, an audience of unconscious systems watching from the dark. She claims the metaphor is doing real argumentative work, not just decorating it — that it's what makes people think global workspace theory obviously explains *access* (availability for report, memory, reasoning, action) while having nothing to say about *phenomenal* consciousness, and therefore that felt-experience-without-broadcast is a coherent thing my character could be having.

What I'd like is an audit of that theater analogy. Give me the correspondences explicitly — what plays the role of the stage, the spotlight, the actors, the wings, the audience, and who or what is the director — and then where the mapping breaks. I suspect some of it is idle: the spatial boundedness of a stage seems to smuggle in a unity and a single serial stream that the theory maybe doesn't need, and a theater needs somebody in the seats, which is just the homunculus coming back in through the stage door; a spotlight also reveals what's already sitting there, whereas the worry is that the broadcast is partly what *makes* the content what it is. But I can't tell which of those disanalogies actually matter for the move my surgeon character is making and which are cosmetic, and I can't tell whether the "broadcast" is better cashed out as content being made available to lots of downstream consumer systems, or as some all-or-none ignition/threshold event, or as the brain coming to represent itself as having the content — since those three seem to me to give different answers about whether the violinist's inner city is even possible.

I've been toying with replacements and want them stress-tested rather than humoured. One is an old telephone switchboard: every line in town, but only one or two get patched through at a time, and patching is what the exchange does, not a property of the callers. The other is an open-mic night: the point of getting on the mic isn't that you get louder, it's that the room — including you, sitting in it — can now respond to you, and there's no separate audience that counts as the one who really heard it. Which of those (or something else better) carries the access-side work of the theater picture without dragging in a spectator, and does swapping the metaphor change which parts of this dispute are empirical questions about brains and which are just verbal? Mostly I want to know whether the conclusion my surgeon is reaching for — that the theory explains the reporting-and-control stuff and stays neutral about the feeling stuff — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the theater was carrying that conclusion the whole time.
