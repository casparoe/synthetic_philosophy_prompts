Grant, for the sake of the argument, the familiar empirical picture from moral foundations theory: the moral judgments of political liberals are driven largely by the care/harm and fairness/cheating foundations, while conservatives respond to those two and also, and about as strongly, to loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, and sanctity/degradation.

I want a credence on a specific claim: that this asymmetry gives a reflective person some real but modest reason to think that typical left-liberal political morality is incomplete — that it systematically fails to register considerations that are genuinely morally relevant — and correspondingly some reason to think that typical conservative political morality is the less incomplete of the two. The claim is meant to sit between two poles: that the finding is purely causal and normatively inert, and that it is close to decisive. Please also say, since it bears on the number, how you are resolving the question: what would count as its being settled, and how far that resolution is even possible.

Give me a number, and then the decomposition, with a number and one line of justification per term:

- the probability that the binding foundations (loyalty, authority, sanctity) pick out genuine pro tanto moral reasons at all, rather than coalitional psychology wearing moral vocabulary;
- the probability that greater responsiveness to a genuine reason is better calibration rather than over-detection, given that it is genuine — a detector can be too sensitive as well as too coarse;
- the probability that genealogical explanations of moral belief are undermining in general, and conditional on that, whether the undermining here is symmetric across the two camps or lopsided;
- the probability that the question has a determinate answer at the level of whole political moralities rather than dissolving into issue-by-issue conflict between foundations.

Show how the terms combine, name the one you are least confident in, and tell me which single result — empirical or philosophical — would move your final number most.

For what it is worth I am at about 0.2 on this and the person I was arguing with was near 0.55; tell me which of those sub-questions we are most likely disagreeing about.

One hypothetical result to price in as well, since it is where I want you to put your probability mass. Suppose we had a calibration measure for each foundation rather than a receptivity measure: not how strongly a subject reacts to a putative violation, but how well the subject's verdict tracks which considerations the case in fact involves, by the lights of careful case-by-case judgment. Suppose that on this measure both camps do equally well on care and fairness, and both do equally badly on the binding foundations — nobody, left or right, is calibrated on loyalty, authority, or sanctity; the right merely reacts more. How much of your credence does that finding destroy, and is any of the asymmetry's normative upshot left standing once it is in?
