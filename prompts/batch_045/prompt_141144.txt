Question (25 points). Some philosophers treat weakness of the will as a synchronic phenomenon: at the moment of action the agent judges, all things considered, that A is better than B, and yet intentionally does B. Hyperbolic and quasi-hyperbolic discounting are often invoked instead to model a diachronic phenomenon: an agent's ranking of two dated rewards changes as the earlier of them approaches. Set out both, and then assess the claim that hyperbolic discounting explains weakness of the will. In your answer: (i) distinguish declining impatience (the interval or common-difference effect) from simple present bias; (ii) distinguish the naive from the sophisticated agent; and (iii) state what the quasi-hyperbolic (beta-delta) model does and does not predict about reversals between two rewards that both lie in the future.

Student's answer. Start with definitions. Weakness of the will is acting against one's own better judgment: at the moment of action the agent holds that A is, all things considered, the better option, and she intentionally does B instead. If the judgment she acts against is not a judgment about what is best here and now — if, say, it is only her earlier self's ranking, which she now rejects — then we have a change of mind rather than akrasia, and the case lacks the peculiar difficulty that makes weakness of will interesting.

Hyperbolic discounting departs from exponential discounting in that impatience is greater over nearby delays than over distant ones: the discount factor relating two adjacent periods rises toward one as both periods recede into the future. This yields the common-difference effect — preferring one apple today to two tomorrow, while preferring two in a year and a day to one in a year — which no constant exponential rate can generate. The quasi-hyperbolic model captures this by discounting each future period at a constant per-period factor delta and applying an additional factor beta, less than one, to any reward available now. And because that extra weight always attaches to whatever is presently at hand, the lapse will recur at every later date: the agent who today ranks the reward at t+2 above the one at t+1 will reverse that ranking once she reaches t+1, and will likewise reverse her ranking of t+3 against t+2 when she arrives at t+2.

This is why the model explains weakness of will. At t+1 the agent still judges, all things considered, that the larger later reward is the better one — which is why she experiences her own choice as a failure of self-control — but the immediacy of the smaller reward, now carrying the extra factor beta, moves her to act against that judgment.

Finally, the distinction I was asked about. The sophisticated agent foresees her own reversal accurately; seeing herself about to do what she judges worse, she does it anyway, and so she is the clearest case of akrasia. The naive agent merely expects to follow through and is surprised when she does not; since she never acted in the light of a genuine all-things-considered judgment about what she was about to do, she is not, strictly speaking, weak-willed.
