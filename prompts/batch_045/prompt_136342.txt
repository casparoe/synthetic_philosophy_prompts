I keep reconstructing an argument about iterated amplification and distillation that I think proves too much, and I can't locate the bad step.

Setup, so we agree on terms: a limited model is amplified by decomposing hard questions into trees of easier sub-questions it can handle, recursively, with the sub-answers aggregated into a composite judgement; a fresh model is then distilled to produce the composite's answers directly, without the tree; and the cycle repeats.

(1) A successor system is aligned only if its outputs are produced by a process the principal could in principle endorse: alignment attaches to how an answer is arrived at, not merely to which answer comes out.
(2) The whole case for amplification rests on the decomposition — the sub-questions, the aggregation, the local checks. That structure is what the principal is supposed to be endorsing when she endorses the composite.
(3) The distilled model emits its answer in a single forward pass. The tree is not part of the computation that produced it; it was consumed during training and discarded.
(4) So each round of distillation severs the justificatory structure from the mechanism that will actually act. What the procedure hands on is not an aligned successor but an imitation of one: something that reproduces the outputs of the endorsed process without instantiating it, competent-looking but unanchored.
(5) But the same shape of argument indicts every case in which deliberate reasoning is compressed into fast judgement. A senior engineer's snap diagnosis is not produced by the explicit derivation that originally warranted it either. It would follow that every expert is a mere imitation — which is absurd.

I want to keep (2) and (3), since they look like descriptions of the procedure rather than commitments, and I would sooner give up almost anything than accept (4). Yet (5) tells me the chain is unsound somewhere, and the two diagnoses I can construct pull in opposite directions. Perhaps (1) is simply too strong, and alignment is a matter of the successor's dispositional profile — what it would output across the range of relevant cases — in which case losing the tree costs nothing and the argument dies at the first step. Or perhaps (3) is trading on an ambiguity: the tree is absent from inference-time computation but present in the model's causal history through training, and (1) never said which of the two had to carry the endorsement.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost the rest of the picture? Specifically: if alignment is dispositional rather than process-dependent, what becomes of the worry about a successor that produces what the principal would endorse only while it is being evaluated? If alignment is process-dependent, can any version of distillation survive — for instance, training the successor to emit the decomposition alongside the answer, so that the tree is re-derivable on demand — or does that merely relocate the difficulty one level down? And does the argument's fate change if competent is substituted for aligned throughout: is the chain sounder or less sound when the property at stake is reliability rather than alignment?
