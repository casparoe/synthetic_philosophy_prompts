Last night a friend and I spent three hours arguing about fakes, and I came away holding a position I am not sure I can state precisely enough to put at risk. He defends the hard formalist line: if two objects are perceptually indiscernible, then every aesthetic property, and hence every aesthetic reason for preferring one, must be shared, so the only remaining difference is curatorial and commercial — prices, insurance, wall labels, the fact that scholars would rather study one than the other. His favourite move was a nocturnal-swap thought experiment: if someone exchanges the authenticated seventeenth-century Dutch interior for its indiscernible 1930s copy while you sleep, and you never learn of it, then nothing about your experience, your judgement of its composition, or its capacity to reward repeated looking has changed, and a difference that no possible informed experience could register cannot be an aesthetic difference. My reply, which I now want to stress-test, is a moderate aesthetic contextualism about achievement: the causal history of an object — who made it, what they had available, what they were trying to do, and what it cost them — is part of the proper object of aesthetic appreciation rather than mere packaging around it, so a known forgery can be aesthetically worse than the original even when looking cannot tell them apart, and this is not just snobbery or the shadow of the auction house. I lean on three distinctions to make it precise: that looking at a surface and seeing a work as an achievement are different acts of attention; that aesthetic value and artistic value can come apart, and my claim is only about the former inheriting something from the latter; and a parity principle — since we already allow a curator's framing, a title, or knowledge of genre conventions to alter what a competent viewer experiences, it is special pleading to single out authorship as the one piece of knowledge that must make no difference. I also conceded to him that the view has to be about properly informed appreciation, not about what a distracted tourist feels.

The cases that actually moved the argument were the awkward ones, and I would like them used as the test battery rather than the standard set-up. First, the unordered pair: two indiscernible canvases, known to be one original and one forgery, with the provenance irretrievably lost, so that you own and display both and cannot say which is which — does my view go silent here, and is that silence a defect? Second, self-forgery: an elderly painter secretly manufactures a lost early work in her own youthful manner and lets it be rediscovered and authenticated; there is real achievement, and the object is indiscernible from what she would have made, but the provenance claim is false — is anything aesthetically missing? Third, the improved forgery: the copyist quietly repairs a compositional weakness in the lost original, so the fake is arguably the better picture on purely visual grounds. Fourth, the workshop case, where a picture is now known to be largely executed by pupils under the master's design, and its aesthetic standing did not collapse when this became known. Fifth, a machine-trained system that produces indiscernible period canvases with no intention, no skill, and no art-historical situation at all.

What I want from you is a map of my exposure, not a defence of me. Rank the things that would count against the position from fatal to merely awkward, including the arguments and the possible findings that should move someone who already holds it and not merely people who reject it from the start; tell me which of these a committed contextualist would concede as genuine defeaters and which she would dismiss as changing the subject or as attacks on a straw version; say what the view predicts that its rivals do not — about how reattribution and deattribution should change properly informed experience, about how museums ought to display indiscernible copies, about which of my five cases should feel any different — and tell me candidly whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability by the move from any difference in experience to any difference in ideal or properly informed experience, and whether that is a problem for a claim of this kind. I would personally be most moved by a case in which the history is known, correctly appreciated, and still leaves no evaluative difference; I would be least moved by bare intuition reports from people who have never handled the objects. Finally, please keep clearly separated what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this — which claims are consensus, which are live disputes, and which are things almost nobody accepts — flag any place where you are extrapolating rather than reporting a position people actually defend, and tell me how confident you are in each part rather than presenting a contested reading as if it were the default.
