I'm a PhD student in philosophy, and this term I'm running the weekly discussion section attached to an upper-level seminar on moral responsibility: 15 students, mostly philosophy majors with a few from law and politics, 80 minutes, a room with movable tables and a whiteboard. Next week's topic is the standing to blame and the case of the hypocritical blamer, and I already know how this will go if I just hand out a case and ask for reactions. Last time I tried it, everything collapsed into one undifferentiated argument: half the room said the hypocrite obviously has no right to blame anyone, the other half said that's just *tu quoque* and a way of changing the subject, and we spent the hour going in circles because the students were using one word — "wrong", "unfair", "out of line" — for at least four things that I want them to be able to pull apart. So I'd like a structured activity, not a handout: something with roles, materials, rules and a timeline that makes them *act* on a distinction they haven't been told about yet.

The four things I want separated, and which the mechanics should force apart rather than name, are: whether the target actually did something blameworthy; whether this blamer is in a position to press the charge at all; how serious or appropriate the blaming is, given the relationship and the stakes; and whether, having blamed, the blamer now owes something to the target or to the group — is liable to a counter-complaint of her own. My idea, which you should improve on or replace, is that every participant gets two or three "standing chips": you may only put a blame to the group by staking a chip, and if before the group rules on your charge someone exposes a fault of yours that is relevant to the norm you're invoking, your chip is forfeited and the charge either dies or passes to another player. That turns standing into a scarce, tradeable resource, which I think will make the "clean hands" idea vivid — and also, I hope, make its arbitrariness vivid, because the room will notice that the chip economy rewards people who are skilled at concealing rather than people who are actually committed to the norm. Please include the cases that do the heavy lifting: the plain hypocrite who blames a fault she shares; the self-deceived hypocrite with the same fault who genuinely does not know she has it, where the question is whether ignorance restores what hypocrisy removed; the discharged hypocrite, same fault, who has already owned it and made amends to her own victim; the counterfactual hypocrite, who has never done the thing but whom everyone agrees would have done it in the target's situation; the outsider who invokes a norm she does not herself accept, on the group's behalf; and the proxy who blames on behalf of a third party who really was wronged, while being guilty of the same thing herself. Cut or compress whichever of these won't fit 80 minutes, and tell me which ones you'd cut. No lecture up front, please: the words "standing", "forfeiture" and "warrant" — and any theorist's name — should not be spoken until the debrief, so the vocabulary of the activity has to be ordinary ("in order", "may press it", "the floor", "she owes us an account").

What I especially want from you is the back half, where I'm weakest. Give me a debrief that maps specific moments I should be watching for during play onto each of the four distinctions above, so I can point at what just happened in the room rather than introduce the distinctions from above. Then, honestly, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this: I expect at least some students to hear "she has no standing" as "what she did was fine", some to turn the exercise into a competition about who in the room is worse, some to over-disclose real faults because the game rewards confession, and the self-deceived case likely to dissolve into "then she isn't a hypocrite at all". Tell me how to head each of these off in the setup rather than in the moment. And be frank about which feature of the debate the game cannot reproduce and which I will have to supply in words afterwards — my worry is the second-personal texture of actual blame: in real life blame has an addressee who can answer back, resent, apologize, or forgive, and a blamer who can withdraw, repair, or be reconciled over time, whereas in any game I can run in a seminar room blame becomes a ruling by a committee about a third party who isn't there. If the activity can't show that, say so plainly and sketch the two or three sentences I should say at the end to mark it.
