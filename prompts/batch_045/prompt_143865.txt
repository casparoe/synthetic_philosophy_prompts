I've somehow got landed with our first-year Paradoxes and Puzzles module again, and I've found myself defending a line in the supertasks week that I'm not sure I can cash out. The line is this: Thomson's lamp isn't a proof that supertasks are impossible, and it isn't a puzzle with a hidden determinate answer either — it's a case where the world would just be indeterminate, where there'd be no fact of the matter whether the lamp is on or off at the minute. More generally I want to say that whenever a supertask's outcome rides on a quantity that has no limit — the number of switchings, which runs off to infinity, so there's no last switch and no parity to consult — the outcome isn't settled, and the lesson generalises: performable supertasks of that shape would leave things metaphysically gappy. A maths colleague at a departmental lunch shrugged at all this and said that in the colliding-balls version the equations simply stop having a unique solution past the accumulation point, so there's nothing there to be indeterminate about. I think he's changing the subject, but I'd like to know whether he's right.

So: what would count against me? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I'd like to know which ones someone on my side would concede, not just what the critics say. Three things I keep failing too answer. First, the variant I cooked up: forget the switch, imagine a slider starting at x = 0 on a track that gets a shove of +1/2 m at t = 1/2, then −1/4 at t = 3/4, then +1/8 at 7/8, and so on, with a lamp wired to be on iff the slider is strictly past a mark. Put the mark at 1/5 m: the slider's limiting position is 1/3, so the lamp is determinately on at t = 1 despite infinitely many alternating shoves and no last one. Move the mark to exactly 1/3 and the lamp is determinately off at t = 1, even though it was on at every odd-numbered step beforehand. Is that a refutation or just a restriction — indeterminacy only where the outcome rides on a quantity with no limit? And if it's a restriction, does the view survive with any content, or has it quietly collapsed into something banal about evaluating a discontinuous function where its argument isn't defined? Second: does my view collapse into the impossibility view anyway? If the lamp can't complete, then if it completed, the outcome would be indeterminate looks like it might be vacuously true, and I've insured myself against everything — is the conditional distinguishable from it's impossible in any way that matters? Third, the case I find hardest: two lamps wired in antiphase so exactly one is on whenever either is, switched on the same schedule, with the same events befalling each. It looks like it should be determinate that exactly one of them is on and indeterminate which — but can I say that without giving up excluded middle, and if I can't, is that a cost or a feature?

Be straight with me about how exposed the position is, including whether it's been qualified into unfalsifiability: nothing empirical could distinguish an indeterminate lamp from a determinate one we can't find out about, so is there anything at all that could settle this, and if not, is that a problem for a claim of this kind or just what metaphysics looks like? What would move me most, personally, is either a case where the outcome settles without any convergent quantity for it to ride on, or an argument that the antiphase pair simply can't be described in my terms. Under 500 words please, in prose — three paragraphs at most, no bullet points or headings, since I'll be reading it out to the seminar.
