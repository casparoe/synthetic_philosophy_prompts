I'm writing an essay on how Einstein's thinking about religion and free will would have met developments that arrived after his death in 1955, and I want a real extrapolation from what he actually held, not a pastiche assembled out of the dice remark and a few familiar epithets.

Take as fixed three commitments I take to be well documented. First, his determinism: the position he expressed by endorsing Schopenhauer's line that a human being can do what he wills but cannot will what he wills — a view he appears to have regarded as disposing of libertarian free will and of quantum indeterminacy in a single stroke. Second, his cosmic religious feeling: reverence for the rational, law-governed order of nature, expressly detached from a personal God, from providence and from prayer, and bound up with the conviction that the laws are unique, comprehensible and beautiful. Third, a strand I take to be less canonical but genuinely his, and which I suspect does much of the work in his denial of free will: his late suggestion that the experience of oneself as a separate subject is a kind of optical delusion of consciousness, a confinement of concern to a fragment of a whole.

Put those commitments in front of five things that happened afterwards.

Bell's theorem and the loophole-free experiments that closed the remaining escape routes: the kind of hidden variables he hoped for — local, separable, restoring a complete description — is excluded, while the surviving options (an explicitly nonlocal dynamics, a deterministic many-worlds picture, or a conspiracy between the settings and the source) are each, for different reasons, exactly the sort of thing he disliked. Say which he would have found least intolerable and what he would have said about it.

Determinism failing inside his own theory: the singularity theorems, the initial cosmological singularity, Cauchy horizons, cosmic censorship remaining a conjecture rather than a result. His field equations, in the hands of his successors, turned out not to underwrite the deterministic picture he wanted — or to underwrite it only locally and contingently. I want to know whether he would have treated this as a defect to be repaired by a better theory or as a reason to reconceive what determinism was ever supposed to mean.

The neuroscience of volition: readiness potentials, the experiments and their successors, and the fact that the indeterminacy he refused would not in any case have delivered the agency he denied. Would that empirical programme have mattered to him at all, given that he seems to have thought the question settled in advance, and would he have found anything in it worth answering?

Fine-tuning and the ensemble answer: the post-1955 realisation that stable chemistry and long-lived stars depend delicately on the constants and on particular nuclear resonances, together with the two live responses — a single law with unexplained numbers, or a vast ensemble in which those numbers vary and our presence selects them. I have a term for what I think is at stake: call it the uniqueness condition, the requirement that cosmic religious feeling attach not merely to a law-governed nature but to laws that are in some sense the only ones there could be. Assess whether his attitude survives the uniqueness condition failing.

Machine minds: whether the third commitment — the separateness of the self being a delusion — pushes him to extend moral concern to artificial systems, or whether his insistence that ethics is a purely human affair with no cosmic sanction stops the extension, and whether an artificial system that reports something functionally like suffering would have struck him as a moral patient, an irrelevance, or a case his metaphysics cannot describe.

What I want is a map rather than a verdict. For each development say what he would have said; distinguish what is forced by the three commitments from what you are supplying from a sense of his temperament; and flag the cases in which two of the commitments pull against each other. I want the defeaters for your own predictions too: what would have to be true for the extrapolation to be wrong, and where you are confident only because the commitments underdetermine the answer and you have quietly picked one reading.

Two further things. First, tell me which of the five would have disturbed him most and why — my own instinct is that the ensemble answer is the most corrosive, because it threatens the uniqueness condition while appearing to explain the very appearances that fed his religious feeling, but I want that pressed rather than agreed with. Second, be explicit about where the extrapolation is simply underdetermined: what further evidence — unpublished notebooks, correspondence, his recorded reactions to comparable reversals in physics during his own lifetime — would settle it, and how, in the absence of that evidence, the essay ought to hedge.
