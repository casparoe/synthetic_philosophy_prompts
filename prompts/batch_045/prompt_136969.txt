I'm putting together the take-home final for my fourth-year seminar on rational choice and the philosophy of action, and I want one question on acausal trade that a student cannot pass by reciting the standard cases. Last year's cohort all produced the same essay: they retold the twin prisoner's dilemma and the hitchhiker, announced that the right decision theory cooperates because the counterpart's choice is subjunctively dependent on yours, and stopped there. Nobody said what that dependence is supposed to consist in, nobody said what happens to the argument when the correlation is less than perfect, and nobody noticed something I now think is the interesting part: real acausal traders would have to spend resources making themselves *predictable* to a counterpart they will never meet, and it isn't obvious they should. A colleague in the economics department insists the whole phenomenon is just a correlated equilibrium with a shared signal, and I'd like the question to force students either to explain why that is the wrong model or to say exactly what it leaves out.

Here is the case I have in mind; please re-parameterise it if any sub-question comes out trivial, degenerate, or ill-posed as I've specified it, and tell me what you changed and why. Two agents, Vega and Altair, are outside each other's light cones and will never interact causally; each knows the other faces the same one-shot prisoner's dilemma, with mutual cooperation worth 3 each, mutual defection 1 each, and unilateral defection 5 to the defector and 0 to the other. Call a pair linked with probability p = τ_V · τ_A, where τ is an agent's transparency: if the pair is linked, the two are running the same decision procedure and their choices coincide; if it is unlinked, Altair's choice is independent of Vega's and is cooperation with probability 1/2. Base transparency is 1/2, but either agent may install a beacon that raises its own transparency to 1 at a cost of 1/5; the choice whether to install is itself made while both agents are at base transparency, so that choice is linked with probability 1/4, with an unlinked Altair installing independently with probability 1/2. Part (a): for a general p and an unlinked cooperation rate of 1/2, at what value of p does cooperation become optimal, and — this is what I actually want tested — what is the agent treating as the dependence between its own choice and the counterpart's? The derivation must make explicit why this is not a causal influence and not a correlated-equilibrium signal, and why the answer changes if the correlation is a mere common cause. Part (b): should Vega install the beacon? I want students to notice the Coordination structure first — both installing is better for both than neither installing, but unilateral installing leaves *both* worse off than neither — and then to notice that the investment decision is itself acausally correlated, and to derive how the verdict depends on an unlinked counterpart's independent probability of investing. Part (c): add a third agent, the Auditor, who can never interact with Vega causally and who plays as follows: against any agent whose choice it cannot predict with accuracy above 4/5 it cooperates on equal terms; against agents it can predict that well it demands tribute, cooperating only if it predicts the tribute would be paid (leaving Vega 2 and the Auditor 5) and otherwise defecting. Does the Auditor make legibility self-defeating? A good answer here has to separate being predictable from being compliant, has to treat the whole policy rather than the individual act as the unit of choice, and has to say whether selective opacity (transparent to fair traders, opaque to extortionists) is even available — and, if it concludes that the case for legibility now depends on the population of possible counterparts, it should say what that dependence does to the expected-value calculation.

What I'd like back is a model answer of roughly 1,000 words and a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit points, with the marks for each and a one-line note on how candidates typically get each one wrong (I expect things like: treating p as a causal lottery; thinking acausal trade requires certainty or exact symmetry; conflating predictability with compliance; applying the decision theory locally to the prisoner's dilemma while ignoring that the beacon decision is itself correlated). Arithmetic slips should cost a mark at most; structural errors should cost four or five. No reading is required and I don't want students citing anything, so please don't build the answer around who argued what — every substantive claim should follow from the specification, and if a sub-question secretly needs a premise I haven't supplied, flag that as a defect in the question. Finally, end the model answer with a short paragraph headed something like Where this answer could be wrong: name the single premise in the derivation that a strong student could reasonably reject, say how the verdict would shift if they rejected it, state plainly how confident the verdict is, and — for the marking scheme — say how such a student should be graded.
