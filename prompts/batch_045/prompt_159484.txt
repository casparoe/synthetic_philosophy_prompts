**Question 4. Answer both parts.** *(25 marks: 12 for (a), 13 for (b).)*

**(a)** Explain Williams's so-called integrity objection to consequentialism, and his related "one thought too many" complaint. In your answer, say what work each of the following notions does in his argument: *ground projects*; *negative responsibility*; the *impersonal standpoint*.

**(b)** Osei has spent eleven years running a small free legal-advice clinic in the city where he grew up. A national charity offers him the directorship of a much larger advice service in another region. The charity's own figures, which we may stipulate are reliable, show that by moving he would secure good legal representation for roughly three times as many people each year; the clinic's existing clients would be absorbed by another service, so nobody is made worse off and nobody's rights are violated. Osei has no complaint about the larger service: he thinks it is well run and does valuable work. He simply does not want to leave the clinic he built and the neighbourhood he has lived in all his life. His attachment is not a hobby — he took the work up because he thought it worth doing, and he still thinks so.

A consequentialist tells Osei that he ought to take the new post. Assess that demand in the light of Williams's argument. Then consider the following reply on the consequentialist's behalf: nothing Williams cares about has been left out of the calculation, since the calculation can register Osei's attachment to the clinic, the distress he would feel at leaving, and the setback to his own life as items of value to be weighed against the extra people helped, and can then tell him either way. Say whether, and why, Williams would regard this reply as answering his objection.

And here is the script, transcribed as written:

> Williams's integrity objection starts from the thought that an agent's life is organised around ground projects — long-standing commitments, relationships and causes which give his life its shape and which he does not regard as one preference among others that he happens to have. Consequentialism requires him to step back from all of them and to assess his action from an impersonal standpoint, in which his own projects carry no more weight than anyone else's. Williams's point is that integrity is a value the consequentialist sum has so far omitted: alongside welfare we should enter the agent's integrity, and once it is entered it can be weighed against the gains available elsewhere and will sometimes outweigh them. Understood in this way the objection is at bottom the familiar complaint that consequentialism is too demanding; integrity is simply the name of the consideration that marks how much an agent can be asked to give up.
>
> The case of Jim and the Indians brings out what is distinctive about consequentialism here. Jim is told that if he shoots one Indian the other nineteen will be freed, and consequentialism tells him he must shoot. The reason is that consequentialism alone holds agents responsible for what they fail to prevent: this is its negative responsibility. Ordinary moral thought, by contrast, holds a person responsible only for what he himself does, and so has no place for negative responsibility at all; that is why it can allow Jim to refuse in a way consequentialism cannot. Williams's interest, though, is not to deny the verdict about what Jim should do — he is willing to allow that Jim should shoot — but to show that consequentialism leaves Jim with nothing coherent to say about the fact that he would rather not have had to do it. A related case is George, who is offered a job in chemical and biological research that he opposes: consequentialism tells him to take it, because someone more enthusiastic will otherwise get it, and Williams's complaint is that this misrepresents the way his opposition figures in his life.
>
> Williams's "one thought too many" complaint says the same thing about deliberation. It is not that the agent may not use consequentialist vocabulary, or may not think about consequences at all; it is that from the impersonal standpoint the fact that it is his wife gives him no reason to save her rather than the stranger, so a further thought has to be brought in — that he loves her, or that he would suffer more if she died — and that further thought misrepresents his reason. The complaint is therefore about what can justify the action, not about what passes through the agent's mind or about whether his motive stays pure.
>
> In Osei's case consequentialism says he must move. Williams would object that this asks him to regard eleven years of work as one more item to be traded, and to see his own life from outside. The case shows that the objection is not a defence of self-interest: Osei's project is a moral one, and what concerns Williams is the standpoint from which he decides, not how much he enjoys the work. The consequentialist reply — that the calculation can include his attachment and his distress — misses the point, since entering these as values to be weighed leaves Osei in exactly the position Williams describes: someone deciding from outside his own life, with the things that matter most to him listed as entries on a list.
>
> That reply would fail on any version of consequentialism. Even rule consequentialism or indirect consequentialism holds that Osei's action is right only because of its consequences, so even there his projects never have any standing in his own deliberation, and the objection goes through unchanged.
