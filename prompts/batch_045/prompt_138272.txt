In ten days I have to give the speech at the ceremony that completes the transfer of my father's engineering firm to the people who work in it, and I have written four versions and thrown away four versions. I would be grateful for help with a fifth. The firm is a foundry and machine shop, founded in 1981, 180 employees. Two years ago my father put sixty per cent of the equity into an employee ownership trust; at this ceremony the last forty per cent goes in and the family seat on the board disappears. I gave up a job in corporate finance in London to come back as finance director, and I stay on as the non-voting chair of the trust board for a three-year handover. The trust deed requires that at least a third of net profit each year be paid as an equal per-capita bonus — not scaled to salary or seniority — with the remainder reserved at the trustees' discretion. It also contains a clause he insisted on and argued for alone: ten years after the final transfer, the employee-members may, by a two-thirds vote, direct the trustees to sell the company to the highest bidder and divide the proceeds per head. He put it to me like this: if they can't be trusted with the door, they're not owners, they're residents. He has also forbidden one word from the ceremony. He uses it himself, in private, and has for years; his reason is that it isn't their word yet, and that saying it from the platform would make the thing sound like his theory rather than their arrangement.

The room will be the 180 employees, about a third of whom have been there twenty years or more and regard the whole scheme as a pay cut with speeches attached; the director of the regional co-operative federation, who speaks after me and will call this the beginning of the end of the wage system in the valley; four of my father's friends from business school who think he has lost his nerve in old age and will be listening to see whether I admit it; a local reporter; and my younger brother, who runs the machine shop, voted against the transfer, and will say so at the reception.

The position I want the speech to be built on — and which is, as I understand it, the interesting part of Mill's late socialism, though I would rather the speech didn't depend on anyone knowing that — is this: that the case for outgrowing the wage system rests on a claim about human nature that no one has tested, namely whether people will work as hard and as carefully and invent as much for a common stake as for a wage under a manager; that argument cannot settle the question, that only trial at real scale and with real money at stake can; that the honest form of socialism in circumstances like ours is therefore the voluntary experiment rather than the programme — a firm here and there, competing in the open market, free to fail — and that this is also why Mill went on defending competition while looking forward to its being superseded, as the discipline people still need while they are as they are, and as the only test that keeps the experiment honest.

So the speech has to hold several things at once. It has to say plainly what is not known: whether people will keep the cooperative rules and still survive against competitors who have no such rules, whether they will tolerate an equal bonus when a senior machinist can point to what he produces, whether in year ten they will take the money — and it has to make that unknown sound like the reason for doing it rather than a hedge. It must not tell them they were always the real owners, or imply that the last forty years are being reversed as an act of generosity, or call any of this justice, or redemption, or the end of something. It must not concede to my father's old friends that profit and competition have been found out. It must not set the federation director up for his peroration, and must not contradict him from the podium either. It has to give the ten-year clause a meaning that is neither a dare nor a threat. Mill may be named once, or at most twice, and only in a sentence a machinist would sit still for; no lecture on his views, no history of ideas. Plain sentences, one or two concrete details from the shop floor — and where a concrete detail would help, leave a clearly marked placeholder rather than inventing one.

Please write it: roughly ten minutes, about 1,200 words. I would rather it end in a willingness to find out than in confidence about how it turns out.
