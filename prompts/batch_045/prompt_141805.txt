I'm a backend engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I've been asked to write down, in about a page, what our team actually believes about user data before a design review next week. I'd like a consistency check on the position set before I present it, because I suspect it doesn't all hang together but I can't tell where. Here is where I've landed:

1. Privacy is control. You have privacy over a piece of information to the extent that you get to decide who has access to it. A violation is a loss of control, not a bad thing that happens afterward.

2. So notice-and-choice is basically the right mechanism. If we disclose what we collect and people keep using the product, they have exercised control, and we have done our job.

3. But "they clicked agree" does not confer control when the user is captive: an employee told to install the internal tool we ship, or a product that everyone in their field has to use. In those cases I still think we violate something, consent or no consent.

4. Privacy has no value of its own. It matters only because of what it protects: candor in relationships, not being managed or nudged, room to be inconsistent across contexts. If nothing downstream is set back, nothing of value has been lost.

5. Aggregate reporting is therefore a solved problem for us. We publish only cohort means with a ten-member suppression floor, so no figure in the dashboard is about any individual, and no one has lost control of anything.

6. Except that two situations involving that same dashboard seem to me to be genuine violations, and I would like to go on calling them that. First, the cohort of twelve happens to be exactly one team at one small customer: nobody is named, but anyone at that customer can read off how that team is doing. Second, we republish each rolling seven-day mean every day, so anyone who saved yesterday's number and subtracts can recover one person's contribution for today. I call these the small-room problem and the differencing problem.

Please audit this for me. Which of (1) through (6) are genuinely in tension with each other, and which only look like they are? For each real tension, say precisely what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me in practice, since I need something I can turn into an engineering rule rather than a slogan. Rank the tensions by severity instead of listing them all as equally bad, and tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Argue from the cases rather than referring me to papers or reading lists; I don't have library access and I couldn't evaluate them anyway.
