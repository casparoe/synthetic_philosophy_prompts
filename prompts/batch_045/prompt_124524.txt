I have no academic training in philosophy or classics; I have just been reading Aristotle’s remarks on money and exchange on my own and thinking them through. As I understand him, money begins as a convention rather than something found in nature. Its job is to be a common measure that lets goods as different as shoes and houses be compared, and to act as a kind of pledge: if I accept money now, I can later obtain what I need even if the person I dealt with has nothing I currently want. He also contrasts two ways of dealing with provision. One is natural and bounded: getting what a household or community needs in order to live well, and stopping there. The other is unnatural and tends to be unlimited: exchange or lending carried on with an eye to increase itself, especially retail trade for profit and, worst of all, lending money at interest, because money is supposed to be an instrument of exchange and is not supposed to produce more money out of itself.

Here is the case I want to use. Imagine a town that adopts a purely local digital token, the tally, for recording prices and debts. The tally has no commodity backing and no use except as a token; it pays no interest; and the town assembly deliberately manages the number of tallies so that a fixed basket of ordinary goods and services stays at roughly the same number of tallies. Lending tallies at interest is forbidden. People mainly use the tally to coordinate provision over time: a builder repairs a roof today and receives tallies she expects to spend on food, care, or tools months later. The point of the scheme is not that anyone should pile up endless tallies, but that today’s work can be turned into a claim on tomorrow’s goods. Still, the tally is a pure conventional token, and some residents do end up with large balances.

Let P be the claim that, if Aristotle’s own concepts were applied carefully to this case, he would classify the tally scheme as belonging to the natural, need-bounded side of his contrast rather than the unnatural, increase-oriented side. I do not mean what he would have said if asked off the cuff; I mean what best follows from the distinctions he actually draws. If you think his distinctions do not reach the case, treat that as a third possibility and give it its own probability, so that your natural, unnatural, and indeterminate probabilities sum to 1.

I would like a single number between 0 and 1 for P, but the number matters less to me than the decomposition. Please break the question down into the sub-questions it depends on. The ones I have in mind are:

1. Does Aristotle’s natural/unnatural contrast turn mainly on the agent’s end, that is, on whether provision is bounded by need or aimed at limitless increase, or does it turn mainly on the kind of activity, that is, on whether goods are produced and used within a household or community or are instead moved through a conventional medium of exchange?
2. Can a purely conventional, interest-free token with a managed stable value still satisfy what he requires of money as a measure and as a pledge of future exchange?
3. Would the town assembly’s management of the token’s supply, in order to stabilise its value, look to him like a legitimate political convention or like a refined form of the unlimited money-making he criticises?
4. Is the absence of interest, together with the ban on charging it, enough to place the scheme outside his specific objection to money producing money out of itself, even though the token is artificial?
5. How much weight should be given to the fact that the scheme is city-wide and future-oriented rather than a matter of a single household’s self-sufficiency, and does that gap make the case simply indeterminate for him?

Assign a rough probability to each sub-question, and to any others you think belong, with a number and one line of justification for each.
