I'm applying for a twelve-month dissertation completion fellowship (call it nine months of genuine research time, since two chapters still need revising) and I need to turn a half-formed idea into a two-page proposal a committee would actually fund. My dissertation sits in the accuracy-first, epistemic-utility tradition: the view that accuracy is the fundamental epistemic good, and that the Bayesian norms — probabilism and updating by conditionalization — can be recovered from that good plus decision-theoretic machinery, without any appeal to what the agent happens to want. I'm broadly happy with that framework and I don't want to spend the year attacking it. What I want to work on is something it treats as held fixed, and which I think it shouldn't: the space of possibilities, or equivalently the set of questions, over which credence functions are defined.

Here is the half-formed thought. Every accuracy argument I know runs relative to two things taken as given: an algebra or partition of possibilities, and an inaccuracy measure scoring credence functions at worlds. The standard results then say, conditionally, that non-probabilistic credences are accuracy-dominated and that conditionalizing maximizes expected accuracy. But nothing in the framework tells you which propositions to have opinions about, or how finely to carve, even though that choice is not normatively neutral: the quadratic-style measures that score you on your whole distribution are sensitive to how the space is partitioned in a way that log-style measures, which score you only on how much credence you gave the truth, are not, and I suspect this difference is doing real but unacknowledged work. So I want to try making question-choice itself an object of epistemic evaluation — evaluate an agent's state as a pair consisting of a question-space and a credence function over it, rather than a credence function alone — and then see what happens in both directions: what veritism says about whether it can ever be epistemically better to ask a coarser question, to refine, or to run a more informative experiment; and whether verdicts about inquiry force a choice of inaccuracy measure that the existing dominance arguments leave open. The application I keep coming back to is the uniqueness-versus-permissivism dispute about priors: if an agent's question-space is in some sense up to her, then a would-be accuracy-based argument that a body of evidence mandates exactly one rational credence function seems to presuppose a carving that it never vindicates, and an agent can look rationally required or rationally permitted depending on a description she was never given a reason to adopt. I'm not sure yet whether that's a real argument or a pun on "up to her."

Two reactions I've gotten, both of which I think miss something but I can't say what: my supervisor says this is just the debate about which inaccuracy measure is correct wearing a fancy hat, and someone in the reading group said it's just the old point that scoring rules can be gamed by redescription. I also worry privately that it may collapse into the small-worlds problem for Bayesian decision theory, or into the framework's problems with logical omniscience.

So: write me the proposal. I need a precise statement of the question that has a determinate answer rather than being a gesture; a short section on why the existing positions leave it open, distinguishing it honestly from the three neighbors I just named; the approach, including why endogenous question-spaces rather than the obvious alternatives (fix the algebra by fiat and get on with it; switch to a partition-invariant measure; treat inquiry-choice as pragmatic and outside epistemology; or abandon veritism for something evidentialist); a month-by-month method with concrete outputs; what the possible conclusions are and which considerations would decide between them; and a candid failure section — including whether the whole thing would turn out to rest on a formalism I can't handle. My formal ceiling: finite partitions, convexity arguments, the basic theory of proper scoring rules and expected-utility dominance; I can't do measure theory, and I'd rather the proposal tell me now if the interesting cases need infinite algebras than let me find out in month eight. Don't include a bibliography or any citations — I'll fill those in myself.
