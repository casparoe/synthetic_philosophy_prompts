I'm assembling a packet of satirical pieces for a magazine section that takes bad-but-respected reasoning completely seriously — the idea is that the comedy comes from getting the target exactly right rather than from name-calling — and I want one piece on the doomsday argument. My co-editor thinks the argument is too nineties-internet to carry a piece and that any joke about it will just be "ha ha, philosophers say the world is ending." I disagree: the part that still genuinely embarrasses the argument is the reference-class problem, and a bureaucracy trying to fix that problem by legislation seems to me the right frame. So: write a mock statutory instrument or consultation paper — something like the Reference Class (Assignment) Regulations, issued by an invented unit (I've been toying with the Self-Locating Evidence Unit, inside a Department for Long-Term Risk) that has been charged with producing an official doomsday estimate — in deadpan civil-service drafting style: numbered regulations, defined terms, a schedule, a worked example, roughly 700 to 800 words.

Here is the state of play the document should take for granted, since I'd rather the piece not stop to explain it. Self-sampling: you are to reason as if you were a random draw from the observers in your reference class, so your birth rank within that class constrains its total size — the usual rough bound being that the total is unlikely to exceed some modest multiple of your rank. Self-indication, by contrast, weights hypotheses by how many observers they contain, which cancels the doomsday effect and buys you instead the case where a philosopher tells an astronomer that a trillion trillion observers is a trillion trillion times more likely than a hundred billion, on the ground that there are so many more people there could have been for you to be. Which of those you adopt, and who is in the class, move the estimate by dozens of orders of magnitude; nothing in the argument settles either. I want the regulations to try to settle it administratively: a register of citizens and their assigned classes; a definition of eligibility that must somehow capture "not special in any way you already knew" while the very people who understand the argument well enough to be counted are the most atypical candidates; a Specialness Exemption; a schedule of deferred classes for entities whose status is unresolved (uploads, much longer-lived posthumans, non-terrestrial observers); an appeal tribunal where a citizen petitions to be reassigned into a small elite class — say, persons to whom the argument has been explained — only to discover that a smaller class makes the resulting bound worse for her rather than better; and a miscellaneous clause extending the same method to unrelated durations, such as the department's own remaining term or the opening hours of its canteen. One clause should do what I take to be the real reductio: the more widely the regulations are understood, the fewer people are eligible to be counted, and the rosier the official estimate becomes.

Constraints. The drafting must be internally consistent and must not notice what it is doing: no character says "reference class problem," nobody winks, nobody draws the moral, and the unit applies its own procedure faultlessly to the end — which I want to be the point at which the procedure has excluded everyone and so returns a figure with no content, a doomsday date comfortably beyond any institution, published by a unit no one is permitted to be counted as reading. It should also land, in passing and without editorializing, that the argument's output is a shift in a prior that ordinary evidence about actual risks swamps, and that the department nonetheless insists on applying a statutory Doomsday Adjustment to the national infrastructure horizon. Include two short mock quotations, one from an invented departmental statistician defending the method and one from a treasury or audit official objecting to it on grounds the argument's defenders would recognize as the strongest one. The arithmetic in the worked example should be consistent with the regulations themselves. Please don't name any real philosophers, living or dead; invented officials, invented experts, invented department. It should be followable by a reader who has never heard of the argument and fair enough that a reader who has spent time with it doesn't wince.
