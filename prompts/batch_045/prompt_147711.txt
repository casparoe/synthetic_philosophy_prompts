You're a professional philosopher who's spent most of your career in the weeds of collective action problems — the inefficacy objection, expected-difference arguments, threshold effects, the whole mess. Please think carefully about this one and do a bit of brainstorming on the structure before you commit to anything; I'd much rather have you work out the shape of the thing first than hand me something smooth and useless.

I sit on the grants committee of a small foundation that funds individual-behaviour-change campaigns — climate stuff mostly, some animal welfare. We recieve about a dozen proposals a quarter, we get 45 minutes per proposal, grants run £20k–£150k, and the rest of the committee is a GP, a retired accountant and a local councillor. Nobody has read any of this literature and they're not going to. We also publish a two-line rationale for every decision, so whatever we use has to be something I can defend in public.

So: give me a decision procedure my committee can actually run, not an essay about why individual contributions matter. Specifically:

- An ordered set of questions, with what counts as a *sufficient* answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to the full board because the issue is really a values call rather than a factual one.
- It has to seperate three things that proposers constantly mush together: (a) the empirical claim that any one person's act makes no difference — no threshold crossed, or a replacement buyer/leakage effect, or the change is below the resolution of the supply chain; (b) the further step from "makes no difference" to "has no obligation", which is a normative move and not an empirical one; and (c) genuinely non-causal reasons people might still have — expressive or solidarity reasons, what kind of person you're being, fairness to other people who are doing their part, conditional obligations that only bite if enough others join in. I want the procedure to say explicitly which of these three our foundation is *allowed* to fund on, and to have a step whose job is to catch campaigns that dress up (c) in the language of (a) — pretend to be making an empirical impact claim when what they're really selling is meaning or belonging. Call that step whatever you like; I want it to be a real test, not a vibe check.
- It needs a rule for pivotality arguments — the "tiny chance you're the one who tips it" move. When is that legitimate (i.e. there's an actual threshold mechanism you can point to and a probability you can defend) and when is it a shell game dressed up as expected-value reasoning? And what do we do when the 45 minutes run out and nobody can produce a number: what's the default, and who eats the cost of that default?
- Each output should end in FUND / NO / BOARD CALL plus a publishable line, and you should flag honestly where the procedure will systematically misfire.

Then run it on three real-ish proposals:

1. A city-wide campaign to cut beef consumption. The supermarket chain wrote to us saying they only adjust orders in 500-case units, so individual people quitting just get absorbed unless they bunch up.
2. A "delete your old emails and cloud photos" campaign to cut data-centre emissions. We funded something almost exactly like this two years ago and it's become a bit of an in-joke internally — if the procedure says stop, I want to see it say stop cleanly, and I want to know whether it stops because of the size of the effect, the absence of a threshold, or something else.
3. A conditional pledge campaign: a heat-pump bulk-buy that only launches if 5,000 households pledge, with the discount kicking in at that number. Here pivotality looks real and the numbers look checkable — but I want you to work through what happens at 4,999 and at 5,001, because I don't think our committee has thought about that and I suspect it matters more than it looks.
