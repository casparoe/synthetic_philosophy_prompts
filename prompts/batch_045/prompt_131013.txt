I got pulled onto an incident review at work last week — we ship a support chatbot, and a customer asked whether their refund had gone through and the model told them yes, approved, processed Tuesday. It hadn't been. Nothing catastrophic, but we have to write the thing up, and the fight in the meeting was over whether the report is allowed to say the assistant believed the refund had been processed and so skipped the verification step. I took notes as best I could; Marco and Jen are both smarter about this than me and neither would budge, so here's roughly how it went.

(1) Marco: Intentional vocabulary is just a compression scheme. The sequence of outputs we got is predicted far more cheaply and reliably by this thing thinks the refund is done than by anything you could say at the level of weights or circuits, and on the real-patterns view that just is what it is for the pattern to be there. Wrapping every mental word in scare quotes is like insisting the center of gravity isn't real. We located the pattern, the pattern is real, write it down.

(2) Jen: The intentional stance is supposed to be underwritten by the design stance. A chess program gets belief-talk because someone built it to play chess well and there's a fact of the matter about what counts as winning. This model was not designed to have true beliefs about refunds; it was trained to emit plausible continuations of text. So the "belief" isn't latching onto anything in the system, it's our own projection, and in an incident report it's worse than useless because it sounds like we found a cause inside the system when all we did was redescribe its output.

(3) Marco: Two problems. Gradient descent isn't a designer with intentions either and there's no spec for a trained net, so if you require a design anchor you lose intentional talk about trained systems entirely — and by the same argument about evolved ones, where selection is the only designer around. And the anchor can't be doing the work even in the clean cases, because the whole selling point of the stance is that you can use it on a system whose innards you know nothing about. If you need the design stance first, they aren't three seperate predictive strategies, they're one.

(4) Jen: Fine, drop the provenance stuff, that wasn't my real point. My point is about projectibility. A real pattern has to earn its keep on data it wasn't fitted to or it's just overfitting — the conspiracy theorist's pattern and the face in the cloud compress the observed data beautifully too. And this attribution is exactly that: we replayed the same conversation with a different seed and it says the opposite about 40% of the time; ask about the refund in multiple-choice form instead of free text and the "belief" flips. There's no way to intervene on what it believes that isn't just editing the input string. It compresses the token stream we happened to get and predicts nothing else.

(5) Marco: That test is too strong, and human belief attributions don't pass it either. You can't intervene on your coworker's beliefs except by feeding them inputs too, and folk-psychological prediction of people is famously so-so. If counterfactual robustness is the bar, nobody believes anything, which nobody thinks. And "predicts nothing else" is just false — the attribution did predict the follow-up turns in the transcript better than the design-level story did, which is the only kind of prediction the stance was ever sold on.

(6) Jen: The human parity is where you're cheating. With a person the intentional pattern is one pattern among several that can come apart: you can check it against reaction times, against what they do when the stakes change, against whether they act on it an hour later, against what they say under pressure. Those channels can disagree, which is what makes the attribution testable at all. With the model the only channel is more text from the same process, and any belief we read off it is guaranteed to be there, because the system was trained on text written by creatures that do have beliefs. A compressor that cannot fail isn't discovering a pattern, it's finding the structure it was built to find. Call it stance collapse: the intentional stance can't go wrong on a mimic, so its success on a mimic is zero evidence.

(7) Marco: That's original intentionality sneaking back in through the window. There's supposed to be no further fact of the matter beyond the pattern's usefulness — you're demanding a mechanistic anchor that this kind of view denies exists even for us.

(8) Jen: I'm not asking for a further fact, I'm asking for a second one. Concretely: treat the belief as real only if some fairly different compression scheme also finds compression there — a nearest-neighbour/retrieval reading of the behavior, or an intentional description writen by someone who never saw the transcript. One scheme is a story; two independent schemes converging is a pattern. Marco: that's just intersubjective agreement in a lab coat, and two people can agree about the face in the cloud.
