Write a short story (roughly 700–900 words) built around the following invented constitutional device, which I'll call the Siren Clause.

In the fictional Republic of Marea, the constitution bars the government from selling assets held in a perpetual trust whose income funds long-horizon public goods. A government facing a declared emergency may suspend that bar by simple majority. But the suspension lapses automatically when the emergency ends, and every measure taken under it becomes void retroactively unless, within a year, a chamber elected *after* the emergency ratifies it by a two-thirds vote. The later assembly is therefore not deciding about the future: it is passing judgment on acts that have already taken effect, and refusing ratification unwinds the contracts, salaries, and pensions that now rest on them.

Set the story in the weeks before that vote. The previous government sold a large part of the trust to stop a currency collapse; the new chamber contains people who think the sale was plainly justified and people who think it was plainly a betrayal, and both camps are being lobbied by the parties who now own the assets.

Requirements:

- Do not settle the tension through dialogue alone. Let the institutional mechanics and at least one irreversible consequence carry it.
- Give one character the strongest version of the claim that the citizens who struck the bargain are not the ones now being asked to pay for it, so the whole apparatus is a confidence trick; give another the strongest version of the claim that this is precisely the situation precommitment exists for.
- Let the outcome of the vote be neither triumphant nor empty.

After the story, add a note of at most 150 words on the ideas it leans on: which claims about Ulysses contracts and constitutional precommitment you treat as settled, and which are genuinely contested among people who work on them. In particular, flag any place where the story presupposes a contested reading of whether a later assembly is in any sense the same agent as the one that bound itself, and whether a device that compels later ratification is a precommitment at all rather than merely an expensive default.
