I teach a short model-risk module to a room of quants and risk managers, and this year I want to run a session on agents that are embedded in the systems they act on — the embedded-agency idea that you can't treat the environment as neatly external to you, that your own hypothesis class probably doesn't contain the true environment because the true environment contains you, and all the self-reference / regress stuff that comes with that. I want one real case to anchor it, and I keep coming back to the 1987 crash.

Here's what I'm taking as given, roughly: on 19 October 1987 US equities fell something over 20 percent in a single session with no news event of remotely comparable size; a noticeable chunk of institutional money was running 'portfolio insurance' programs that mechanically sold stock index futures as prices fell, hedging a synthetic put; the official post-mortem leaned hard on that mechanical selling as a cause, and later work pushed back and argued the programs were too small relative to the market to have moved it that much. I'm not fussy about exact percentages or about who chaired which commission — if you think I've got the causal story wrong, say so in your first section and then argue from the version you'd defend.

What I want is the mapping done properly, and then the theory put on trial. Specifically:

- Treat the portfolio-insurance program as the agent and say exactly where the agent/environment boundary was drawn in the model and where it actually lay. Name what the model took as exogenous that wasn't.
- I've been sorting the failure modes into four: (1) boundary failure — the agent's own output is an input to the process it's predicting; (2) realisability failure — the hypothesis class can't contain the true environment, partly *because* the environment contains the agent, so there's no grain of truth to converge on; (3) self-reference — modelling your own market impact means modelling a system that contains your model of it, with the usual regress worry; (4) second-order embeddedness — the other traders were modelling the insurers, and *their* expectations were also part of the environment. Commit to which one is actually doing the work here, and say why the other three are the wrong primary diagnosis. Don't just list them.
- Say whether case (4) makes things worse or better than case (1). My intuition is that second-order embeddedness can be stabilising in a way first-order isn't, because reactions to a predictable seller can be self-defeating, but I genuinely don't know if that's right.
- The critical one: is embedded agency saying anything here that an ordinary risk person wouldn't already get from reflexivity, Goodhart's law, the Lucas critique, or just plain 'your model has endogenous feedback in it'? Commit — does the framework earn its keep on this case, or is it bounded rationality in a fancy hat?
- And be honest about the direction of fit: what would a team have had to observe in, say, mid-1987 to flag this in advance, and would embedded agency have told them anything they didn't already have? I'd rather you tell me it's a retrodiction gloss than pretend it predicted something.
- Where you're guessing, say so, and say what would move you off your diagnosis.

Give it to me in these sections: "Bottom line" (~100 words, committed, no hedging), "The mapping" (~300), "Diagnosis" (~350, one primary failure mode defended against the others), "Could anyone have called it" (~200), "Is this a distinct problem" (~250), "Where I'm guessing" (~100). Don't balance the options and leave me to pick — pick.
