I'm a senior at a large public high school, and I run a small student forecasting club. Every Monday we post a board — in the hallway and online — with roughly ten predictions for the week and the month ahead: whether the baseball fields will be playable on Saturday, whether the creek behind the school will top its banks in April, when the first frost will hit the community garden, how many people will come to the fall play, whether the courtyard will be dry enough for an outdoor assembly three weeks out. The groundskeeper, the garden club's faculty sponsor, the athletic director, and — somewhat alarmingly — a couple of local reporters read it and act on it. We've been doing this for a year. We have a rooftop weather station that logs temperature and rainfall, a spreadsheet, and a track record that includes two calls I'd rather not be reminded of: a harvest we organized two weeks early, and a field treatment the groundskeeper applied ahead of a rain that never came. Nobody on the team has had differential equations; we can do arithmetic, logarithms, and basic statistics. I have about an hour a week for this, and a faculty advisor has to approve anything before it goes up.

The reason I'm writing is an argument we keep having, which is now getting in the way of the actual work. One member says the world is deterministic, the equations fix a single future, so every failed forecast is just a measurement problem and better instruments plus a bigger computer would eventually get everything right. Another says that because of chaos anything past ten days is random in exactly the way a coin flip is, so there is nothing to choose between forecasts and no point in trying. I don't think either can be right, but I can't say why in a way that settles it, and I suspect the truth is a set of distinctions rather than a compromise.

What I'd like is a written procedure my team can run on each new request in ten or fifteen minutes, something we can tape up next to the whiteboard. For each prediction we're asked for, in order: the questions to ask, and what would count as a sufficient answer to each one. I have in mind a few distinctions I only half understand, and I'd rather you correct them than flatter me. First, the difference between not knowing the current state precisely enough and having equations that are themselves slightly wrong — I gather these fail in different ways, that the first kind of error grows and then levels off while the second drags the forecast systematically in one direction, and that this difference should show up in our own past errors if we look at them the right way. Second, the difference between predicting what happens on a particular day and predicting how many days in a month will be above some value; I've been told the first can be hopeless while the second is fine, and I'd like to know exactly when that gap opens and how wide it is. Third, the thing where a model produces a perfectly self-consistent story that no real sequence of events actually matches — whether we should treat our own run as evidence at all. And fourth, the arithmetic that tells us what better instruments would actually buy: I gather that each extra digit of precision in the starting conditions buys a fixed amount of extra time rather than an ever-growing amount, and I'd like that turned into a number-of-days calculation we can do on a spreadsheet, because it converts a philosophical argument into one we can settle at a whiteboard.

I also need the procedure to know when to stop. There should be a point at which it says: do not issue this as a date or a number, issue it as a range or a probability or nothing at all, and here is how to tell which. And there should be a point at which it says: this is not a question about the system, it is a question about what we're willing to risk — whether a false alarm that sends volunteers out for nothing is worse than a missed frost that kills the tomatoes — and that belongs to the advisor and the garden club, not to us. Please mark those hand-offs explicitly, and be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, because I'd rather know now than discover it in front of the newspaper.

Finally, please run the finished procedure on three of our real requests. The first is the first-frost date for the garden, which is the prediction we're proudest of — we've been close two years running — and I have a suspicion the procedure will tell us we've been getting lucky and can support nothing sharper than a window, which I will not enjoy hearing but would rather hear. The second is the April creek-flood question, where what people actually need is a yes or no about a threshold rather than a daily river level. The third is the outdoor assembly three weeks out, which I think the procedure may tell us not to answer at all, and where I'd like to know what we should post instead that isn't just a shrug. For each, show the steps as my team would actually work through them in fifteen minutes, including where we'd have to write "we don't know" for a quantity we'd normally just guess. And please end by saying plainly what a procedure like this cannot do — which questions about predictability it has no purchase on, so that we don't start trusting it in places where it is only giving us the appearance of having thought.
