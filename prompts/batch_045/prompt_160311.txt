hey — I run this really informal philosophy group, we meet at a pub every other sunday, five or six of us, none of us have any academic background at all, we just read stuff online and then argue about it. I'm the one who ends up writing the questions, and we each scribble an answer for about 45 minutes and then tear each other's apart. My problem is that if I ask the obvious question everybody just writes back the same paragraph they read on a blog that week, so I've started building in some angle that people actually have to think through.

This week we're doing the bug thing: the argument that there are such stupidly many insects — numbers with like eighteen zeros in them, I've seen different estimates and I can never remember which is which — that even if the chance that any individual one of them feels anything at all is small, insects might still be the biggest deal in the world morally, and we should maybe be spending way more attention on them than on cows or whatever. Someone in the group calls it the scale argument, I dunno if that's a real name for it.

Here's the angle I want the question built around. It's my own hobby horse and I've never seen anyone make it, so if part of it is confused please say so and build the question around whatever survives. The thing we're adding up, I think, shouldn't be bugs but bug-time. An insect that lives three weeks in a state where it might be feeling something contributes three weeks; one that gets eaten an hour after it hatches contributes an hour. And apparently most insects die super young, so on the one hand the total felt-time might be way smaller than the naive eighteen-zeros number suggests, but on the other hand short lives mean way more individuals pass through per year, so the amount of felt-time per year might be way bigger. I also can't tell whether the thing that matters is the number of possible subjects or the amount of experience, and whether those two come apart here. And then there's the ant thing: my mate thinks for ants and bees the unit should be the colony, not the animal, which would cut the numbers by a lot.

So can you write me:

- The actual question, for roughly a 45 minute handwritten answer, in plain words. Nobody in the group knows any jargon and I don't want them to have to, and we don't allow looking anything up once we start.
- A model answer of about 500-600 words, also in plain words.
- A marking scheme out of 20: five must-hit points, the marks each is worth, and for each one a one-line note on how someone is likely to get it wrong or wriggle out of it.

What I care about most is that the question can't be answered by writing "insects might be conscious and there are a lot of them," or by just shouting expected value or Pascal's mugging or fanaticism at it and stopping there. A really good answer should get to some version of these, and you should pick whichever ones you think the real crux is: whether the same move can just be run again for mites or nematodes or anything else with a lower chance and a bigger number, and what could stop that without being arbitrary; whether the chance we're multiplying is even the kind of thing you can put a number on and multiply, given that we mostly just don't know; and whether the argument ends up telling us to make insects better off or to make fewer of them exist at all, since that seems to depend on something the argument never says out loud.

One last thing: please don't let the question or the model answer rest on any specific published thing — no papers, no author names, no "so-and-so argues that such-and-such", no dates. We can't check anything mid-session and half the group would just guess and get it wrong and then we'd spend the whole night arguing about that instead.
