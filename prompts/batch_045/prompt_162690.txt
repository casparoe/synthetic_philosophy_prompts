I work on the design side of retirement savings policy, and I keep running into a package of measures whose parts seem to me to presuppose incompatible things about when a person's own will is doing the work. I would like the implicit theory reconstructed, and I would like to know which parts of it are strictly required and which are optional extras.

I am not asking about any particular country's statute. Take this as a stylized description of a design that exists in recognizably similar form in several workplace savings systems:

- Workers are enrolled by default rather than invited to join. They contribute unless they actively opt out, and the opt-out is a short form that takes effect within a pay period.
- The contribution rate rises automatically whenever the worker gets a pay rise, again unless she opts out. She is never asked whether she wants that; the escalation is justified by the thought that she will not feel the difference in take-home pay and would have chosen it had she thought about it.
- The balance that accumulates is, by contrast, tightly constrained. It cannot be withdrawn before a set age except under narrow hardship rules, cannot be borrowed against, and must be invested through a capped menu of options selected by the regulator rather than by the worker.
- Exiting is cheap at the point of enrolment and expensive later. Opting out at the start is frictionless; cashing out after a decade is not. Meanwhile the state runs campaigns presenting enrolment as simply what people like you do, which predictably makes the default stickier.

Here is the tension I cannot resolve. For the purposes of joining and of escalating, the state treats an outcome produced by inertia — no reflection, no endorsement, often no awareness that a decision was available — as the worker's own, and counts it as her choice being respected rather than overridden. For the purposes of investing and withdrawing, the state treats her active and deliberate decisions as untrustworthy and overrides them. The same person, the same money, two different accounts of what makes an outcome hers.

Some specific things I want:

1. The most charitable reconstruction. What view of agency, preference, or autonomy makes it coherent to treat inertia-produced outcomes as respect-worthy while treating active decisions as constrainable? Is the operative distinction the one I have heard drawn between interfering with a person's means and disputing her ends, or is it the idea that she is binding her later self, or something about the choice architect standing in a fiduciary relation to her, or simply that defaults are cheap to resist and mandates are not? Which of these does the practice actually need in order to be defensible, and which does it merely sit comfortably with?

2. Where it is underdetermined. Give me at least two theories with different foundations that would license the whole package as it stands — for instance a welfarist one that prefers nudges because they are cheap and reversible, a self-binding or Ulysses-style one, and one whose real motive is fiscal: keeping future retirees off means-tested support, which has nothing to do with the worker's own good. If all three generate the same institutional shape, what observation would distinguish them?

3. A name and a verdict on a pattern I think I see. I have been calling it agency laundering: the outcome of the default is credited to the worker's own agency when that credit is useful for legitimacy, and quietly withdrawn when crediting it would block paternalistic constraint. Is that a genuine feature of the practice or my confusion? What would a defender answer — I assume something like: nothing is laundered, because the default was never credited to her agency at all, only to the cheapness of escape, while the constraints are justified separately by consequences. If that reply works, what does it cost the practice's self-presentation?

4. What would change under a rival view. Suppose we accept that some architect must set every default, that no arrangement is therefore neutral, and that the only real question is which architect is trustworthy and accountable. Does the distinction between nudging and mandating collapse under that view, or does it survive in the cheapness of exit? Would anything in the policy actually have to change, or only its justification?

5. A second-order worry I want tested. The state also decides whether the default applies to you at all, sets the terms of exit, and campaigns to make the default sticky. Does the republican thought that what matters is not being subject to another's arbitrary power apply to the power to set and adjust the default itself, in a way that an easy individual opt-out does not answer? Or is an opt-out available to each person individually sufficient to defeat that worry?

Finally, a contrast that sharpens point 1 for me: in clinical settings, we will assume you consent to the operation unless you say otherwise would be scandalous, and doctors are required to secure informed, competent, voluntary consent — no defaults at all. What is the practice presupposing such that defaults are illegitimate there and legitimate here? Is it about the stakes, the expertise of the party proposing, the existence of a fiduciary duty, or something about how hard it is to reverse the decision afterwards? And if the answer is reversal cost, note that the savings design deliberately raises reversal cost over time.
