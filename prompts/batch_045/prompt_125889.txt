In a seminar on Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments, I tried to spell out his account of demerit and punishment in terms of the impartial spectator, and I ended up with an argument whose conclusion I cannot accept. I would be grateful for a diagnosis.

(1) In Smith's account, the judgment that an action is demeritorious and justly punished is grounded in the resentment of the impartial spectator: we represent the agent's injurious will, feel a sympathetic resentment, and thereby judge the action deserving of punishment in proportion to that felt resentment.
(2) Like any sympathetic passion, this resentment is aroused by the imagination of the injury; its natural intensity depends on how vividly the injury is represented, so an identifiable, nearby sufferer moves us far more than a merely reported or statistical one.
(3) Consider a colonial magistrate who can either divert grain to prevent a famine that will kill ten thousand distant subjects or rescue one local child from a burning building, but cannot do both. The distant deaths are known only through dry reports, while the child's peril is vivid and immediate. If the impartial spectator's resentment is simply natural sympathetic resentment corrected for personal interest and partiality, then the magistrate's neglect of the famine will seem less demeritorious, and deserve less punishment, than his failure to rescue the child, because the spectator's felt resentment for the distant ten thousand is comparatively faint.
(4) So Smith's theory predicts that the greater harm to the many distant subjects is, from the standpoint of desert, less serious than the lesser harm to one near child.
(5) But Smith plainly wants the impartial spectator to correct for nearness, acquaintance, and the mere liveliness of perception; he insists that justice concerns the conformity of actions to general rules that protect all, and that the murder or oppression of distant strangers is no less, and arguably more, demeritorious than a comparable injury to a neighbor. He would not accept that the neglect of ten thousand lives is less demeritorious than failing to rescue one child.
(6) Therefore, if the argument is sound, Smith must either reduce desert to the salience of imagined injury, which would make his theory a nonstarter, or concede that his own machinery cannot deliver the impartial verdicts he relies on. Since I cannot accept either result, one of steps (1) through (4) must be rejected or reinterpreted; but I cannot tell which, nor whether the flaw is an equivocation in the move from (3) to (4).

I am most reluctant to give up (5), and I would also like to keep the central Smithian commitments in (1) and (2) if possible. I genuinely do not know whether the trouble lies in the account of sympathetic intensity in (3), the inference to (4), or the claim in (2) that the impartial spectator is only corrected natural sympathy. Please tell me which step you would reject or reinterpret, explain why that step looked plausible, and indicate what each of the two nearest alternative diagnoses would cost.
