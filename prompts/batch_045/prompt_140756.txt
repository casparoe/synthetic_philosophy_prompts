I am a journalist writing a long feature on merit in science — on whether the machinery that distributes credit (a national research council's grant, a journal's paper of the year, a named chair) could ever be distributing what people deserve, as opposed to running a forward-looking incentive system that has borrowed the vocabulary of desert. Midway through the piece I want a single thought experiment in which the two ideas visibly come apart. I need it built to order, to the following specification.

The setting: two working scientists, one indivisible prize. One paragraph, realistic, no science fiction, no hypothetical choosers or ideal observers, no philosophical furniture on the surface — a reader should be able to picture the committee room. Everyone in the discussion agrees on every non-evaluative fact about the two candidates.

The verdicts required. A backward-looking desert principle — credit tracks what an agent made of the opportunities she had, measured against her own baseline — assigns the credit to A. An allocator that is sincerely trying to track desert, and is subject only to constraints it would be right to adopt, assigns it to B.

Anti-cheating conditions:

1. The allocator's failure to credit A must not be a failure of measurement. It may know everything about both candidates, including their baselines; more data, finer-grained criteria, better-calibrated reviewers and a larger panel must leave the verdict unchanged. Tell me why.
2. It must not be a failure of rule design either. The procedure must be one the allocator would be right to adopt and to announce in advance, applied without favouritism — not a crude heuristic that happens to misfire on this particular pair.
3. Nobody in the case is wronged: no bias, no corrupt referee, no violated rights, no accident or windfall the institution could have insured against. If the case needs an injustice to generate the divergence, it is a failure.
4. The two candidates must be close on both measures. The reader should feel the pull of both verdicts, not be invited to dismiss one of them.
5. Do not let the case depend on the move that effort and talent are themselves undeserved because they are products of luck, upbringing or genes. That argument appears in every article on this subject and I am not running it; the desert theorist in your story gets to keep her baselines.

One further constraint, and the reason I am commissioning this rather than lifting a case from the literature. Everyone I interview insists that a credit system has to be legible: the people inside it must be able to see in advance what would earn them credit, and to check afterwards that the rule was applied to them. Call this the publicity condition. Please make the publicity condition do real work in generating the divergence — part of the work, not necessarily all of it — and tell me plainly if you think it cannot be made to do that work, and why.

Deliverables beyond the paragraph:

- The two principles you are using, stated precisely enough that I can quote them in the piece: one desert principle, one allocator's principle.
- A step-by-step walk-through of why each principle yields the verdict it yields in this case.
- The single feature of the case that is doing the work, plus the variant: change that feature and nothing else, and report whether the divergence survives, or whether the case collapses into the familiar one in which institutions merely see proxies for what they value. If it collapses, say so and tell me what that shows.
- The strongest attack available to a desert theorist who wants to say the allocator got it wrong, and your answer to it — one paragraph each, in prose my readers will follow.
- One paragraph on how far this generalises: is what your case exposes a structural feature of allocating credit as such, or an artefact of the institution you happened to choose? I have to know whether I can lean on it in the closing section.
- A one-sentence version of the case that I can run as a subheading.
