Write me a dialogue, 700-900 words, between two philosophers, Rae and Sam, who are killing time in a hotel bar during a power outage and end up arguing about this case.

The case: their university's course-registration server went down at 3am. At 9am the whole team knows two things — that it went down at 3, and that there are only two candidate causes on the table: a bad deploy that went out at 2:58, or a power cut in the building (nobody has checked the building logs yet, and everyone thinks a power cut is unlikely). Both of them feel fine saying "If the deploy didn't crash the server, the power cut did", and neither of them feels fine saying "If the deploy hadn't crashed the server, the power cut would have". Then at 9:15 somebody checks the building logs: there was a power cut at 2:55, the UPS only holds for five minutes, so the power cut on its own would have taken the server down at 3 anyway, and the deploy made no difference. Now the second sentence sounds fine to both of them, while the first one sounds exactly like it did at 9am — and nothing about the world changed between 9:00 and 9:15, only what they knew.

Rae thinks there's one semantics for both sentences: you pick out the relevant "A-worlds" somehow, and the only difference is which worlds get to count — with the indicative you hold fixed everything the speakers are taking for granted (that the server went down at 3, that those are the only two candidates), and with the counterfactual you let the crash itself un-happen and ask which way things would actually have gone. Sam thinks theyre two different kinds of thing: how acceptable the indicative is just is how confident you'd be in the consequent once you suppose the antecedent, whereas the counterfactual is a claim about what the actual setup would have done, so what the speakers happen to know shouldn't be doing any work there.

Make both of them sharp, no strawmen, and make them actually respond to each other's points. I want Sam to press Rae on how a counterfactual can flip from unacceptable to acceptable at 9:15 if the worlds that matter were already fixed by how things actually were, and I want Rae to press Sam on why the evidence moves the first sentence while the facts don't, and on whether "suppose the antecedent and see how confident you'd be" can really be spelled out without smuggling in the same world-selecting machinery the counterfactual needs. By the end a reader should be able to say exactly what they still disagree about — one meaning plus context, versus two different things altogether — and neither of them should cave. Ordinary spoken language, no citations and no name-dropping of theories; the technical moves should come out of the example itself.
