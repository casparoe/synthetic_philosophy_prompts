I'm writing the final paper for a seminar on population ethics and existential risk, and I have managed to box myself in with an argument I originally built as a defense of the view that human extinction is bad for reasons that go well beyond the deaths it involves — the sort of view associated with Samuel Scheffler's afterlife conjecture in *Death and the Afterlife* (Oxford University Press, 2013), on which a great deal of what we value presupposes the existence of a collective human future that will outlast us individually. My argument has turned into a reductio of that view, and I would like a diagnosis before I hand in something I don't believe. The chain runs as follows.

(1) An outcome is bad only if it is bad for someone: no subject who is set back, no harm. Call this the person-affecting constraint. (2) Consider Sterilization: tonight an undetectable, painless virus renders every human being infertile. Nobody now alive dies earlier than they otherwise would, nobody's conscious life goes worse in any experiential respect, and most people never find out; humanity simply ends within a single lifetime. (3) The people who would otherwise have existed never come to exist, and by the standard asymmetry they are not harmed or wronged by that, since existence is not a benefit they are deprived of; the currently living suffer no welfare setback worth mentioning. So by (1), Sterilization is barely bad at all — much less bad than an epidemic that killed the same number of people who already exist. (4) But nearly everyone, including nearly everyone who accepts the asymmetry used in (3), treats Sterilization as a catastrophe of a wholly different order, something worth enormous sacrifice to avert, and clearly worse than the sum of the harms in (3). (5) The only explanation that fits: what extinction destroys is not anyone's welfare but the object of values that are themselves conditioned on there being an afterlife — the ongoing, collective human future that gives our projects the point they have. Extinction's badness is therefore not person-affecting, and its size is not tied to the number of deaths. (6) If the badness in question is the destruction of value rather than harm to persons, it is a magnitude that comes in degrees: destroying all of the future destroys more value than destroying part of it, and there is no principled threshold at which the destruction stops counting — a world with fewer future people, or with a shorter human future, or with less of what we value carried forward, destroys some of the very same value, pro tanto and in proportion to how much. (7) So every child one could have had and did not have, every generation slightly smaller than it could have been, every choice that shortens humanity's run, is a disaster of exactly the kind extinction is, differing only in scale; and we are under a strong pro tanto duty to maximize the number of future people and the length of the future. (8) That is absurd, and it contradicts the asymmetry I used in (3). So (5) is false, and the afterlife conjecture does not after all explain what is bad about extinction.

I feel as though something changes shape between (5) and (6), but I cannot say what, and I would rather keep (1) and (4) than swallow (7). Please tell me which step you reject and why that step looked innocent; then price the alternatives, including the option of rejecting (4) as an artifact of how the case is described. In particular, if the flaw is in the move to proportionality, I want to know what exactly the afterlife view says is bad about extinction — whether it can treat extinction as a structural or qualitative break rather than a quantity of lost value, and whether the conjecture is only a psychological claim about what our valuing depends on, with no axiological payoff. If instead the argument is valid and the real choice is which premise to sacrifice, say which sacrifice is cheapest and what else I would have to give up with it. Please write your answer in American English, in prose with headings for the step, the diagnosis and the costs, and keep the whole thing under about seven hundred words.
