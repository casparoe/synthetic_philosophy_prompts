You are a philosopher of mind and historian of artificial intelligence who has written on the mid-century cyberneticists. Please think carefully, and begin with a short brainstorm — a paragraph before your main answer — about what kind of thing could even count against a methodological thesis like the one below, given that it says where the difficulty in a research programme lies rather than predicting a measurable outcome.

My friend and I spent two hours arguing about Turing on learning machines last night, and I want a map of how exposed the reading I ended up defending is. The position: in the learning-machines section of the 1950 Mind paper, Turing proposes that instead of trying to write a programme for the adult mind we build something nearer the child's — comparatively little built-in organisation, a great deal of unused capacity — and then subject it to a course of education driven by reward and punishment, and I read this as a substantive and risky methodological claim, namely that the binding bottleneck on machine intelligence is the design of a curriculum (an ordered sequence of tasks, environments and feedback) rather than the design of the architecture or the accumulation of data and compute. On my reading the last fifteen years vindicate the letter of the proposal while quietly abandoning its substance: we did build childlike general architectures and we did train them, but education was replaced by one enormous, roughly unordered data distribution plus a thin final shaping stage, and the groups that got furthest spent least on pedagogy. Call the thing I think is missing the curriculum-substitution test: if, holding architecture and data fixed, you cannot substantially change what a system ends up able to do merely by restaging or reordering what it is shown, then the talk of education is decorative and Turing's actual proposal has still not been tried.

What would count against this? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and give me the ones a sympathetic Turingian should concede, not only the ones my friend would enjoy. Specifically: is there a reading of education on which pretraining simply is education, so that my distinction collapses into a verbal one? What would the world have to look like for the curriculum claim to be false — strong negative results about data ordering or staged training, scaling behaviour in which order becomes irrelevant — and how much of that evidence actually exists rather than being merely conceivable? How far has my position been insulated by the looseness with which I have defined curriculum, and is the resulting unfalsifiability the same objection my friend is making or a different one? He also pressed a historical point I could not answer: that Turing does specify an initial organisation and a definite training signal, so my blank-capacity gloss is rhetoric rather than exegesis. Is that fatal to the reading or only to how I dressed it up? I would personally find most persuasive any defeater that turns on the curriculum-substitution test already having been satisfied or already refuted by work in machine learning, rather than a purely conceptual one; tell me which of your defeaters are of that kind.
