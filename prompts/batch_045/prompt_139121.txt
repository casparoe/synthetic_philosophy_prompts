Exam question (20 marks)

Dennett distinguishes four grades of mind — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian and Gregorian creatures — and presents them as successive levels of a single tower of generate-and-test.

(a) State what capacities distinguish each grade from the one below it, and explain the sense in which the grades are said to be cumulative. [8]

(b) Consider a hypothetical species, the Kirr. Individual Kirr are born with a small fixed repertoire of foraging routines and an otherwise undifferentiated brain; over their first two years they acquire a stock of travel routes, food-preparation techniques and tool designs by copying their parents. When experimenters confront a Kirr with a novel obstacle, it tries out options in the world until one works and then retains the successful one; nothing in its behaviour suggests it evaluates candidate actions before performing them. Nevertheless, the transmitted tool designs improve over generations — handles shorten, blades acquire an angle — in ways no single individual could have worked out, and no individual Kirr shows any sign of grasping why the newer designs are better. Are the Kirr Gregorian creatures? Does answering this require settling whether they are Popperian creatures? [7]

(c) Does the case of the Kirr show that the four grades mark differences of degree in generate-and-test power rather than differences in kind of mind? [5]

Candidate's answer

Dennett's four grades sort creatures according to where the generate-and-test work gets done. A Darwinian creature is stuck with whatever natural selection has built into it: its useful behavioural tricks are in place at birth, and all the testing happened over evolutionary time at the expense of its ancestors. A Skinnerian creature can additionally be shaped within its own lifetime by operant conditioning: it generates candidate actions and the world tests them, but the creature itself has to pay the cost, actually performing the risky action and suffering the consequences. A Popperian creature has an internal surrogate for the world, so that bad candidates can be discarded before they are ever acted out. A Gregorian creature — the grade Dennett named after the psychologist Gregory Bateson — has a brain redesigned from the outside by imported mind-tools: words, maps, numerals, artefacts. It outsources part of its thinking to the cultural environment.

The grades are cumulative, but in a particular way: as a lineage climbs the tower, each new grade supersedes the one beneath it. A thoroughly Popperian creature no longer has to learn the slow Skinnerian way, and a properly Gregorian creature hardly needs to run hypotheses in its head at all, because culture simply hands it designs already tested by earlier generations.

On the Kirr, the field evidence shows no in-head preselection, and this is decisive. Popperian preselection is not merely a matter of behaviour being sensitive to how things would turn out; to preselect is to consider an alternative as an alternative and reject it for a reason, which only a creature with propositional attitudes, and hence with language, can do. The Kirr are therefore Skinnerian creatures at most. And because the grades are cumulative, if they are not Popperian they cannot be Gregorian either; so the transmission of the tool designs must be Darwinian after all — an innate template unfolding, with copying of parents an illusion or a by-product of the fixed routines. The appearance of cumulative design is misleading.

The case does show something, though. The four grades track how much risk a creature can shift off itself, so they are really differences in degree of generate-and-test power rather than four distinct kinds of mind: between the Kirr's templates and our words there is no bright line, only more of the same outsourcing.
