I write about science and ideas, and for about a year now I've been going back to the same moral philosopher — call her Dana — whenever I need someone to sanity-check a claim about why people do what they do. She describes herself as an internalist about reasons, in the Williams line: a reason, she keeps saying, has to hook up with something already in the person's own motivational set, not just be a fact about what would be good for them.

Except the verdicts she's actually given me on cases dont obviously line up with that, so I've started keeping notes. Here's the track record:

- Guy stuck in the desert wants a cold beer, has only warm beer, actively doesn't want warm beer. She says he has a reason to drink it.
- Man who's never given retirement a thought and has no desire to save, but who would want to if he sat with the numbers for an hour. She says he already has a reason to save now.
- Retired guy whose one enthusiasm is bottle caps. She says yes, he has a reason to go to the bottle cap convention — a reason, just a terribly thin one.
- Woman with severe melancholic depression who wants nothing at all, including treatment, and can't picture any future in which she wants anything. She says yes, she has a reason to get treated.
- Woman raised in a community where women aren't supposed to have ambitions; she's not mistaken about any fact, there's no deliberative route from what she now wants to wanting anything else, and she sincerely doesn't want job training. Dana says there's still a reason for her to look into the program.
- Man who lost his hearing, used to love playing music, has spent a year rebuilding his life around other things and now genuinely doesn't want to play or to go through the implant program. Dana says no, he has no reason to sign up for it.

New one, from a piece I reported last month. Marta is 47, has been on a heavy antipsychotic for most of twenty years, lives in supported housing. By her own account she's content. She wants very little, doesn't want work, doesn't want company, and doesn't want her medication changed. Her psychiatrist says a newer drug would probably bring back a much wider range of feeling and wanting within a month or two — she'd likely start wanting things again, a job maybe, friends maybe — but there's a real chance it also brings the psychosis back, and no way to find out without trying. Marta has no interest in trying.

I interview Dana again Thursday. What I need from you is the rule she's actually running, because it isn't obviously the one she advertises: strict current-motivational-set internalism, some flat externalism about what's good for a person, a view keyed to what Marta would want if her capacity to want were restored, or something about whole-life endorsement of the kind she seems to be using in the hearing-loss case. Tell me which of those fit her six verdicts about equally well, what each one implies about Marta, which way you'd bet Dana goes and why, what the closest analog among the six cases is, and what extra case I should put to her on Thursday to separate the rules that are still tied. And if the honest answer is that her six verdicts just aren't consistent with any single rule, say that instead and show me where the inconsistency is.
