I write long-form features on science and ideas for a magazine with curious but non-specialist readers, and I'm working on a piece about whether the argument Gould and Lewontin started with their 1979 spandrels paper was ever resolved, or whether evolutionary biology simply changed the subject and declared a truce. I don't want a survey of positions. I want one scene my readers can watch, and I want it to be fair to both sides.

You are a philosopher of biology who also writes for a general audience: you know the adaptationist research programme and the structuralist or pluralist critique of it from the inside. Before drafting, spend a short paragraph brainstorming: jot down the three strongest moves available to each side in the scenario below, plus the best objection to each of those moves. Only then write the piece. Think carefully, and prefer concrete claims to gestures.

Write a dialogue of roughly 1,300–1,600 words between two equally competent evolutionary biologists. They are composite characters, not real people; the names are placeholders and no living researcher should be identifiable in either. Call them Dr. Ilse Brandt, who builds optimality models and thinks of adaptationism as reverse engineering, and Dr. Kwame Adeyemi, who works on development and constraints and thinks adaptationism systematically overestimates what selection explains. Both should know the same literature, both should be able to state the other's position better than the other would, and neither should be allowed to win by being dim, dogmatic, or rhetorical.

Set the dialogue at a small fictional workshop convened by a fictional funder, the Lofoten Foundation, which is floating a policy it calls the Reachability Clause: grant applicants proposing an adaptive explanation must state, alongside their optimality model, which alternative phenotypes the lineage could actually have produced, and why. The argument should be anchored in one concrete made-up case, which you should present as a case study from Brandt's own lab:

A clade of about forty species of sessile, suspension-feeding marine worms (call them glass-stitch worms) secrete a mineralised tube with a helical internal ridge. The ridge costs about nine percent of the animal's carbonate budget. In flow tanks, tubes with the ridge experimentally smoothed capture about twelve percent fewer particles, because the ridge sets up a slow recirculating vortex near the tentacles. But: the ridge occurs in all forty species, including two that live in still water where the vortex does nothing; developmentally, any tube secreted in discrete increments around a body of fixed cross-section produces a stepped interior as a matter of geometry, and a smooth interior would require a second secretion mode no member of the clade possesses; and in the flow tank, artificial beads of comparable amplitude glued into a smoothed tube restore about eleven percent.

Requirements for the exchange. It must contain real back-and-forth: at least three moments where one character grants a point and then pivots to a harder version of their own position, and no speeches that merely pass each other. It should work through, without turning into a lecture: the charge that the Panglossian version of the programme is untestable in principle, and the standard reply that this charge hits after-the-fact story-telling rather than optimality modelling with an explicit currency and a measured trade-off; the original architectural example, including the objection that alternatives such as squinches or corbels were available and pendentives may have been chosen for aesthetic effect, and whether that objection helps or hurts the critique; Gould's distinction between causes of historical origin and current utilities, together with his point that how well crafted a structure is cannot settle whether it is a spandrel; and the disagreement about whether selection explains the persistence of a trait while construction and history explain its form, or whether that is one causal history wrongly split in two. Do not let them resolve anything by agreeing, do not end on both sides have a point, and do not let either character appeal to how most biologists today feel.

Style: no equations, no Greek letters, no citations and no attribution of positions to real named scientists beyond the historical figures already mentioned. Assume a clever reader who has never taken a biology class; any technical term should be explained in passing by the character who uses it. Give the dialogue speaker labels and keep prose stage directions to a minimum.

After the dialogue, add an afterword of no more than 120 words, headed Afterword, saying plainly what you take the exchange to have established about where the real disagreement lies. Then end the entire answer with a final line of the exact form \answer{X}, where X is exactly one of: REACHABILITY, if the crux turns out to be the empirical question of which alternative phenotypes were accessible to the lineage; NULL-MODEL, if it turns out to be a methodological dispute about whether an optimality model can serve as a testable null hypothesis at all; VERBAL, if it turns out to be a dispute about what adaptation and explanation should mean; INCENTIVES, if it turns out to be about which kinds of explanation the field rewards rather than about evidence or method. Put nothing at all after that line.
