Okay so me and my friend got into a huge argument last night at trivia night about filial responsibility laws and I cant stop thinking about it. I'm taking a night class on comparative ethics right now and we spent like twenty minutes on Confucian role ethics, and now I'm confused about whether these laws are secretly Confucian or just boring liberal cost-shifting. Here's the setup: in a bunch of US states there are statutes on the books (people call them filial responsibility laws) that say if an elderly parent becomes indigent and ends up in a nursing home or on state medical support, the state or the facility can sometimes go after the adult kids to make them pay. They're rarely enforced, and when they are it's usually some sensational case, but they exist. Meanwhile the exact same legal culture refuses to compel basically any other adult family relationship: no law makes you visit your mom, call her, or provide her with companionship if you don't want to, and family law generally treats adult ties as things you at least partly choose. So we do X here but Y there. My friend says the laws are obviously just about protecting the public fisc and don't really say anything deep about families: the state picks the nearest deep pocket, and it picks children because they usually have some relationship and maybe some assets, not because the law thinks being a son or daughter is intrinsically normative. I said no: the fact that the duty is triggered purely by the biological or legal fact of being someone's child, and that it doesn't seem to matter whether you were close, whether they were a good parent, or whether you ever agreed to anything, looks a lot like the Confucian idea that some of your duties are attached to a role you occupy rather than to a contract you signed. In Confucian role ethics, if I remember right, you're partly constituted by your relationships, parent-child is one of the five key relationships, and xiao or filial piety is supposed to be a basic norm you don't get to opt out of just because you don't feel like it. But I also don't want to overstate it: these statutes only kick in when the parent is broke, they only cover money for basic care, and nobody is legally required to love, respect, or visit. So maybe they're perfectly compatible with a hard-nosed individualist view too.

To make the argument more precise I started using a dumb distinction in my notes: call it a thin role-occupancy duty versus a thick role-performance duty. A thin duty basically says the bare fact that you occupy the role of child is what makes you the default addressee of a cost-shifting rule; a thick duty says the role itself comes with norms about how you're supposed to inhabit it, like care, attention, respect, maybe even obedience, and the law ought to enforce some of those. Which of those, if either, do these statutes actually presuppose? Can you help me reconstruct the implicit theory? What does the practice strictly presuppose about persons, roles, and obligation, and which rival views does it quietly rule out? Is the Confucian story something the practice needs, or just one narrative it fits well with, alongside reciprocity, gratitude, or liberal anti-free-riding? Where is it philosophically underdetermined, so that several different theories would license the same statutes? I also want to know what would have to change if we swapped the presupposed view: if the real justification is thin cost-shifting or anti-free-riding, would the law be better written as a general tax or as a claim against any deep-pocketed associate rather than against children specifically; and if we instead took thick Confucian role ethics seriously, what would the law have to add, like duties to visit or provide emotional care, duties on parents as well, legal enforcement of respect, or something else entirely? Please say where the analogy to Confucian role ethics breaks down as well as where it holds, and don't just force the two together to make my side of the argument win.
