Me and my friend (she's mid-PhD in philosophy of biology, I'm just a TA for our upper-level phil bio seminar) had a four-hour argument last night after a class on functions and I'm still not sure who won, so I'm turning it into an exam question instead. I've been asked to write one essay question for the final: 60 minutes, in class, closed book, 20 marks. I want something where you can't get above a B by opening with "the etiological account traces function to selection history, the causal-role account to what the trait currently contributes," listing three stock objections to each, and closing with "both have insights."

Here's the case I've been building, which you can rewrite but please keep the structure. A burrowing rodent has a bony ridge above the eye. The ridge was originally shaped by selection for deflecting the strike of a particular predator. That predator vanished from the range about 8,000 years ago, so the ridge no longer does that. It now does something else useful: it channels groundwater toward the corner of the eye and keeps the cornea from drying out, which helps the animal's vision and thereby its survival. But the population has been small and isolated for the last couple of hundred generations, and the best available estimates suggest the reproductive advantage this channeling confers is small relative to drift, so it has probably not been effectively visible to selection during that period. Two loss-of-function mutants have appeared in that window and both disappeared, but plausibly by chance. My friend calls the ridge's status here "vestigial momentum" — selected for something it no longer does, doing something beneficial it hasn't yet been selected for — and she distinguishes an origin-selected function from a maintenance-selected one. I think that's just exaptation with extra steps; she says it's not, because exaptation normally gets cashed out as a trait that got co-opted and then selected for its new role, whereas here the new role may never have fed back into differential reproduction at all. Settling that disagreement is most of what I want the question to do.

So: give me the question as I'd hand it to students, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific must-hit moves, the marks each is worth, and one line per point on how people typically get it wrong — enough that I can tell an A from a B from a C. The crux I want them to hit is not "which account is right" but what each account has to say about how recent the relevant history has to be: does the etiological view require the original selection episode, any selection episode, or ongoing selection, and if it requires ongoing selection, does it collapse into the causal-role view in the limit? I'd also like a second part: a lab-built organism whose lineage never had the ridge but into which an identical structure is engineered and which does the channeling perfectly. Does the disagreement about that organism show the two accounts are answering different questions, or is that pluralism a real resolution rather than a shrug? And my friend insists the question should make them say what function-talk is for — explaining why the trait is there, explaining what it does for the organism now, or telling us when it's malfunctioning — because she claims the malfunction case is where the causal-role account has to quietly smuggle in a norm it isn't entitled to.
