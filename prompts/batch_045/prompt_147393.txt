You are a research-level philosopher who specialises in normative ethics and population axiology, and who has been asked to build teaching material for a graduate seminar on the separateness of persons. Take your time with this: it is a design task, and the value of what you produce depends almost entirely on whether the details jointly hold up. Before you commit to anything, brainstorm several candidate setups and check each of them against every constraint below; discard the ones that fail, and say why. Do the arithmetic explicitly rather than asserting verdicts. Answer in labelled sections.

The task. Construct a forced-choice case that is a close cousin of the familiar torture-versus-dust-specks case, but that does its work without astronomical numbers and without torture. The original intuition pump runs three separations together: summation of tiny harms across many different people, summation of tiny harms within a single person's life, and a severity threshold below which harms are not supposed to accumulate into something worse than a severe harm. Your case should pry those three apart, by making three coherent views fan out over one mundane decision.

The three views, defined stipulatively (use these definitions and no others; do not appeal to how any named theorist is supposed to have held them):

- ADD, unrestricted additive aggregation: rank outcomes by the sum of individual lifetime welfare losses across all affected people; lower total is better; if two outcomes tie, ADD is indifferent between them.
- SEG, segmented aggregation: harms add within a single person's life and never across persons. Compare outcomes lexicographically over the list of individual lifetime harms sorted from worst to least bad: compare the worst-off individual first; if those tie, compare the second worst-off; and so on. Fewer or smaller at the first point of difference is better.
- LEX, lexical threshold with headcount: fix a severity threshold t, measured in the same units. Stage 1: an outcome containing fewer individuals whose lifetime harm meets or exceeds t is lexically better, and no gain at later stages can compensate for loss at this one. Stage 2: among outcomes tied at stage 1, the one in which fewer individuals suffer any nonzero harm is better. Stage 3: among outcomes still tied, lower total harm is better.

Specifications the case must meet:

1. Forced and exhaustive: exactly three options, one of which must be chosen. No fourth option, no doing nothing, no deferring, no lotteries, no probabilities, no uncertainty — every consequence is known with certainty in advance.

2. Realistic and small: one ordinary institutional decision of a kind that actually gets made (a hospital rota, a warehouse, a school, a municipal depot). No invented technology. The same twelve people, each with a role, are the only individuals affected in every option; nobody is added, dropped, or replaced, and nobody outside the twelve is affected.

3. One dimension, honestly measured: every harm in every option is a quantity of the same kind of experience, so that the magnitudes cannot be challenged as incommensurable. State the unit explicitly, and anchor the threshold t by naming a concrete harm that plainly lies above it. The worst single-individual harm anywhere in the case must be something an ordinary risk manager could cause — a fracture, a night of severe pain — and not death, torture, or permanent injury; the mildest harms should be genuinely trivial but not zero. All magnitudes are integers that a reader can recover from the prose of the scenario (durations, counts, repetitions), not free-floating stipulated numbers. Exactly one option contains any above-threshold harm, and it contains exactly one.

4. No dominance: no option may be better than another for at least one person and worse for no one.

5. Antecedent symmetry and irrelevance of identity: the twelve are antecedently equally well off and equally deserving, and no option may be supported by considerations of rights, consent, promises, autonomy, desert, precedent, or the agent's motives; each view must reach its verdict from the distribution of harms alone. Consequently, permuting which person suffers which harm within an option must not change any view's verdict, and you should confirm that it does not.

6. The fan-out: ADD must recommend the option containing the only above-threshold harm; SEG must recommend the option with the lowest worst-individual harm; LEX must recommend the third option. The three top choices must be pairwise distinct. Each view's full ranking of all three options must be derivable from that view's own rule applied to the numbers, and you must show the derivation rather than stipulate the result.

Deliverable, in this order:

(a) Brainstorming: three candidate setups, with one or two sentences each on why you discard two of them.

(b) The scenario: at most 250 words, with the three options, the twelve people, and magnitudes readable off the prose.

(c) The harm table: each option as an ordered vector of twelve individual lifetime harms, plus the unit, the anchor for t, and the total.

(d) Verdicts: for ADD, then SEG, then LEX, apply the rule to the vectors step by step, showing the arithmetic and naming the stage at which each pairwise comparison is settled; give each view's complete ranking of the three options.

(e) Ablation: identify the two or three stipulations that do the work. For each, remove it while leaving everything else fixed and report which pair of views collapses onto the same recommendation. Show at least one removal that collapses the fan-out into a plain two-way disagreement, and at least one that collapses it back into the original dust-specks shape of many trivial harms against one severe one.

(f) Robustness: state the range over which the fan-out survives — how far each key magnitude can move before some view changes its recommendation.

(g) Adversarial notes: for each of the three views, name the single stipulation of the case, or of that view's own tie-break rule, that a defender of the view would most want to attack, and say what they would propose in its place. Then say plainly whether you think the case is rigged, and against whom.

(h) One closing paragraph on what this case shows about the torture-versus-dust-specks dialectic that the original, with its astronomically many people, cannot show.

Finally: if you find that some subset of these specifications cannot be met together, say exactly which one fails, explain the tension in a sentence, and propose the minimal repair to the specification that makes the rest satisfiable. Do not quietly relax a constraint and present the result as if it had met the brief.
