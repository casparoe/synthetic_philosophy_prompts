I am a fourth-year PhD student writing a literature-review chapter on Dutch book arguments, and I have hit the point where I can predict every move in every paper, including the reply to the move and the reply to the reply. I want a diagnosis of why nothing moves, not another verdict on whether probabilism is true.

Here is the shape of the dispute as I have it reconstructed. The core result is not in doubt: if an agent's betting quotients violate the probability axioms, a finite set of bets at those quotients can be arranged so that she suffers a net loss in every possible state; and if they satisfy the axioms, no such set exists. The fight is entirely about what that result establishes. On one side: the argument is supposed to show that incoherent credences are *irrational*, full stop. On the other: the argument at best shows that incoherence plus a willingness to bet at your own fair prices leaves you exploitable, and the step from "exploitable" to "irrational" is either a non sequitur or a suppressed normative premise that does all the work. The main defensive maneuvers I can count are: (a) depragmatizing the argument, by using the betting setup to extract a representation theorem linking credences and utilities to preferences, so that the conclusion is about coherence of attitudes rather than about money; (b) the deliberative reading, on which the point is not that a bookie might fleece you but that you can see, from your own standpoint and in advance, that the package of bets you would each accept is one you would reject as a whole; (c) the scaling reply to the complaint that a sure loss of a few cents is no great matter; (d) the redundancy claim, from accuracy-first quarters, that the betting apparatus is inessential theatre and that whatever norm is available comes from a different quarter entirely; and (e) the dynamic version for updating, which seems to me to add its own assumptions rather than inherit the static argument's force.

Two things I have worked out myself, which I would like you to engage rather than take on faith. First, the theorem is protocol-relative in a way the literature often skates over. Take Ada, whose quotients on a three-cell partition are 0.3, 0.3, 0.3, and Cyrus, whose are 0.4, 0.4, 0.4. If the agent is only required to *buy* at prices no worse than her own quotients, Cyrus can be booked (he pays $1.20 and receives $1 whatever happens) but Ada cannot, and indeed the only bets available at her own quotices leave her a sure gain. Ada is booked only if she must also be willing to take the other side of a bet at her own quotient. So "incoherence iff vulnerability to sure loss" is not one theorem but a family, and the equivalence with the full probability axioms holds only under a two-sided protocol that agents with gappy or imprecise attitudes will simply reject. If that is right, part of the dispute is not normative at all — it is a question about which betting protocol the argument is entitled to assume, and different papers are quietly proving different theorems.

Second, two variants of my own, which I offer because they seem to break the usual deadlock and I cannot tell whether they actually do. Call the first the *fee-for-a-price-list*: the bookie pays you a penny merely to state your fair prices for a fixed finite list of propositions, and no bet is offered until afterward. This appears to close the recluse reply — the claim that the argument's conclusion is merely conditional and that a sensible response is to decline to bet at all — because here the exploitable position is reached by accepting a free penny, and declining it costs you something. Call the second the *bookie-free* or *internal-audit* book: a finite set of bets, each priced at the agent's own quotients, each individually acceptable to her, whose net payoff is negative in every state to which she assigns positive credence. No bookie exists and no money changes hands; the defect, if there is one, is that her attitudes do not fit together. My question about the second is whether it answers the force objection or merely relocates it one step back, and whether, once the money and the bookie are gone, it is secretly the accuracy-based argument in betting costume, in which case the framework is dispensable and the persistence of the debate is an artifact of notation.

My working hypothesis is that there are three questions traveling under one heading — a formal one about what the sure-loss results deliver under which protocol, a bridge one about what credences commit you to by way of betting behavior, and a normative one about whether exploitability is a defect — and that the debate stalls because a defense typically addresses the bridge question while its objector is pressing the normative one, so that the exchanges never touch. But I am equally open to the hypothesis that the whole thing is a verbal dispute about "rational," or that the accuracy-first literature and the betting literature are simply answering different questions, one about coherence and one about truth-directedness.

What I want: a diagnosis of which of these is right, argued from what each position actually claims rather than surveyed, including a check on my two examples and my two invented variants. And then, given the diagnosis, an honest account of what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result about the protocols, an account of the credence-to-choice link, an abandonment of the question — or an argument that no progress should be expected, and why.
