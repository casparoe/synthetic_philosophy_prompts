I was turned down for a loan by an automated system, and the letter explaining the decision didn't give me a rule or a reason in the ordinary sense. It gave me a what-would-have-happened: your application would have been accepted if your debt-to-income ratio had been eight points lower. I gather this form of explanation is now the default in a lot of places — benefits, insurance pricing, hiring screens — and that it was chosen deliberately over two alternatives: publishing the system's rule, or giving people a plain statistical statement like "people with your profile are approved 34 per cent of the time."

What I can't get straight is what this practice assumes. It seems to assume that sentences about things which didn't happen have a pickable truth — that among all the ways things could have gone differently there is one difference that is *the* explanation, even though in my case plenty of changes would have flipped the decision (higher income, a co-signer, a shorter term, being twenty years older) and the system printed just one, chosen by a shortest-distance rule across applicant features, with someone's decision about how to weight those features baked into what counts as short. It also seems to assume the difference it names is one I could bring about, which is strange, because the sentence is written in the past tense about an application that is already closed: what I want, and what the letter is really promising me, is that if I pay down the debt then next time I'll be approved.

So I'd like the implicit theory made explicit. Is this practice committed to a what-would-have-happened being a fact about the system — had the input been different, the output would have been — or to its being a fact about the world, about what my income or my debt would actually have done to my chances? Those come apart, don't they, when the system leans on something like my postcode, which I can change without changing anything that matters? Which reading does the practice need, given that its whole point is to tell me what to do? Where is it philosophically optional rather than committed — for instance, in refusing to print the differences that are true but unactionable or impolite? And what would actually be gained and lost if we dropped it for the plain statistical statement, which never claims anything about a case that didn't occur but also never says what to do?
