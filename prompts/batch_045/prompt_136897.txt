I have been working through the Dutch book arguments — the family of arguments that try to get from "an agent with such-and-such attitudes could be reliably parted from her money" to "such-and-such attitudes are irrational" — and I find that my reactions to the individual constructions do not line up the way the arguments seem to assume they should. I would like a principle that captures them, or a frank account of which of them I have to abandon.

My verdicts are these.

Clare gives A a credence of 0.6 and not-A a credence of 0.6 as well, so she will pay sixty cents for a ticket worth a dollar if A and sixty cents for a ticket worth a dollar if not-A; a bookie sells her both and she is down twenty cents in every outcome. I say she is criticisable as a thinker, and I would say it even if no bookie ever approached her.

Dan prefers the red bike to the blue scooter, the scooter to the skateboard, and the skateboard to the bike, and will give up a penny each time to get the thing he prefers; walk him round the cycle three times and he has his bike back and three fewer cents. I say he is criticisable as a thinker too.

Dora has exactly Dan's ranking, sees the cycle coming, forms in advance the standing intention to take the first trade and then refuse the second, and keeps to it; she ends with the scooter, having spent a penny. I do not condemn her, or I condemn her far less — and yet her preferences are Dan's preferences and only her resolution differs. This is the case I keep reversing on, and I would like it settled rather than respected.

Evan's credences are merely finitely additive: in a fair lottery over the positive integers he gives each individual ticket credence zero and the whole field credence one, and so he will sell you for nothing a ticket paying a dollar if ticket n comes up, for every n, while also buying from you for a dollar a ticket paying a dollar whatever happens. Every outcome costs him a dollar. I am unmoved: the book has infinitely many parts, and I do not think an agent is defective for declining a package he could never finish.

Fiona plans to respond to evidence she now regards as weak by becoming nearly certain of the hypothesis it supports, rather than by moving to her present conditional credence; a bookie who knows her plan in advance sells her a bet now and buys it back afterwards at a guaranteed profit. I say criticisable, but my confidence is visibly lower than in Clare's case, and I am bothered that the argument needs the bookie to know her update rule in advance.

Gil's credence in rain tomorrow is the interval from a third to two thirds: he will buy a dollar-if-rain ticket at thirty cents but not at thirty-five, and sell one at seventy cents but not at sixty-five. No book gets off the ground. I say he is in perfectly good standing, and I am glad the argument agrees with me.

Hana puts credence one on a claim that is in fact false; everything else about her is coherent. No book reaches her, since certainty cannot be booked. I still want to say something has gone wrong with her, though I do not want to convict her of irrationality merely for being wrong, and I cannot say what the further thing is.

Iris is incoherent in exactly Clare's way but declines every bet at any odds, on the ground that anyone keen to bet against her probably knows more than she does; she never loses anything. I say she is not criticisable — prudent, if anything. But if Iris is fine, then whatever is wrong with Clare is not that she can be made to lose money, and I am not sure what it is instead.

The rule I have been testing against these is: a set of attitudes is defective just when there is a finite collection of transactions, each of which the agent would accept as fair or favourable by her own lights, which together guarantee her a net loss in every outcome, where the guarantee uses no information the agent lacks. It appears to sort the cases the way I do. But I do not think it reproduces my actual judgments: in Clare's case I wanted a defect that is there whether or not anyone offers her a bet, whereas my rule makes her defect consist in what she would do if approached, which is precisely what acquits Iris; and it acquits Dora for having made a good plan, which feels like the wrong kind of reason, since a cyclic ranking looks like a fault in the attitudes rather than in the scheduling.

Please do three things. First, test the rule against the eight cases, then repair it or replace it, keeping the list of clauses as short as you can and telling me which case each clause is there to handle and what breaks without it. Second, tell me honestly which of my verdicts the result cannot accommodate, and say which of them you would overturn and why. Third, run whatever principle you land on over three cases I have not decided: a man who knows the objective chance of heads is 0.7 while his own credence is 0.3 and can be booked by someone who also knows the chances, where I want to know whether the book or the chance itself is doing the work; a woman whose evidence is never certain enough to condition on, so that she updates by spreading a shock across a partition instead, and who cannot obviously be booked at all; and a man whose present credence in a hypothesis, conditional on his future self's credence being 0.9, is only 0.4, because he knows he is about to be given a drug that will make him overconfident, and who therefore refuses to defer to the person he will become.

Write your reply as continuous prose, in the manner of a long email to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
