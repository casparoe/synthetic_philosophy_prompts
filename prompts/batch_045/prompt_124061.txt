So this has gotten weirdly relevant to my life over the last few months. My building got hit with a rent hike, and I ended up helping start a tenants' union with nine other people. It's gone from ten of us in someone's living room to about 400 dues-paying members, and now we have to decide what kind of thing we actually are.

Two decisions are on the table next week. First: a national housing coalition has offered us staff time, a legal fund and money for canvassing, on the condition that we sign onto their platform and that nobody from our union publicly contradicts any part of it. Second: our own internal rules. One group wants the standard setup — a committee drafts a program, we vote on the whole thing, and after a vote everyone defends the line in public even if they voted no. Another group, which I've somehow ended up in since I'm the one who keeps taking minutes, wants what we've been calling no-collective-voice: every statement goes out only under the names of the people who actually believe it, the vote tally and the losing arguments get published alongside every decision, spokespeople get picked by lottery and rotate, and we only ever mobilize around specific actions rather than a general program. A third compromise floating around is a whip on our three founding demands and free votes on everything else, and I honestly can't tell if that's wise or if it's the worst of both.

What's bugging me: we ran an anonymous poll, and nearly everyone privately disagrees with at least one plank of the platform we're about to adopt, and nearly everyone is going to vote yes anyway. When I said that seemed bad, someone said that's just what solidarity costs and that the alternative is a debate club.

I read an old essay a while back by some French philosopher — died young, wrote about factory work and mysticism, name escapes me — arguing that organizations like this don't merely make mistakes but are structurally set up to corrupt the judgement of the people inside them: that a group like ours comes to care more about its own existence than about whatever it was created for, and that the real damage is done to the members' ability to think rather than to any particular decision. It's been rattling around my head.

Is that a fair description of what we'd be doing to ourselves, or am I being precious about wanting to think out loud? If it's roughly right, does the no-collective-voice setup actually escape the problem, or does it just relocate it somewhere harder to see? And is there a point at which a thing like us turns into the thing she was describing — or are we already there? I'd like something concrete to look at when I go into that meeting, not just a vibe.
