I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I've got myself into a mess. My current book is set in a mill town in 1911 and turns on a woman named Ada who edits the town newsletter; near the end there's a trial scene where her lawyer stands up and gives this big speech about why the state has no business punishing what she printed. I wanted it to be a real argument rather than courtroom theater, so I sat down and tried to build it straight out of Mill's harm principle, and I built it too well: it now seems to show that nobody ever causes harm by opening their mouth, which would get my perjurer and my barn-burner off as well, and the scene collapses into farce. My writing group — one of them is a law student and is definately too pleased with himself about this — can't agree on which plank to pull. Here's the thing as I've got it:

(1) The harm principle: the only ground the state has for coercing someone is that his conduct causes harm to others. Harm, not offence or shocked feelings or the fact that most people hate what he said; and not harm to himself.
(2) Uptake-mediation: an utterance can get into the causal history of a harm only by way of uptake — it has to change what some hearer believes, or wants, or takes herself to be permitted to do. Print and noise are just ink and air; nothing bad follows unless somebody takes them up.
(3) Own-agency: when a hearer acts on uptake, what explains her act is her own deliberation — she weighed what she heard and chose. Giving someone a reason to act isn't causing her act in the sense the harm principle needs, or else the chemistry teacher causes the bombing. So uptake-mediated harms are harms the hearer brings about, not the speaker.
(4) Parasitic harm, generalized: the corn-dealer case Mill uses (or the version of it I remember, anyway) — a man prints that corn dealers starve the poor, a crowd reads it and smashes a dealer's windows, and we still don't punish the printer, because that harm exists only because third parties chose to respond to an arguable opinion by rioting. But every uptake-mediated harm is parasitic in exactly that way: it happens only because a free hearer responded as she did. So no uptake-mediated harm is the speaker's harm either.
(5) Warrant-uptake is nothing special: the objection I keep hitting is that sometimes the hearer isn't merely informed, she's licensed — she acts because Ada said so and would say "she told us we could," so Ada's say-so is part of why the thing happened and Ada is a kind of co-author of it. But that's still uptake. Treating somebody as an authority is a belief the hearer formed and then acted on, and if own-agency screens the speaker off in the belief case it screens her off here too. Call it warrant-uptake if you like; it's uptake with extra psychology, not a different causal shape.
(6) So nobody causes harm by speaking — not the perjurer, not the man who pays a neighbour to burn a barn and settles it with a wink, not Ada when she prints that the typhoid is only in the houses on the north side of the river, which is a lie and gets a family driven out of town.
(7) So if the harm principle is the whole of what the state can do about speech, all of it is protected and Ada walks.

Which I obviously don't believe, and neither does my law student, but neither of us can say where it breaks, and I can't put a speech on the page that a sharp reader sees through in a paragraph. Two things would help most. First: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd much rather keep (2) — uptake-mediation feels like the true thing about speech and it's the bit the novel is actually about — than keep (7), so don't just hand me (6) back with a shrug. Second, I care a lot about (5): I want the trial to turn on whether Ada is an authority in this town (people do defer to her, that's the tragedy of her) rather than just some stranger shouting in the street, so if (5) is where the argument dies I need to know what the difference is supposed to be, and what the prosecution could point at to show that her readers took a warrant rather than a belief. Also: if I have to give up (3) or (4), do I lose my answer to the "we can't print it, there'll be a riot" argument that Ada's publisher keeps using against her? Because if I do, her side of the last chapter has nothing left in it.
