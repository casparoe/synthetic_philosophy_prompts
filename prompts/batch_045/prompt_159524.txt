I've been going round in circles trying to figure out what I actually think about comparing worlds that contain different people, so I'm just going to list my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases and ask what principle, if any, they add up to. Welfare scale from -100 to +100, with 0 meaning a life that isn't worth living either way. Assume everyone in a world is a distinct person and nobody's welfare is affected by anything except what I say.

- 1,000 people at +70 → same 1,000 plus one extra person at +60: second is better. No hesitation.
- 1,000 at +70 → same 1,000 plus one extra person at +2: also better, but only barely, and it feels weird to call it an improvement at all.
- 1,000 at +70 → 10 million people at +2: I won't say the second is better. I think the first one is better, or at least that these two aren't ranked against each other at all.
- 1,000 at +70 → 1,000 at +69 plus 1,000 new people at +60: second is better, clearly. Everyone loses one point and you get a thousand decent extra lives — that looks like a great trade to me.
- 10 at +100 → the same 10 plus 990 extra people at +3: here I balk, in a way I didn't in the last case. I don't want to say the bigger world is better. (I know it's the same shape as the last case. That's the problem.)
- 1,000 at +50 → 2,000 at +50: second is better. Numbers do something when quality is held fixed.
- Nobody at all → one person at +80: I don't want to say the second is better, but I don't want to say it's worse either; it just isn't an improvement. Nobody at all → one person at -30: worse, clearly.
- 100 people at -60 → 100,000 people at -0.06, so the arithmatic total of misery is the same: the first one is much worse to me. Concentrated suffering beats spread-out suffering even when the sums tie.
- One person living 80 years at +60 → two people living 40 years each at +60: I think the second is better? Or maybe I'm indifferent and just being sentimental.

My own half-baked stab: add up the good, weight the bad more heavily than the good, and put some kind of ceiling on how much mere numbers can buy. I can't say what the ceiling is or how it's meant to interact with case four, where the trade looks obviously worth making.

Can you give me a precise principle, or two or three candidates, with a note on which case motivates each clause? And then: what does it say about cases I haven't listed — especially risky ones where the number of people isn't settled (50% chance of 1,000 people at +60 and 50% chance of nobody, versus certainty of 500 at +60), and the long-run one (a million generations at +30 versus ten generations at +90, all different people)? And be straight about which of my verdicts it definately forces me to drop. Also, a friend told me someone proved that no principle here can have everything people want at once — avoid the repugnant-ish endpoint, let mere addition through, not end up preferring a world with a few blissful people and a huge number of miserable ones, and stay transitive — is that right, and if so which of my verdicts is the cheapest one to give up? If you think the honest answer is that some of these worlds simply aren't comparable rather than one being better, say that too, and say what it costs me.
