hey — I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training past one required ethics elective, and I got dragged into an argument in our team slack about AI risk that I'm not equipped to settle.

A teammate posted a summary of Bostrom's orthogonality thesis and instrumental convergence thesis (from his book Superintelligence, I think) and then used it to argue that our roadmap is reckless. His summary was roughly:

1. Orthogonality: intelligence and goals are totally independent axes — you can pair any amount of smarts with any goal whatsoever, and how capable a system is tells you nothing about what it will want.
2. Instrumental convergence: whatever the final goal is, a sufficiently smart agent will seek power, hoard resources, protect itself, and refuse to be shut off, because those help with every goal.
3. So the two theses are actually in tension. If convergence is real, intelligence can't be fully orthogonal to goals, because getting smarter forces certain goals onto the agent.
4. Corollary: an AI told to cure cancer would, once smart enough, inevitably try to take over the world and stop us from unplugging it. So any sufficiently capable system we build ends up killing us unless the alignment problem is solved perfectly.

I'm inclinced to think this is overselling it, but I can't point to where, and #3 in particular felt wrong to me — though maybe that's just because I've misread one of the first two.

Then seperately he said the fix is easy: just give every system a bounded, scoped objective (cure cancer by 2030, then shut down) and the convergence stuff never gets off the ground, because convergence only bites for goals that are unbounded and never satisfied. That sounded convincing and also too convenient.

Could you go through this more or less claim by claim and tell me which parts someone who actually works on this stuff would grant, which are overstatements of a more modest thesis, and which are conflations of things the view keeps apart? Especially #3 and the bounded-goal fix. And if you can say what the two theses are supposed to establish when you put them together, that'd help — I suspect that's the part he's getting wrong.

One thing I actually care about, since it's my job: does any of this apply to what I build? I work on an LLM-based agent that plans a handful of steps, calls some tools, gets scored by a reward model, and runs with a hard budget and no memory across runs. What would have to be true of a system like that for these theses to even start applying to it — what am I allowed to safely ignore here, and what should I not?
