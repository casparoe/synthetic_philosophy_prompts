I host a small, audio-only philosophy podcast (no whiteboard, no slides, so anything that needs formulas on screen is out — though gesturing at them in words is fine). Next week's guest is Dr. Hallie Voss, who's just published a book called *Stakes, Not States*, and her claim, roughly, is that the classical no-free-lunch results for optimization and supervised learning are just Hume's problem of induction in a lab coat: averaged over all the ways the world could be, no method of extrapolation beats any other, so induction has no a priori edge and never did. Her way out is what she calls the asymmetry move — the tie only holds if you score methods with a loss that depends purely on which states or labels you got right, and it breaks, she says, as soon as the loss also depends on what's at stake in the world (missing a real predator costs you everything, hallucinating one costs you a sprint), so we don't need nature to be uniform, we need our mistakes to be unequal, and that's a fact about us rather than about the furniture of the universe. She's very good at deflecting with two lines I've heard her use in every other interview: "that's a theorem about averages, not about us" and "uniformity was never my friend anyway." I want a sequence that makes both of those progressively harder to reach for.

So: draft me eight to ten questions in the order you'd ask them, none of which just has her restate the setup, and each with one follow-up written against the answer she's most likely to give (she tends to say things like "the loss function isn't freely chosen, it's fixed by the organism's situation" or "I'm happy to be a pragmatist about method choice"). Grounds I want covered, roughly in this order: whether the uniform average over problem instances is doing work Hume never needed (his skeptic doesn't need a measure over possible worlds, only the claim that we can't rule out a change in the course of nature) and so whether no-free-lunch actually strengthens inductive skepticism, is irrelevant to it, or — the thing that keeps bothering me — cuts the other way entirely, since if every method ties a priori then induction isn't in worse shape than any alternative and the view starts to look like permissivism rather than skepticism; the technical hinge of the theorems, i.e. the way performance on one target function is paired off with performance on a permuted one, and what has to be assumed for that to work (finite spaces, closure under permutation, off-training-set error rather than fit to the data already seen) and what's left of her argument when you relax those idealizations; her asymmetry move in particular, whether a world-dependent loss really breaks the permutation argument or just relocates it one level up, and whether any old stake-weighting will do or whether she's landed in a relativism about which regularities get projected; Goodman, because it looks to me like stakes can only address Hume's old riddle and leave the new one completely untouched — the costs of being wrong don't seem to discriminate between two predicates that agree on every observed case; how her proposal compares to the other symmetry-breakers people reach for, e.g. simplicity priors or the meta-inductive strategy of imitating whichever predictor has been doing best lately; and Reichenbach's pragmatic vindication, whether she's reinvented it in decision-theoretic vocabulary, and whether a pragmatic vindication is even the kind of thing that should move anyone's credence rather than just their behaviour. Close with one question about what could count as evidence against the view, ideally one she can't answer with either catchphrase.
