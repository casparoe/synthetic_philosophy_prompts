You are a professional epistemologist who has published on the paradoxes of rational acceptance. Think carefully before you write: begin with a short candid brainstorm (five sentences or so) about what an account of rational acceptance is even supposed to be for, and only then produce the deliverable.

I want a specification: the requirements any adequate theory of when it is rational to accept a proposition must meet, given that its purpose is to underwrite norms of assertion and of inquiry for working researchers — telling a group what it may state flatly in a paper, what it must hedge, and what it must retract. Give the case or consideration behind each requirement, and mark it non-negotiable or a preference of one camp.

It must obviously handle the two standard cases: the fair lottery with many tickets, where the doubt is symmetric and the evidence purely statistical, and the author's preface, where the doubt is distributed over claims each of which she is happy to assert. It must also handle two variants I have not seen treated:

- The localized preface: the author has specific evidence that chapter seven, and only chapter seven, is shaky. Intuitively the blanket disclaimer in the preface is now the wrong speech act — she should either flag chapter seven or accept that chapter seven contains an error. So the theory must distinguish localized from distributed doubt and say what the first one obliges her to do.
- The opaque lottery: the agent holds a ticket but has no grip on how many tickets were sold or on the mechanism, only that she is very likely to lose. Any theory that works by a numeric probability threshold is silent here, so require that the theory deliver verdicts under imprecise or partially specified probabilities.

Beyond the cases, address at least: consistency of the acceptance set; closure under known entailment, single-premise and multi-premise separately; fallibilism, i.e. acceptance compatible with an acknowledged chance of error; action-guidance; the distinction between merely statistical and individual evidence about a case; higher-order evidence about one's own unreliability in a domain; collective acceptance by co-authors; and stability, so that the acceptance set does not progressively collapse as more propositions are added.

Flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and name the single requirement that excludes the most candidates. If you think the whole list is unsatisfiable, say which requirement to drop and what we lose by dropping it. Close with a brief scorecard — a table is fine — of four contenders: threshold-style views, anti-luck or tracking views, knowledge-first views, and views that split acceptance off from belief. Argue from the cases rather than from the literature; I do not want citations, a bibliography, or any reading suggestions.
