Two of my close friends, Nadia and Peter, are the children of a woman I'll call Miriam, and I sat in on their care-planning meeting last month at their request — I was there as a neutral party, said almost nothing, and took notes. Now they've both asked me to referee, and I genuinely can't tell who has the better of it. I'd like your adjudication.

The case: Miriam was diagnosed with early-onset Alzheimer's at 54. While still fully competent she executed a detailed advance directive. It refuses life-prolonging intervention after a specified stage, requires that she be cared for at home, and refuses placement in residential memory care. It also contains an anticipatory clause: if she later appears content in circumstances she would then have found demeaning, that contentment is not to be counted as evidence of her will. At 61 she has moderate dementia: she no longer reliably recognizes her children, she cannot follow the reasoning she once could, and she is now settled and cheerful in a memory-care facility whose routines she likes and would be distressed to leave. Her equanimity depends in part on a low-dose daily anxiolytic that the directive explicitly excluded.

Their jurisdiction has recently adopted what it calls the "persistence rule": a directive binds unless the patient has displayed, over at least six consecutive months, a stable pattern of new values or preferences that conflicts with it, in which case the current pattern governs. Nadia argues the persistence rule is not satisfied. Peter argues it is. Neither thinks the statute settles the moral question.

Their exchange, as closely as I can reproduce it:

1. Nadia: Precedent autonomy — a person's own earlier considered judgment should govern later treatment. Miriam made this decision with full knowledge of her diagnosis, precisely because she foresaw that she would later be unable to make it. Identity over time is what makes this her directive rather than a stranger's request; we don't get to discard a competent woman's instructions because a later stage of the same woman happens to disagree.

2. Peter: The authority of the earlier self presupposes enough continuity to make them the same subject of concern. Severe dementia is where that breaks down. What we now have is someone who is actually suffering if we move her, and actually content if we don't. Honoring the directive means imposing real distress on a living person to defer to the preferences of someone who isn't here anymore. Current well-being should govern.

3. Nadia: That reasoning voids every advance directive at exactly the moment it was written for. A directive that never binds you against your later inclination binds nothing at all. And by Peter's standard no one could ever tie themselves to the mast: any Ulysses arrangement is, when it bites, the earlier self overriding the protesting present self.

4. Peter: The Ulysses case is disanalogous in a way that matters. There the agent's ends stay fixed — Ulysses wants to get home alive — and what fails is his judgment about means while the sirens are on him. The earlier ruling serves the later self's own ends. That is why it is respect rather than domination. Here we have no reason to think the ends survive; if what has changed is not Miriam's grip on the means but the ends themselves, then deferring to the earlier self is just domination by a ghost. This is what the persistence rule is trying to detect, and stability over six months is evidence that what we're seeing is a new set of ends rather than a mood.

5. Nadia: Two replies. First, the ends/means distinction isn't usable here, because dementia dismantles precisely the diachronic, narrative-level capacities that make something an end rather than a passing affective state; what Peter calls new ends are local pleasures, and calling them Miriam's will simply assumes the answer. Second, the anticipatory clause is not a confusion: discounting in advance the evidence you know you will later produce is what a binding commitment is. Someone who says "if I beg you to let me quit tomorrow, don't listen" has not made an epistemic error.

6. Peter: On the second point: telling your future self's evidence to be ignored is exactly the practice we condemn everywhere else — it is a prediction protected from refutation. And on the first: even if the current enjoyments are shallow, they are the only ends anyone in that body has. Notice also who benefits if we honor the directive: not Miriam-as-she-was, who no longer exists to be benefited, and not Miriam-as-she-is, who is made worse off. The benefit accrues to Nadia's loyalty. A gain to a third party's sentiments is not a right against the patient. Also, the medication objection is separable: if the anxiolytic is what holds the distress at bay, withhold it and you have a terrified woman; your real complaint is with the drug, and you don't get to resolve it by moving her.

7. Nadia: The "no longer exists to be benefited" criterion proves far too much. It would dissolve promises whose beneficiary dies before performance, and it would dissolve ordinary prudence — saving at forty for a sixtieth year whose enjoyments "aren't yours yet." More than that, being the kind of agent whose commitments bind when inconvenient is partly constitutive of having a life at all; a creature whose undertakings never constrain its later stages isn't a person in the relevant sense.

8. Peter: But the authority of a commitment is normally earned by the fact that the same agent who made it will be there to answer for it — to bear the consequences, to endorse it or revise it. That is what makes precedent autonomy more than a bequest. Miriam will never answer for this decision; she will never look back on it. And a will disposes of what is still yours after you're gone, whereas this directive disposes of a life someone is currently living.

9. Nadia: Then decide it at the level of rules rather than cases. Imagine choosing the governing rule from behind a veil of ignorance about which temporal stage of your own life you will occupy. You would choose a rule that lets you bind your future self, because that is what lets you give shape to a whole life — to refuse a late state you despise, to protect projects that outlast your momentary inclinations. The expected gain to you as a temporally extended agent is large, and it is available only under the strong rule.

10. Peter: The same veil yields a weaker rule. You might be the later stage, and the harm to that stage is certain and vivid while the "shaping" benefit is speculative and often self-deceived — people are poor predictors of what they will later value. A rule that honors directives except where they worsen the well-being of the person who then exists secures nearly all of the planning value — you can still refuse treatment, still make a will, still protect your projects — without licensing the imposition of suffering on someone who cannot consent to it. The strong rule is over-specified: it buys a little extra control at the price of the only person left.

Please do the following. Go through the exchange move by move and rate each of the ten moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification for each rating, saying explicitly what in the move survives and what doesn't. Then say who is more right overall, and why. Then identify what both of them are missing — the framing, distinction, or consideration that neither side introduces and that makes the case look different once it's on the table. Finally, name the single factual question whose answer would do the most to settle the case, and say how the answer would cut. Throughout, tell me where the persistence rule tracks what is genuinely at stake and where it doesn't, and whether the six-month stability test is the right proxy for the thing it is being used to detect.
