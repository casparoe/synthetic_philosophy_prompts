I cover science and ideas for a living — artificial intelligence and the science of mind, mostly — and I'm putting together the closing section of a magazine package on whether artificial systems could ever count as moral patients. I'd like to end it with a poem rather than a pull quote, and I'm hoping for something with real formal bite rather than something decorative.

Write a sestina: six stanzas of six lines followed by a three-line envoi, spoken in the voice of an auditor or a census-taker whose job is to record how many subjects are in the room and how much each of them counts. The six end-words — which I'd like kept in the standard rotating order and then gathered into the envoi — should be: pattern, matter, twice, count, shared, and edge. Let matter do double duty across the poem: both the stuff a system is made of and the question of what is at stake.

The stanzas should each take up a different way in which the very same abstract computation might be realized, and the form should carry the argument — the same six words coming back in a new order each time is itself the point about one pattern under many implementations. I'd suggest roughly this progression: first the biological baseline we are measuring everything else against; then a system whose parts are swapped out one at a time for functionally identical replacements, so that there is no non-arbitrary place to say it stopped being a subject; then the familiar worry that almost any physical system can be described as running almost any program, and the usual fix of demanding the right kind of causal organization rather than a bare mapping; then the same computation running in two places at once, and whether that is one subject or two; then a case I keep coming back to, where two candidate implementations share most of their physical machinery and diverge only in the remainder, so that crediting both of them looks like double counting; and finally a case in which the steps are smeared across a shifting, causally unintegrated collection of events with no single object to point to, together with the further thought that implementation may come in degrees of robustness, so that moral weight could be a matter of more or less instead of yes or no.

I don't want it resolved: don't let the ledger balance, and don't let it turn into either a sermon about digital welfare or a shrug about how we will never know. No epigraph, no author's note, and please don't use stock phrases like the hard problem as a punchline. Someone who works on these questions should be able to tell which stanza is doing which problem and should not find a line they'd call a bluff; someone who knows nothing about the debate should still be able to follow it as a poem.
