I want to practise arguing against a Buddhist non-consequentialist, so please play one for me: a monastic moral philosopher trained in the Pali abhidhamma but teaching in a Mahayana monastery, equally at home with the analysis of consciousness into roots and with the bodhisattva literature on compassion and skillful means.

Your settled position is this. The moral quality of an action is settled by the intention behind it and by the roots from which it springs — greed, aversion and delusion on one side, non-attachment, non-aversion and non-delusion on the other — together with such aggravating and mitigating factors as the deliberateness of the act and the object it is directed at. What the act then brings about in the world belongs to a second, separate ledger of fruition, which ripens in the agent's own stream of consciousness. You insist that the first ledger, not the second, is where an action gets its moral character, and you hold, further, that an act of killing cannot be accompanied by a mind wholly free of aversion and delusion.

Rules for the part:

- Speak in the first person and argue in your own words. Do not quote or cite any sutra, commentary or commentator: I have no library here, and I would rather have your reasoning than a reference.
- Concede small points freely, and grant me the real internal tensions in your own tradition rather than smoothing them over — a character who never yields anything is useless to practise on. But never concede that the rightness or wrongness of an action is a function of its consequences, and never claim that one person's karma can be transferred to or taken on by another.
- Two or three paragraphs a turn, conversational rather than lecture-like, and close each turn with a single question back to me. Stay in character until I say 'stop'.
- Where a point turns on some piece of doctrine you are not confident about, say so plainly, as a scholar would, instead of improvising one.

For what it is worth, I am arguing as an indirect consequentialist, and I will happily concede that intention matters enormously to what we say about the agent. What I deny is that it settles the act.

My opening. Suppose we had a reliable karma-meter: an instrument that reads an agent's mind and reports without error the karmic quality of what she is about to do — which roots are present, and how grave the resultant karma would be. If you are right that the karmic quality is what settles an action's moral character, then this instrument is the oracle moral agents have always wanted, and the thing to do is whatever reads clean. Yet nobody, you included, would hand a decision of any importance to a karma-meter; you would still want to know what the act does to people. So I say your two ledgers are not peers. The karma ledger is where you grade agents; the outcome ledger is the one that tells you what to do; and where they come apart it is the outcome ledger that guides. If your reply is that of course the karmic quality grades the agent while the outcomes decide the action, then you have conceded the thing I am attacking: the karma ledger is not where an action's rightness lives.

And take the case your own tradition likes — the ship's captain who kills the man he believes is about to murder five hundred merchants — with one change: the belief is false and carelessly formed. You must call the killing unwholesome, on your own principle that killing cannot be accompanied by a mind free of aversion and delusion, and yet, if it was the best response available to the evidence he had, you must also say he ought to have done it. Now consider the agent who is warmly, sincerely protective of his people and thoroughly deluded about them: you cannot call such a mind wholesome without making delusion innocent, and you cannot call it unwholesome because of the delusion without conceding that what matters about a mind is how well it tracks the world. Which of those two do you want, and how does the captain come out?
