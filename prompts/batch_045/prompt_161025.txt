I host a podcast series on philosophy of mind for an audience made up mostly of philosophy graduate students and working cognitive neuroscientists. Each episode is a single forty-five-minute conversation with one guest, and I prefer to open with no more than a sentence of setup before the first question. Next week's guest is a philosopher of neuroscience who has just published a book defending integrated information theory against its recent critics; I'll refer to her below simply as my guest.

The book's positive thesis is the thing I most want the interview to press on rather than summarize. She argues that IIT's reputation for being hostile to computational neuroscience rests on a misreading: Φ, on her account, is not a quantity of information processing at all but a measure of a system's intrinsic causal power, and the theory's instruction to look for the description at which causal power is greatest is, she claims, the very same formal move that appears in work on causal emergence, where a coarse-grained macro-level description of a system can carry more effective causal power than the fully specified micro-level one. On her reading, IIT is not a rival to computational neuroscience but a constraint on which coarse grainings a computational story is allowed to use. She has two characteristic ways of deflecting objections, and I want the questions to anticipate them. To empirical objections she replies that Φ is not something you measure with a proxy but something you compute from the true causal structure, so any mismatch with current measurement is a problem for the measurement rather than for the theory. To objections of the form "surely that system isn't conscious," she replies that IIT is in the business of revising our intuitions about which systems are subjects, not of accommodating them.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions, each in spoken register and no more than two sentences, with a one-sentence follow-up attached to each and keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, assuming the two deflections above. No questions about her career, her route into the field, the reception of the theory, or the history of consciousness science, and no question that asks her to name papers or researchers, since we do not do citations on the show. Include at least one question apiece on the following, and use the remaining question or two for whatever you think the interview most needs:

1. The move from the axioms about the character of experience to the postulates about physical systems, and whether the axioms could turn out to fail for a subject whose experience is structured very differently from ours.
2. The exclusion postulate: what the theory says when there are two overlapping complexes whose Φ values are very close, and whether a small peripheral change — an added module, or a bit of noise — that shifts which one is maximal can really switch which entity is the conscious one.
3. The fact that computing Φ is intractable for systems of realistic size, and what a proxy measure, such as the perturbational complexity index computed from the electroencephalographic response to transcranial magnetic stimulation, can and cannot be evidence for.
4. Structures that look like poor candidates for consciousness despite enormous neuron counts and intricate connectivity, the cerebellum being the standard example, and whether the usual IIT reply about such structures is a prediction the theory made in advance or a retrofit.
5. Artifacts that look like strong candidates on the theory's own terms: critics argue that a regular grid or network of very simple elements can apparently have very high Φ, and I want to know whether she accepts the consequence and, if so, what the experience of such a system is supposed to be like.
6. The argument that a system can be unfolded into a functionally equivalent one with a wildly different causal structure, so that two systems performing exactly the same computation differ in consciousness, and two systems with identical causal structure can differ in function.
7. Her causal-emergence reconciliation in particular: whether maximizing causal power over coarse grainings is genuinely the same formal move as IIT's maximization, and whether, once macro-level descriptions are allowed to beat micro-level ones, the theory has quietly become compatible with the global-workspace-style views it was advertised as replacing.
8. The quality claim — the ambition of recovering the specific character of an experience from the shape of a conceptual structure — and how we could ever tell that we had mapped such a structure onto experience correctly, given that the theory is sceptical of the first-person reports we would otherwise treat as ground truth.
9. Graded consciousness and moral status: if Φ can be arbitrarily small, whether there is a threshold below which nothing is conscious, where such a threshold would come from, and what follows for how we treat insects, brain organoids, or patients under general anaesthesia.

Close the sequence with a single question about what result could count against the theory, phrased so that neither deflection she habitually uses — that the mismatch is the measurement's fault, or that the theory is in the business of correcting intuition — is available to her as an answer.
