Not a philosopher — I read this stuff on the bus and have nobody to ask. There's a pattern I keep running into in the free will material and I can't find the word for it, and I'm worried the reason I can't find it is that I'm describing it wrong.

So: you get these Frankfurt-ish setups. An agent is going to do X anyway, there's a backup arrangement that would step in and make them do X if they showed any sign of heading the other way, the sign never comes, they do X on their own, and we're meant to conclude they're responsible even though they couldn't have done otherwise. Then the reply arrives: the backup had to key off *something*, so there was still something the agent could have done or failed to do — not shove, not twitch, not furrow their brow — and that's an alternative possibility, the principle survives. So the builder goes back to the workshop and produces a version with no trigger at all: continuous monitoring, or the mechanism wired in so the competing path can't even begin, or the deciding done by the agent's own neurons. And then the reply arrives again, pointing at a new place the wiggle room has gone.

The thing I keep noticing: the alternative possibility never actually gets eliminated. It gets *moved*. Make the decision fully determined and it reappears in whatever the backup keys off — a micro-twitch, an earlier hesitation, the agent's own prior deliberation. Remove the trigger and it reappears inside the apparatus: the randomiser could have fired, the neuron could have gone the other way, the monitor could have been a millisecond slow. Drop the apparatus entirely and let the agent's own psychology plus the laws do the forcing, and it reappears at the other end — in the initial conditions, in the laws themselves, in the possibility that determinism is false. Every repair relocates it rather than destroying it. It reminds me of a conserved quantity: you can shove it around but you can't get rid of it.

Here's what I don't want, because I've bumped into it already and it isn't quite my worry. One: people saying the leftover wiggle room is too thin or too irrelevant to responsibility to do any work (a flicker, I think it gets called). That's about the leftover being too *small*. My thing is about the leftover always existing *somewhere* and always being findable, however small. Two: people saying the whole style of case assumes what it's trying to show. Also adjacent, but that's a complaint about the method, not a description of what the method keeps producing.

So the questions:

1. Does this relocation pattern have an established name — the fact that alternative possibilities keep surfacing at a new location in each repaired case?
2. If it does, how exactly is it marked off from the two neighbouring things I mentioned above, and from the plain observation that these cases are hard to build? Which of those is the best fit and where do the others fall short?
3. If it doesn't, coin something and define it properly, with boundary cases. Concretely: what would a case look like where the alternative possibility genuinely has nowhere left to go — not just nowhere we've thought to look — and is such a case even describable, or is the pattern part of what it is to describe a case at all?
4. Supposing the pattern is real, what does it actually show? My candidate readings: (a) the principle requiring alternative possibilities is being shielded from counterexample by construction, so the whole case-building project is pointless; (b) it's an artifact of how the cases get built and tells us nothing; (c) the real question was never *whether* there's an alternative possibility but *where* it has to be located for responsibility, and the pattern is evidence for that reformulation; (d) something I haven't thought of. Which one, and why?

I'll be annoyed if I just get a label. I want to know what this thing is, what it definitely isn't, and where its edges are.
