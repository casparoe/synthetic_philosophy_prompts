Hey, I need a hand drafting something. I'm on the Food Values Committee at my college — small liberal arts place, about 1800 students, one dining hall — and we owe the Board's finance committee a recommendation by the 14th on what the default protein should be in the next dining contract. Can you write the memo I circulate? I mean an actual memo: pick something, defend it, tell the Board what to do and on what timeline. Not a balanced overview that concludes we need more data.

Here's the mess. Four options on the table: (A) keep the current default, conventional feedlot beef; (B) switch the default red-meat slot to industrial broiler chicken; (C) go local and grass-finished, using the 600 acres the college owns and currently leases out as a soy/maize rotation, converted to pasture; (D) plant-forward default — pulses, grains, tofu — with meat as an opt-in.

Our analyst's numbers, rough and we all know they're soft, per 1000 servings at about 110 g of protein item:

A: ~0.5 animals slaughtered, ~1.5 acre-years of land (~0.4 of that row crop for feed, the rest pasture and hay)
B: ~70 birds, ~0.3 acre-years, essentially all row crop for feed
C: ~0.5 animals, ~3 acre-years, basically all pasture and hay on our own 600 acres
D: 0 animals, ~0.08 acre-years, all row crop

Wild vertebrate deaths we're working with: about 8 mice or voles per acre-year on row crop (tillage, harvest, rodenticide), about 5 per acre-year on hay ground from mowing alone. Insect deaths from insecticides: tens of thousands per acre-year on row crop, near zero on pasture — and two members of the committee think that's the whole ballgame while two think insects don't count at all, and I don't want us to paper over that.

Things that make it harder than the table:

1. Which metric you pick flips the answer. Per animal killed, B is roughly 140 times worse than A. Per animal-*day* lived in production it's more like 11 or 12 times worse, since a steer is out there for a year and a half and a broiler for six weeks. Per acre-year, A is five times worse than B. I want the memo to say plainly which unit we're adopting and why, and where exactly the ranking flips.

2. Substitution. When we pulled beef off the hot line last spring as a trial, 55% of those meals went to chicken, 30% to the plant option, 15% to nothing. And on plant-forward days hall attendance definitly dropped around 20%, with people driving to the burger place off Route 9, which is the same supply chain plus a car trip. So the paper difference and the realized difference aren't the same thing, and I'd rather we say how much we're discounting for that than pretend the table is the world.

3. Leakage on option C. If we take 600 acres of soy out of production, that soy gets grown somewhere else by somebody — our analyst's guess is about 0.8 acres come back for every acre we convert. So the row-crop deaths don't vanish, they move. Half the committee thinks that kills C's rationale entirely; half thinks relocation is still a wash. Also the student group Field Notes keeps pointing out that hay cutting on pasture kills voles too, so C isn't the wild-animal free lunch it looks like on a slide.

4. Baseline. Whether a field mouse's life is on balance good or bad isn't something this committee can settle, and I don't want the memo to pretend to settle it. But I do want it to say what follows either way — is there an option that's defensible whether those lives are net positive or net negative, and if not, which assumption is actually doing the work in your recommendation?

5. Politics, ugh. The alum fund that pays our sustainability coordinator's salary told us privately it won't renew if we adopt anything that raises the number of animals killed per meal. That's not a philosophical argument, and it isn't nothing either.

So: recommend one of A–B–C–D — or, if the honest answer is a phase-in, name the one the Board should actually vote on as the default and lay out the phases. State the metric you're using and where the ranking would flip under the alternatives. Give the strongest version of the case against your own recommendation, a paragraph, not a strawman. And give us a timeline plus a couple of concrete conditions, including what we'd measure over a two-semester pilot that should make us reverse course.

Keep it under about 900 words, and don't cite anything — we can't check it and the Board won't read footnotes. Final line, on its own, exactly:

\answer{X}

where X is A, B, C or D.
