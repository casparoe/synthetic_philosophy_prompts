I'm 71, spent about 35 years in experimental surface science (reaction kinetics on catalytic surfaces, so I have literally spent a career measuring response functions and then telling myself they were mechanisms), and since I retired I've been going to a philosophy reading group at the university near me. They've asked me to give a talk and I said yes, which I may regret.

The setup: 45 minutes plus half an hour of questions, joint seminar of the philosophy department and the divinity school, maybe 25 people. As near as I can tell the room will be: a late-career metaphysician who is a very hard-line Humean about laws; a powers/dispositions person who will have heard enough of this already; two philosophers of science of the manipulationist, interventionist stripe; one scholar of kalam who reads Arabic and will care what the text actually says; a philosopher of religion who works on divine action; a handful of scientists like me who came because of the title; assorted grad students.

What I'm defending, in four moves:

1. Al-Ghazali is not denying that cotton burns when it touches fire. The claim is that the necessity isn't in the objects. Everyone says that; it's the next bit that's mine.
2. The underdetermination he points at survives the best tool we have for finding causes, which is intervention. Whatever set of manipulations you run, both hypotheses — fire has the power to burn the cotton, versus contact with fire is merely the occasion on which the burning gets created — return the same response profile, and they return it by construction, because the occasionalist defines the occasion by whatever pattern the realist was calling the cause. I've been calling this occasionalist underdetermination. The only counterfactuals on which the two part company are ones about the habit being suspended, and those are exactly the counterfactuals an interventionist framework has no way to assign a value to, since no manipulation can realize the antecedent.
3. So the production content of a causal claim isn't weakly supported by evidence, it's structurally invisible to our methods — and I want to point out that physics more or less dropped production on its own: what you actually have is a state, an evolution law and boundary conditions, and nothing in the formalism is pushing anything. So the live question was never about production; it's about what grounds the uniformity.
4. And there occasionalism has an advantage I didn't expect: a habit has a ground, so the future's resembling the past isn't a brute hope. The price, which worries me, is that the ground is a will, so it's contingent and suspendable. I have a speculative last slide where I wonder out loud whether a habit, as opposed to a law, ought to be expected to be patchy and domain-bounded, maybe even to wear out, and I genuinely cannot tell whether that's a prediction or a metaphor.

- For each: a reply I could actually say out loud in about 90 seconds, in plain English, as a physicist who can't read Arabic and doesn't want to pretend to be an Arabist; plus an honest note on where that reply is soft.

My own guess is some version of: if your two hypotheses agree on every response profile there could ever be, what am I being asked to believe, and why isn't this just bookkeeping?
