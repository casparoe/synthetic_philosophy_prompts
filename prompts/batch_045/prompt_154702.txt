So I'm a novelist (four books, two of which you could actually find in a decent library) and last year I let myself get talked onto the board of the Aldridge Prize for fiction — mid-sized, one winner, money plus a residency, funded by a foundation that mostly keeps to itself. We have a vote in three weeks on a reform petition that a bit over two hundred of our members signed, and I'm the one who has to circulate the recommendation memo beforehand. I need you to write it.

The complaint behind the petition is that the prize has become a machine for anointing the same kind of book. Over the last six years maybe a dozen people have sat on the jury, and a handful keep coming back: our current chair has judged four times, and she's been on panels with two other repeaters often enough that people have started calling them the permanent jury. Two of the last three winners came from the same small press. Nobody's done anything corrupt — it's just that when the same eight people read for the same prize every couple of years they develop a house style and start mistaking it for quality.

The petition has two parts. First: three of the six jury seats filled by lot from the membership — anyone who's published a book of fiction is eligible, you opt into the pool — while the board keeps appointing the other three. Second: hard term limits, nobody serves more than twice in any ten years, chair rotates every cycle. There's also a variant that a couple of members keep pushing in emails and that I find more interesting than either of those: hold the draw at the *end* of the process, keep the appointed readers for the longlist slog, then pull three names by lot out of everyone who actually returned scores on time. So the lottery would select for people who did the reading rather than for representativeness. I go back and forth on whether that's a clever fix or a total misunderstanding of what sortition is even supposed to be for.

What I want from you is the memo, addressed to the board, bottom line up top: adopt as written, reject, or adopt with conditions — pick one, and if it's conditions, make them concrete and few. Who's in the pool (opt-in or everybody)? Same honorarium and same reading load for drawn members, or a reduced one? What happens when someone drawn declines? Can the chair be a drawn member? And please settle the end-of-process variant one way or the other.

Two more things that have to be in it honestly rather than buried. The first is my own conflict: I got my one real break when a repeat juror went to bat for my second novel in a room where nobody else had read it, and I've judged twice myself and would be termed out. But there's a non-selfish version of that worry and I want it stated at full strength — the career people have read four hundred novels in the form, they can tell when a book is doing something difficult that looks like a mistake, and a randomly drawn member is likely to sit quiet for two meetings and then vote with whoever spoke most confidently. If that's right, sortition gives you the look of openness while the real decision still gets made by the two loudest people in the room, which is worse than the current arrangement. Say what you think of that; don't just nod at it.

The second is the foundation director. She's neutral in public, but her three staff schedule everything, chair the meetings, draft the citation, and quietly do a lot of the shortlisting when jurors fall behind on the pile. So part of what I want the memo to face is who captures the process under each option, given that the staff is the only continuous thing in it.

Don't give me citations, reading lists, or studies show anything — I can't check it and neither can most of the board, so argue it out in plain terms. Aim for something a board reads in ten minutes and can vote on at the end. And definately don't give me a memo that hedges into no recommendation; I need to stand up and move something.
