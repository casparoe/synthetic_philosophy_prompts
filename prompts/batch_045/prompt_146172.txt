You are a philosopher of language who also works on classical Indian Buddhist philosophy — Madhyamaka and Buddhist logic in particular. Before you assess anything below, spend a moment reconstructing how the four-cornered negation (catuṣkoṭi) is actually deployed in Nāgārjuna's examination of causation, and what work the third and fourth corners are doing there.

A well-reviewed popular book on paradox has a chapter on Nāgārjuna. Compressed but, I think, faithful, the chapter says this: every question admits four answers — yes, no, both, neither — where ordinary logic acknowledges only the first two; Nāgārjuna applies this to causation, arguing that an effect arises neither from itself, nor from something else, nor from both, nor from neither, because causation as we naively imagine it would need something with an intrinsic nature and nothing has one; emptiness just is this absence of intrinsic nature, so things are empty rather than unreal, and their emptiness is the same fact as their dependent arising; since all four corners are rejected, Nāgārjuna is really asserting nothing, and the upshot is that reality outruns logic and cannot be described, which is why Madhyamaka is often compared to mystical traditions that tolerate contradiction; and in any case his denial of holding any thesis is itself a thesis, so the position collapses into self-refutation.

The author then argues for a reading he calls presupposition collapse: the four corners are not four answers to one question but two questions run together — whether there is a subject that exists independently, and whether, granting such a subject, the predicate holds of it — and once these are pried apart the tetralemma stops being an assault on non-contradiction and becomes an argument that no independently existing subject is available for predicates to attach to intrinsically; the neither corner is thus not a fourth answer but the rejection of a presupposition the other three share, and Nāgārjuna can keep classical logic intact and still get everything he wants. The thing that made me suspicious: earlier the chapter says all four corners are rejected, but here it seems to identify Nāgārjuna's own position with the fourth one.
