You are a professional philosopher who specialises in the philosophy of time and who has years of experience running hands-on, activity-based seminar sessions. Take your time with this one. Before you write the activity itself, spend a short paragraph brainstorming two or three candidate mechanics you considered, and tell me briefly why you rejected the others and chose the one you did. Then give me the full activity, ready to run.

The setting: I teach a second-year metaphysics module, 18 students, one 75-minute session. They have had exactly one prior session on light cones and the relativity of simultaneity, so they can read a Minkowski diagram, they know what spacelike and timelike separation are, and they know that distant simultaneity is frame-relative. They have not met any of the philosophical argument built on that result, and they have not heard the names for the metaphysical positions. The room is a flat seminar room with a large clear floor area, and I have masking tape, balls of string, index cards, pins, a whiteboard and a projector. No student computers, no money, no hallway.

What I want them to feel is not that simultaneity is relative — they already believe that, and it bores them. I want them to feel the pull of the step from there to the conclusion that every event, including the ones they would happily call future, is already real: the Rietdijk–Putnam style argument, in whatever version you think works best in a room. I myself do not hold that conclusion; I think the argument fails. So I do not want an activity rigged so that a debunk lands neatly at the end. I want the pull to be genuinely experienced first, and I want the moves of resistance to come out of the students as discoveries about what they would have to give up, with the costs stated in a form a defender of the conclusion would recognise as the real cost rather than a strawman.

Please build the session to the following specifications.

One. The stage. Give me a concrete set of roughly twelve to fifteen events — some on Earth, some on a ship, some on a planet in Andromeda, spread across a few thousand years of diagram time — that I can lay out as a large tape diagram on the floor (one time axis, one space axis). Tell me the coordinates to use, which pairs are spacelike and which timelike, and how to mark them up. Each event card should also carry a marker for when news of that event reaches Earth, so that the difference between what is simultaneous with you and what you can already have observed is visible in the room and not just asserted.

Two. Roles. Six teams of three, each team assigned an inertial frame: at rest relative to the diagram, slow outbound, fast outbound, inbound, and one team instructed to reverse direction partway through the session. Each team has a length of string laid at the fixed tilt corresponding to its frame (give me a small tilt key, and do not ask anyone to do transformation arithmetic beyond comparing tilts and directions). Teams may slide their string along the diagram but not change its angle, except the turnaround team, whose angle changes once at a moment you specify.

Three. The rounds, with minute markers across the 75 minutes. I want at least these moments to occur, and I want them to occur because of the rules rather than because I announce them:
  (a) Each team privately compiles a ledger of which events are happening now for them, corrected for signal delay, and commits to it.
  (b) Teams wager credibility chips on what another team's ledger will say, and predictably lose chips because they silently used their own slice as the default. This is the moment I most want engineered carefully: give me the payoff scheme, the starting chip allocation, what the chips convert into at the end that is worth having but costs nothing, and how many rounds of wagering to run.
  (c) The turnaround team re-runs its ledger after reversing, and the room has to confront a large jump in what is on their slice — whether they take themselves to have discovered something or changed something.
  (d) A chain-building round: starting from one event on the diagram and ending at another that is timelike separated from it, teams must build a chain of hops, each hop pinned by string actually lying across both events in some team's frame. Because the endpoints are timelike related, the chain has to zig-zag through distant events rather than sit on one slice. Give me the sequence you expect them to find, and the facilitator prompt that gets them there without supplying it.
  (e) A bookkeeping round in which one or two students are given the job of producing a single global table recording, for every pair of events, whether the two are real together — and who run into the fact that the table will not close. They should be able to see four ways out: let the table contain contradictions, drop transitivity of co-reality, posit a privileged slicing that no one can detect, or relativise reality to a frame. Give me these four exits framed as questions students can arrive at, and give me the single sharpest question to put to each.

Four. The debrief, 20 minutes, mapping each of the moments above onto the premise of the argument it is meant to make vivid — relativity of simultaneity, the thesis that simultaneity with a real event confers reality, the transitivity of that relation across the chain, and the conclusion — and saying plainly which premise is doing the real work and which is the one I think is false. The terms block universe, eternalism, presentism, growing block, and fragmentalism should appear for the first time here and nowhere earlier; tell me where in the debrief each gets introduced. Also tell me what the two or three strongest resistance moves are that the activity will not have surfaced on its own, since I will have to supply those myself.

Five. An honest list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, with a handling note for each. I am especially worried about: students treating the issue as one about knowledge or prediction rather than about what exists; teams nudging their string angles; the student who says the whole thing only shows that simultaneity is frame-relative and asks what the fuss is about; the spatial dimension the floor diagram suppresses; and the arithmetic of the turnaround team's jump looking like a trick.

Six. An honest note on which feature of the argument the activity cannot reproduce at all and which therefore has to arrive as testimony from me. I have in mind something like the difference between an event's being real and its being determinate or knowable, and the fact that the whole exercise presupposes a single global flat-spacetime frame structure that the theory does not strictly hand you — but say what you think the real limitation is, not just the ones I have guessed at.

Do not give me a reading list, citations, or suggestions for further reading; I want the activity and nothing else.
