I write novels, and I have agreed, perhaps rashly, to give the closing talk at a two-day conference on consciousness and narrative hosted by a literature department three weeks from now: forty-five minutes, then twenty minutes of questions. I have no graduate training in philosophy; I read philosophy of mind as a serious amateur, and the organizers invited me partly because of that. It is also why I am nervous.

The view I want to defend is a version of illusionism about phenomenal consciousness — the family of positions associated with Dennett and Frankish — but with a mechanism and a stopping rule that I think the familiar statements of the view leave obscure, and it is the mechanism, not the conclusion, that I expect to be attacked.

Sketch of the argument, so that the objections have something to bite on:

1. An introspective avowal — saying, or inwardly affirming, that one's pain is throbbing now, or that the blue of the wall looks like *that* — is the output of a monitoring subsystem that publishes a compressed, action-guiding summary of the organism's current state, both for the organism's own downstream systems and for broadcast to other people.
2. That summary is constructed from partial, noisy signals under time pressure and is edited for usability. Call such an output a *draft* rather than a transcription. My slogan: introspection drafts, it does not read.
3. What we call the seeming — the insistence that it still seems to me, even now, as though there is something it is like — is not a further state that the draft is about; it is the draft being issued, plus the fact that the system will reissue it under challenge.
4. Introspective ascent has a floor. One can attend to one's attending, and to that attending, but only two or three levels down; the state at the bottom of the stack is not something one can attend to, and so there is no *how it seems* left over there. Call this the *recursion floor*, and say that the floor is where the regress of seemings stops — not because the floor state is qualitatively loaded, but because nothing looks at it.
5. Because avowals are drafts, their correctness is not accuracy but something like serviceability: an avowal is *adequate* when it is good enough to steer by and good enough for others to take up. Call this draft-adequacy.
6. With the drafting mechanism, the recursion floor, and draft-adequacy in place, phenomenal character does no explanatory work: the reports, the subjective conviction, the behavior, the neural story, and the practical and moral weight of pain are all accounted for without it.
7. So there are no phenomenal properties. The conviction that there are is itself a draft — the self-model's standing editorial line about itself, reissued on demand.

Two things I most want to keep: that something real is going on in us when we avow (I am not an eliminativist about the machinery — I want the monitoring subsystem, the drafts, and the floor to be as real as anything in cognitive science), and that pain retains its full moral seriousness. If forced to choose, I would rather give up the conclusion than either of those.

The room. Roughly fifty people. Most are novelists, literary scholars and critics: they are committed to first-person authority and to the idea that fiction is the art form with privileged access to interiority, they are suspicious of anything that smells like scientism, and they will object in the vocabulary of lived experience, of what it is like, and of what my view does to the practice they care about. Two are analytic philosophers of mind: one a convinced realist about phenomenal properties who has written on the knowledge argument and who will press the hard problem in its most patient form; the other sympathetic to illusionism, and therefore more dangerous, because she will object from inside the view — that I have gone too far, or not far enough, or have quietly helped myself to what I claim to have removed. One is a cognitive psychologist who works on metacognitive confidence and introspective accuracy; she will want to know what would change my mind, what the view predicts, and why this is not something the confabulation literature established decades ago in less theatrical language. Finally, a dozen graduate students from a medical humanities programme who write about pain and suffering, and who will want to know whether a woman in severe pain is, on my account, being told that the most important thing about her situation is a draft.

What I would like is my Q&A preparation sheet. Give me the eight to ten objections I will actually get, ordered by how much damage they would do, each attributed to the person or group who will raise it and phrased the way that person would phrase it — the literary scholars should not sound like the philosopher of mind, and neither should sound like the psychologist. For each: a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Tell me which objections require a real concession up front rather than a reply, and what to concede. Distinguish, please, the objection that would lose me the *argument* from the one that would lose me the *room*, since in this audience I suspect they are not the same objection, and say which single reply has to land for the talk to survive. Include as well the question I am privately hoping nobody asks: my own candidate is whether, if avowals are drafts whose correctness is adequacy rather than truth, the sentence *there are no phenomenal properties* is itself merely adequate — in which case I have not said anything true. But if you think the real landmine is somewhere else in the argument, say so and prepare me for that one instead.
