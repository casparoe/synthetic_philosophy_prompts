hey, I need a thought experiment built to order for something I'm writing on what matters in survival. I want a *diamond* case rather than the usual Y case: a teletransporter variant where a persons stream splits in two and then, later, the two branches fuse back into a single person.

Three positions I want verdicts out of (my labels, I dont care who really holds them):

- R: what matters in survival is psychological continuity and connectedness however it's caused, and it can hold more or less strongly. Branching in itself costs nothing: if my stream branches, what matters holds with both branches, undiminished, and the fact that there are now two of me isn't a loss.
- U: what matters is that same stuff plus uniqueness — at any time at which I exist there has to be exactly one stream that's mine. Branching switches what matters off for as long as the branches run in parallel.
- D: what matters is that same stuff, but it comes in a fixed total amount that gets divided among branches — two branches get half each, and fusing puts the halves back together.

What I want from you:

1. The scenario, one paragraph, ordinary teletransporter kit only (scan, rebuild, destroy) — no time travel, no three-way splits, no further fact about identity, no souls. Two branches, then one fusion. Both branches have to be full-strength, neither is a degraded or partial copy, and the seperated period has to be long enough that each branch lives its own life for a few months and picks up new memories and relationships. You'll also need to say in a sentence how the fusion deals with the fact that the branches have drifted into being slightly different people — that's the bit I keep getting stuck on.

2. Then walk through the verdicts. For each of R, U and D: does what matters obtain (a) before the split, (b) during the split, (c) after the fusion — and why, from that view's own machinery. Don't stipulate the verdicts; show me which feature of the case each one is read off.

3. The verdicts have to be decision-relevant or the whole exercise is verbal. The person knows the setup in advance and is deciding whether to press the button. Tell me, for each view, whether pressing is as good as an ordinary night's sleep, somewhat worse, or as bad as death, and roughly how much (if anything) they'd pay to avoid it. If you can't turn one of the verdicts into a real difference in the person's reasons, say so plainly — I'd rather hear that than get three labels that sound different and amount to the same thing.

Constraints, because it's very easy to cheat here: strip out everything that normally makes fission cases look bad but isn't the branching itself. No rivalry between the branches, no competition over the same body, job or partner, nobody worse off, nobody deceived, both branches informed and consenting, practical stuff like wills and promises settled in advance. If any of that leaks back in, then U's no is just inherited from the collateral mess and the case proves nothing.

4. Say which single structural feature is doing the work — I'm guessing it's the fusing-back rather than the branching — and what happens when you remove it. If the branches never fuse (plain old fission), do any of the three verdicts actually change? And if the split lasts a millisecond instead of a few months, does the disagreement between U and D collapse?

5. Be honest about which of the three views your case treats unfairly, and how a defender of that view would attack it. And tell me straight if you think the three-way split can't be generated cleanly without smuggling something in — that's a useful answer too.

What it's for: I'm trying to work out whether the uniqueness requirement (U) and the divisibility requirement (D) do any work that isn't already done by asking how strongly R holds, or whether they're just two ways of asserting that branching is bad and then arguing about the arithmetic. So the ways to cheat here are: stipulating verdicts instead of deriving them, making one branch damaged or diminished so that R itself fails, or letting the badness come from anything other than the bare structure of split-then-fuse.
