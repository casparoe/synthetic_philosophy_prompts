so my group chat (very EA-adjacent, lots of wonk) has been going around on discount rates again, and I keep reaching for an analogy that I now suspect is doing all the work: distance in time is just like distance in space. The version I used: nobody thinks the welfare of someone in another country counts for less just because they're far away, so caring less about someone in 2200 merely because they're later is the same species of parochialism -- a -ism with a worse name -- and the upshot is that pure time preference is zero, and the only discounting that survives is for the chance the benefit never lands (extinction, collapse, the whole chain just not arriving) and for the fact that future people will probably be richer, so a unit of consumption is worth less to them. That's the decomposition everyone quotes, rate = pure preference + elasticity times growth, and the entire fight is whether the first term is 0 or something like a couple percent.

Someone pushed back and i think he's right that I was cheating: the analogy proves too much, because basically everyone accepts spatial partiality -- you give to your local food bank, you care more about your own countrymen, nobody calls you a bigot -- so either it licenses nothing (spatial discounting is fine, so temporal discounting is fine) or it only has teeth if you've already bought a very demanding impartiality, in which case the analogy isn't what's carrying the conclusion.

But I also think space has features that time just doesn't, and I can't tell which of them matter: spatial distance is symmetric and traversable, and the people there exist whether or not I do anything, whereas the future is causally downstream of us, the near future contains us-in-thirty-years, and who exists later partly depends on what we do now. There's also no market with the future -- nobody in 2200 can compensate us or trade with us -- so whatever an interest rate is measuring, it isn't an exchange rate between present and future people. And I have a suspicion that a lot of what looks like pure time preference in cost-benefit work is actually probability-of-non-arrival wearing a hat. One more thing i keep tripping on: caring less about your own headache next Tuesday feels like a different animal from caring less about a stranger's headache in 2200, and I don't know if the analogy is even aimed at both or only at the second.

Can you audit this properly? I want the correspondence table -- what plays the role of distance, of the stranger, of the transport cost, of the border -- then which disanalogies actually bite on the conclusion I wanted (rho = 0; and if it isn't 0, what it's allowed to be) versus which are trivia. Especially: does the causal-downstream-ness of the future cut for my conclusion or against it, and does the no-reciprocity point turn out to be an argument for discounting (invest now, hand over more later) or against it? And if the space analogy is bad, is there a better one -- future generations as a bequest to a child who wouldn't exist otherwise, or as your own seventy-year-old self, or something else -- or is the honest answer that no analogy should be doing this work?
