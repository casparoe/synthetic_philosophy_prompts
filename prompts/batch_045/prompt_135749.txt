I teach a third-year seminar on rational choice and its failures — about 22 students, mixed philosophy and behavioural-economics majors, one 90-minute session. I have run the dollar auction in this class twice (open-outcry escalation: a prize of fixed value, a minimum increment, the highest bidder pays their last bid and takes the prize, the runner-up pays their last bid and gets nothing) and it always lands in the same shallow place: the bidding sails past the value of the prize, everyone laughs, someone says sunk cost fallacy, and we move on. I want it to do more this time, and I would like you to design the session.

The thing I want them to run into is that the step-by-step reasoning in the middle of the auction is not obviously irrational. At any node, raising can be the best available act given that you have already bid, and the comparison that recommends it is forward-looking, not backward-looking. So the charge of fallacy is too quick, and the interesting question is where the error — if there is one — actually sits: in the individual bid, or in the policy of entering at all. I would also like to pry apart the structural trap from the psychological story about commitment, responsibility for the initiating decision, and loss of face, and I have a crude idea for doing that which you should build on or replace: seat them in two-person desks, and have the partner who did *not* make the earlier bids take over the bidding once the total passes the value of the prize. Then the person deciding whether to throw more in has no personal history with the decision that started it. If escalation continues anyway, something other than self-justification is doing the work.

Constraints and requests:

No money may change hands (department rule), so the payoff scheme has to be tokens endowed at the start and converted afterwards into something with a little real pull — lottery tickets for a voucher, exemption from a quiz, your suggestion. I need the stakes to be large enough that people actually escalate and small enough that the runner-up's loss is not resented.

I can spare about 60 minutes of play and 30 of debrief. Within that, I would like several short auctions rather than one long one, with two desks bidding at a time and the rest of the room observing and sealing a prediction of where the bidding will stop before each round starts — I want the gap between what observers predict and what participants do to be part of the material. Please include a variant with a fixed, commonly known number of rounds: I have been told that with such a cap backward induction unravels the game and nobody should enter, whereas in the open-ended version there is no clean place to start the induction at all. I would rather they discovered that contrast than heard it from me, so please tell me whether the contrast is right and how the cap has to be set relative to the prize's token value for the claim to hold — I do not want to assert something false at the front of the room.

Finally, before the last round, each desk seals a number: a self-binding rule that the facilitator enforces, dropping them out when the bidding reaches it. I want to see whether knowing the trap makes them pre-commit not to play, or only to stop somewhat later, and I want the debrief to make the difference between those two lessons visible.

Please give me: materials and setup, the exact rules and payoff scheme, a minute-by-minute timeline, the debrief mapping specific moments in the play onto specific parts of the analysis, an honest list of what will predictably go wrong the first time (including what to do if the relay partner simply folds, or if nobody escalates at all), and a frank note on which feature of the problem the game cannot reproduce and which therefore has to be supplied by me in the last ten minutes. Nothing should be explained up front: they should hear the phrases sunk cost and escalation of commitment for the first time in the debrief, and by then they should already have the material those phrases name.
