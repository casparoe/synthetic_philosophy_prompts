I run a twice-monthly reading group drawn from the local rationalist and EA scene: about fifteen people, mostly working programmers and data scientists, two econ PhD students, an appellate lawyer, and a handful who picked up the vocabulary of the sequences — shut up and multiply, cached thoughts, scope insensitivity — without any of the formal machinery behind it. Last month we worked through Arrow, so independence of irrelevant alternatives and the general shape of an impossibility result are already in the room, which I suspect is as much a hazard as a help. Next session I have ninety minutes and a whiteboard for Sen's impossibility of a Paretian liberal.

Don't explain the result to me. Plan how I should explain it.

First, the dependency structure. What has to be on the board before the result can even be stated, and in what order? My own instinct is that the load-bearing move is getting them to see that the cycle lives in the social ranking we asked for and not in anybody's head, and that this is a cost of wanting a social ordering at all rather than a discovery about how irrational collectives are. Tell me what depends on that distinction, what is safe to defer to the last ten minutes, and what would collapse if I let them blur social preference with social choice.

Second, the carrying example. I was going to use Sen's own — two readers, one copy of a scandalous novel, three outcomes: Prude reads it, Lewd reads it, nobody reads it. Prude ranks nobody-reads first but would rather read it himself than let Lewd read it; Lewd would rather Prude read it than read it himself, and would rather read it himself than see it unread. Prude is decisive over his own pair, Lewd over his, unanimity then pushes Prude-reads above Lewd-reads, and the three go around. I am worried the example does two bad things at once: it makes the move of excluding other-regarding preferences look obviously correct, and it invites sniggering. Should I run it as is, denose it into a variant with the same formal shape, or open with something else entirely? Say what the example has to have and what it has to lack for the cycle to feel like a result about rights rather than about busybodies.

Third, the misconceptions, in the order this audience will actually form them, and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off rather than answered later. The ones I expect: that the cycle shows collectives are irrational; its mirror image, that we can just stop asking for a social ordering; that this is Arrow with extra steps (is it, or does it not use independence at all?); that the obvious thing to drop is Pareto; that the obvious thing to drop is the rights condition; that rights are side constraints on aggregation rather than inputs to it, so the framing begs the question against the Nozickian in the room; and the big one for this crowd, that you dissolve the whole thing by switching to cardinal utility and adding up. Which of these should I let someone raise so I can knock it down, and which do I need to pre-empt before it fossilizes?

Fourth, the part I actually want your judgment on. I have a variant I have been playing with, and I want to know whether it belongs in the session at all. Replace the binary condition — person i is fully decisive over one protected pair — with a dial: on i's protected pair, i's preference intensity must count at least λ times as much as the summed intensity of everyone else who is moved on that pair, granting cardinal and interpersonally comparable intensities for the sake of the model. At λ going to infinity you recover full decisiveness; at small λ you get something close to plain aggregation. My rough sense is that equal-weight utilitarianism satisfies the condition exactly when λ is at most one over n minus one, so the strength of right you can buy from adding up decays like one over the population size, and that the cycle reappears above that threshold. Is that right, and does the threshold move with n? More to the point: is this a good closing ten minutes, because it turns the shut up and multiply reflex into a quantitative answer, or does it muddy the classic result for people seeing it for the first time? If it stays, give me the minimal setup I need on the board so it costs ten minutes and not thirty.

Finally, one question I can put to the room at minute eighty whose answer tells me they have the result and not just the punchline, and a short list of what I should explicitly refuse to get into — I am thinking the choice-functional escapes and anything strategy-theoretic — with a line for how to decline without sounding like I am hiding something.
