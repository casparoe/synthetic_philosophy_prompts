I'm a third-year undergraduate writing the final paper for a philosophy seminar on punishment and social sanction, and I want to read Ibsen's *An Enemy of the People* (1882) as a case study in informal social sanction — the kind of thing now called deplatforming, though the play predates the word by a century. I don't need a plot summary; I need a reading I can argue with.

The frame I've been developing in my notes is a set of conditions that a sanction might have to meet to count as *punishment* rather than as the ordinary working-out of other people's freedom of association. I want you to use them, but also to tell me where they are mis-specified.

1. **The discharge condition.** Standard punishment has a terminus: the sentence is served, the fine paid, the apology accepted by someone with standing to accept it. Informal social sanction characteristically has no such terminus — there is no one with standing to declare the debt discharged, so the sanction can run indefinitely. Call a sanction that fails this *undischargeable*.
2. **The attribution condition.** Punishment is normally imposed by an agent who can, in principle, be asked to stop. Much of what happens to Dr. Stockmann is produced by a sequence of individually permissible refusals — an editor declines the article, a printer declines it, a hall is withdrawn, a contract is terminated, a tenancy is ended, a homeowners' association resolves that no one should employ him — no one of which is punitive on its own, and no one of which any single participant can be asked to reverse. Call the aggregate a *distributed sanction*. Is the aggregate punishment if no part of it is?
3. **The transparency condition.** Communicative theories of punishment want the sanction to be an address to the target as a moral agent: you did this, it was wrong, you are being condemned for it. In the play, most of the sanctioners' operative reasons are not their stated reasons — the newspaper kills the article over circulation and the town's economy, the crowd because their livelihoods depend on the baths, Stockmann's father-in-law because his tannery is the main source of the pollution. The sanction is communicative in form but not in content. Call this *interest-opaque*.

Two further distinctions I want used. First, the **commons baseline**: Stockmann treats the town's liberal newspaper and Captain Horster's hall as public infrastructure, while his brother and the printer treat them as private property to lend or withhold. Second, **platform withdrawal versus silencing**: Stockmann is never literally prevented from speaking — he still has a voice and a window — but he loses his reach. So the play lets me ask whether the wrong of deplatforming is the loss of the right to speak or the loss of hearers, and whether that distinction survives contact with the crowd shouting him down at the meeting he is never allowed to finish.

What I want: a reading of the play through these distinctions, scene by scene where it helps, that says what the play dramatizes about social sanction as punishment, what it complicates, and where it resists the reading. In particular I want you to press on three worries I can't settle. (a) Ibsen gives us the best possible victim: Stockmann is verifiably right, disinterested, and willing to pay for it. The hard real-world cases are ones where the epistemic status of the sanctioned claim is genuinely contested, so the play may stack the deck against the sanctioners. (b) Stockmann's own contempt for the crowd, his social-evolutionary oratory about the compact majority, and his closing insistence that the strongest man is the one who stands alone may make him a poor vessel for the anti-deplatforming reading — the play may be about a certain kind of person's relation to the majority rather than about silencing. (c) Ibsen gives the mayor a real argument: quiet remediation rather than public ruin of the town. If that argument has force, the sanction is not straightforwardly undeserved, and my three conditions may be doing work they shouldn't.

Please end by saying what the play adds to the philosophical debate that argument alone doesn't — I suspect it is that the sanctioners here are not expressing condemnation at all, so the play shows something failing the transparency condition while still functioning as punishment, but I want that claim tested rather than confirmed.
