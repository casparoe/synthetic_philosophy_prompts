I teach a first-year medical school course, and I have one fifty-minute small-group session to get students to see the dispute over whether disease is a descriptive or an evaluative concept as a live disagreement with clinical stakes rather than as a matter of wording. They are midway through their pathophysiology block, have no philosophy background, and are impatient with anything that does not change management — which means the function-based account arrives already looking like common sense. I do not need the dispute explained to me; I need a plan for teaching it. Please give me:

1. The order in which the components must be introduced, and what collapses if I reorder them. Specifically: where the distinction between the pathological condition, the person's experience of being unwell, and the socially recognized status of being sick belongs, and whether it has to come before they hear the two views at all; where the point belongs that both sides grant that medicine is value-laden and disagree only about whether the value sits in the concept of disease or merely in what we do once we have identified it; and where in the hour I should bring in the conditions defined entirely by future risk — prediabetes, osteopenia, a borderline blood-pressure reading — whose cut-points move when a cheap and well-tolerated drug comes to market. I want them to see that even if the function-based account is granted in full, the line between having the disease and being at risk of it shifts with the cost and side effects of the available intervention. Tell me whether that observation is evidence for the evaluative side, and if it is not, what it actually shows.

2. The one case that should carry the whole session, together with what it must have and what it must lack. It should leave the biology uncontested by both sides, it should let the question of whether to treat come apart from the question of whether there is a disease, and it should be something they will actually meet on the wards rather than a talking point. Nothing that engages their identity commitments — that eats the hour. If you would use the sickle-cell trait, say why, and say why not the shifting-threshold case instead.

3. The misconceptions, and the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. I predict four: that the function-based view just means statistical rarity, so red hair and harmless anatomical variants come out as diseases; that the evaluative view amounts to whatever we say goes; that the whole dispute is verbal, since both views can license the same clinical practice; and that once we have a definition of disease, it settles whether to screen, treat, or tell the patient. Tell me where in the hour to defuse each, which one I should instead let surface and put to work, and which ones I have missed.

4. The check and the cut. One question I can put to them in the last five minutes — not to define the two views — whose answer would show me that the structure has landed rather than been memorized; and what I should openly decline to settle at this level, saying out loud that I am declining it.
