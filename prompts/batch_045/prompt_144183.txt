Here is where I have got to. As I understand the behaviorist side of the subject, "utility" was supposed to be rescued from being a private, unverifiable feeling by being defined in terms of what people actually choose: a person's utility function is just whatever ordering rationalizes her choices across the budget sets she actually faced, and the standard axioms tell you whether such an ordering exists and let you recover numbers from finite choice data. The payoff is supposed to be that you can say how well off someone is, and evaluate a policy, without making claims about her inner life.

My worry is structural, and I have started calling it the vanishing-overlap problem. Consider Ada and two goods, x and y, over three years, with income always 10:

- Year 1: prices (1, 1). Budget constraint x + y ≤ 10. She chooses a = (6, 4).
- Year 2: prices (3, 1). Budget constraint 3x + y ≤ 10. She chooses b = (2, 4).
- Year 3: prices (1, 3). Budget constraint x + 3y ≤ 10. She chooses c = (1, 3).

I have worked out the revealed-preference relations myself, and I think the only ones the data support are: a is revealed preferred to b (b cost 6 at year-1 prices, so b was available), a is revealed preferred to c (c cost 4 at year-1 prices), and b is revealed preferred to c (c cost 6 at year-2 prices). Nothing else crosses: a costs 22 at year-2 prices and 18 at year-3 prices, and b costs 14 at year-3 prices, so a and b are never available when the later choices are made. Correct me if I have this wrong.

What bothers me is this. Both of the following seem to fit that data perfectly. First: Ada has had one stable ordering all along, with a at least as good as b at least as good as c. Second: Ada's ordering changed — she re-ranked the two goods in year 2 and again in year 3, and each year's choice is rationalized by that year's ordering, whatever it was. The second hypothesis looks to me like it can fit any data at all, which would mean that passing the axioms is not evidence for stability unless you have already assumed stability. And the deeper point: the only cross-year tests available are the ones above, they exist only because the budget sets happen to overlap in a small region, and the bigger the change in prices the less the old and new budget sets overlap. So revealed preference seems to have the fewest testable implications exactly in the case of a large structural change, which is exactly the case where a policy judgment about whether someone is better off is hardest and most needed. The one thing that would genuinely test stability here — year-1 prices coming back, with Ada then choosing c — is precisely what a permanent change forecloses.
