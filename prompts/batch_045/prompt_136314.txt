I have to teach Sen's result on the impossibility of a Paretian liberal in a single fifty-minute section to second-year philosophy, politics and economics students. They saw Arrow's theorem stated informally three weeks ago — the conditions, no proof — and they can read a preference table over a small set of alternatives; they have not met social decision functions, and none of them has heard of Sen's result. I know the result myself, so don't explain it to me. Plan how I should teach it.

Four things I need.

One: the order. Which idea has to be on the board first for the cycle to feel derived rather than produced by sleight of hand? In particular, must I state the Pareto principle and minimal liberalism as conditions on a social decision function before touching Sen's example of the prude and the lascivious reader, or should the example come first with the conditions read off it? Say why, in terms of what depends on what.

Two: the carrying example. I don't want to lead with the prude and the lascivious reader: the mechanics are perfect, but my students will spend the hour on the salacious framing and on whether anyone really has preferences like that. Give me a different example to build the hour around, state what it must have and lack for both the Pareto move and the rights move to fall out of it, and say what Sen's own example buys that mine won't.

Three: misconceptions. Here is my list — tell me which are genuine, which I have misdescribed, and at which step each must be headed off rather than corrected afterwards. (a) Reading the Pareto condition as the claim that the outcome must be Pareto-efficient, instead of as a condition on social preference. (b) Taking liberalism here to be the political doctrine, and Sen to be arguing against liberal rights. (c) Hearing decisiveness over a single pair as dictatorship in miniature. (d) Thinking the cycle is an artefact of the eccentric content of the example. (e) Concluding that the moral is to drop the rights condition and maximise welfare.

Four: the last ten minutes. I want them to vote on a variant I wrote myself, which as far as I know is not in the literature. Two flatmates each control the window in their own room; the alternatives are the four states of the two windows; each flatmate is decisive over their own window given the state of the other's; Ada prefers her window open exactly when Bo's is open, Bo prefers his open exactly when Ada's is shut, and the stated reasons are about draught and air flow, so that on the face of it nobody wants to interfere with anybody. I think the cycle survives, but I cannot tell whether the example is doing real work or smuggling meddlesomeness back in under a physical description, or whether it needs a stronger rights condition than Sen's. What should I expect them to say, where will the example mislead them, and what do I need them to see by the end? Also: what should I refuse to explain at this level, and say openly that I am refusing?
