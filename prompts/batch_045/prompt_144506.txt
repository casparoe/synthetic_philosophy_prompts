I've been reading the same Fermi-paradox threads since the mid-2000s, and the move that always does the load-bearing work is the multiplication: a heuristic argument lists some steps between dead matter and a galaxy-spanning civilization, gives each a small probability, and treats the product as the expected number of visible civilizations. I want a poem that makes that move visible, and that smuggles in the worry that the factors were never independent — the steps plausibly share a driver, each one being the same trick of getting matter to do something harder with less — so that the honest default for the conjunction is what the marginals alone give you, somewhere between one minus the sum of the failure probabilities and the largest single success probability, and nowhere near the product. The upshot to get across is that the many-small-filters-spread-along-the-chain picture is in worse shape than the one-hard-step picture, and that the product needs a license nobody has written down.

Form: a pantoum, four quatrains in rhymed ABAB, in the voice of the multiplication itself rather than a human observer. Lines 2 and 4 of each stanza should recur as lines 1 and 3 of the next, and each recurring line should be one step of the chain reappearing under a new description, so that by the third pass the reader can see it is one step and not two. The closing return to the opening line should land as the argument's conclusion turning out to have been its premise. Someone who has followed the filter literature should find the dependence point actually rendered rather than named; someone who hasn't should still be able to read it as a poem about a machine that keeps eating its own output.

Then a note of about 150 words, committed rather than hedged: say which dependence claim the poem is strongest on, where it leans on a contestable reading (for instance whether a shared driver really favours a single early filter rather than a late one), and what the poem would have to change if the other reading were right.
