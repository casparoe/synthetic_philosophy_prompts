I help run a fortnightly philosophy-and-probability reading group at my university -- about 16 people, mostly maths and philosophy undergrads plus two physicists who are far too confident, 90 minutes, a room with a whiteboard and no projector, and I can get hold of opaque bags, numbered ping-pong balls, index cards and poker chips, but no actual money changes hands (last term someone suggested real stakes and the department got twitchy). We did the anthropic material already and it went badly: I explained the argument first, one person had seen it online, and the rest of the hour was everyone comparing whether they had heard of it before. So this time: no lecture up front, and I don't want the words doomsday, anthropic, self-sampling or reference class to come out of my mouth until the debrief.

The thing I want them to feel is the move where you treat your own position in the sequence of births -- your birth rank -- as if it were a random draw from some class of observers, and then conclude that the total size of that class probably isn't vastly bigger than your rank. I want them to feel the pull of that before they can name it, and then I want them to discover that its whole force came from a choice they made themselves in the first ninety seconds, before any evidence arrived. Here's the shape I have in mind and you should feel free to tear it apart: in minute one, before I've said anything about bags or balls, everyone secretly writes on an index card which group of beings they'd count themselves as an ordinary random sample of (options on the board: everyone who has ever lived; everyone alive right now; everyone who will ever live; all mammals; only people who have thought about arguments like this one; or something of their own), seals it and keeps it. Then the draw: two opaque bags, SHORT with balls numbered 1 to 20 and LONG with balls numbered 1 to 2000, one bag picked by a coin flip I don't reveal, a single ball drawn for the whole room with its number shown to everyone, and each person then stakes chips on which bag it came from. Then the bit I'm least sure about: a mid-game offer that anyone may reopen and change their sealed card, but it costs a chip and they have to write one line saying why; everyone stakes again. Only after that do we open the cards and find that people who saw exactly the same number came to different views because they'd sealed different classes, and ask whether the class is something you discover or something you choose, and whether shopping for one is cheating.

I want to end on the so-what: everyone has to allocate the last fifteen minutes between a task that would do something real about a civilisational catastrophe and something they'd just enjoy, and we see whether the people whose stakes moved also moved their fifteen minutes -- and if they didn't, whether that's inconsistency or actually the right answer. Please design this so I can run it on Thursday: materials and setup, the exact wording of the one card or instruction that does the real work, the round-by-round rules and a chip payoff or scoring scheme that makes people state honest numbers rather than bet for fun (no money), and a debrief that maps each moment in the room onto the part of the argument it's meant to land. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time: I'm especially worried about the two physicists, about an honest draw producing a big number and short-circuiting everything, and about someone pointing out that with a fair coin flip no betting rule can beat fifty-fifty in expectation -- if that objection is right, say so, and say what the game should produce instead of winnings. Also be honest about which feature of the argument no game can reproduce and I'll just have to say out loud. Answer as exactly five numbered items, one or two dense sentences each, no intro and no sign-off -- I'd rather have five overloaded items than a tidy handout.
