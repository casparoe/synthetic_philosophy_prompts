I work in mechanistic interpretability, and a dispute in my research group has been completely static for about a year, so I want a diagnosis of why rather than a verdict on who is right.

The surface question is whether trained neural networks genuinely have representations of features, or whether representational talk is a vocabulary we project onto a system that is only doing computation. Both sides cite the same evidence. For the realists: directions in activation space can be found that track independently specified features; intervening on those directions changes behavior in the targeted way and not in others; the same features and roughly the same directions reappear across inputs, across models, and across fine-tuning runs. For the anti-realists: every such claim is answerable only to behavioral and interventional tests, so it never says more than what the system does; polysemanticity and superposition mean no single unit or direction cleanly means one thing; and when the feature dictionary is enlarged, features shard into finer-grained ones, which suggests the grain of the recovered feature list is fixed by our method and our compute budget rather than by the network.

My half-formed diagnosis is that this is three questions being run as one: (i) internal states carry information about features; (ii) the network's own downstream computation consumes that information, so that behavior is counterfactually sensitive to it; (iii) there is a single correct decomposition — one privileged dictionary at one privileged level of grain — that we are recovering rather than choosing. I suspect the realist needs (iii) for interpretability to be a science of features rather than a collection of useful probes, that (iii) is the claim actually threatened by feature splitting and by the ease of training a probe to read out almost anything, and that most anti-realist arguments are aimed at (i) while their real force is against (iii). A related worry I can't place: content is normally supposed to allow for misrepresentation, and I don't know which of (i)–(iii) is meant to secure that.

Is this the right way to carve the disagreement? More importantly, tell me what kind of stalemate it is: a verbal dispute about the word 'representation,' a substantive disagreement about which standards of evidence matter, two projects answering different questions under one heading, or a hidden empirical question about the universality and stability of features that nobody has isolated yet. Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a sharpened distinction, a formal criterion for one decomposition's being privileged, an experiment — or is there a good argument that the question should be abandoned as ill-posed?
