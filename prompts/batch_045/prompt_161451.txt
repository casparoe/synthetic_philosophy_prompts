I'm putting together a small anthology of philosophy-adjacent comic writing — pieces that are funny because the philosophy is right rather than funny because a famous name appears in them — and I'd like a contribution on the narrative view of the self, specifically on Dennett's suggestion that a self is not an extra thing in the head but an abstraction: the center of narrative gravity of the stories that get spun around a body, in the way a center of gravity is a useful theoretical posit rather than a further object to be found. A friend of mine insists that because the view is deflationary it is also politically inert, and I want a piece that puts pressure on that without ever arguing with him directly.

What I have in mind is a parody of an official document: Draft Statutory Guidance issued by a fictional Office of Narrative Continuity in a jurisdiction that has taken the view literally and now has to administer it — numbered clauses, a short definitions section, and one worked case file at the end, about 750 to 850 words in total. The comedy has to come from the content of the view and from the administrative problems it genuinely cannot solve in its own terms. So the Office should have to grapple with: how much narrative is enough to constitute a self at all (infants, the extremely private, the comatose, the silently suffering); the fact that narratives are spun rather than told by anyone in particular, so that most of the narrative mass around a person is contributed by other people — employers, relatives, biographers, the state — which raises whether institutional narrative mass counts, and whether unauthorized co-authorship is an offence; what the Office does when the authorized biography is false, self-serving, confabulated, or imposed on someone; how to measure gravitational drift over a life, and what counts as the same self across a rewrite; and whether a self can be created or dissolved retroactively by the revision of a record. I'd like the worked case file to be an elderly citizen with dementia whose three surviving relatives tell three incompatible stories about her, and whose file cannot be closed.

The ending should be a reductio that arrives without announcement: by applying the view's own logic faithfully — the self as posit, narratives as the only material, no fact of the matter beyond the stories — the Office ends up doing something the reader recoils from, namely determining and thereby constituting who people are, all while insisting in its own deadpan voice that it has discovered nothing and created nothing and that the self was always only a convenient fiction. Keep the tone bureaucratic and sustained: no winking at the reader, no escape hatch in which the real, robust self is quietly reaffirmed, no named targets living or dead, and nothing that a sympathetic defender of the view could dismiss as a straw man — the Office should never assert anything the view does not actually entail, only act on it. The target is the view and the genre of administrative prose, and the piece should be legible to a reader who has never heard the phrase center of narrative gravity while still being fair enough to make a specialist wince in recognition.
