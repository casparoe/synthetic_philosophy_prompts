I'm a junior at a large public high school, and I sit on our student giving committee — six of us, four students and two teachers — which decides how to spend whatever the student body raises each year. This year the fall walkathon brought in about $9,000, we have four requests in front of us, and we have to announce the allocation at an assembly in ten days. I've been asked to draft the memo the rest of the committee will work from, and I'd like you to write it for me. Please take your time with this and think it through properly: before you draft anything, spend some time working out for yourself what each of the three standard critiques of effective altruism actually commits someone to — the institutional critique, the objection to earning to give, and the measurability bias, the one about how money drifts toward whatever shows up on a spreadsheet — and only then start writing. Write as someone who has both worked on the methodology of effective altruism and sat on grant committees that really had to say yes or no. One thing I'd ask you to avoid: please don't cite studies, charities, or authors by name. We can't check any of that, and this committee will discount anything that leans on a citation rather than on an argument.

The four requests are these. First, an international health charity that distributes insecticide-treated bed nets and publishes an estimated cost per life saved from randomized trials; its student chapter here is asking for the entire $9,000, and on paper it is far and away the strongest option. Second, a student-run lunchtime tutoring program that also functions as a place to be for kids who are failing classes or have nowhere to go at lunch; a senior started it two years ago, right after the district cut two of our four counselor positions, and it is asking for $4,000 for food, transit cards for the peer tutors, and a small stipend for the teacher who stays to supervise. It has no control group and no data beyond a sign-in sheet. Third, a mutual aid fund run by parents in the district that gives small no-questions-asked cash grants to families in crisis, asking for $2,000. Fourth, the finance and economics club wants $1,500 for a speaker series and interview coaching aimed at steering seniors toward high-paying internships, on the argument that if even three of them end up donating a tenth of their income later, that beats everything else on the table — earning to give, as a line item in a high school budget.

I want the memo to recommend a concrete split, with the justification for each recipient worked into the prose rather than listed, and I want it to say how we defend that split at an assembly where someone will inevitably stand up and ask why we sent money out of the country while our own counselor's office is half-staffed. I'm genuinely torn between the bed nets and the lunchtime room, so don't just tell me what I want to hear.

Two ideas of my own have been going around in my head, and I want you to test them rather than flatter them. The first is what I've been calling the narratability discount: when one option's case is made entirely of numbers and another's is made of what the people affected say about it, treat the gap between the measured estimate and the told story as an artifact of measurement and discount back toward the story by some fixed fraction. My worry is that this just launders a preference for whatever is visible, since how moving a story is has nothing to do with how much good gets done; tell me whether it is a real correction or a bias with extra steps. The second is the offload test: the lunchtime room exists because the district made a cut, and funding it arguably helps the district not have to reverse that cut. But if I apply that test consistently it seems to condemn the bed nets too, since some government could point at our donation and trim its own malaria budget — so the test either proves too much or needs a scope condition I can't articulate, and I want your view on which it is and on what the condition should be if there is one.

I'd also like the memo to be honest about where these critiques run out: whether a student body is even the right kind of agent to be doing cost-effectiveness reasoning, what we owe the students who put money in the walkathon under only a general promise, and any part of the case the framework simply doesn't decide. Please finish with a short paragraph, addressed to the committee, saying how confident you are in the recommendation overall and naming the single claim you are least sure of.

Write it as continuous prose, the way I'd write an email to our faculty sponsor — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables — and keep it to roughly seven or eight hundred words.
