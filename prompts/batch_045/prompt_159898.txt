A small working group I sit on is producing a taxonomy appendix for a report on minds — natural, artificial and collective. We have adopted without much discussion the four grades Dennett lays out for kinds of minds: Darwinian creatures, which are hard-wired by selection; Skinnerian creatures, which can be shaped by their own overt trial and error; Popperian creatures, which can discard candidate actions inside their own heads rather than by performing them; and Gregorian creatures, which have been fitted out with thinking tools — words above all — supplied by the culture around them. The chair of the group keeps the running table of rulings, and every row in it is hers. I now have to write up a new case before she sees it, and I would rather anticipate her verdict than guess badly in front of her.

The rows so far, abbreviated:

- Digger wasp running through its fixed nesting routine: Darwinian.
- Slime mould shortening its own body to solve a maze: Darwinian.
- Ant colony selecting the shorter of two routes to food, where no individual ant represents the route: Darwinian, with the note that the colony is not a separate creature with a mind of its own.
- Rat learning a maze over repeated runs: Skinnerian.
- Pigeon acquiring a novel response by shaping: Skinnerian.
- Border collie with a large trained vocabulary of spoken words, used to respond to commands: Skinnerian, with the note that having words does not by itself promote a creature.
- Chimpanzee that appears to try out several routes internally and then sets off along one it has never tested: Popperian.
- Crow that bends a wire into a hook after apparently working the problem out in advance: Popperian.
- Vervet monkey with its three innate alarm calls: Darwinian, with the note that hard-wired signals are not words.
- Human child of four: Gregorian.
- Child raised with no exposure to language: Popperian, with the note that membership of a Gregorian species does not automatically make one a Gregorian creature.
- Literate adult using numerals, maps and notation: Gregorian, and she declined to add a fifth grade for people with especially good tools.

The new case is an invented one, deliberately constructed. Call it Species K, a long-lived social bird. Its members are born with a fixed innate inventory of about forty calls, each tied to some feature of the world — a shape of predator, water, ripe fruit, kin, a direction. These are combinatorial: birds assemble them into sequences, and the sequences are not innate. Each bird learns which sequences work by operant conditioning on the behaviour of other birds. Crucially, the sequences are also run silently: a bird on the point of acting can be shown, by what it does next and how long it waits, to run through several candidate sequences and reject them before moving. But nothing accumulates. Sequences die with their inventors, no bird acquires one by copying, each generation re-derives what it needs, and a sequence that would have been useful two hundred years ago is not available now. There is communication but no transmission. Finally, a flock of K, using the system, locates resources and routes that no single bird could find, and the flock improves over the course of a single season by pooling the failures of its members — though it retains nothing across seasons.

What I want from you:

1. What grade do you expect her to record for Species K, and with what note?
2. Set out the two or three grading rules that fit her existing rows equally well — I can see a rule that grades by the kind of selection the creature performs, one that grades by whether the items it thinks with have a cultural history of their own, and one that treats inner rehearsal of externally shaped sequences as covert Skinnerian behaviour rather than genuine Popperian pre-selection, but I suspect there are rules I have not thought of. Say what each predicts for K, and where they diverge.
3. Give your best single guess, with the reasons for preferring it over the alternatives, and say which of the existing rows is doing the most work in constraining the choice.
4. Two further questions. First, should the flock get a row of its own, and if she denies it one, does that commit her to denying one to a research laboratory or a firm, which does accumulate tools across generations of members? Second, name the single further case — of your construction, if you like — that would best force the competing rules apart; I would rather put that in the appendix as a worked example than leave the table ambiguous.
5. If we want the appendix to be consistent with itself, what principle are we committing to whichever way we rule, and how should we word the entry so that a reader can see what was being assumed?

One last thing, because the same argument is coming to us next month: whatever rule you recommend, say plainly what it implies about a system trained on the accumulated text of a Gregorian species and prompted to work through candidates before answering. I would like to know now whether our rule quietly settles that case.
