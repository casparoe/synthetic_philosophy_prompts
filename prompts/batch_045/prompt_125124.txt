I'm finishing an MA seminar on archives and power — philosophy of history meets archival theory — and I've been asked to close the last session with a short poem we can read aloud in about two minutes. I want it to be about archival silence, but not in the consoling key: no giving voice to the voiceless, no whispering archives, no moment where a recovered fragment redeems anybody. Two distinctions from the seminar matter to me. The first is between the record that is simply missing, where nothing was ever written down, and the record that is present but hostile: the person is in the archive, and is in it permanently, only under the recorder's categories — as defendant, as wife of, as cultivator, as absent, as unknown, as one of a number. The second is something our group half-invented and has gotten attached to, the compounding: once one kind of document survives in bulk, the scholarship follows the bulk, so the disproportion in the archive reproduces itself in the historiography, and whatever was thin gets thinner with each generation of research that builds on the same sources. I want the poem to be right about both — accurate enough that someone who actually works on this would recognize the distinctions rather than just see them named — and I want it to leave the discomfort open rather than resolve into either a sermon about complicity or a shrug about how the past is just like that.

Formally, I'd like a sestina: six six-line stanzas and a three-line envoi, with six end-words drawn entirely from the register's own administrative vocabulary — ordinary words like absent, cultivator, unclaimed, riotous, wife of, unknown. List the six words with a one-line gloss each before the poem starts, noting what kind of person each word does its work on. Each stanza should be a different entry from the same monthly return in the same district, so that what the reader watches is the same six words coming back attached to a different life, until it's clear that the vocabulary, not the people, is what has continuity across the series. Nobody in the poem should get to speak in the first person as the person recorded; they may appear only as the record names them, and the poem should never step outside the record's idiom to tell us what they really thought. The envoi should be the turn: the moment the poem notices that it is itself a source, that whoever finds it in a hundred years will read it for us the way we read the register for them, and that our seminar will be in it only as the idiom of whoever wrote it.

We're reading it cold, with no introduction, so please don't add notes, sourcing, or a list of what to read next — just the glossed end-words and the poem.
