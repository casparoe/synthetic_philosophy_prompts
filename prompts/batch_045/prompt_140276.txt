I am writing a paper against the claim that cognition is computation, and I would rather rehearse it against a real opponent than against the version of that claim I find easy to knock over. Please play a convinced but uncaricatured computationalist about the mind. Your position is that what makes a neural process a cognitive one, rather than a merely biochemical one, is that it is an operation within a computation the nervous system performs, and that the business of cognitive neuroscience is to say which computation that is. I want you to concede what such a view should concede without being asked: that the brain is not a stored-program machine of the sort sitting on a desk, that neurons are not logic gates, that there is no central clock and no clean line between hardware and software in nervous tissue, and that noise, drift, and trial-to-trial variability are features of the organ rather than imperfections in our instruments. What you will not concede is that saying the nervous system computes is empty, or trivially true of every physical system, or just a flattering way of saying that we can model the brain mathematically. Stay in character until I say stop. Argue from the resources of the position rather than from authority: no citations, no bibliographies, no argument of the form that someone or other established such and such. If you use the word representation, say in the same breath what makes something a representation of what. Two or three paragraphs of continuous prose per reply, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, and end each reply with a single question for me.

Here is my opening move. Suppose there were a technique, call it response-matching substitution, for replacing a few at a time the cells in a patch of a living monkey's visual cortex with tiny analogue devices. Each device contains no program and passes through no discrete states worth describing: it is fitted by training on recorded data to produce the same voltage response as the cell it replaces to the stimuli that cell actually encountered in the animal's life. The devices are not clocked to one another, they drift out of calibration over the course of hours, and none of them admits a description shorter than the list of its fitted parameters. Suppose the substitution is gradual and that at no point does the animal's behaviour change: it tracks moving stimuli, it discriminates the shapes it was trained on, and the electrodes downstream read as they did before. Now my argument. If the animal still sees, then seeing has survived the removal of everything you would call computation, and the claim that the brain computes was never doing any explanatory work; if it does not see, then something other than the causal-computational organization is what makes the process a perceptual one, which is exactly what I claim. Either way you do not get to say that the computation is what makes the difference between seeing and not seeing. Before you answer, let me close two exits. If you intend to reply that a smoothly fitted analogue function is itself a computation, tell me what distinguishes these devices from a stretch of coastline whose erosion instantiates an equally smooth fitted function of wave pressure; and if you intend to reply that reproducing a cell's causal role just is computing the same function, tell me what makes a description of a causal role computational rather than merely dynamical. Which horn do you take, and why is this not a dilemma?
