I should say first why this is not an idle request. For two years I have been caring for my mother, who has mid-stage Alzheimer's and now lives largely in the 1970s. A few months ago I caught myself — embarrassingly, since I was trained as a physicist before I left the field — seriously entertaining the thought that the scenes she describes are in some literal sense still going on, and that this is not obviously false. I have since agreed to convene a monthly discussion series at our public library on the argument that the relativity of simultaneity forces all times to be equally real: the one that starts from two people strolling past each other in opposite directions and disagreeing, with neither making any error, about which event on a distant world is happening now, and then chains such disagreements together until every event is somebody's present.

I want to stage this argument rather than present it, and I would like your help designing the session.

**Setting.** About sixteen adults, wildly mixed: a retired actuary, two software engineers, a hospice nurse, a high-school student, several people who last did science at sixteen. Ninety minutes. A library meeting room, 18 m by 9 m, carpeted, with chairs around the edge. No budget. I can print things, and I have masking tape, string, index cards, marker pens, three stopwatches, a tape measure, and a metronome app. Assume no participant will do algebra under time pressure; assume one or two will try to win.

**What they should come away seeing.** (i) That two people in the same place, both careful, can disagree about which distant event is happening now without either being confused about signal delays or about what they can see. (ii) That this disagreement is generated by their motion, not by their ignorance. (iii) That by composing several such disagreements you can get from an event dated in the 1900s to one dated in the 2100s, where every link in the chain is some participant's genuine now. (iv) That the step from (i)–(iii) to the conclusion that all of those times are equally real is a further step, and that they can say what it would cost to refuse it. Nobody should hear the words relativity, frame, simultaneity, spacetime, presentism, eternalism, or block universe until the debrief; the operational vocabulary should be things like which is happening now, send a signal, and meet.

**The device I have in mind, and where it breaks.** Make it a world with a slow invariant speed: signals crawl across the room at a metre per second or so, and we stipulate that one second of our time is a year of theirs, so that a walking pace shifts a distant now by decades. My own attempt failed twice over — at an invariant speed of 1 m/s a brisk walker is effectively superluminal, and at any speed where walkers stay subluminal the shift came out too small to feel. So please give me explicit numbers that jointly satisfy: signal crossing time of at least eight seconds (so people can watch a signal travel); no participant ever moving faster than the invariant speed; a slow, sustainable walking pace producing a shift of at least twenty years across the hall; and the whole protocol runnable twice in ninety minutes. If those constraints are jointly unsatisfiable, tell me which one to relax and what the smallest relaxation is.

**Three further requirements.** First, no participant should have to stop, turn around, or come back to produce the effect — otherwise someone will say the acceleration did the work, and I would rather the design simply not hand them that move; if the fix is to pass something from walker to walker, say exactly how. Second, the chain should have at least three links, each produced by a different person's motion, and should leave a physical artifact on the floor — cards and string — that the group can stand around and read. Third, two participants should be seeded in advance with objections to raise at a fixed point late in the session (around minute 55): one pressing that everything so far concerns what people see rather than what is happening, the other pressing that we simply chose a convention for setting distant clocks and that a different convention gives everyone one shared answer. Both objections should be answerable out of what the group has already done, and I would like to know which moments of the activity supply the materials.

**What I want back.** The rules, setup, and materials; a minute-by-minute timeline; the single card that lets people compute a now-shift without algebra; the debrief, with each moment of the session mapped onto the part of the argument it is supposed to make vivid; a bail-out map giving the three points at which a participant can refuse the conclusion and what each refusal costs; at least four things that will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, with a fix for each (including what to do with the person who works it out in the first ten minutes); and a frank account of what the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to say myself — I assume at minimum that it cannot bridge the gap between there is no absolute simultaneity and every time is equally real, and it cannot show why the effect being unnoticeably small in everyday life is beside the point.
