I'm a junior and I lead one discussion section a week — fifty minutes, about fifteen first-years — for our intro course in comparative ethics. The students have just come out of two weeks on Aristotle (aretē, habituation, the function argument, phronesis, and a lot of practice applying the doctrine of the mean), and next week the syllabus gives me exactly one session on early Confucian ethics. I want to spend it on the reading of that material as role ethics rather than as virtue ethics: the point of the unit is the contrast with what they just learned, and I'd rather they not file Confucius away as Aristotle with Chinese names. So please don't explain Confucian role ethics to me — plan how I should teach it in that slot, treating the Aristotle they've just had as simultaneously my best hook and my biggest obstacle.

Concretely, four things. First, the order the ideas have to go in, with the dependencies made explicit rather than asserted. My instinct is that nothing lands until they see the constitutive claim — that on this kind of view a person is made up of her roles rather than being an antecedently existing individual who then takes roles on — and that this has to be in place before either filial piety or humaneness gets introduced, because otherwise the one reads as a duty attached to an already-given agent and the other as generic benevolence. Tell me whether that really is where to begin, and what else has to be on the board first. Second, the single case or example that should carry the whole session, and what it has to have and lack. My candidate is the exchange in the Analects where Confucius is asked about a son who turns in his father for stealing a sheep and answers, more or less, that father and son should cover for each other and that being upright consists in that concealment: it forces the point that the very same act changes its valence with the relationship, and it makes the class's first objection audible instead of my having to invent it. But I'm afraid it will eat the hour in a debate about corruption and nepotism, or collapse into so it's all just relative, and I keep considering alternatives — something mundane and modern, like a teaching assistant who is also someone's friend, or the move from family to state that the texts themselves make — though I suspect the latter will just make them hear patriarchal hierarchy. Argue me into one of these or into something better, and say what the carrying case must contain (improvisation within a role? a real collision between two roles?) and what it must not contain.

Third, the misconceptions they will form, in roughly the order they'll form them, and the exact point in the sequence where I should head each one off. I'm expecting role ethics says do whatever your society expects as the big one; also that it's virtue ethics with the virtues renamed; that if the person is relational then there is no self left and nobody is responsible for anything; that filial piety means obedience — I want gently remonstrating with a parent to appear somewhere, and I'm not sure where in the arc it belongs; and that the view has nothing to say about how to treat strangers, so it isn't a moral theory at all. Add the ones I haven't listed, including any that my Aristotle framing specifically invites. Fourth, one question for the last five minutes that would show me they've got the constitutive claim and are applying it rather than parroting it — I was going to hand them a role we never discussed and ask what being good at it consists in, but tell me if that's gameable, and what the tell would be. And then tell me what I should openly decline to cover at this level: I'm inclined to skip the scholarly fight over whether the early texts are better read as a species of virtue ethics or as something irreducibly role-based, and to skip the disagreement about whether human nature is already disposed toward the virtues or has to be shaped by training and ritual, but say plainly if cutting either of those costs me the session.
