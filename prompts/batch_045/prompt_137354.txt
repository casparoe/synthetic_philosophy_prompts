THE QUESTION

Question 4. Consider the following argument against the possibility of genuine, irresolvable moral dilemmas:

(1) If S ought to φ, then S can φ.
(2) In a putative genuine dilemma, S ought to do A and S ought to do B.
(3) In such a case, S cannot do both A and B.
(4) Therefore the supposition that genuine dilemmas occur is inconsistent.

(a) Is the argument valid as it stands? If not, state the additional premise it requires, and explain which premise a defender of genuine dilemmas is best advised to reject, and why.
(b) Suppose the defender is right and genuine dilemmas do occur. In such a case, does it follow that the agent who does A has no reason for any residual regret, or that the requirement to do B was merely apparent? Explain the distinction between a requirement that is overridden and one that is left unfulfilled, and say what each account of the case implies about the rationality of regret.
(c) A critic says the whole dispute is merely verbal: 'ought' just means 'that which there is decisive reason to do', and there cannot be decisive reason for each of two incompossible actions, so there is nothing here for the defender of genuine dilemmas to be right or wrong about. Assess this response.

THE STUDENT'S ANSWER (paragraphs numbered for your comments)

1. Start with (a). As it stands the argument is not valid. Premise (1) applied separately to each of the two claims in (2) yields only that S can do A and that S can do B, and that is perfectly consistent with (3), which denies only that S can do both. To generate a contradiction the argument needs a further premise linking separate 'oughts' to a conjunctive one, namely the principle of agglomeration: if S ought to do A and S ought to do B, then S ought to do A and B. With that premise in place, (1) yields that S can do A and B, which contradicts (3). So the argument is valid only once agglomeration is added, and the real work is being done by a premise that was never stated.

2. Which premise should the defender of genuine dilemmas reject? Not (2), since the conjunction of the two 'oughts' is what a genuine dilemma is supposed to consist in, and not (3), which merely describes the kind of case in question. That leaves (1), ought-implies-can. So the defender's position requires rejecting ought-implies-can.

3. This is less damaging than it sounds. The defender need not be committed to the absurd claim that an agent can be required to do the impossible. In a genuine dilemma each act is individually performable: the agent can do A and can do B, considered on their own. So the defender can reject (1) as a completely general principle while retaining it for the case in which a single requirement is in play, and the cost of the rejection is confined to cases of conflict, which is where it is needed.

4. On to (b). Neither of the two consequences mentioned follows from the defender's being right.

5. The distinction to draw is this. An overridden requirement is one that is defeated by stronger competing reasons, so that in the circumstances the agent is not required to perform it at all: the balance of reasons points the other way, and doing it would at best be optional. An unfulfilled requirement is one that still holds after the agent has acted and is simply not discharged. In a genuine dilemma the requirement to do B is of the second kind. It is not overridden, because nothing in the case makes it cease to apply; it is left standing and unmet.

6. That is what makes residual regret rational. The agent regrets not that she did A — A was required of her — but that a requirement of hers remains unfulfilled, and a requirement that survives the action is a perfectly good object of regret. This is also why the residue is regret rather than guilt: guilt would imply that she did what she was not permitted to do, whereas her complaint is that she could not do everything she was required to do. Her regret is therefore rational even though her action was, in the relevant sense, what she ought to have done.

7. It is worth being explicit about what kind of requirement these are. To say that both A and B are what S ought to do in a genuine dilemma is to say that they are prima facie requirements in Ross's sense: considerations that count in favour of an act but that can be outweighed by competing considerations. Read that way, the appearance of contradiction in (1)–(4) was always an artefact of misreading the two 'oughts' as all-things-considered verdicts. Once they are read as prima facie, the case turns out to be one of the weighing kind after all, and the defender's thesis reduces to the claim that the balance of reasons is sometimes genuinely indeterminate.

8. On (c): the critic's charge fails as stated, because it simply legislates a meaning for 'ought'. The defender of genuine dilemmas can stipulate that she is using 'ought' to express a requirement that persists unfulfilled, and once that stipulation is in place the critic's premise has no purchase. The debate is therefore not verbal: it is a substantive dispute about whether an agent can be under two such requirements at once, and the defender settles it in her own favour by fixing the sense of the term.

9. One last clarifying point. Nothing in any of this turns on the agent's being uncertain what to do. A genuine dilemma is not a case of not knowing which of two things one ought to do, and conflating the two is what makes the literature look like it is solving a merely epistemic puzzle.
