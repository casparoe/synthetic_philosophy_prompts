Two years ago I was appointed sole trustee of a small trust set up for my younger brother, who has a degenerative illness. The terms are unusually specific: in nine years the trust must fund a residential care placement whose cost is contracted in real terms, currently about $1.4M, against assets of roughly $620,000 today. Anything left over passes to my niece, whom I love but who is comfortably provided for; a shortfall, on the other hand, would mean moving my brother somewhere neither of us wants. So my practical objective is not really terminal wealth at all — it is close to maximizing the probability of funding that placement by that date, and I care very little about how far above or below the target I land except on the one side of it.

Deciding the asset allocation has pushed me into a literature that, as far as I can tell, has not moved in decades. On one side are the growth-optimal or Kelly people, whose case I take to be roughly this: maximize the expected log of wealth; in the long run the log-optimal investor almost surely ends up wealthier than anyone following a materially different policy, and by a factor that grows without bound; overbetting is punished faster than underbetting, since growth declines quadratically in the fractional deviation and turns negative once you exceed twice the log-optimal fraction; and the standard cautions — half Kelly buys three-quarters of the maximum growth rate for half the volatility of log wealth — are matters of arithmetic that nobody disputes. On the other side are expected-utility people, whose case is that the dominance result is a limit theorem about an infinite sequence of bets, that nobody faces an infinite sequence, that for any finite horizon and any objective except log itself the optimal policy is different, and that almost-sure dominance in the limit is perfectly compatible with being worse than the alternative with probability close to one at every horizon a human being actually lives through. Samuelson-style objections of that shape are as old as the criterion itself. There is also a newer line, associated with ergodicity arguments, saying that the ensemble average is not what a single investor experiences and that the time-average growth rate is the object with a claim on the word rational.

Everyone on every side knows all of this, and nobody appears to have shifted. Before I ask you for anything else, I want you to check a calculation I did, because it is what made me suspect the debate is mis-framed. Assume, heroically, that real wealth follows geometric Brownian motion with expected excess return μ and volatility σ, that cash earns zero real return, that a constant fraction f of wealth is held in equities and continuously rebalanced, and that I ignore bankruptcy and borrowing constraints. Writing L for the log of the target multiple and T for the horizon, I get that the probability of being at or above the target at T is Φ( (μ/σ)√T − fσ√T/2 − L/(fσ√T) ), which for positive f is maximized at f = √(2L) / (σ√T), with a maximum value of Φ( (μ/σ)√T − √(2L) ). Comparing that with the log-optimal fraction μ/σ², the goal-chasing fraction exceeds Kelly exactly when L exceeds μ²T/(2σ²), i.e. exactly when my target lies beyond the greatest expected log growth any policy could deliver over the horizon; and the ratio of the two fractions is the square root of L over that quantity. With μ = 5%, σ = 17%, T = 9 and L ≈ 0.81, this gives a probability-maximizing fraction of about 2.5 against a Kelly fraction of about 1.7, and a maximum chance of roughly 35% of making the placement. I would like to know whether that is right, and whether the constant-fraction restriction is doing much damage — I suspect the genuinely optimal policy is a glide path that de-risks as the target comes within reach, which would make my figure a benchmark rather than an answer.

My hypothesis about the impasse is that at least three different disputes are sharing one label, and that the loudest one is not about decision theory at all. Maximizing the probability of hitting a fixed target by a fixed date is itself expected-utility maximization, with a Bernoulli utility that is a step at the target: discontinuous and non-concave, but a utility nonetheless. So the expected-utility people have no formal quarrel with my objective, and the Kelly people, if they are honestly reading log as one utility among many, have no quarrel with expected utility either. What is left looks like a dispute about which object a decision rule is supposed to be evaluated over: a single choice under uncertainty, or a policy evaluated under repetition; and about whether a limit theorem about the latter can supply a reason for someone in the former situation. Underneath that, I suspect a hidden empirical question is doing more work than anyone admits: how large the estimation error in μ is relative to μ itself, whether returns are predictable enough that log-optimality stops being myopic and demands a dynamic program, and how heavy the tails are — because if fractional Kelly is best understood as a hedge against parameter error rather than an expression of risk aversion, then a large part of the practical argument is really about econometrics dressed up as decision theory.

What I want is a diagnosis, with reasons and not a survey: which parts of this disagreement are substantive, which are verbal or a matter of two programmes answering different questions under one name, and which, if any, reduce to an empirical question that could in principle be settled. I don't need citations or a reading list; I've read the standard statements on both sides. Tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a theorem relating asymptotic criteria to finite-horizon goal attainment with explicit error bounds, a distinction between a criterion and a decision procedure, a cleaner separation of the estimation problem from the risk-preference problem, or nothing at all — and if your honest conclusion is that no progress should be expected, say why. And if the upshot for my own case is that the honest advice is not a cleverer fraction but the admission that the target is probably unreachable, I would rather hear that than a number I can act on.
