I'm in Year 13 and I chair our school's Food and Wellbeing Committee, which sounds like a trivial thing to be doing but has turned into an argument about paternalism that I can't settle on my own. We have been given permission to rearrange the canteen, to set defaults on the booking system for lunchtime study sessions, and to change the order in which options appear on the school's online forms, so we are now in the business of arranging other people's choices, and before we write any of this into a policy document for the governors I want to know whether the principles I have been voting on actually hold together.

Here is where I think I have landed. First, an intervention is justified only when it helps someone do what they already want to do: I am happy to move people towards their own aims when they reliably fail to reach them through inattention, impatience or simple hassle, but not to substitute our view of what a good life, or a good afternoon, looks like for theirs. Second, I do not accept "you are steering people's choices" as an objection, because there is no arrangement of a canteen, a form or a menu that does not steer — something has to be nearest the door, something has to be the pre-ticked box — so influence as such cannot be the wrong-making feature, and any real objection has to be about something else. Third, I hold a transparency condition: we should be willing to stand up in assembly and say exactly what we are doing and what psychological effect we are relying on, and the intervention has to be one we can still defend once it has been described out loud; what makes steering objectionable, in my view, is having to hide it. Fourth, getting out must stay cheap and obvious, so the option we disprefer remains reachable at negligible cost; putting obstacles on the exit turns a nudge into something other than a nudge. Fifth, and this is the one my deputy thinks is quietly doing all the work: where we could instead remove the failing itself, by teaching pupils how defaults and placement work or by giving them a better way to organise their own revision, we should prefer that to steering around the failing, and treat a nudge as a stopgap only where teaching is too slow or impossible. Sixth, whenever a nudge gets us close enough to the aim, we should prefer it to a ban, a charge or a requirement, precisely because it leaves people able to choose otherwise.

The case that has made me doubt the package: last week we voted to enrol every pupil in Year 12 automatically into a lunchtime supervised study slot, with exit only by means of a signed form handed in at the school office during a short window on Tuesday mornings. When we ran the same thing as opt-in with a QR code, almost nobody signed up, and the pupils our Head of Year thinks would benefit most were the least likely to; the awkward exit is deliberate, because an easy one would undo the whole scheme. I voted for it and would vote for it again, and I do not think it breaks my first principle, since nearly everyone in Year 12 says they want better grades and is annoyed with themselves for how little work they do — but my deputy says it breaks something else, and that my fourth and fifth commitments cannot both survive it. So please audit the set. Which of my six commitments genuinely pull against each other, which only look as though they do, and in each case what would have to give and what giving it would cost me in terms of the policies we would have to withdraw or redesign; rank the problems by severity rather than listing them as equal, and do not spare the principle that is doing the least work, since if one of them is only a prejudice I have picked up from reading about nudging I would rather know. I would also like to know whether the justification I have given for the study-slot policy is really the one I am relying on. Please write in British English, in continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, rather as you would write to someone who has to take the answer into a meeting.
