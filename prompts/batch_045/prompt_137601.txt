Hi — practical question, and I'd rather have your instincts than a literature dump.

I'm on a panel that has to spend a small pot of money: roughly six funded people for eighteen months, mostly political philosophy with some formal decision theory, no econometrics worth speaking of. The funder has asked that a good chunk of the portfolio go to futarchy — governance by conditional prediction markets, the vote-on-values-bet-on-beliefs setup — because they want something a policy shop could pick up inside two years. I've been asked to draft the call for proposals and I keep going back and forth, so: what should the call actually ask for?

What I want is a defended ordering, not a survey. Assume five or six candidate lines of work, tell me which two or three this group should actually be on, in what order, and on what grounds — importance to whether anyone should ever build the thing, tractability with our toolkit rather than an econ or ML group's, how neglected it is, and how much of the rest of the agenda sits downstream of it. Then tell me what would move things: what result, or what event out in the world, over the next two years would push a line up or down the list.

Here's what I'm toying with, in no order.

1. The metric problem, and a workaround I half-invented: futarchy needs a measurable proxy for what we want, and everything interesting about the proposal seems to hide in that gap. The workaround: don't fix a metric in advance — have the conditional market settle on what a citizen assembly, seated after the outcome is in, retrospectively rates as having gone well. Bet on the ratification, not on the index. My worry is that this just relocates the value question into whoever gets seated, and that it wrecks liquidity, and that it creates a lovely new incentive to influence the assembly's composition rather than the policy. Is this actually a way forward or is it a distraction dressed up as a fix?

2. The epistemic question, updated: the old worry is thin markets and manipulation. The newer version I can't shake is correlation. If most of the liquidity ends up coming from a handful of desks running near-identical forecasting models — and ahead of us, models that share training data and architecture — the market may be aggregating one model's blind spot rather than many people's information. Someone floated to me a market-maker that subsidizes trades conditional on traders' model provenance being decorrelated. Is that coherent, is it even the right variable, and is this a job for philosophers or should we just not touch it?

3. Legitimacy, and the seperate worry that futarchy quietly inherits the old aggregation complaints: even granting that it picks better policies, why should someone on the losing side treat the price as a reason? Is the values stage where all the real disagreement is hidden, so that the betting stage is doing less work than advertised? This is the one my department will care about and the one the funder will find least useful, and I can't tell which of those is the better guide.

4. Scale. States are never going to adopt this, but grantmaking bodies, corporate boards, and university budgeting might, and there the metric problem is milder and you can iterate. Is a small-scale trial evidence about futarchy at all, or is the state-level version a different institution wearing the same name? I'd of thought this one is cheap and concrete, but maybe it's just a way to feel busy.

5. Manipulation where it matters: anyone who can affect the outcome can trade against their own future action, and the domains we'd most want this for — monopoly policy, war, pandemics — are exactly the ones with concentrated actors who can move the variable. Does that make the proposal unstable in precisely the cases that motivate it?

Tell me which of these, or which things I'm not listing, deserve eighteen months of six people. Be explicit about the trade-offs between your criteria: if you're picking something for tractability over importance, say so and say what we give up. If you think the honest answer is that the whole framing is a mistake and the money should go somewhere else entirely, say that and give me the alternative first line instead. Prose, argue for it, don't be diplomatic, no bullet-point summaries of the literature.
