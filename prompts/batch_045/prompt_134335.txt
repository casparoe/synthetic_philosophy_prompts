Not a research question exactly — I'm drafting a novel and one of my characters is turning into a bore about it, so I need to get the argument right before I let her talk for three pages. Setup: my protagonist, Ines, is 34, a paediatric anesthesiologist, and she's choosing between staying in Boston, where she gives away half her salary to a malaria charity, and moving to a rural clinic where she'd do more good per hour face to face but earn a fraction of the money. Her brother thinks the whole thing is narcissistic; ignore him, he's a subplot. What I want Ines to defend at a dinner party is this: yes, the long-run ripple effects of either choice — who she meets, who they meet, whether some descendant of someone she saves goes on to do something terrible in 2300 — are probably bigger in expectation than anything she can see over the next fifty years, and yes, nobody can predict them. But they're symmetric. For every way the ripples could come out catastrophically there's an equally likely way they come out gloriously, so they cancel to roughly zero, and she's entitled to just total up the foreseeable near-term effects and maximise those. Her dinner companion, a philosopher I'm using as a foil, is supposed to call this craven. I don't see why: if the signs are random, ignoring the magnitude looks like exactly the right response, not a cop-out.

My writing group had two objections I couldn't answer. One friend said this is just Pascal's mugging with extra steps, and that no sane decision rule lets enormous speculative stakes swamp the ones you can actually count. Another said that if the far-future effects really are that large and that unpredictable, then consequentialism itself is finished — you can't tell which act is better, so there's no fact of the matter about what Ines ought to do — and that Ines's "just count the near term" move is a consequentialist answer to a problem that has already eaten consequentialism. She also muttered something about how, once you admit you're clueless, you have to pick some decision rule for acting under ignorance, and that picking the rule raises the same problem one level up, but I'd lost the thread by then and there was wine.

So: is Ines's argument sound? Where does it go wrong, if it does — is the symmetry point enough to license ignoring the far future, or is "they cancel to zero" itself the thing that needs defending? Is my friend right that this collapses into the Pascal's mugging problem, or are those actually different problems wearing the same coat? And concretely, what would the foil say at the table that would really sting — I want a line that a smart reader will feel rather than have explained to them.
