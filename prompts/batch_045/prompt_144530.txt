I am a novelist, and the book I have been failing to finish turns on a scene I have now rewritten nine times. A clinical-trial coordinator — conscientious, badly overworked, two days behind on her inbox — tells an enrolled patient, in perfect sincerity and on the strength of the safety summary she read the previous week, that the trial drug "hasn't shown any serious side effects." The latest safety memo, which she has not opened, complicates that sentence without quite falsifying it. Nobody lies to anybody; there is an unread email. Returning to it for the tenth time, I find that none of the standard accounts of assertion tells me what I want to know about her. The view I have drifted into, which I would like stress-tested rather than defended, is this: assertion is permissible just when the speaker is in a position to know, where the warrant may be partly borrowed from testimony, instruments and institutional chains the speaker has not personally audited, so long as the borrowing is not reckless — and where the amount of warrant required is calibrated not to the speaker's practical stakes but to what is at stake for the hearer. Call it the borrowed-warrant view with a hearer-stakes rider. It earns its keep, I think, on cases like my coordinator's (her own stakes are trivial; the patient's are not; the criticism she merits seems to be a criticism about calibration rather than about ignorance) and on cases where the divergence is reversed: asserting a well-confirmed scientific result to a stranger in a bar, or a stubborn true claim to someone who will be made worse off by believing it, seems fine on ordinary warrant even though the hearer's stakes are, respectively, nil and perverse.

What I want from you is a map of this view's exposure, not a defense of it. What would have to be true, or to happen, for it to be false? I would like the defeaters sorted by how much damage they actually do — fatal, serious, merely awkward — and I want you to separate what a committed defender would concede as counting against the view from what she would, rightly or wrongly, wave off. Please include: cases where hearer-stakes calibration and my intuitions come apart (extremes of hearer stakes that seem not to raise or lower the bar at all; hearers whose stakes are self-imposed or hostile); the suspicion, raised by a friend in my writing group, that the view is only the familiar knowledge norm wearing a different hat, and that if it collapses into a rival the interesting question was never which norm it is; the actionability worry that a speaker usually cannot know what is at stake for her hearer, so that a hearer-relative norm may not be one anyone could follow, together with whatever the view should say about ought-implies-can here; the no-hearer cases — diary entries, soliloquy, a narrator's assertions in a novel, which matter to me professionally, since if assertion in fiction is governed by no norm at all I want to know whether that is a principled exemption or an ad hoc one; and any consequence the view is stuck with that its rivals avoid. Also tell me, honestly, whether it predicts anything its rivals do not, or whether it has been quietly qualified into a form that nothing could dislodge — and if it has, whether for a view of this kind that is a defect or simply the normal condition of a norms-of-assertion theory.

For my own part, I am moved much more by concrete cases where my view and the knowledge norm diverge and my intuition sides against me than by arguments from the point or constitutive aim of the practice of assertion, which always seem to me to be redescribable to fit whatever norm is on offer; and I am more worried by the collapse objection than by any single counterexample. Structure your answer however you like, but give me your bottom line in the very first sentence — the one consideration you find most damaging, or a flat statement that the view is better insulated than I think — and only then the reasoning, with no preamble, no restatement of what I have asked, and no throat-clearing about how contested these matters are.
