Our book club picked The Goldfinch and I somehow ended up leading the discussion, and I don't want to just do "was Theo a bad person" for two hours. While I was reading I fell down a rabbit hole about why an original is supposed to be worth more than an indistinguishable fake, and I've been chewing on it on my own with no philosophy background at all, so please go easy on the jargon. Here's roughly where I've got to. The argument everybody gives is causal: the original was touched by the artist, the fake wasn't, and that history is part of what you're buying. But I made up this test that I can't get out of my head: imagine that tonight someone swaps the painting in the museum for a perfect copy and nobody ever finds out. Nothing visible changes, no expert is ever embarrassed, no future test distinguishes them. Has anything of value been lost? My gut says yes and also I can't say what, and I suspect the "yes" is doing all the work in the normal argument. I also keep separating two things people mush together: provenance (who owned it) versus authorship (who actually made the marks). A real old painting that gets passed off as by a more famous hand, versus a copy made openly, honestly, two hundred years ago by someone who just loved the picture and signed their own name to it. The second one feels way less offensive to me and I want to know if that intuition is defensible or if I'm just being sentimental. And then there's the thing a friend told me, which I may be remembering wrong, about the guy who forged Vermeers so well that once he was exposed his fakes became valuable again as what they really were — authentic objects with a different, weirder history. If that's right it suggests an object can have value that comes from having successfully deceived people, which seems to me like a genuinely different kind of value than either "made by the master" or "looks good," and I don't see it discussed much. Oh, and one more: a restorer who replaces so much of a damaged piece that almost none of the original material is left. Is that a preserved original or a slow-motion forgery, and does it matter that the restorer never lied to anybody?

So here's what I want. Read the novel through that whole mess. I'm interested in Hobie's workshop, where reproductions and real antiques sit side by side and the skill is the same skill either way; in Theo's own forgeries near the end, made by someone who loves the objects and is doing it under duress; in the fact that the painting itself is small and chained up in a gallery in The Hague in real life and is, in the book, this enormous private thing for Theo that's really about his mother and about having survived; and in the criminals who use it as collateral, since they clearly don't care at all whether it's genuine as long as other people think it is. Does the book have a position on any of this, or is it just using art-forgery as atmosphere? Point me at the two or three scenes you'd actually make the group read out loud.
