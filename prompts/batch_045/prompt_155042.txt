I've been thinking about this on my own, without any philosophy training, and I've ended up somewhere I don't trust: I think the usual ranking of lying and misleading has something backwards, and I want to know what would knock me off it.

Here is my view. The variable that matters isn't falsity, and it isn't really intent either; it's what I call accountability residue, meaning what a deception leaves behind in the world for other people to work with. A lie deposits a proposition the speaker authored: it is checkable, attributable, and available to a third party, so it can be tested and the speaker can later be held to it. A successful piece of misleading leaves only the hearer's own inference, which the speaker never asserted, which is hard to show they invited, and which they can disown without contradicting anything they actually said. On my account that residue is what the moral difference really tracks, and it cuts both ways: misleading is sometimes worse than lying, because there is nothing on the record to correct, nothing to correct it with, and no way to establish that the speaker is an unreliable informant; and it is sometimes less bad, because the hearer had to supply the false step themselves and a more careful hearer need not have.

Tell me what would count against this, ranked from fatal to merely awkward rather than listed neutrally. I'm most interested in cases that break the correlation I'm leaning on, that is lies that leave almost no residue and misleading that leaves a great deal; in the charge that I have quietly made the whole thing epistemic, about detectability or expected harm, so that the distinction between lying and misleading ends up doing no work in my own theory; and in the suggestion that residue is only a proxy for how confident the hearer was entitled to be. For each one, say whether a person holding my view should concede that it counts against them, or whether they are entitled to shrug it off as a case my view already covers. I'd also like to know what my view predicts that the rival view — the one that treats assertion itself as special and so lying as the worse of the two — does not predict; if it predicts nothing distinctive, say so plainly. And tell me honestly whether I have built this so that any counterexample can be absorbed by saying it depends on the case, and whether that is a genuine defect in a claim of this kind or just the shape a claim at this level of generality has to have.

What would move me most is a single case where someone is misled in a thoroughly disownable way and I would still want to insist that the lie would have been worse, or the mirror image of that.

Please argue to a conclusion and give your reasons rather than laying out the options and leaving me to choose; I would rather be argued with than informed. And write it as continuous prose, in the way you'd write an email to someone who asked you a hard question: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
