I am helping to organise a small closed workshop on comparative perpetration, and I want the centrepiece of it to be a written adversarial collaboration between two historians who have spent years talking past each other:

- **The situationalist** (in the line of Christopher Browning's *Ordinary Men*, 1992): the men who shot Jews in the occupied East were mostly not selected for ideological fanaticism; they were middle-aged, largely working-class conscripts, and the best predictors of who killed are situational — legitimate authority, unit cohesion and peer pressure, careerism and fear of being shamed, routinisation and the division of labour, escalating commitment after a first massacre, and the brutalising context of the war.
- **The culturalist** (in the line of Daniel Goldhagen's *Hitler's Willing Executioners*, 1996): these men were bearers of a specifically German, broadly shared cognitive model of *eliminationist antisemitism*, which made the killing not merely compliant but willing, and which is the monocausal explanation adequate to the behaviour, including its gratuitously cruel and humiliating excesses that no order required.

Assume both have argued in good faith for a decade and both want a document they can sign. Please write the joint statement they would produce. Do not adjudicate between them, and do not dramatise an exchange: the deliverable is a single constructive text, in a formal register suitable for a mixed audience of historians and political scientists, about 1,200–1,800 words.

Three things I want built into the document, because I think they are where the real work is:

**(1) A decomposition of what is being explained.** Ask them to distinguish four separable outcome variables, and to say for each whether their theory is primarily a theory of it: (i) *uptake* — whether a man killed at all when an exit was offered; (ii) *latency* — how much priming, example-setting, or alcohol a man needed before his first killing; (iii) *excess* — gratuitous cruelty, torture, humiliation, or initiative beyond what the order required; (iv) *avowal* — the reasons men later gave in interrogation, which may be post-hoc rationalisation rather than cause. I suspect the two camps have partly been explaining different rows of this table.

**(2) A distinction of causal roles.** A factor can be a *trigger* (what made this man kill on this occasion), a *selector* (what determined who was assigned to killing units in the first place), or a *sustainer* (what kept participation stable over months and prevented exit). Have both sides state, for each of their preferred factors, which role it is claimed to play. My own hypothesis is that much of the acrimony comes from one side offering a trigger and the other offering a sustainer while both use the word *cause*, but leave room for the document to say this is not what is going on.

**(3) A hypothetical but realistic new evidence base.** Suppose a forthcoming project — call it the **Order Police Personnel Corpus** — links, for roughly eleven thousand men of the Order Police, (a) their home-district records, including pre-1933 proxies for antisemitic mobilisation; (b) unit rosters and rotation logs, so that each man's exposure to killing operations can be dated; and (c) about three thousand post-war interrogation transcripts coded on a common scheme covering uptake, latency, and excess. Suppose also a smaller satellite corpus covering Volksdeutsche and non-German auxiliaries tried in the same courts. Everything in the document should be answerable from these materials plus ordinary reasoning: no citations, no bibliography, no archival sleuthing.

Structure the statement as follows.

1. **Agreed claims.** A numbered section of twelve to fifteen propositions both can sign: about the size and shape of the phenomenon, about the refusal rate and the fact that refusal was possible and went unpunished, about the role of orders, about what a merely sufficient situation would look like, about what a merely sufficient cultural transmission story would look like, and about what kinds of evidence are relevant at all. Each claim should be substantive rather than a platitude.

2. **Disagreements.** A table in which each residual disagreement is given twice: once in wording the situationalist would accept for his own view and once in wording the culturalist would accept for hers. Columns: the crux, the situationalist's formulation, the culturalist's formulation, and whether the disagreement is genuine or about words.

3. **Cruxes and tests.** For each genuine crux, name one test, case, or pattern in the hypothesised corpora that both agree would bear on it, and record in advance what each side predicts. Useful candidates, which you may use, replace, or supplement: the *invariance test* (hold the situation roughly fixed — same orders, same locality type — and vary measured home-district antisemitic mobilisation; does uptake or excess move?); the *transport test* (hold cultural background fixed and vary situational pressure — an exit offer, a commanding officer present, a first killing delayed by a fortnight); the *substitution test* (would a man who refused shooting accept a non-shooting role in the same operation, and what does that show about what he was objecting to?); and the *auxiliary comparison* (do non-German auxiliaries, differently socialised, show the same uptake but different excess?). For each, state what result would actually move which side, and — separately — what each side counts as evidence against its own view.

4. **The dispute we no longer have.** A short closing section listing the parts of the quarrel the collaborators now treat as verbal, saying precisely which term was doing the work (I would look hard at *willing*, at *ordinary*, at *culture* as an explanation versus *culture* as a redescription of aggregate behaviour, and at *monocausal*), and stating what is left once those are dissolved.

Where a formulation would collapse into something neither side could dissent from, flag it as vacuous rather than signing it.
