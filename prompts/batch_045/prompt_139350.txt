Hi — I'm recently retired (ion channels, thirty-odd years, mostly patch clamp on cardiac tissue) and I've drifted into reading philosophy of science on the side, which is more or less how I ended up as the token non-specialist on the committee that oversees an in vitro safety-signalling register. Labs send in anomalies — a compound does something nobody predicted in a standardised assay — and the register either pools the new report into an existing mechanism entry or splits it off as a new one. The curator makes the call. Call her Ines. I've watched her do it for two years and I still can't tell what she's running. Her charter's criterion is something like: two anomalies involve the same mechanism when the same organised set of entities and activities produces them. Everybody nods at that and it settles nothing.

Her verdicts so far (I'm writing these from memory, so the details are rough but there right in substance):

1. Two compounds, same hepatocytes, calcium signal oscillating with the same period and the same amplitude. One does it through ER-store depletion driving store-operated influx; the other through a plasma-membrane channel that inactivates on its own with no store involvement at all. Split.
2. A kinase inhibitor that raises the level of the very phospho-protein it should lower, because it binds one member of a dimeric kinase and allosterically switches on its partner. Then the same paradox in yeast, with the yeast orthologues playing the same roles. Pooled.
3. A reporter that bursts after a stimulus: signal to kinase to scaffold to polymerase release. Second cell line, same burst, same kinetics, same size, but there the kinase phosphorylates the release factor directly and the scaffold isn't in the picture at all — knocking it out does nothing. Split.
4. A phenotype traced to a compound's binding at its intended target, where the compound also binds something else and the second binding has been shown to change nothing about the phenotype. Pooled.
5. An anomaly that depends on a protein's catalytic activity. New report: same anomaly, catalysis-dead mutant, still there, and it goes away when you knock out the protein's scaffolding interaction instead. Split.
6. Same chain as an existing entry, but run in a different growth medium, where the anomaly is only partly knocked down by an inhibitor of the top kinase. Turned out the inhibitor just gets in less well in that medium. Pooled — she called the changed sensitivity a pharmacological artefact rather than a mechanistic difference.

Now the one actually in front of us. A report where the anomaly looks like it's produced by exactly the registered chain: same trigger, same intermediates in the same order, same terminal event, same cell type, same readouts. The catch is this. In the original reports, knocking out the middle step abolishes the anomaly outright. In this new one, knocking out the middle step leaves about 70% of it, because a second route that is silent in the original reports is switched on here. Unperturbed the two cases are indistinguishable; under perturbation they come apart.

So: does Ines pool this or split it? More useful to me than the verdict itself: what rule is she implicitly running, what other rule fits all six of the above just as well and disagrees here, which of the two you'd bet on and why, and what single further case would pry them apart. Also tell me whether it should matter that the backup route carries 70% of the signal rather than, say, 5% — I can't decide if there's a threshold hiding in this or whether that's a fake precision. And if you think the whole question is ill-posed and the register should just track whatever serves early warning and stop pretending there's a fact of the matter about sameness, say that too, because honestly half of me agrees.
