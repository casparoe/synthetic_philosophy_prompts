i'm the editor on a patient-education team at a large hospital system. We put out maybe forty one-page explainers a year — chronic kidney disease, afib, immunotherapy, sepsis, dementia, vaccines — at about an eighth-grade reading level, and every one has to be signed off by a clinical lead before it goes into the patient portal. Our readers are mostly over sixty, a lot of them with limited English, and house style is that every hard concept arrives by analogy, because with this audience it is the only thing that works. The problem is that the writers pick analogies by feel, I have no way to argue with them, and the arguments we do have dead-end at "but patients like it."

What I want from you is a procedure, not an essay. Something a junior editor with zero cognitive-science background can run in about ten minutes on a single analogy and come out with a verdict — cut it / keep it with a guard sentence / keep it — plus a short note for the clinical lead justifying the verdict.

The theory I want it grounded in is the surfaces-and-essences view from Hofstadter and Sander's Surfaces and Essences: Analogy as the Fuel and Fire of Thinking. The bits I care about: analogy isn't the garnish on thinking, it is the thinking; categorization and analogy are the same mechanism on a continuum rather than one being a special case of the other; a reader hits the surface of your sentence and retrieves an essence from a completely different domain; and that essence keeps doing work after the leaflet is in the recycling bin, because the same surface cues a somewhat different essence in a different reader and the mapping quietly slips as they learn more. So an analogy in our leaflets isn't illustrating the concept the patient walks away with — it is, in large part, the concept they walk away with.

Here is what I think the procedure has to make us do, though tell me if I've got the order wrong or if I'm asking for a step that can't be done:

- Write the mapping out explicitly — which slot of the source domain maps onto which thing in the target, and which slots are left open. I'm fairly convinced the open slots are the real danger, because the patient will fill them at 2am without telling us, and they will not fill them the way the writer would.
- An extrapolation test: what does the analogy predict that is false? Not only on first read, but at three horizons — right now, at the next appointment when the doctor uses vocabulary that touches the same domain, and a year into the disease.
- Ask what work the analogy is actually doing: is it carrying the causal structure, or only carrying a feeling? Sometimes the feeling is the point, but then someone should have to say so out loud, and I think that's a values call for the clinical lead rather than something my procedure should settle.
- Ask who does not have the source domain. We translate everything into English, Spanish, and Hmong, and an analogy that depends on a specific object, sport, or idiom can evaporate on contact with that.
- Check whether the emotional tone of the analogy contradicts the clinical content, e.g. a fight metaphor in a leaflet that also has to prepare someone for a palliative decision.

Hard cases I need it to handle: the analogy that's true at the first appointment and false by the third; the analogy that quietly implies a treatment works by a mechanism we don't actually claim; the one that rests on an idiom or object that doesn't survive translation; and the case where the writer can't articulate the mapping at all, which happens more often than I'd like.

And be honest about what the procedure can't do. I'm pretty sure it can't tell me whether our readers actually have the source domain — that seems to need real reader testing — and I suspect it can't choose between two analogies that both pass. Say so.

Then run it on three:

1. Our single most-used analogy, which the clinical lead loves and will fight to keep: cancer as a war, the body as a battlefield, chemo as an attack, the patient as someone fighting. I think this one fails and I want to know exactly where it fails and whether any guard sentence could rescue part of it.
2. The immune system as an army that defends you, with immunotherapy as either calling in reinforcements or taking the brakes off.
3. A writer just handed me this one for the new CKD leaflet: the kidneys are a coffee filter, and when the filter clogs, waste backs up in your blood.

At least one of the three has to come out with a verdict I won't like, and I want that note written for real and addressed to the clinical lead, not hedged into mush. Finally: if a step in your procedure is really a judgment call dressed up as procedure, mark which step and don't pretend otherwise.
