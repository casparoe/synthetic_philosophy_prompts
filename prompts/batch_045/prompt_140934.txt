I'm closing the final session of a small advanced seminar on self-knowledge and the norms of assertion, and we've spent the last two weeks on Moore's paradox — the fact that although the sentence *it is raining but I do not believe that it is* is perfectly consistent and could even be true of someone (the self-deceived, or someone whose conviction has not caught up with the evidence in front of her), nobody can flatly assert it in the first person, present tense, while the third-person and past-tense versions are entirely comfortable. The room will be tired and slightly hostile to cleverness, so I want to end with a poem of about ninety seconds read aloud rather than a summary — and I have a device in mind that I'd like you to attempt before you tell me it can't work.

Two columns, or two alternating voices, sixteen to twenty-four lines each, no rhyme scheme or at most slant rhyme: the constraint should be the mirroring, not the music. On the left (or first) a plain third-person report about a woman at a window — call her M. — of whom the sentence is true: she sees the rain, she has been told about the rain, she will not or cannot believe it. On the right (or second) the same sequence in the first person, present tense, as nearly a line-by-line translation of the left as the line lengths allow. The left column can run on indefinitely, since a report of someone else's Moorean state is just a description of error; the right column should break down — retracting, sliding into the past tense, converting the assertion into a question or a hunch, or falling silent — and it should break down in the wording itself rather than by being told that it has broken down. Where and how it breaks is the poem's real subject. Four things I care about, in no particular order: (i) nothing should imply that the sentence is a contradiction or describes an impossible state of mind — if anything, let the left column insist that the state is ordinary (self-deception, the lag of conviction behind bad news, the way a belief trails the evidence); (ii) there are two canonical forms, the commissive one that asserts p while denying belief in it, and the omissive one that avows belief while denying p — my sense is that the omissive one has a non-absurd reading, along the lines of a hunch reported and then withdrawn, and I'm genuinely unsure it belongs in the poem at all, so decide and tell me why in the note; (iii) somewhere in the middle there should be a stretch where the sentence is sincerely sayable, the case of someone shown the rain whose belief has not arrived, and it should be distinguishable from the flat commissive case — my guess is that *I do not believe it* is doing something different there from what it does in the flat case, but I can't say what; and (iv) I think the sentence improves when embedded under a supposition, so if you agree, let the last surviving line of the first-person column be something like that rather than a bare assertion — and if I have that datum backwards, say so and don't do it. Please don't resolve any of this into a moral: no closing gesture about how we are opaque to ourselves, no little sermon on self-knowledge. End with the paradox still standing. (One of my students pointed out that a poem can write the line precisely because a poem doesn't assert it; if you want that thought, build it into the structure rather than winking at it in the final couplet.)

After the poem, give me one paragraph of prose: which reading of the paradox the poem actually commits you to — the one that puts the defect in the speech act, so that Moorean absurdity is a failure of assertibility governed by something like a belief or knowledge norm, or the one that puts it in the nature of belief itself, on the transparency thought that the question whether I believe that p collapses into the question whether p — where the poem takes liberties with either reading or leans on a contested version of it, and which single line you would cut if I asked you to shorten it. And if the two-column device is really just a diagram with line breaks, or if one of the distinctions I've asked for rests on a muddle, tell me that plainly and tell me what I should have asked for instead; I'd much rather have the objection than a polite attempt at a poem I can't use.
