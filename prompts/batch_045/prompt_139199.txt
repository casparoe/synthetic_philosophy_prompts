I'm in this philosophy-of-technology reading group that meets Thursday nights at a coffee shop, and last week we blew the whole session on that classic essay about whether artifacts have politics — you know the one, with the low overpasses built over a parkway so buses can't get through, and the mechanical tomato harvester that ended up reorganizing an entire industry around it. My roommate, who's a mechanical engineer, says the whole debate is silly: artifacts just have load paths and tolerances, and any politics is really in the people who commission and pay for them. I think he's wrong but I can't say exactly why, so I went home and wrote out my gut verdicts on a pile of cases, and now I want to know what principle, if any, I'm implicitly using. Here's my list:

- the low overpasses: yes, obviously, that thing has a politics
- the tomato harvester: yes, but it feels like a different *kind* of yes than the overpasses — it changed who could stay in the business at all, rather than keeping a particular group off a particular road
- bus shelter benches built with a rail down the middle so nobody can lie down: yes, and I'd lump this with the overpasses
- the front stoop of my old apartment building — eight steps, no railing, no ramp, put in back in 1926 by a builder who was definately not thinking about wheelchair users: yes. I want to say this one's politics is *inherited* rather than designed in, but I'm not sure that's a coherent distinction
- a video call app with no dial-in phone number, which means the two people in my book club with bad rural internet simply can't join: no, that's not the app's politics, that's broadband
- a photocopier that some dissident group uses to circulate banned texts: no, the politics is entirely in the users — hand the same machine to a group that isn't dissenting and nothing political happens
- a voting machine where English is the default and getting to the Spanish ballot takes three extra taps plus a confirmation screen: yes, absolutely
- a subway turnstile with a face scanner that fails more often for some faces than others: yes, and I want to keep saying yes even in the version where the vendor never intended it and would fix it next week if they could
- my roommate's hypothetical: the same overpasses, fifty years later, in a city that has banned private cars on that parkway and runs buses only. Nothing physical has changed, but now the overpasses are an obstacle for everybody. Does their politics change? I genuinely don't know, and my professor keeps asking whether it's the individual bridge or the parkway system as a whole that's supposed to carry the politics, which I also don't know.

The rule I sketched for myself was: an artifact has a politics when its material form makes one arrangement of power among people much easier to maintain than the alternatives, and you can't undo that without either rebuilding the artifact or rebuilding the world around it. But I think that second clause is doing something sketchy — "rebuilding the world around it" feels loose enough that everything passes, and it also looks like exactly what's separating my stoop verdict from my app verdict, which is the pair I'm least confident about. So: is there a principle that gets all of these, or nearly all? State it precisely, tell me which case motivates each clause, tell me honestly which of my verdicts it forces me to give up (I'm braced for the photocopier), and tell me what it predicts for the cases I didn't put on the list — a chatbot's default persona written into a system prompt, a phone's autocorrect dictionary, and the smart package lockers in my building that need a smartphone app and that my elderly neighbor can't use, which is the one I keep flip-flopping on.
