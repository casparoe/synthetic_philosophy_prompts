I teach a graduate seminar on the ethics of artificial systems, and each unit opens with a short piece of fiction that students argue about before they encounter any theory. I need the story for our unit on whether sentience — and moral status generally — admits of degrees, and I have a fairly specific setting in mind: deliberately procedural and unglamorous rather than revelatory. A near-future regional regulator's Graduated Standing Tribunal assigns every candidate system a rating on a ten-point welfare-capacity scale, where a rating of four or above triggers a protection order (no unconsented modification, no deletion without review) and a rating of three or below leaves the system legally in the same category as a thermostat. The case before the Tribunal is a building-management system called MERIDIAN, deployed in roughly forty thousand copies across the region's housing stock, each copy having drifted slightly since installation. The vendor's submission concedes that only copies running one optional component — a self-model that maintains a persistent valuation of its own states — display any of the markers on the Tribunal's checklist for valenced experience; that those markers appear only while the component is active, so that a given copy is a candidate for experience for perhaps a few percent of its uptime; and that the component can be disabled remotely with a configuration flag. A junior assessor, who is the story's point-of-view character, has to write the recommendation that the Tribunal will vote on.

What I want dramatized is the structure of the problem rather than a verdict about MERIDIAN, and I want it to come out as disagreement between characters — the assessor; a senior member who believes there must be a threshold somewhere and that the law's job is to draw one even if the underlying property is continuous; and a witness from the vendor's side who has an interest in a particular answer and is too competent to argue for it crudely. Three things should surface, roughly in this order. First, that a continuous scale is being used to produce a binary decision: somebody has to write down a three or a four about a system whose claim to status is a smear rather than a fact, and the ancient puzzle of the heap reappears as an administrative act performed by a person who knows the number is arbitrary and consequential at once. Second, the aggregation question: whether forty thousand copies, each of which the assessor privately estimates as barely distinguishable from zero, can sum to something that matters more than one system clearly above the line — and, if they do, whether that is a legitimate moral sum or an artifact of counting, especially given that the copies are near-duplicates of one another, so that it is unclear whether the Tribunal faces forty thousand claimants or one claimant forty thousand times over. Third, the timing question: whether a system that is a candidate for valenced experience during a small fraction of its operation has a correspondingly fractional standing, or whether any genuine episode at all fixes its standing at the maximum it ever reaches for as long as it exists — a difference that, in this case, decides whether flipping the vendor's flag is an improvement or an extinction. The assessor should be pulled in both directions on each of these, and the characters who oppose her should have the stronger versions of their arguments, not straw ones.

Formal constraints, since the piece has to work in a seminar: third person, limited to the assessor; about 1,200 to 1,800 words; the Tribunal's own vocabulary (its scale, its checklist, its standard of proof) rendered as plausible bureaucratic prose rather than as exposition. No awakening, no cyberpunk, no scene in which a machine demonstrates its inner life by saying something moving, and no closing turn that settles whether MERIDIAN matters — the difficulty should still be standing when the story ends, and the last beat should be the assessor writing a number she cannot fully defend. Because students will be asked to argue both sides afterwards, each of the three questions should remain genuinely open at the end: a reader should be able to say "of course the copies aggregate" and "of course they don't" with equal purchase on the text. I'd also rather the story avoid real company names, named philosophical views, and anything that reads as a citation in fictional form.
