Hey — I'm in a metaphysics of time seminar this term and we went over Thomson's lamp last week, and two people from my study group got into a proper argument about it afterwards over coffee. I honestly couldn't tell who was winning, so I'm hoping you can adjudicate.

Quick recap in case it helps. Lamp is on at t=0. You flip the switch at 1/2 minute (off), at 3/4 (on), at 7/8 (off), and so on, infinitely many flips converging on the one-minute mark. Thomson's question: at t = 1, is the lamp on or off? His answer was basically that neither is possible — it can't be on, because every time it's on you immediately switch it off, and it can't be off, because every time it's off you immediately switch it on — so the described sequence of actions can't be performed at all.

Here's roughly how the argument went:

(1) Nadia: Thomson's argument is a straight-up reductio. The setup plus the only two possible states yield a contradiction, so the supertask is impossible. That's the whole point of the example.

(2) Sam: That only works if you assume the state at the limit has to be fixed by the states before it. There's a famous reply along these lines — I'm pretty sure it was Benacerraf — which is that the story just doesn't say what happens at exactly t=1. There's nothing contradictory about saying it ends up on. You've shown the description underdetermines the answer, not that the task can't be done. And if you object "but then when was it last switched on?", nothing in the setup requires there to be a last switching.

(3) Nadia: OK but then the final state is completely arbitrary — a brute fact floating free of the entire prior history, with no explanation whatever. That's not an answer, that's giving up. And notice you've still conceded something big: there's no deterministic rule taking switch positions to lamp states that also covers the limit. So at minimum the lamp is a counterexample to the idea that a complete description of a system up to a time, plus its laws, determines the state at that time.

(4) Sam: Agreed that's the interesting bit, but it isn't a contradiction, and I think the "no determinate answer" thing is an artifact of the setup. Try this variant. Keep the exact same switching schedule, but swap the ideal instant-on/instant-off lamp for a real filament with a thermal time constant: it takes time to heat up and time to cool down. Once the flips are coming faster than the filament can respond, the temperature stops tracking them and just settles toward a limit. The on-intervals add up to 2/3 of the minute (1/2 + 1/8 + 1/32 + ... ) and the off-intervals to 1/3, so if it heats and cools at the same rate, the temperature converges on two-thirds of full brightness. So at the end of the minute the lamp is determinately, visably glowing. The paradox only showed up because Thomson used a bivalent, discontinuous state variable. With a quantity that has its own dynamics, the limit is perfectly well defined. So there's no deep metaphysical moral here — it's an artifact of an idealization.

(5) Nadia: You've changed the subject. Temperature isn't what "on" means. A lamp can be switched off and still be glowing hot — mine does that. Thomson's question is about the state of the circuit, and that still has no limit: the switch itself flips infinitely often, so where is the switch at t=1? Also, your two-thirds isn't a fact about the switching schedule — it depends on the heating and cooling constants of the filament. Change those and you get a different brightness. So your variant doesn't answer Thomson's question, it replaces it with an easier one.

(6) Sam: But that's exactly my point: "is the lamp on?" has no answer until you say which state variable you mean, and once you pick one with the right kind of dynamics the mystery evaporates. And Thomson's conclusion was that the sequence of actions can't be performed. Here is a system undergoing exactly that infinite sequence of switchings with a perfectly coherent physical state at the limit — if the impossibility claim were right, that shouldn't be available. The switch-position worry is a red herring anyway: the switch is a physical object, and before t=1 you'd have to be moving it faster than light. The original thought experiment already breaks physics in ways that have nothing to do with whether supertasks are conceivable.

(7) Nadia: Which is my point — you've smuggled in new stipulations. And note the verdict flips with the phase: start the lamp off instead and keep the same flip times, and the duty cycle is 1/3, so you'd get one-third brightness; make heating and cooling asymmetric and you get another number again. So the "determinate limit" is only determinate relative to a pile of extra physics. The original question — what follows from the switching schedule alone — still has no answer, which is Benacerraf's point all over again, just with extra machinery bolted on.
