my friend and I got into it for about two hours last night about what emotions even are — she thinks they're basically judgments, I think that's obviously wrong — and by the end I'd had to actually write down what I believe, and now that I look at the list I can't tell if it adds up. numbering them so you can refer to them:

1) emotions are not judgments or beliefs. I can be terrified on a plane while fully judging that flying is safe, and I can find something funny while judging that it isn't. so whatever they are, they aren't endorsements of a proposition.
2) but they are assessments of some kind: they represent their objects as having evaluative properties, and they can be correct or incorrect, fitting or unfitting, depending on whether the object really has that property. the dog isn't dangerous, so the fear of it is unfitting.
3) they work more like perceptions than like inferences — immediate, not arrived at by reasoning, and they don't just evaporate when you change your beliefs. this is most of why I reject my friend's view.
4) a recalcitrant emotion is a genuine rational failing, not just a mechanical glitch. the person still scared of flying while knowing it's safe is being irrational, it's a defect in them, and telling them so is a legitimate criticism rather than just a description of their wiring.
5) emotions aren't under voluntary control, so you're not blameworthy or praiseworthy for having one. nobody can be asked to just stop.
6) what makes an emotion the emotion it is is its formal object — the dangerous for fear, the loss for grief, the contaminating for disgust — not how it feels and not what typically brings it on.
7) the felt side of an emotion isn't just its representational content. there's something it's like that doesn't reduce to what the emotion says about its object.

plus three verdicts I don't want to lose: being scared by a horror movie is completely in order and not a failing of any kind, even though nothing on screen is actually dangerous; grief that goes on for years isn't for that reason any less rational; and I do think emotions can be reasons to act, that fear really is a reason to back away.

so: which of these are actually fighting each other? the one I keep circling is 4 and 5 — how can something be a rational failing if you can't help having it — but my friend never even went there, so maybe that's a red herring and the real problem is somewhere else. I also can't tell whether 6 and 7 are in genuine conflict or just answering different questions, though I'll say that being creeped out feels like a different emotion from being afraid and I'd be hard pressed to say the formal object is different. and the horror movie thing sits badly with 2 somehow but I can't say why.
