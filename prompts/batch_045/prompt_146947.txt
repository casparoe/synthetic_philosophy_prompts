I work in security for a mid-sized transit system and Ive been going in circles about one thing for about a week, so here goes.

Every shift a program spits out a patrol route from a random number generator, and were explicitly told not to override it. The reason given is that if someone wanted to hit us and could work out where our people were going to be, theyd just go somewhere else, so the whole point is to be unpredictable. But in the same training manual officers are told to use their judgement and "think like someone casing the station", meaning deliberately reason about what a hostile person would expect and then act on that. Those two instructions seem to me to pull against each other, and I want to know what has to be true about prediction for either one to do anything at all.

Someone summarized a philosophy puzzle for me the other day, Death in Damascus I think it was called, where a man meets Death, Death says hell be waiting tomorrow in whatever city the man ends up in, and Death is never wrong. The part that stuck with me is that in that situation randomizing is completely worthless. A coin cant help you against a predictor who predicts coins. But we randomize. So our practice must be quietly assuming the people we worry about are not like Death. What is the exact limit it needs? That they cant see the output of the draw, that they commit to a target before the draw happens, that they can model our reasoning but not our RNG seed, just that their information is stale? And does it matter that a route becomes public the second the officer walks it, so were only unpredictable for the few hours before the shift?

Then theres the version I actually lose sleep over. Suppose its not a guy with binoculars but a model: someone scrapes a year of our posted schedules, or pays a clerk who knows the scheduling software, and forecasts tomorrows route. The generator stops helping. But judgement doesnt obviously help either, because whatever I reason my way to, the model may have learned. At that point is there anything left, or is this just Death in Damascus with extra steps, where no policy beats any other? I have a strong feeling there is a real difference between being predicted by Death and being predicted by a statistical model trained on your own past behavior. Something like: against Death nothing you do helps, but against the model you can change what kind of system you are, or how legible you are to the thing doing the predicting. I cant make that precise and I cant get from it to what we should actually do on Monday.

Two more bits of the practice, in case they matter. Sergeants override the generator for cause maybe a tenth of the time, and the overrides cluster, which I'd guess makes us partly predictable again. And we never randomize the stuff we deliberate about and then announce, like which stations get cameras or which doors get hardened. Is that an inconsistency or do those cases differ in the right way?

So the question: what does the practice of randomizing patrols, and of not randomizing other things, actually presuppose about prediction, choice, and what the randomness is even for? Reconstruct the theory it needs, tell me which parts it strictly requires versus which are just habits that happen to go well with it, and tell me what wed have to change if we decided the people on the other side were closer to Death than to a guy with binoculars.
