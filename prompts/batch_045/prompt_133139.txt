Hey — I price weird one-off risks at a small specialty insurer: satellite launches, film shoots, music festivals, one custom turbine going overland through three countries. I write maybe 5–8 quotes a week and every single one needs a rate for something that has never happened in exactly this form before. My boss wants a written procedure, and I've been told to ground it in whatever philosophy actually knows about the reference class problem. But it has to be runnable by a junior analyst with a stats minor and no philosophy, in about half an hour, and it has to survive an audit where the regulator points at a file and says "why this class and not that one?"

Here's the mess I actually live in. The same event slots into a pile of different classes — small-launch-vehicle flights, flights using this engine family, flights using this engine with the revised turbopump, flights with a second stage assembled in the last eighteen months, flights where the payload customer is a first-timer — and those classes give rates that differ by an order of magnitude. Right now people either grab whichever class produces the number they were hoping for, or they average two of them, which I'm fairly sure is just incoherent. We've also had at least one case where someone built the class *after* seeing a couple of losses (three claims all involved the same rigging subcontractor, so new jobs got priced as "jobs involving that subcontractor"), and I genuinely can't tell whether that's cheating or whether it's the correct move.

Stuff the procedure has to handle, none of which I know how to do:

- How do you even generate the candidate classes, and which features are allowed into the description? Machine age, operator hours, weather window, supplier, whether the client has done this before. Some of those are doing causal work and some are just correlated with the thing, and half the time we can't tell which.
- How narrow do you go? The narrow class always feels more honest until you realize it has four exposures, or zero events, or one event out of one and a 100% loss rate that we obviously cannot charge.
- What happens when the broad class and the narrow class disagree by like 10x? Do we escalate, and who signs — me, the senior underwriter, or the chief actuary? Right now: nobody knows, it just depends who's in the office.
- Classes defined partly by the outcome, or by facts we only learn later. My rule of thumb is that you can only use what was on your desk at quote time, but I can't say why, and I suspect it's not quite that clean.
- Cases where there is no rate: nobody has ever done this, or the world moved (new engine revision, new regulation, new supplier) so the old frequency means nothing. I would much rather the procedure say "no defensible number, decline to quote" than that it manufacture one and we find out later.
- Output format. Every file needs a "class string" — a one-line description of the class and where the rate came from — because renewals come back a year later and a different analyst has to re-derive it, and we've already been burned by someone writing "similar jobs" in the box.
- Our home-brewed rule of thumb, which nobody can defend: go to the narrowest class that has at least ten exposures and whose rate is within 10x of its parent class. Is that doing real work or is it a superstition? Should I throw it out? Same question for our other internal rule: never quote more than 50% below the broadest defensible class rate.

What I want from you: the questions, in order; what counts as an adequate answer to each one; where the procedure stops with a no; and where it hands the question to a human because it's a question about appetite, fairness, or values rather than a fact about the risk. Be honest about which steps are judgment calls with a number stapled to them, and be honest about what the procedure fundamentally cannot deliver — my worry is that there is no uniquely correct class and the whole thing is just damage control with better paperwork. If that's true, say so and tell me what a well-run damage-control procedure looks like.

Then run the thing on three live-ish cases:

1. A first-ever music festival on a river floodplain. Twenty years of flood records for the region, four years for that particular stretch of river, no track record for the promoter, and a fixed date we can't move.
2. A satellite launch on a new small vehicle: two flights, one failure. The engine family has sixty flights with three failures. There's also a narrower subset — this engine with the revised turbopump — with nine flights and no failures. Three different analysts here picked three different classes and I can't tell which of them is wrong.
3. A fleet policy for a delivery company. The sharpest class we can actually defend statistically is built partly out of driver age bracket. Compliance is going to lose their minds. I want to know whether your procedure tells me to use that class anyway, or whether it says "this stopped being a reference class question and became a fairness question" — and if it says that, I want to hear it even though it's not what I want, because I was kind of hoping the machinery would get me off the hook here.

Oh, and one more: the client in case 3 has offered to let us use a telematics score instead, which predicts about as well but is way harder to explain to a regulator. If the procedure has anything to say about picking between two classes that perform the same, I'd like to hear it, because I suspect it doesn't.
