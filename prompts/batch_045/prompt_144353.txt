**Order effects in probability kinematics are a convergence artifact (working title)**

Setup: partition {E, ¬E}; experience moves your credence in E from P(E) to q without giving you any proposition to conditionalize on. JC: P'(H) = q·P(H|E) + (1−q)·P(H|¬E), justified by *rigidity*: P'(H|E) = P(H|E) and P'(H|¬E) = P(H|¬E).

*Step 1 (a characterization I half-remember).* I believe JC can be characterized as follows: over a finite set of possible worlds, P' is the unique distribution that minimizes relative entropy D(Q‖P) = Σ Q(ω) log(Q(ω)/P(ω)) subject to Q(E) = q. Sketch: Lagrange multiplier on the constraint gives Q(ω) ∝ P(ω) within each cell of the partition, with the cell totals pinned at q and 1−q, which is exactly JC. Note this is min relative entropy, *not* max entropy — they only coincide when the prior is uniform. So JC is already "the minimal change that accommodates the new constraint."

*Step 2 (the toy example).* Four cells, prior: P(E∧F) = .30, P(E∧¬F) = .10, P(¬E∧F) = .10, P(¬E∧¬F) = .50. So P(E) = P(F) = .40. Two uncertain inputs: credence in E goes to .60, credence in F goes to .70.

- E first, then F: after the row update the table is (.45, .15; .0667, .3333), so P(F) = .5167. After the column update: (.6097, .0931; .0903, .2069). But now P(E) = .7028, not .60 — the first constraint has been violated by the second update.
- F first, then E: after the column update (.525, .05; .175, .25), P(E) = .575; after the row update (.5478, .0522; .1647, .2353), and P(F) = .7125, not .70.

So P'(E∧F) is about .610 on one order and about .548 on the other.

*Step 3 (diagnosis).* The second update breaks the rigidity conditions of the first. The row update holds P(F|E) = .75 fixed (.45/.60 = .30/.40); the subsequent column update moves it to .8676. Equivalently: the column update holds P(E|F) and P(E|¬F) fixed, and that's exactly what pushes the row margin off .60. So non-commutativity isn't a mystery about the rule — it's that each application of JC is only locally minimal, and a second application on a different partition undoes part of the first.

*Step 4 (the proposal).* Applying JC to two partitions in sequence is one pass of iterative proportional fitting (the procedure survey people use to adjust a table to known margins — "raking", or the Deming–Stephan thing, I'd have to look up the name). One pass doesn't converge. If you iterate to the fixed point, you get the unique distribution that satisfies *both* marginal constraints while preserving the prior's odds ratio (here 15), which works out to P(E∧F) ≈ .5436, P(E∧¬F) ≈ .0564, P(¬E∧F) ≈ .1564, P(¬E∧¬F) ≈ .2436. This is also just the min-relative-entropy distribution subject to *both* constraints — so it's the same norm as JC, applied to the total evidence rather than to one input at a time. And it's order-independent by construction. So: the order effect is a symptom of taking one pass, and the fix is not to pick a better partition or to add a sufficiency condition, it's to stop treating the inputs one at a time.

*Step 5 (scope, and the bit I'm least sure about).* Two worries. (a) The fixed point exists only when the two sets of margins are jointly satisfiable; if the prior puts structural zeros in the wrong cells the constraints can be infeasible (e.g. only the off-diagonal cells have positive prior, and the target row and column margins sum to more than 1), whereas the sequential rule always returns *some* number. Maybe that's an argument for the sequential rule, or maybe it shows those cases are pathological. (b) The deeper ambiguity: is an input like "your credence in F should be .70" a constraint on your *final* distribution, or a constraint that holds *at the time the experience arrives*? The fixed point reads it the first way — and note that on the E-first path you're at .5167 for F when the second experience arrives and you end at .70, which looks wrong if you think the experience is what moved you to .70. I want to say the second reading is confused, because what the experience fixes is the total evidential impact, but I can't quite make that stick. I also think there's a distinction worth drawing between *one* experience described in two ways (the Judy Benjamin-ish family of cases, whose details I always mix up, hence my own example) and *two* genuinely separate experiences, and that only the latter even tempts you toward the sequential reading — but I'm not sure the distinction survives scrutiny.

*Step 6 (why anyone should care).* The fixed-point treatment generalizes cleanly to constraints given as intervals or inequalities ("my credence in E ended up somewhere between .6 and .7") — you just minimize relative entropy subject to the relaxed constraint set. The sequential treatment has no natural generalization there, since "apply JC with an interval" isn't a well-defined operation. That looks like a real advantage to me.
