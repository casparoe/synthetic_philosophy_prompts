In the group I work in, work on learned systems is governed by two conventions that sit awkwardly together, and I would like the implicit theory behind them made explicit.

The first convention governs postmortems. When a deployed system misbehaves, the write-up must explain the behaviour at the level of mechanism: which component, which training signal, which data, which decoding choice produced the output. Reviewers send back any write-up that says the system believed something, wanted something, decided something, or was trying to do something, on the grounds that such language is anthropomorphic and therefore not an explanation.

The second convention governs capability and risk assessment, and here the same reviewers use agent vocabulary freely, because the safety case depends on it. Evaluations ask whether the system would mislead a grader if it could gain by doing so, whether it pursues a goal across contexts it was never trained on, whether it is deliberately underperforming on capability tests, whether it tracks what a user does and does not know. A system described purely as a distribution over token sequences is not something a deception risk assessment can be written about at all — yet the model card for that same system describes it as "just" a statistical predictor.

I have started calling the movement between these conventions stance arbitrage: the intentional stance is treated as licensed for prediction and alarm, and as illicit for explanation and excuse. Assume the familiar three-way distinction between the physical, design, and intentional stances.

1. Reconstruct the implicit theory of intentional ascription that this practice needs. In particular, does it presuppose that intentional claims can predict without explaining, and can that asymmetry be defended? Give the strongest version of the theory, and distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what merely fits it comfortably.

2. Say which rival views the practice quietly rules out, and where it is underdetermined. Could an instrumentalist, a realist about model beliefs, and someone who regards the vocabulary as useful fiction all sign off on both conventions? Identify the points at which the conventions are philosophically silent and the practice could have gone another way.

3. Test the reconstructed theory against three systems where the stances pull apart: a thermostat; a chess engine's evaluation function, whose mechanics are fully known; and a large model whose behaviour across many prompts is best predicted by attributing to it a goal it was never trained to have, where no mechanism is known. Does the theory deliver the verdicts the practice actually gives, and is that pattern of verdicts defensible?

4. Address the real-patterns question. Suppose we say that an intentional ascription is warranted when it compresses a system's behaviour into a description cheaper than any mechanical description available, and that this compression is what makes the pattern real rather than projected. Notice that whether a compression is available depends on who is asking — what they have measured, what they already know, what they can compute — so that a pattern may be real for the safety team and not for the interpretability team. Does the practice have the resources to handle that relativity, or does the relativity undermine the ban in the first convention?

5. Finally, the reform question. If the ban on intentional explanation were lifted, what exactly would change in a postmortem — which new claims would become checkable, by what test, and what kind of evidence would settle them — and what would not change? And if instead the practice dropped intentional vocabulary from risk assessment, what would be lost that no design-stance description could replace?
