I have been auditing a graduate seminar on acting for the right reasons, and I have been keeping a record of the leader's verdicts on the cases she has discussed so far. She uses the phrase strictly: for her it names a property an action can lack even when the action is right, the outcome is good, and the agent is not blameworthy, and she has said she is not interested in praise or blame, only in whether the action has this property and in what degree. Here are her six verdicts, with the consideration she gave in each case where she gave one.

One. A shopkeeper in a tourist town has never knowingly short-changed anyone; he is scrupulous because his regulars tip well and a complaint would cost him more than the change, and he says without hesitation that if he were certain no one could tell, he would pocket it. Verdict: no moral worth. The action is right; the agent is not acting for the reason that makes it right.

Two. A woman sits with her brother through a long night in an emergency department. She stays because she loves him and cannot bear to leave; the thought that she owes him anything does not occur to her, and she would be baffled by the suggestion that she was doing her duty. Verdict: moral worth in the fullest degree, and not merely equal to the dutiful case but superior to it.

Three. A warehouse worker helps a man who is hiding in the loading bay from people the worker believes are lawfully pursuing him. He helps because the man is frightened and in pain, and he goes on helping while believing the whole time that helping is wrong, and afterwards he is ashamed. Verdict: full moral worth. He was moved by the right thing; it does not matter that he would have denied that it was a reason, and it does not matter that he felt guilty afterwards.

Four. The treasurer of a small charity returns an overpayment to a donor she dislikes, who was rude to her and who will never notice. She does it because the rules require it and because she has made it a fixed policy never to let her feelings decide; she takes no pleasure in it and would rather have kept the money. Verdict: it has worth, but only in a second-rate or fallback grade. She wants to count it fully, she said, and something about the case leaves her cold.

Five. A donor with a large sum and no opinions of her own about charity writes the cheque where her brother-in-law, an economist she trusts, tells her it will do the most good. She cannot say what makes that cause better than the alternatives; when he later revises his advice she revises with him without asking why; and she would have given the same amount had he told her that sincerity matters more than the cause. Verdict: no, or at best derivative. She was moved by his say-so rather than by the feature of the cause that made it worth supporting, and she had no grip at all on what that feature was. Reliable deference, the leader added, is not enough on its own.

Six. A nurse of thirty years' standing notices a patient's distress before anyone else does and sits down with her. She does not deliberate, she would be unable to say what she was responding to if asked, and she could not have walked past. Verdict: full moral worth; no deliberation is required, and being unable to name what moved her is no objection.

The case she has set for next week is this. Dana sits on a hospital committee that must assign one portable ultrasound machine to one of two wards. The committee's own charter fixes the right-making feature in advance: the machine should go to the ward where more patients have outcomes that depend on early imaging. By that criterion Ward A is genuinely the more urgent, and Dana believes this; she has visited both wards and could say why. But the score sheet placed in front of her at the meeting contains a clerical error that inflates Ward A's numbers, and it is the number on the sheet, not her own sense of the wards, that actually moves her hand: she says afterwards that she went by the sheet. Two further facts. Had the error not been there and the wards been exactly tied on the score, she would still have chosen Ward A, and would have given the reason: more people, worse prospects. And when the error comes to light and the true numbers turn out to be very close, she is unmoved and says she would make the same call again. Dana did the right thing, and she had a true belief about what makes it right, but on this occasion that belief did no work; the sheet did.

What I want from you is this. Predict her verdict on Dana, and say how confident the prediction is. Then state the rule she is running as sharply as you can, and set out the other rules that fit all six of her earlier verdicts just as well; the interesting case is the one where her actual motivation does not include the right-making feature but her dispositions do, and I want to know what each candidate rule says about it and why. Tell me which of her six verdicts is doing the most work in discriminating between them, and which of the five other verdicts would have to be revised if she abandons it. Then name the smallest variation on Dana's case that would separate the surviving rules from one another: I have two candidates in mind, one in which the sheet is silent and the wards are tied, and one in which the sheet errs in the other direction and Dana follows it against her own belief, and I want to know how much either would really tell us. Finally, say whether the verdict you predict commits her to holding that moral worth is luck-dependent in ordinary cases, and if so whether that is a cost she can absorb given what she has already said about the shopkeeper and the nurse.
