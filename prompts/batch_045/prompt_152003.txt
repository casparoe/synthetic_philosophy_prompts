I run a tiny discussion group at my local library (one short thing a month, none of us have any training) and next month it's vagueness. I've read a bit on my own and I think I get the basic shape: the epistemicist says there's a sharp line somewhere in the murky middle zone and we just can never find out where, and the supervaluationist says there isn't one line, there are lots of equally acceptable ways of sharpening the word, and "true" means true on every one of them. I want a dialogue between the two of them, but please don't let it turn into another go at the heap/sorites thing, we already did that and it just went round in circles.

Here's the bit I made up and want them to actually chew on. Say I show someone a run of photos of men's heads, one at a time, asking "bald?" for each, and I sneak a few obviously bald or obviously hairy ones in at the start as warm-ups. Everyone I tried it on shifts where they put the cut depending on which warm-ups they saw. The part that got me: even when I tell them up front exactly what I'm doing and they say they're correcting for it, it still happens. So the place the line lands moves with stuff that has nothing to do with the head in front of them. Has the line itself moved, or only their grip on it? And I specifically don't want either of them to get away with just saying "oh that's a contextual/pragmatic effect, irrelevant to the semantics" — the other one should push back on wether that move is really open to them, or wether it's cheating.

Then, at the end, give them something with real consequences: I was thinking a hospital rule where a growth counts as "large" and that gets you into a fast treatment track, where treating too early does real harm and treating too late is worse. Let each of them say what their view actually tells someone to do there, and whether they honestly think their view helps at all or whether this whole debate is just bookkeeping about a word.

Make them equally good at this, no strawmen, and make them answer each other's actual points rather than each delivering a little speech. Keep it in plain words, no jargon I'd have to look up, and don't have them name-drop any papers or authors — we can't check anything and I'd never know if you were making it up. Give me exactly five numbered exchanges, a few sentences each, no intro and no wrap-up at the end.
