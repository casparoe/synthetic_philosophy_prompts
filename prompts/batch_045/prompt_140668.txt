I am organising the final session of an undergraduate seminar on David Hume's philosophy of religion, and we have just finished a close reading of his essay Of Miracles, the tenth section of An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding (1748). Rather than close with a prose summary, I would like to hand out a poem. Over the term the class invented a home-made test case to sharpen the argument: a drowned sailor is said to have been publicly restored to life on the quayside at Leith, the port of Edinburgh, in front of a large number of witnesses of good sense, education, learning, and undoubted integrity, in a populous and celebrated part of the world, so that if their testimony were false the falsehood itself would be a greater violation of uniform experience than the resurrection it reports. In discussion we kept returning to the structure of Hume's maxim: no testimony can establish a miracle unless the falsehood of the testimony would be more miraculous than the fact it endeavours to establish, and even in that case the two arguments destroy one another, so that the superior gives only an assurance proportioned to the force that remains after the inferior is deducted, and the decision always goes against the greater miracle.

Could you write a sonnet—fourteen lines, with the first eight lines forming an octave, the ninth line opening the turn and the next three lines developing it, and the final two lines a closing couplet—in which this structure does the work? The octave should build the baseline Humean case: the uniform, exceptionless experience that the dead do not rise, the law of nature as a regularity of experience, and the sheer weight of improbability against the event. The turn should introduce the concession case, the Leith witnesses whose collective falsehood would be the greater miracle, so that the miracle of the event is no longer the greater of the two. The closing couplet must say exactly what a careful Humean is left with: a mutual destruction of arguments, the rejection of the greater miracle, and a residual, proportioned assurance rather than either a restored certainty or a pious affirmation that the miracle occurred. It should be accurate enough that a Hume specialist would not wince—in particular, the direction of the weighing must be right, and the poem must not let the limiting case collapse into full belief. Please write the poem in British English, using British spelling and idiom (for example, scepticism, endeavour, honour).
