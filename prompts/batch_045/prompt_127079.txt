I'm putting together a position paper for a small reading group on personal identity — the group skews toward narrative-based, broadly Dennett-flavoured treatments of the self, and I volunteered to write up where I have actually landed, which turns out to be less tidy than I expected. I've been working on this while spending a lot of time with my mother, who is in the later stages of dementia, so a couple of these commitments are load-bearing for me in a way that isn't purely academic. Here is the set, roughly as I would defend it, with names attached to the two cases so we can refer back to them.

(1) The self is a center of narrative gravity: a real but abstract thing, posited to make sense of a creature's behaviour, memory, dispositions and self-description over time, and made of nothing extra — no inner agent, no Cartesian residue, no shadowy further fact over and above the embodied patterns it summarizes. Real, though: not a useful fiction, and not something to be eliminated. (2) Where the ordinary psychological-continuity facts run out — fission, branch-line teletransportation, the usual puzzle cases — there is no further fact of the matter; the question is settled by decision or convention if it is settled at all, and going looking for a hidden fact is a mistake. Call this the no-hidden-facts rule. (3) Prudential concern should be graded rather than binary: it should track narrative connectedness instead of an all-or-nothing identity relation. I think of it as a narrative half-life. It is not irrational for me to care less about a very distant future stage of myself, or about the child I was, in proportion as the narrative thins; and I think the same is true of my mother — the self I knew has thinned dramatically, and I do not think there is any determinate moment at which it stopped being there. I do not think any of that touches her moral status or what she is owed, and I do not think I need to fudge anything to say so.

(4) *The Dossier Duplicate.* A meticulous dossier of a living man's life — every diary entry, recording, medical file, message and testimony, enough to reconstruct his dispositions and his first-person narrative in exhaustive detail — is used to build a distinct human organism whose brain is arranged so that the resulting person is, at the moment of construction, psychologically type-identical to him: same apparent memories, same projects, same self-description. The original goes on living, unchanged; there is no causal link from his life to the duplicate's states beyond the dossier itself. My verdict: the duplicate is flatly not him, has no claim on his relationships, his projects, or his debts, and his life is not continued in the duplicate in any respect. And here is the part I am least sure of: I do not think this is a matter of decision or convention. I think it is settled by the facts — in a way that (2) seems to say it is not. (5) *The Second-Life Convert.* A woman undergoes what I will stipulate as total narrative rupture: complete loss of episodic memory for her earlier life, a comprehensively replaced set of projects, dispositions, commitments and self-descriptions, to the point where she cannot recognize or reclaim her earlier self in any of it — while remaining the same living human organism with an entirely unbroken bodily and causal history. My verdict: she is straightforwardly the same person as before; her claim that she is a new person is not merely socially inconvenient or something we are entitled to overrule, it is false; and what she owes — her promises, her debts, and her guilt for what she did before the rupture — survives at full strength, not attenuated. (6) The ownership condition on blame: I am blameworthy for something only if I can now own it, in the sense of incorporating it into the narrative I currently live from. Where that capacity is genuinely absent, blame is a category mistake — the person may still be dangerous and may still need to be managed or restrained, but that is a different justification with a different shape. (7) First-person authority is not absolute: someone's avowed story about themselves can be wrong, self-deception is real, and there can be facts about what a person is up to that their own narrative misrepresents — even though, by (1), the self is supposed to be nothing over and above a narrative.

What I want is an audit, not a summary or a tour of the literature. Go through the seven and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, and rank those tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad: for each, say exactly what would have to give and what that revision would cost me somewhere else in the set. I also want you to tell me which of the tensions are only apparent and dissolve on inspection — I have a feeling at least one or two of these look worse than they are and I genuinely cannot tell which, so please say what the dissolving distinction is rather than just asserting that the pair is fine. Two further things. First, note that (4) and (5) push on (1) from what look like opposite directions — one demanding that the narrative have the right history, the other that the history be enough without the narrative — and I would like to know whether that squeeze is real or whether I have misdescribed one of the cases. Second, if I could revise exactly one commitment and leave the other six comfortable, which should it be, and what would that revision commit me to that I might not want? Please be specific about which theses have to go rather than telling me that the package is broadly Parfitian or broadly Dennettian; I can already say that much myself.
