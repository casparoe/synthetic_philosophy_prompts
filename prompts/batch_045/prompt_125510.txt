You are a professional philosopher who works in the Wittgenstein and rule-following literature — someone who is genuinely at home with the sceptical paradox about meaning, the quus-style deviant interpretations, the failure of dispositional accounts, the regress of interpretations, and the point of a merely sceptical solution that swaps truth conditions for assertibility conditions — and who can also write deadpan comedy for a reader who has never opened the book everyone calls Kripkenstein. I am a PhD student; I help run a graduate reading group on the later Wittgenstein and I TA the second-year metaphysics seminar, and my supervisor insists that satire is just a cheap way of appearing to make a point you cannot argue for. I want to hand her something that proves otherwise: not a paper, but a document in which the structure of the problem is visible to anyone who reads it carefully, including undergraduates who have never heard of the paradox, and in which a specialist can check every joke against the actual argument and find it fair.

The piece I want is the surviving internal documentation of the Office of Conformance Assurance at a mid-sized insurance firm — formally, a numbered standard operating procedure, around 800 to 1000 words, titled something like SOP-CONFORM-1: Determination of Correct Continuation, written in flat corporate-bureaucratic register. The backstory, which the document should only hint at in its preamble: an actuarial macro returned 5 when asked to add 68 and 57; everyone agreed the output was wrong; the ensuing investigation could not state what fact made it wrong; the Office was created in consequence. Please include, in this order and as numbered clauses: (1) Scope and Definitions, defining correct, the same rule, and again, in which the definitional regress shows up but is treated as a minor drafting problem to be fixed in a future revision; (2) a Training and Onboarding clause explaining how new staff are brought to continue a series, where every training device presupposes the capacity it is supposed to install; (3) a Best-Efforts clause that falls back on what staff are disposed to answer, with the circularity admitted somewhere quiet, such as a footnote or an appendix; (4) Dispute Resolution, which bottoms out in the judgement of a Conformance Review Panel, together with a sub-clause for the case where the panel is unanimous now and unanimously reverses itself later; (5) a Risk Register of known unresolved exposures, including the exposure that no clause in the document states a fact about correctness at all, and an entry recording that the brute-fact option is unavailable from the vendor; (6) at least one worked artefact — a filed bug ticket or audit finding — about a function that agrees with addition on every pair of numbers below some threshold and returns 5 above it, processed entirely through the Office's own machinery, so that the reader feels the deviant case cannot be excluded rather than being told that it cannot; and (7) a clause covering junior staff and the purely private case, an employee whose rule no one else can check, where the prose should visibly seize up. Before you draft, spend a few lines brainstorming the six or seven distinct places in the argument where the regress genuinely bites, so that the jokes are distributed across the whole structure rather than piled on the deviant-function example.
