I chair the programme committee of a small endowed scholarship trust whose deed restricts its awards to students born in one named country. For a year now the trustees have been arguing about whether to petition the court to widen that restriction, and the argument keeps turning on questions none of us can settle: whether our freedom to pick beneficiaries really includes freedom to pick them on grounds we would otherwise call discriminatory, whether a dead donor's intentions generate claims that survive him, whether the fact that an applicant is poor through no fault of her own makes a difference to what we owe her, and what it would even mean for a charity to exclude someone. By coincidence, the trust's annual public lecture this spring is to be given by a political philosopher whose recent book defends a right of political communities to exclude would-be immigrants, and I have agreed to interview her on stage afterwards. I would like to be better prepared than my own trustees' meetings have left me.

Her position, as I take it from the book, has three moving parts. First, she grounds the right to exclude in the freedom of association of a community's existing members together with their collective ownership of public institutions and territory, so that the state's right is derivative of the rights of its members rather than a sovereign prerogative of its own. Second, she insists that the right to exclude is a right to refuse admission and not a right to make people leave: a state may decline to admit, may deny welfare benefits to those who arrive regardless, and may shut them out of the formal labour market, but may not deploy coercive force to remove them; she also holds the familiar view that open borders and a generous welfare state are in tension, and thinks the tension should be resolved in favour of open territory with restricted access to benefits. Third, she accepts a reciprocity proviso: a state whose own policies have materially contributed to the conditions that make people want to leave forfeits, against those people in particular, its right to keep them out.

Please draft ten to twelve questions for a forty-minute conversation, none of them biographical or generic, that press on the joints of this position instead of asking her to restate it. Where possible, anticipate her most likely reply and give me the follow-up that reply invites. She has a stock defensive move in print, the claim that an objection is really an objection to enforcement rather than to the right itself, and I would like at least one sequence of questions that makes that move progressively harder for her to fall back on. Beyond that, I would like at least one question on each of the following, and any others you think belong:

How a coercive exclusion can be justified to someone who has never consented to the authority of the excluding state, and what is left of exclusion at all if her enforcement is meant to be minimal: someone who crosses anyway and lives on private charity seems to wrong no one, so press her on whether her view has quietly become open borders with an asterisk.

Whether the restricted-benefits half of the view is stable. If arrivals may not lawfully work and may not claim support, their presence is materially impossible, so the carve-out either achieves too little to satisfy anyone who wants restriction or achieves exclusion through destitution, which looks like coercion under another description; and whether a ban on hiring newcomers is really an associative right rather than protection for incumbent workers.

The composition problem in the associative analogy. Freedom of association seems to presuppose actual associates with shared purposes, whereas in a large pluralist state most citizens have no associative relationship with most others. Follow up on whether her account therefore reduces to joint ownership of territory, whether ownership yields a right to set terms of use rather than unlimited exclusion, and how on her account citizens differ from long-term resident non-citizens who pay taxes and use public institutions.

Whether the associative right includes the right to exclude for bad or arbitrary reasons, such as race or religion, given that freedom of association is normally thought to protect exactly that. She will likely say that the state as a corporate agent is bound by equality constraints that its individual members are not, and the follow-up should press whether the associative right is then doing any independent justificatory work at all, or whether she is really a partialist about members' interests with a side-constraint.

Third parties. Excluding a migrant also prevents citizens from employing, marrying, worshipping or organising with her, so whose associative right prevails when associational rights conflict, and does this force her into the kind of interest-balancing she says she dislikes.

The reciprocity proviso and causal overdetermination: how to handle diffuse collective causation such as trade policy, arms sales or emissions; what to do when those who would bear the costs of admission are not those who caused the harm and those harmed are not the ones who arrive; and whether the proviso, once taken seriously, leaves anything of the general right.

The asymmetry she draws between refusing admission and expelling, and whether long presence, work, children and taxpaying generate membership claims of their own, so that she owes us an account of regularisation that fits a right to exclude.

Whether the derivative structure of her argument commits her to saying that sub-state associations, a town, a congregation or a small endowed trust, hold exclusionary rights of the same shape, and whether the anti-discrimination constraint she imposes on states also binds private associations. I would like one question here that lets me bring the discussion home to my own trust's nationality restriction, without turning the conversation into a consultation.

Finally, one question about what would count against the view: what finding, case or argument would make her give up the right to exclude, or the enforcement minimalism, or the proviso.

Assume an audience of educated non-specialists but do not thin out the philosophy. Nothing in the questions should require her to name, cite or recommend any literature.
