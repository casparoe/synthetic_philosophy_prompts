Consider this contrast. Norway's Halden prison (and Nordic policy generally) is built around the normality principle: cells look like ordinary rooms, officers are unarmed, and prisoners shop, cook, use tools, and move about fairly freely, so life inside is suppose to resemble life outside. In much of American mass incarceration, even for nonviolent offenses, daily life means locked cells, counts, searches, restricted property, and sometimes long segregation; any freedom is a revocable privilege, not a right. Both systems offically say they are preparing people to rejoin society and are not just inflicting pain, yet many US practices treat prisoners as having lost the standing to make ordinary responsible choices, while the Nordic setup treats them as retaining the same basic capacities for agency and merely confines them.

What philosophical commitments does each model actually presuppose about moral responsibility, agency, punishment, and the state's relation to the offender? Distinguish what each practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits with, and say where it is underdetermined and could be licensed by more than one theory. If we took the Nordic view of preserved agency seriously, what would have to change about American good-time credits, parole discretion, and the use of solitary or administrative segregation as a non-punitive tool? And if a Halden-style prison imposed long-term segregation on prisoners judged risky, would that be an American practice in a nicer building, or would it contradict something internal to the normality principle?
