My co-author and I have been going back and forth for something like four months on a chapter about luck egalitarianism, and I've started to think we are stuck in exactly the place the literature is stuck in, just with less polite language. So what I want is a diagnosis, not a survey, and defintely not a reading list.

Here's the shape of it as I see it. The distributive camp all accept some version of the core idea: inequalities that trace back to what people chose are acceptable, inequalities that trace back to unchosen circumstances are not. The relational critics say the whole framework is demeaning, that it installs an audit in which the badly off have to establish that they aren't at fault before they qualify for anything, and that equality is about how people stand to each other rather than about the shape of a distribution. The luck egalitarians have answered by bolting on constraints: don't humiliate people in the process, don't let the cautious freeload off those who took risks, set the baseline with a hypothetical insurance market, add a floor. My co-author thinks the entire fight is verbal, two groups using the word equality for different things. I dont think that can be all of it.

I think there are three arguments stacked on top of each other and maybe only one of them is a moral argument.

First, what equality is for: relational versus distributive. That one looks like bedrock, a difference in starting pictures of what a political ideal is supposed to do, which nobody can argue their way out of.

Second, the currency dispute, resources versus welfare or access to welfare versus capabilities. This looks to me like a proxy fight about which theory of justice you already accept, plus a measurement problem, since you can't settle which currency is the right one without already being able to measure the others.

Third, the in-house technical one: whether to equalise ex ante, compensating before the risky choice at the level of option sets and prospects, or ex post, compensating once outcomes are known. That one smells like a live formal disagreement, about attitudes to risk and about where you think agency is located, in picking among gambles or in living with what you got.

And then there is the thing I keep coming back to and can't make precise, which is the real reason I'm writing to you instead of rewriting the section again. It seems to me that the luck/choice decomposition isn't unique. Take someone who took a risky, badly paid job and ended up worse off than someone who took a safe one. Is that option luck, since she chose the gamble, or brute luck, since being the sort of person who takes that gamble, or having nothing better on the menu, isn't something she chose? You can get either verdict depending on how finely you carve up the option set and at what level of description you locate her choice. The old expensive-tastes example looks like the same problem in a different hat. So I suspect that a lot of the ink is being spent on a choice of modelling level that nobody states, and that the substantive moral claim is being carried by what is really a convention. Sort of like a gauge freedom: the underlying facts are invariant, the description isn't, and people are arguing about the gauge.

So, concretely:

- Is the three-layer story right, and is the currency layer really a proxy fight, or is there a genuine argument about currency that doesn't just presuppose a whole theory of justice underneath?
- Can the gauge-freedom thing be made precise? Is there a way of stating the non-uniqueness of the luck/choice split that actually does work, and if it can be stated, does it dissolve part of the dispute or just relocate it one level down?
- For each layer, what would count as progress, a formal result, a distinction, an empirical fact about how real transfer systems actually operate, or simply a change of question? And is there any layer where the honest answer is that no progress should be expected, and why?

Push back on any of that, especially the gauge business. If it's only a fancy way of restating the old regress worry about whether the self is itself a matter of luck, say so plainly and tell me why the restatement is or isn't worth anything.
