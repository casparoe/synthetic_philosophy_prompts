I chair the research ethics board at a university medical center, and we have a first-in-human feasibility protocol for closed-loop deep brain stimulation in treatment-resistant depression, eight patients. Electrodes are implanted in a mood-related circuit; an implanted controller reads a neural signal the investigators have validated as a proxy for momentary negative affect and adjusts stimulation in real time so as to push that signal down. Titration and ongoing optimization run on the same proxy plus a once-daily single-item self-rating. The primary endpoint is change in the proxy, and the safety monitoring is the patient's own daily rating, spontaneous adverse-event reporting, and a monthly clinician impression.

I need a decision memo I can circulate before our next meeting, and I want the worry spelled out rather than gestured at: this design strikes me as close to literal wireheading, and I want to know how close. Please separate three failure modes that our discussion keeps running together: proxy-goal divergence, where the loop lowers the signal in a way that does not correspond to the patient getting better (blunting, apathy, a hypomanic indifference that reports as relief); corruption of the readout, where stimulation artifact or electrode drift contaminates the very channel being sensed and optimized; and tampering with the reward process itself, in the sense that the intervention acts on the patient's own report, so the instrument we would use to detect harm sits downstream of the thing being optimized. Tell me which of these the protocol creates, which it merely makes possible, and whether the distinction does any work here or collapses once you notice that the patient is inside the loop. I also want the skeptical framing answered: the device is not an agent with preferences, nothing is being reinforced in the classical sense, and the controller optimizes a signal it did not choose, so is wireheading the wrong description or does the mapping hold at the level that matters?

Then give me the bottom line: approve, reject, or approve with conditions. If conditions, make them the kind an investigator could actually implement: what counts as an outcome measurement genuinely outside the loop, how to detect that the proxy and the patient's actual condition have come apart, what the stopping rule should be keyed to, and what consent language and post-trial arrangements would handle a patient who reports feeling better while their life goes badly and who demands the device stay on.

Finally, state fairly the strongest case against my worry, including the parts that make me uneasy: these patients have exhausted every other option, open-loop stimulation already produces exactly the effects I am describing and we tolerate it, and a rule that discounts patients' own reports of feeling better has an ugly history in psychiatry. If that case should carry the day, say so, and say how the memo should read instead.
