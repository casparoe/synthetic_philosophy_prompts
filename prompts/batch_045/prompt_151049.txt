I write about science and ideas for a national newspaper, and our standards desk has asked me to run a forty-five-minute session for the newsroom before we decide whether to trial an automated fact-checking tool. The room will be about fourteen editors and reporters, none of whom has taken a probability course; two are reflexively hostile to anything with numbers in it; and our standards editor reads enough statistics to catch me if I hand-wave. I have a whiteboard and nothing else.

The occasion is a vendor pitch. The tool queries the same underlying language model five times under five different prompt framings, treats the five answers as a panel of "jurors," and returns the majority verdict. The deck claims the arrangement is licensed by Condorcet's jury theorem and closes with a slide about the wisdom of crowds. I think the pitch is wrong in a way that is worth teaching rather than just asserting, and I would like a plan for how to teach it — not the explanation written out for me.

What I need is the sequence and the reason for it: which idea has to be in place before the next one means anything, and why. My instinct is that per-juror competence above chance has to come before the asymptotic result, and that the independence condition has to be stated as independence conditional on the truth rather than as "they don't talk to each other" — but tell me where I have the order wrong.

I also want one concrete case to carry the whole session, and I want you to say what such a case must have and what it must lack. My candidate is a photograph that arrives with a claim about where and when it was taken — a binary question with a fact of the matter. I take it the example needs genuine ground truth and genuinely separate private signals, and must not be the kind of editorial judgment (is this story worth the space, is this headline fair) where there is no correct answer for the theorem to track.

Here are the misconceptions I expect, in rough order of how loudly they will be voiced: that the theorem shows more heads are always better, when below-chance competence inverts it; that 51% accuracy plus enough jurors gets you anywhere soon; that our five model calls are five jurors; and that this has something to do with Condorcet's paradox about cycling majorities, which a couple of people will have half-remembered. Tell me which of these I am missing, which one is actually the load-bearing error, and at exactly what point in the sequence each should be headed off — including the place where the vendor's slide should collapse.

The idea I most want placed well is this: each juror's signal can be treated as part private and part drawn from a common source, and as the common share grows the effective number of independent jurors falls toward one no matter how many heads you count. I want the plan to say where that decomposition goes, what it does to the five-calls-from-one-model picture, and how to convey it without a formula on the board. It should also note, in passing, that when competence varies the sensible weight is roughly the log-odds of each juror's accuracy, so five equally weighted samples from one model are misweighted twice over — and that if the tool ever reports confidence scores rather than verdicts, majority voting is the wrong aggregator regardless.

Give me one question to end on that tells me whether the idea landed rather than whether they enjoyed the session — something like handing them a variant case and asking what the theorem does and does not say about it.

Finally, tell me what to refuse to explain at this level and say so out loud: I assume the asymptotic argument itself, and I suspect the base-rate arithmetic (a checker that is right 90% of the time on a stream where only a small share of items are false will still mostly cry wolf) deserves its own session and should be named and declined rather than squeezed in.
