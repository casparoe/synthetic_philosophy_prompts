I'm a couple years into a philosophy PhD and I keep bumping into the same knot of practices, and I want someone to lay out what they actually commit us to.

The pattern: our handbook requires a dissertation to contain an "original contribution to knowledge". Referee reports reject papers for being unoriginal — the point's already in the literature, we've known that since forever, nothing new here. So novelty is mandatory and re-saying is worthless. But at the same time there are almost no graduate-level textbooks, seminars assign the same primary texts that were being assigned decades ago, nobody can hand you a list of results the field has settled, and the line we give undergrads (and half beleive ourselves) is that the big questions are as open as ever and that what philosophy does is clarify them rather than solve them. Then there's citation practice: a philosophy paper will argue with two or three interlocutors and cite essentially nobody else, even though the whole idea of a novelty requirement is that there's a stock of stuff already said that you're supposed to be adding to.

So: what theory of progress does that bundle presuppose? Is it one theory, or two that don't fit together? Please split progress up instead of treating it as a single thing — something like convergence on verdicts; permanent elimination of positions (an argument kills a view and it stays dead, instead of coming back twenty years later under a new name); questions and distinctions getting better articulated so they become permanent tools everyone uses; and new formal or conceptual machinery. Which of those does the novelty requirement actually need? Which does the no-textbooks-and-same-old-reading-lists part rule out? Can someone who thinks philosophy only progresses in the last two senses still make sense of rejecting a paper for being unoriginal?

I also want the underdetermination bit: which of these practices would several rival theories of progress license equally well, and which strictly commit us to something? And then the counterfactual: if we really believed the strong accumulation picture — a standing stock of results that grows, refutations that are permanent and on record — what would have to change? I'm imagining something like a canonical registry of dead positions, so a referee can just write "this is entry #47, denied", real graduate textbooks layered over the primary texts, and a norm of citing everything relevant rather than only your sparring partners. Is that what the picture implies, or does the picture survive without any of that? And if we went the other way and embraced no-progress-except-clarification, does the original-contribution requirement collapse, or does it survive as something thinner, like a requirement of original formulation?
