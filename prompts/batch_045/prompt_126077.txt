I teach a seminar on the philosophy of mathematics, and for the first session on infinity I would like to hand out something other than an argument: a piece of deadpan promotional copy that I can use diagnostically, since who laughs where will tell me whether a student has actually internalized the machinery or has merely memorized the slogan that some infinities are bigger than others. What I want is the in-room welcome folder of the Hilbert Grand Hotel — that glossy binder a five-star hotel leaves on the writing desk, with short sections, a sidebar, and the earnest second-person voice of luxury hospitality: management regrets, the concierge will be delighted to, for your comfort and safety. About 700 words. The hotel is, of course, the one that is permanently fully occupied and permanently able to accommodate you: the No Vacancy sign is lit and true, and the desk clerk's standing instruction is that the guest in room n is asked to move to room 2n, which frees the odd-numbered rooms. I would like sections along these lines: arrival and room numbering, including the observation that the hotel has exactly as many rooms as it has odd-numbered rooms, and that an establishment which forbade rehousing its guests in a proper part of itself would be a fundamentally different sort of establishment; group bookings, where a single coach with countably many passengers is no trouble and countably many such coaches arriving on the same night are no trouble either, and I want the folder to give the actual device — prime powers or a diagonal enumeration — rather than a wave of the hand, whereas a delegation whose members carry name-badges labelled by the real numbers between 0 and 1 cannot be housed at all, and the manager's explanation of that refusal must be an honest one; dining, with a master list of every possible room-service order and a guest who asks for the meal that differs from the nth meal on the list in the nth respect, which the kitchen both can describe and cannot prepare; the new Ordinal Wing, where the rooms are labelled by ordinals that admit a finite notation, so that there are still only countably many of them and the wing too is always full, but the lift only moves one successor step at a time, so that no guest ever arrives at a room with no immediate predecessor, and the fire drill — each guest proceeds to the room whose number is the immediate predecessor of their own — leaves the occupants of exactly those rooms standing in the corridor; and, finally, the cloakroom and the lamp in the residents' lounge, where the attendant takes in two coats and hands back the lowest-numbered one at the half hour, the quarter hour, and so on, so that at ten the cloakroom is empty although it had been getting fuller at every earlier moment, and where nobody can say whether the lamp is on or off at ten.

Two constraints matter more to me than the jokes. First, every boast in the folder should be true under the standard set-theoretic picture, so that the comedy comes from the mathematics being right rather than from nonsense: a constructivist colleague in the mathematics department will read this, so no step may rely on the axiom of choice, and if one really needs it, the concierge should admit as much in a footnote. Second, the sidebar should be a note from the night auditor, who is a strict finitist, and he must not be a straw man: everything he says should be paraphrasable in the language of the potential infinite, so that he speaks of what he can do for any finite number of guests you care to bring him rather than of completed infinite totalities he is supposed to deny, and his objection should be one a real finitist could sign. Nothing inside the piece should name Cantor, Dedekind, or any theorem; the mathematics should be legible from the hotel's own self-description, and the reader who knows none of it should still be reading a comedy about an absurd hotel rather than a lecture. A delegation from a Society for Strictly Finite Accommodation may picket outside, but their placards should be slogans their position actually licenses.
