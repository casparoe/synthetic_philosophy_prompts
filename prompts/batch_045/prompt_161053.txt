Hey — I keep bumping into the same shape of argument in philosophy discussions and I want to know whether its a known thing with a known name, or whether I'm actually lumping three different things together.

The shape: somebody is defending a claim P against a doubter, and instead of giving evidence for P they point out that the doubter couldn't even get the doubt off the ground without helping themselves to P, or to something sitting right next to it. Three times I've seen it recently:

- In a reading group on skepticism someone said: you can't doubt whether there's an external world, because doubting means asking whether your experiences match how things really are, and that contrast only makes sense if there are things to be right or wrong about. The skeptic in the room said "ok, I can't phrase my doubt inside your vocabulary — that doesn't show me the vocabulary is tracking anything," and everybody felt that reply was a bit lame but also a bit unanswerable.
- A friend argues that every reason for action is really a self-interested one. Someone answered: offering that argument is itself giving us reasons to believe it, and those reasons aren't self-interested ones. My friend said "sure, and? that's just a fact about what I'm doing when I open my mouth."
- Someone says all our thinking is just causation in the brain so nothing is ever really up to us. Reply: then your own argument gives us no reason to accept it either, since reasons would just be effects.

What I keep noticing: (1) the move doesn't seem to hand the doubter a reason to believe P, it hands them a reason to shut up; (2) the person making it then goes on to use P as if it had been established, rather than as if one route to denying it had been blocked off; and (3) the doubter is left in this weird state where they concede they can't press the point and still don't feel answered.

So: is (1), (2) and (3) one phenomenon or three? Does the gap in (1) have a name — the difference between "your denial can't be articulated" and "P is true"? Is there a word for the leftover stance in (3)? And is there a real distinction to draw between cases where the denial literally uses the very thing being denied (the reasons one feels like this to me) and cases where it only uses something in the neighbourhood (the external world one feels like this), or am I just bad at spotting the same trick in a different costume?

Also: how is any of this different from plain circular reasoning, and from just pointing out that someone holds two inconsistent beliefs? People have told me Kant does something like this and I dont really care about the history — I want to know what exactly is going on and where the edges are, so a label on its own won't do. Not after a reading list either.
