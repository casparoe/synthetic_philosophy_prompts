Hey. I'm on the board of a loose network of about sixty small vegetable farms in the same region, all selling into basically the same wholesale market. Prices have been sliding for years and everyone knows the reason: no single farm can say no to a buyer's lowball offer, because if they do someone else takes the order, so everyone says yes. In the office we've started calling it "the Moloch thing" — the usual framing where each person's individually sensible choice makes the whole group worse off and nobody can unilaterally stop making it.

We can realistically push through maybe two fixes this year, so I want the candidate list ranked rather than discussed. Before you rank, two tests I invented and can't find anywhere else, which matter more to me than elegance:

- the churn test: we replace roughly a third of our members every year — retirements, sell-offs, new farms. A mechanism passes if it still holds when a big share of current members never agreed to it, have no reputation with the others yet, and don't expect to be around long.
- the hostage test: a mechanism passes if running it doesn't itself require solving another coordination problem of the same shape. Anything that depends on everyone voluntarily punishing defectors fails, since punishing is costly and everyone would rather someone else did it.

The candidates:

1. A written floor price that everyone signs, with nothing behind it except the signature.
2. Publishing every member's actual sale prices to all the other members each month.
3. A most-favoured-buyer rule: if you sell below the reference price to one buyer, you have to offer that price to every buyer you sell to.
4. A rotating price leader: one member sets the asking price each month and the rest follow it.
5. Membership dues that rise the further below the reference price you sell, so undercutting costs you inside the network.
6. Going at the demand side instead: buyers who jump to whoever was cheapest get moved to the back of the queue next season.
7. A bond you forfeit if you leave the network and then undercut within two years.
8. Stopping selling a commodity at all — collective branding and grading, so farms compete on something other than price.
9. A three-year sunset: the whole arrangement dissolves automatically unless members vote to renew it.

Sort these into tiers — something like 'do this first', 'worth trying', 'probably won't hold', 'likely makes it worse' — with a sentence on why each one lands where it does, and mark for each whether it passes or fails my two tests. If one of the tests is just a bad test, or measures the wrong thing, say so and say what you'd swap it for; I'd rather that than a tidy answer that takes a weak test seriously.

Sections, roughly: "Bottom line" (60 words or so, just the two you'd actually do), "The tiers" (about 250 words, the list itself with your one line per item), "Why a couple of them land where they do" (about 200 words, on whichever two or three are least obvious), and "What would move something between tiers" (about 150 words — which fact about our buyers or our members, if it turned out differently, would reshuffle the list, and where is your reasoning most likely to break down).
