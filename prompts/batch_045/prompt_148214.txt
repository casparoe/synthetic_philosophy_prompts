I'm in the final year of a PhD in philosophy (decision theory and social choice, with a side interest in global priorities research), and I've been asked to write an internal agenda memo for a small foundation that funds work on how to prioritize causes. They have roughly one postdoc-year of conceptual work to allocate over the next two years — call it four short commissioned papers, or one junior researcher's year — and the question put to me is this: which unresolved conceptual questions about cause prioritization itself should a funder commission work on, and in what order? The counterfactual matters, because I have to be able to say why a conceptual question deserves a slot: the alternative use of the same money is another round of empirical reports on particular cause areas.

What I do not want is a survey of the literature or a catalogue of the familiar objections. I already know the standard list: that the importance, tractability, and neglectedness factors are supposed to combine multiplicatively, and that little has been said about why that combination rule is the right one; that tractability and neglectedness both look like ways of getting at marginal returns and may therefore double-count; that the framework is silent about the units in which each factor is measured and about how those units are traded off against one another; that it scores causes one at a time and so says nothing about portfolios, sequencing, or complementarities between causes; and that it yields a ranking without yielding a size, telling you which cause is best but not how much of a fixed budget to commit to it. Assume all of that is on the table. What I want is a defended ordering of a subset of these questions, plus questions I have not listed, with the ordering argued for rather than asserted.

Three half-formed thoughts of my own, which I would like ranked against the others or dismissed outright:

First, a granularity worry. The score a cause receives seems to depend on how finely the space of causes is carved up: subdividing a cause can make each part look more neglected and less tractable without anything changing in the world, which suggests the framework has no principled level of application. Is this a genuine defect or a confusion about what the framework is for, and is settling it upstream of the double-counting worry?

Second, a shape-of-the-return-curve worry. If we treat value produced in a cause as a function of resources devoted to it, the three factors look like a local, first-order summary of that function. But the questions people actually use the framework to answer concern how much to fund, and those may depend on the global shape of the curve — thresholds, lumpiness, regions of increasing returns — which a local summary discards. Is the worthwhile project here repairing the framework or replacing it with something like an explicit funding-return curve, and which of the standard objections would then simply evaporate?

Third, a reading worry: whether the framework is best understood as a claim about what makes a cause a good one in the first place, or as a heuristic for allocating a fixed budget under severe uncertainty. My suspicion is that several of the standard disputes are really disputes between these two readings, and that separating them dissolves some apparent problems while making others considerably harder.

For each of the four or five questions you put at the top, I want: what it is upstream of, in the sense that settling it would change the answer to other questions or change an actual allocation decision; who could settle it within the budget, given that a philosopher working alone is a different prospect from a project needing empirical input the funder is not buying; what an unexciting answer would look like, since conceptual work often resolves a question in a way that leaves practice untouched; and what argument or evidence in the next two years should move it up or down the list. I also want you to name two or three items on the standard list you would defund, with the reason — effectively settled already, too dependent on empirical contingencies to reward a conceptual paper, or simply not decision-relevant — and to flag where my three thoughts above are confused rather than merely underdeveloped.

One methodological point I want addressed rather than set aside. This whole exercise applies something very like the framework to the question of which questions about the framework to fund: importance for whom, tractability given what background, neglectedness measured against which population of researchers. If the framework cannot be applied reflexively without circularity, say so, and tell me what to use instead for the ordering. Please do not include a bibliography or reading list; I will fill in citations myself.
