Our group ran a field experiment two years ago and got a result we have never explained: an after-school math program raised test scores, but only for pupils who were in the middle of the score distribution at the start — not for the weakest, not for the strongest. We have known this the whole time; it is baked into how we all think about the programme now. Last month a colleague wrote down a model of how the programme works, and the middle-only pattern falls out of it as a consequence. She treats that as strong support for the model.

Two people disagree, for reasons that seem to pull apart. One says the pattern cannot support anything any more: we already knew it, so it is already folded into what we believe, and only a prediction about data we have not yet collected could move us — so we should disregard the fit entirely and wait for the new test we could run next month. The other grants that the fit is evidence but says it is weak evidence, because the model was built backwards from the pattern after we had tried a number of variants, and we would have been far more impressed had we found the same model in a drawer before looking at the data.

The first complaint sounds like a general point about evidence and belief; the second sounds like a point about the order in which we happened to find things out, which feels as though it should not matter to whether the model is true. Are these really two different complaints, and is either of them right? Trying to settle it has also exposed a further disagreement: how likely we would have said the middle-only pattern was beforehand — which my colleagues answer differently, and which seems to decide how much the fit is worth either way.
