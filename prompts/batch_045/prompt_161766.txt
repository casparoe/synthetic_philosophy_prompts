I want a decomposed credence on a question about insect pest control, and I want the machinery shown rather than a number handed over with a shrug.

The setup, stipulated so we don't re-litigate it: a decade-long malaria vector-control program in a high-transmission region — indoor residual spraying plus larval source management. Take the epidemiology as given. The program averts a large number of human deaths, mostly children under five, plus a much larger number of non-fatal cases, and it causes no meaningful off-target ecological damage. I am not asking about the human term, about cost-effectiveness in the usual sense, or about resistance evolution. I am asking only about the insect-welfare term.

The proposition: take a symmetric welfarist who counts the valenced states of the target insects — adult Anopheles and their aquatic juveniles — at whatever moral weight she assigns them, and show her only what the program does to those insects. Her judgment of that term is positive.

Two cases need separating, and I want both numbers. If the true moral weight of these insects is zero, the term is empty rather than positive, so score that branch as a no and give me the unconditional figure; then give me a second figure conditional on the weight being non-negligible. Say which one you think is the more useful object.

Among the terms I want decomposed, at minimum: whether the insects have valenced states of non-trivial moral weight at all; the sign of the average life of a mosquito in the target population, given larval competition, starvation, desiccation, failed host-seeking and predation; whether a death caused by the program is on net a harm, via deprivation of future positive welfare, or on net a benefit, via aversion of a life that would have gone badly; how large the disutility of the dying process itself is relative to a whole life, which is where the mode of action matters — pyrethroid convulsions versus an insect growth regulator versus a Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis larvicide are not the same event; whether suppression actually lowers the equilibrium number of insect-years lived or is largely offset by density-dependent rebound, in which case the welfare action is in how the insects die rather than how many of them do; and, meta-level, how much of your number is driven by irreducible cluelessness rather than by anything you take yourself to know. Add terms you think belong and drop any you think are inert.

The headcount point matters to me and I want it addressed explicitly rather than waved at. The affected populations are astronomically large, so the sign of the aggregate is governed by the sign of the mean with essentially no variance buffer: a small shift in your estimate of the mean welfare of a mosquito life flips the sign of a term involving trillions of lives. Tell me whether that pushes you toward a near-0.5 report of cluelessness or whether you think the mean is estimable well enough to support a confident number. If the honest answer is that we cannot tell, say so and show me which term is generating the cluelessness — do not manufacture confidence to fill the section.

For calibration, give me the break-even: how many insect-hours of moderate pain per under-five death averted would it take for the insect term to flip sign, state explicitly the insect-to-human welfare weight ratio you used to get there, and show how the break-even moves across the range of weight ratios you consider live. I suspect most of the disagreement in this area is a disagreement about that ratio wearing other clothes.

Then the practical question. Rank the plausible intervention types — neurotoxic adulticides, insect growth regulators, biological larvicides, sterile-male release, gene-drive population replacement that leaves population size roughly intact while rendering the vector refractory to the parasite — by expected insect-welfare profile, with the caveat that slower modes of action may trade a lower-intensity death for a longer one. And tell me whether the availability of a non-lethal replacement route dissolves the whole debate: if replacement delivers the same epidemiological benefit as suppression, then the policy question was never whether to control the vector but which method to use, and the insect-welfare literature has been arguing about the wrong variable. I want a straight answer about how much that reframing takes the wind out of the original proposition.

On the spread: I am at about 0.45 on the unconditional figure. One person I argue with sits near 0.15 and another near 0.8, and both of them grant the insects non-trivial moral weight, so their disagreement is not the sentience question. Diagnose where it actually lives and tell me which of the two has the better case on the crux as you see it.

Finally, name the two or three findings or arguments that would move your number most, with a rough magnitude for each.
