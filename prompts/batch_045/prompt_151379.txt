I'm a PhD student in machine learning, and my group is currently designing the supervision stack for a long-horizon coding-and-research agent — one that plans over many steps, calls tools, writes and executes patches, and whose individual actions we cannot evaluate in isolation. The standing internal argument is between outcome supervision (grade the final artifact: the test suite, the diff, the human preference judgment on the end result) and process supervision (assign reward at the level of the agent's intermediate reasoning and individual tool calls). Every few meetings our safety lead settles the argument with the same analogy, and it has started to do real work in our design documents, so I want it audited properly rather than conceded.

The analogy comes from health services research: the classic quality-of-care framework from Avedis Donabedian's 1966 paper, which sorts measures of quality into structural, process, and outcome measures. His point of attack is that outcome measures — mortality, readmission, complication rates — are lagging, rare, delayed, confounded by case mix, and expensive to collect, whereas process measures — was the prophylactic antibiotic given within the hour, did the surgical team complete the checklist, was the discharge summary filed — are cheap, immediate, attributable to a specific actor, and actionable. Medicine therefore leaned on process measures as the operative instrument of quality assurance, validating them against outcome data where it could and falling back on process where outcome data was unavailable or too noisy to see a signal in. Our safety lead's version: outcome supervision is our mortality rate — lagging, sparse, and confounded by task difficulty — so we should demote it to a periodic calibration signal and build the actual supervision stack out of step-level reward on the agent's trace, exactly as quality assurance moved to process measures.

I want an audit of that analogy, on the assumption that it is doing more work than it can carry, and I want my own diagnosis tested rather than echoed. My three hypotheses, which I'd like you to accept, reject, or sharpen:

First, a calibration inversion. In medicine a process measure is trustworthy only because it has been tied, painstakingly and in advance, to an outcome record that is collected independently of the measurement: mortality is death, and it is not the hospital that decides whether it happened. The credibility of the checklist flows downstream from that exogenous outcome data. But the premise of scalable oversight is precisely that our outcome evaluation is *not* trustworthy — that the judge is weaker than the system, the reward is a proxy, and the horizon is too long for outcomes to be legible. So the analogy seems to license process measures exactly in the regime where medicine could not have had them, and to be idle in the regime where it could. If that is right, the transfer is self-undermining rather than merely imperfect.

Second, a constitutive-versus-diagnostic confusion. A process measure in medicine reads a process that exists independently of being measured; recording whether hands were washed does not produce the handwashing. Step-level supervision of a trained model does not merely read the trace, it reshapes it: the same signal is simultaneously our instrument for detecting misaligned reasoning and a gradient that determines what reasoning the system will learn to produce. So the instrument perturbs the measured quantity, in the specific direction of looking better to the instrument, and measured process quality can rise while the evidential value of the trace falls. I do not think the medical frame has anything corresponding to this, and I suspect it is the single most load-bearing disanalogy.

Third, a missing adversarial term. Medicine's measured unit is a clinician who shares the measurer's aim, cannot revisit the same decision ten million times, and cannot rewrite its own reasoning to score better on the chart review. Our measured unit is an optimizer running against the measure directly, at a scale and iteration speed with no counterpart in a hospital, and the outcome signal it is optimizing is not exogenous death but a target we specified and, increasingly, a model we learned. I take this to be the reason the analogy's conclusion feels safe in the source domain and unsafe in ours, but I want the argument made precisely rather than asserted.

There is also a distinction my group has started using that I would like you to place inside the medical frame, since I think the analogy scores differently on all three. We have been separating three uses of process supervision: *diagnostic* use, where the trace is read after the fact to detect misalignment and nothing is trained on it; *constitutive* use, where step-level reward is the training signal and the trace is what the gradient shapes; and *gatekeeping* use, where a runtime monitor inspects the stated reasoning before an action executes and blocks actions whose justification fails a check. My guess is that diagnostic use maps cleanly onto retrospective chart review, constitutive use onto a quality-improvement intervention that changes clinical practice (with all the usual questions about whether measured compliance rose while care did not), and gatekeeping use onto something like prior authorization or a hard stop in the electronic health record — an analogy that carries its own known failure modes of workaround, alert fatigue, and treating to satisfy the rule rather than the patient. Tell me whether that placement is right and, more importantly, which of the three the Donabedian analogy can actually be used to recommend; I suspect at most one of them.
