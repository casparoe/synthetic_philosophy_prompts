I'm a third-year undergraduate and I TA one discussion section of our department's "Minds, Animals and Machines" course — 24 students, mostly mixed majors (a lot of psych and CS, a few philosophy minors), one 75-minute section, a room with movable chairs and a whiteboard but no screens, and a budget of essentially nothing. We've had one lecture on the four-fold hierarchy of kinds of minds — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian and Gregorian creatures — and I don't think it landed. Half the section came away thinking the hierarchy is just a scale of more-and-more intelligence, and the other half thinks the top level means "the smart ones use tools." The thing I want them to feel instead is that each transition is a change in *where selection happens and what does the dying*: genetic lineages across generations, an individual's own behaviour within a lifetime, a hypothesis inside one head that is allowed to die in the agent's place, and then, at the top, something genuinely different in kind — a creature whose mind is largely built out of inherited objects (words, marks, procedures) that were shaped by their own transmissibility and need not have been good for the creature carrying them. I tried a version of this last term where I just had teams solve a maze on the floor, and it degenerated into everyone shouting instructions at each other; I now think that degeneration *is* the point, but I couldn't get anyone to see it.

So: design me a runnable in-class game for that section, with rules and materials, that gets them to run into the differences before they are named. The facilitator should talk for under five minutes up front and must not use the four labels until the debrief. I'd like distinct stations or roles whose permitted *operations* differ rather than whose difficulty differs: a Darwinian cohort that must commit to a fixed policy in writing before receiving any feedback and can only change it by dying and being re-seeded with a mutated copy; a Skinnerian cohort that may revise only after a payoff has actually landed; a Popperian cohort with a deliberately scarce budget of "probe" tokens that let them test a hypothesis without acting in the world; and a Gregorian cohort that inherits a written lore-packet from a previous generation, may invent notation, and then has what it invented copied forward through a noisy, time-pressured transcription step. Give me the hidden world they're all operating on (I was imagining a small grid with a stable hazard and one rule that changes between generations, so that inherited lore can go stale and a second generation can be actively hurt by a tool that used to work), the scoring system — points or candy, no money, no grade stakes — a minute-by-minute timeline for 75 minutes, and a rotation so that everyone plays at least two kinds. Please also build in two probes I've been sketching and tell me what each one's outcome would actually show: a fifth station of "solitary Popperians," who can form and test hypotheses internally but are forbidden to communicate them, to isolate whether the Gregorian leap is about language or about accumulation; and one round in which the new generation of Gregorians must follow the inherited lore for a full round before they are allowed to test it, so they feel what it is like to be steered by an instrument they did not choose.

Then the debrief, which is where I most need help: a mapping table from specific moments in play to specific features of the hierarchy, plus the resistances I should surface rather than smooth over — "we were doing Popperian things the whole time even when the rules said we weren't," "the Gregorian win is just cheating," "isn't a Skinnerian with a good memory simply a Popperian," "why does the hierarchy have four rungs and not forty" — and an honest note on what will predictably go wrong: Popperians burning their probes immediately, Gregorians hoarding lore, a world easy enough that all four kinds converge, one confident student who games the constraints, and the deeper problem that participants cannot switch off their own language and culture, so the rules are only stand-ins for design constraints and the activity cannot reproduce what it is like to *lack* a Gregorian mind. Say plainly which part of the idea the game cannot deliver and the facilitator has to state in words afterwards. Please don't rely on any particular author's exact wording or on anyone's having done the reading — build it so it works from the ideas alone. Finish with a last line beginning "Summary:" that states, in one sentence, the single thing the session is supposed to make the students discover.
