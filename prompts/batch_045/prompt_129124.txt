Imagine the following fictional but scientifically coherent scenario. In the 2040s cosmologists converge on what they call diachronic fine-tuning: precision data show that a handful of the constants of physics have drifted slightly over cosmic history, and that each drift increased the chemical complexity the universe could carry. The pattern is far too coordinated to be chance, and nothing in known physics explains it. Two readings come to dominate the discussion. On the providential reading, a single transcendent mind has repeatedly intervened to steer the cosmos toward life and reason. On the simulation reading, our universe is software running in a physically different parent universe, and the adjustments are the work of programmers who are themselves finite creatures within it.

Suppose Isaac Newton, having been brought up to date on the relevant cosmology and having accepted the data as genuine, is asked to adjudicate. Take for granted what his own theology actually committed him to: that the laws of nature are contingent decrees of a sovereign will rather than necessary truths; that God rules the world as a lord governs a dominion rather than as a soul animates a body, and so is not to be identified with the world or with space; that a created being worshipped as God is an idol, and that Nicene Trinitarianism was a late corruption of primitive Christianity; that inert matter cannot explain gravity's action, which is why, as Leibniz objected, the world-system periodically stands in need of reformation; and that the providential history of churches and empires can be dated from the books of Daniel and Revelation, with the final renovation of the world still centuries away.

How, given those commitments, would Newton be likely to receive each of the two readings? Which would he reject as impious, and which, if either, could he accept — and would he treat it as confirming his own argument from design, or as a fresh instance of the errors he diagnosed in Trinitarian and mechanical philosophy? In particular, how would he classify the programmers of the simulation reading: as creatures owing worship to none, as rival gods of the sort Scripture condemns, or as entities whose existence he would decline to pronounce on at all? Explain where his verdict would fail to coincide with that of present-day theists who invoke fine-tuning, since the answer should turn on his specific theology rather than on generic design reasoning.
