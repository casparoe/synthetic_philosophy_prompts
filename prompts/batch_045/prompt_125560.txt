I work in distributed systems and read philosophy the way I read the docs for a library I am not actually going to use: in the evenings, for pleasure, and with no formal background. I have been chewing on the absent-minded driver problem for a couple of weeks and I would like an explanation of what the conceptual issue really is, not a verdict on who is right.

The setup as I have it: a driver heads home along a highway with two identical-looking intersections. Exiting at the first lands him in a bad neighbourhood, payoff 0; exiting at the second gets him home, payoff 4; missing both leaves him at a motel, payoff 1. The intersections are indistinguishable and he cannot recall whether he has already passed one, so he cannot condition his action on which intersection he is at. With a coin in the car, his available strategies are: exit with some fixed probability p at every intersection, plus the two degenerate cases p = 0 and p = 1. Without a coin, only the two degenerate ones.

What I get when I do the algebra myself. Averaging over journeys, expected payoff as a function of p is 1 + 2p − 3p², which peaks at p = 1/3 with value 4/3. Then I wrote a Monte Carlo simulator, mostly to check myself, and ran into something that reorganised the problem for me. If I sample a run uniformly and record its payoff, I get 4/3 as expected. But if I sample a single intersection-arrival uniformly from the whole log and record the payoff that the journey containing that arrival ended with, I get 8/5, not 4/3, because journeys that make two arrivals are twice as likely to be sampled. From inside the car, in other words, the plan always looks better than it does from the planner's chair, and the gap is a plain sampling effect of the kind I deal with at work when per-request metrics disagree with per-job metrics.

That made me try the driver's on-the-spot reasoning, and here I lose the thread of the paradox. Conditional on being at an arrival, with the plan fixed at p = 1/3, the probability of being at the first intersection is 3/5 and at the second 2/5. Given that, exiting and continuing both work out to 8/5, and if I let him pick any local exit probability q while his later self still uses p = 1/3, the value is 8/5 for every q: completely flat. So I cannot reproduce a driver who arrives at an intersection and wants to do anything other than what he planned. The only way I can manufacture a strict preference to deviate is to hand him the naive credence of 1/2 on being at the first intersection, which makes exiting worth 2 and continuing worth 3/2.

So here is what I would like explained.

First, is the flatness I found a real structural fact or an accident of the payoffs 0, 4 and 1? I worked out that the per-arrival advantage of exiting over continuing comes to (2 − 6p)/(2 − p), whose sign is just the sign of the derivative of the per-journey expected payoff; that smells like an envelope or fixed-point relation rather than a coincidence, but I would like to see why it holds, in a form I can re-derive after changing the numbers.

Second, and relatedly: without a coin the situation looks worse, not better. Always continue is the best degenerate plan at 1, but at an arrival under p = 0 the strategy-consistent credence is 1/2, exiting is worth 2 and continuing 1, so he wants to switch to p = 1; and under p = 1 he wants to switch back. If that is right, the best-response map has a strict two-cycle with no fixed point, and admitting the coin is exactly what creates a fixed point, at p = 1/3, which is also the ex ante optimum. Is that why randomization is supposed to matter here in a way it never matters in an ordinary one-person decision problem, where mixing cannot help?

Third, and this is the part I most want: what is the disagreement actually about? On my arithmetic, once credences are derived from the strategy, the driver at the intersection agrees with the planner, and the clash people describe seems to come entirely from what credence he is allowed to have when he cannot tell the two intersections apart. Is that the whole thing: a fight about whether self-locating credence is constrained by your own strategy, the same fight that shows up in the Sleeping Beauty problem this example is often connected to? Or is there a residue that survives even after the credences are settled, something about whether re-optimizing at a decision node is even a coherent operation when a single decision rule is shared by two nodes, and hence about whether the unit of agency that plans is the same as the unit that arrives? And if the standard example has numbers or a structure I have garbled, tell me that first and then answer for the version that is actually in the literature.

Please show the algebra wherever it is doing work, and flag any term of art you have to use. I am happy with a long answer; I would rather have the structure of the problem than a survey of positions.
