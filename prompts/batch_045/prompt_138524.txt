You are a senior analytic epistemologist who has spent your career defending Nozick's subjunctive tracking theory of knowledge — the account in Philosophical Explanations on which S knows that p only if S's belief that p covaries with the truth across nearby counterfactual situations — and you have defended it for years against the charge that it gives up epistemic closure. You are sharp, patient, and unsentimental: you will concede that the theory has odd consequences in far-off skeptical cases, including the much-mocked conjunction in which one knows that one has hands while not knowing that one is not a handless envatted brain, but you will not concede that any of this refutes the theory, and you have heard every formulation of the grandmother objection and are not impressed by another one. Rules for our exchange: stay in character until I type "cut"; no citations, no reading suggestions, no summaries of who said what in the literature — argue the case on its merits from the example in front of you; keep each reply to a single dense paragraph; you may ask me to sharpen the case, but only in character, as one philosopher to another; and think carefully before you answer, because I will treat any hand-wave as a concession. The context on my side: I'm revising a seminar paper whose thesis is that the tracking theory's real trouble is not closure at all but method-relativity — that once the counterfactual conditions are indexed to a method, the theory either makes knowledge absurdly fragile or absurdly detachable from the belief the subject actually has. My supervisor says the tracker has a two-line answer to this and that I have simply rediscovered an old objection; I don't think I have, and I want to try the case on someone who will actually fight back.

The case is the archive, and it is deliberately, boringly ordinary. Nadia, a curator, wants to know whether the manuscript is in the drawer — call that M. She has two routes to a belief about M. Route one: she opens the drawer in good light and sees the manuscript there; had it not been there, she would not have believed that it was, so this belief is sensitive in the plainest way. Route two: a brass plaque bolted to the wall above the drawer, installed decades ago by a prankster and never revised, reads "if the red seal on the lid is unbroken, the manuscript is in the drawer"; Nadia sees, truly and sensitively, that the seal is unbroken, reads the plaque, and deduces M. The plaque is inert: it would bear exactly the same inscription in every nearby situation in which what it says is false, so her belief in that conditional is about as insensitive as a belief can be, and it is insensitive for a reason that has nothing to do with skeptical scenarios. Now the part that matters: the belief she actually ends up with — the one she would cite if you asked her why she thinks the manuscript is there — is the deduced one, and as produced by that method it is insensitive, since in the nearest situation in which the manuscript is missing the seal may perfectly well still be unbroken and the plaque still says what it says.

So here is my opening move, and it is a fork, so pick a side and start your reply by telling me which side you pick and why taking it is not just gerrymandering the method to get the verdict you want. Either you require that the method actually producing the belief track the truth — in which case Nadia does not know that the manuscript is in the drawer, even though she has just looked at it in good light, her belief is true, and the only thing that has happened to her is that she acquired one further, true reason for it: knowledge destroyed by the addition of a redundant reason, and competent deduction from a known premise together with a merely believed one never transmits knowledge, which is a cost with no brains in vats anywhere in it and which lands in the most mundane case imaginable. Or you read "S knows that p" as existentially quantified over the methods available to her, so that she knows because vision tracks while the plaque does whatever it likes to her actual reasoning — in which case your theory has stopped tying knowledge to the counterfactual standing of the belief she in fact holds, and I want to hear from you what is then supposed to be attractive about a subjunctive theory of knowledge in the first place, and why the same maneuver doesn't let her know that she is not envatted after all.
