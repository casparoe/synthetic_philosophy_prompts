Hey — hoping you can sharpen (or just settle) an argument that's been going around my reading group. We have this dumb hobby where we invent fake countries and then try to litigate their constitutional crises, and the current one has us properly stuck.

The Free City of Marrowgate adopted its Charter in 1948, right after a nasty civic breakdown, and the founders were obsessed with making sure the place couldn't be hijacked by a temporary majority. All the amendment machinery is in §44: the Charter may be amended only by a resolution of two-thirds of the entire Assembly, ratified by a majority of the cantons, and the last sentence of §44 says that no amendment may alter §44 itself. Then this happens. In 1974 the Assembly — properly, by two-thirds of the entire Assembly plus cantonal ratification — amends §*45*, an ordinary provision, so that "two-thirds of the entire Assembly" is from now on defined as two-thirds of the members present and voting, quorum being forty. Nobody at the time thought this was about amendment at all; it was sold as a fix for absenteeism. In 1979, using the new counting rule, they add a §44A: "notwithstanding §44, any provision other than this section may be amended by three-fifths of the members present and voting, without cantonal ratification." In 1986, under §44A, they repeal §44 outright and re-enact §44A as the sole amending provision, now at a simple majority of those present and voting, with no entrenchment of itself. By 1990 the Assembly can rewrite the Charter with a show of hands, and it does: cantons abolished, its own term extended to seven years, and the Constitutional Chamber stripped of jurisdiction over amendment questions. After the 1994 election a reformist majority wants the 1948 settlement back and asks the Chamber to declare the 1974–1979–1986 chain void.

One of my friends says this is easy and I'm being mystical: every step complied with whatever rule was in force at the moment it was taken, so the end state is valid, and that's all there is to check — call it *stepwise validity*. I'm sure that's wrong but I can't put my finger on where. The 1974 step in particular looks completely innocent in isolation (surely a charter is allowed to define its own supermajorities?), yet it's obviously load-bearing in the chain, and if you void it you also void a bunch of mundane statutes passed under the new counting rule that nobody objects to. Please argue my side: that the Chamber should hold 1986 void, and that dismantling an entrenched amendment rule in steps is no better than doing it in one step. A few things I'd especially like you to deal with: where exactly the defect lives — is 1974 already tainted by what it was *for*, or is it clean and the rot starts in 1979, or only at 1986? what principled test separates a legitimate two-step amendment (changing the counting rule and then using it to pass an ordinary statute seems totally fine) from an illegitimate one that just walks around §44's last sentence? why the entrenchment in that last sentence should be read as constraining the *path* the system can take and not just the *destination*? and, awkwardly, why a Chamber whose jurisdiction was abolished by the very amendments it's being asked to strike down gets to decide this at all. Please argue it from the structure of the problem rather than from comparative law — I know real courts somewhere have wrassled with "unconstitutional amendments," but we're trying to do it from principle here, so don't reach for cases or citations.
