I'm finishing a dissertation chapter on diachronic coherence, and I keep failing to write down a deference principle for future selves that matches what I actually think case by case. The textbook statement of van Fraassen's Reflection says that if P is my credence function now, Pt my credence function at a later time t, and r a value such that P(Pt(A) = r) > 0, then I should set P(A | Pt(A) = r) = r. My working repair adds two provisos: (a) the principle applies only when I expect to lose no evidence between now and t, and (b) it applies only when I cannot now identify any specific defect in the process that will produce Pt(A). Proviso (a) is my patch for forgetting cases, (b) my patch for impairment cases. I don't think either is right, and I don't think the two together are enough. Here are my verdicts, with the cases given concretely.

1. Conference dinner. I know I will be slipped a drug that reliably induces 90 percent confidence in whatever the last person to speak to me asserts, and I know a flat-earther is seated next to me. My anticipated credence in the earth's being flat is 0.9. Verdict: I stay where I am. No deference.
2. Collaborator's measurement. A collaborator emails me tomorrow the reading of a calibrated instrument; conditional on a positive reading I will be at 0.85 on H, conditional on a negative reading at 0.15, and I am 50/50 on which reading I will get. Verdict: conditional deference; I should now be at 0.5.
3. Forgotten proof. I have checked a proof myself and am at 0.95 on the lemma. I know I will lose the notebook in a move and forget the details, retaining only the memory that I once checked something in this vicinity, and that I will then be at 0.6. Verdict: I stay at 0.95 while the proof is in front of me. No deference, even though I can specify exactly what will be lost.
4a. Bad argument I can already dissect. At Thursday's seminar I will hear an argument for Q that I have already seen in the slide deck and can see now to be an instance of base-rate neglect; I know I will be carried along and land at 0.8. Verdict: no deference.
4b. Argument I now think is bad, but on an argument-type where my track record of diagnosing badness is poor (I have been wrong about 60 percent of the time). Same structure; my anticipated credence is again 0.8. Verdict: partial deference, to roughly 0.6. This is the pair I can't justify: 4a and 4b look structurally identical and I treat them differently, and the only difference I can point to is a reliability judgment I cannot cash out in the formalism.
5. Fission with divergent evidence. I will be scanned; a fair coin is then flipped. On heads, both successors are shown a red light; on tails, one is shown red and the other blue. Each successor knows the setup and updates on the light it sees, so the red successor will be at 2/3 on heads and the blue successor at 0. Verdict: I should now be at 0.5 — the weighted mixture across successors — and Reflection applied separately to each successor seems to demand something incoherent. I suspect the principle has to quantify over expected future credence rather than over each possible future credence, but I can't see what that does to the rest of the cases.
6. Abandoned vocabulary. After a year inside a research group that has reformulated the whole framework, I will no longer have credences over the algebra in which A is stated; A will not be expressible in my later idiolect. Verdict: I don't know what deference even means here, but I am unhappy with the answer that the requirement simply lapses.
7. Unauditable model. I will run a forecasting model I cannot now audit, whose track record on analogous questions is well calibrated; my anticipated credence in A is 0.6, my current credence 0.35. Verdict: full deference to 0.6, even though by clause (b) I cannot certify the process as defect-free.
8. Invertible distortion. I know that at altitude my credences are reliably over-extreme in a way I have measured: distances from 0.5 are scaled by 1.4. I anticipate a future credence of 0.86 in A. Verdict: defer, but to the de-biased value (about 0.76), not to 0.86. Contrast with case 1, where the distortion is real but I have no model of it that I can invert.
9. The case I keep changing my mind about. I anticipate that a year in a different methodological tradition will change how much I take observational data to support causal claims; no new first-order evidence, no impairment, but my confirmation standards shift, and my credence in X causes Y will fall from 0.7 to 0.4. Verdict: none. I go back and forth weekly.

Please repair the principle, or replace it. I want: a precise statement of the best candidate (or two candidates if they trade off), each clause tied to the case that motivates it, honest verdicts on my nine cases including the ones the candidate cannot accommodate, verdicts on cases I haven't listed, and a verdict on case 9 with your residual uncertainty stated. I also want to know whether what you end up with is a requirement of rationality or a defeasible heuristic, and, if you can say briefly, which of the standard arguments for Reflection survives the repairs.
