I serve on the seven-member board of a regional domestic-violence shelter; I'm the member who looks after the finances, and I have no legal or philosophical training. I've been asked to draft the recommendation memo that goes out to the rest of the board before Thursday's emergency meeting, and I'm stuck on something that I think is more of a puzzle about language than about employment practice. Our executive director — who founded the organisation eighteen years ago — sent the board chair a message at 1:40 a.m. last Saturday that read, in full, "Please accept this as formal notice: I resign as Executive Director, effective immediately. I can't keep carrying this." She had just come off a week in which one of our advocates was threatened and our overnight line lost its funding. At 8:15 a.m. she called the chair and said she hadn't meant to resign, that she had recorded a tearful voice note to herself on her phone late at night, that an AI writing tool on that phone had turned the note into the message and emailed it to the chair without her reviewing it, and that she in particular never wrote or said "effective immediately." The chair had replied "Acknowledged — thank you for everything" at 7:02 a.m., before the retraction, and two board members had already begun quietly sounding out interim candidates. Our part-time lawyer says the Employment Standards legislation is silent here and that our bylaws are the only positive rule we have: they state that "a resignation is effective upon receipt by the Board Chair and may not be withdrawn without Board consent," with no definition of what resigning is. So the board has to decide what counts.

The board is split three-and-three, with our seventh member away, and both halves have said things that sound right to me. One half says the bylaw is a bright line: the words arrived, the chair received them, and an institution cannot be asked to investigate what people privately meant at 1 a.m., because anyone could unsay anything the morning after. The other half says a message the director never read, that added a phrase she never used, that she disowned within seven hours, is not her resigning at all but something that happened to her — like a signature forged by a pen. I've been thinking about this on my own since a friend argued over dinner that saying "I resign" is just reporting a fact about your intentions, the same kind of thing as saying "I'm tired," so of course it can't take effect until the board accepts it. That feels wrong to me, but I can't say why: "I resign" seems to me much more like making a promise or saying "I do" in a wedding than like a report, and yet I also think a promise mumbled in a fever is different from one made at a desk. I'd also like the memo to settle a forward-looking question, because this will happen again: should we amend the bylaws to require some confirmation step for any resignation or other formal notice that a member or staff member sends through an AI tool or an assistant, and if so what exactly should the amendment say.

Please draft the memo I can circulate, addressed to the board and under 900 words — our lawyer will read it but will not rewrite it, and both factions have to be able to see their own worry taken seriously in it. I want a committed document, not a survey: give a definite recommendation with your reasons, treat the strongest version of the case for the opposite outcome honestly enough that its proponents would recognise it, and tell us plainly where the board is making a value call that no amount of conceptual clarification settles, and what single new fact would most change your verdict. After the memo, add a JSON object with exactly these fields so I can paste it into our minutes: "recommendation" (one sentence, the bottom line); "office_status" (one sentence stating whether the Executive Director role is vacant, and from what point); "next_steps" (an array of objects, each with "action", "owner", "deadline"); "decision_rule" (two or three sentences stating the general test the board should use, in future, for whether a communication counts as the act of resigning — written so a future board of lay people could apply it without any theory); "rule_applied" (an array of objects, each with "condition" — the name of one requirement in your test — "finding" — met, not met, or contested — and "reason", one sentence); "strongest_case_against" (two or three sentences); "why_it_loses" (one or two sentences); "bylaw_amendment" (the wording you recommend the board adopt, or a null if you recommend none); "confidence" (an integer from 0 to 100 that this is the right call); and "confidence_basis" (two or three sentences on why that number and what would move it).
