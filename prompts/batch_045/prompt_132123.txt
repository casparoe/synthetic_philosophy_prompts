Bit of an odd request, but here goes. I'm retired now — thirty-odd years in atmospheric radiation and satellite remote sensing, an unhealthy amount of that spent staring at radiance time series — and a few of us from the old institute started a philosophy reading group a couple of years back, which is how I've ended up reading epistemology at my age. Last week we got onto the luminosity thesis and the KK principle, and I have to admit the word "luminosity" bugged me for the first half hour, since in my old line of work it means something perfectly measurable; in this literature it apparently means something like: whenever you're in a given mental state, you're in a position to know that you're in it. The argument against it that ate most of the session was the sorites-flavoured one: you line up a long series of cases running from freezing-cold at dawn to perfectly warm by noon, each one differing from the last by an amount nobody could notice, and then you add the margin-for-error requirement that if you're in a position to know you're cold in one case, you must actually still be cold in the neighbouring case. Since you're plainly not cold at the warm end, there has to be some case where you're cold but not in a position to know it. I half think I follow it and half think somebody's pulled a fast one.

So: could you write me a joke about all this? The setting I have in mind is my camper van on a field site at a stupid hour of the morning — me, an old colleague from the lab, and the junky two-stage thermostat on the wall that I've nicknamed Kay-Kay, on account of it being a double K — and the joke should turn on the actual machinery rather than just the vibe of it: the slow drift across neighbouring cases, the margin-for-error requirement, and/or the KK principle's claim that knowing p puts you in a position to know that you know p, and so on up the ladder. What I specifically don't want is "ha ha, the philosopher can't tell whether he's cold," which would land just as well for someone who'd never heard of any of this. The punchline should be the sort of thing that makes my reading group groan precisely because they have seen the argument; a reader with no background should get maybe two-thirds of it and sense that the rest is a reference to something.

One more thing, in case it's useful as a seed: at the session I floated a bit of an engineering-style fix, which I called the deadband patch — you stop demanding one sharp cut-off between "cold" and "not cold," the way a real thermostat leaves a gap between the temperature at which the heater clicks on and the temperature at which it clicks off again. Feel free to have a character try that as a way of escaping the argument, and to have it come apart for a reason that anyone who's actually followed the reasoning would recognise. Keep it to a single joke, a couple hundred words at most, and nothing that requires looking anything up to write.
