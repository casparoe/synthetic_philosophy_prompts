I write about science and ideas, and I'm doing a piece on the Anthropocene — but not another is-it-real piece, I've read a dozen of those and they all say the same three things. What I can't get straight is the plumbing. So far as I can piece it together: to start a new epoch officially, nobody votes on the idea itself. You pick one layer in one place — a lake bed, a coral, an ice core — and a marker goes into that layer, and a fairly small committee of stratigraphers ratifies that spot as the definition of the boundary for the entire planet. The criteria for choosing the spot are oddly practical. Sedimentation has to be continuous with no change in the kind of deposit. The marker has to be datable by radiometric methods and traceable in sections of the same age on other continents. And the site has to be usable: reachable from an international airport, on roads, protected so it won't be built over, open to researchers of any nationality. The Holocene's own start, I gather, is fixed in a Greenland ice core rather than a cliff face. Meanwhile half the people who use the word Anthropocene — historians, climate people, activists, the odd lawyer — mean something like the time when we became a geological force, and get visibly annoyed when the answer they get back is a date and a lake in Ontario.

So here's what I actually want from you. Treat that ratification procedure as a practice with a philosophy inside it, and reconstruct it. What must a historical period be for this to be the right way to fix when one begins? My guesses, which I want you to clean up or demolish: that a period is a material thing with a synchronous physical onset; that the onset is discoverable in one place and correlatable everywhere; that units of geological time are found rather than made, even though the choice of marker is obviously conventional and settled by a vote; that a period has one defining process rather than being a composite of regional ones; and that a name for a period is legitimate only if it's anchored to something in the record.

Then, the parts I care most about:

Which of those are strictly presupposed by the procedure, and which are just things it happens to sit well with? The airport requirement, for instance, doesn't look like a metaphysical commitment, but the synchrony one might be. Don't flatten that difference.

Is the practice consistent with itself? It talks as if epochs are out there in nature waiting to be demarcated, but the actual rules read like engineering constraints on a measuring instrument, and the decision is made by ballot. Is that a contradiction, or just what any operationalization looks like?

The diachrony problem. The candidate onsets people float are centuries apart: bomb radionuclides in the mid-twentieth century, an atmospheric CO2 minimum around 1610 that's been tied to the die-off of Indigenous populations in the Americas and the regrowth of forest on abandoned land, much earlier clearance and farming signals, old lead in ice from ancient smelting. Under this procedure, picking one means picking which process gets to define the period. What does the procedure presuppose to make that a well-posed choice rather than an arbitrary one?

Two ideas that got tossed at me in interviews, which I want stress-tested rather than endorsed. First, the ratchet: don't put the start where human influence first shows up, put it where going back stopped being possible on any human timescale — so the boundary is fixed by a counterfactual about what it would take to reverse things, not by a layer. Is a boundary defined that way even the same kind of object as a stratigraphic one, and what theory of periodization does the ratchet view commit you to? Second, latency: this unit is being named from inside it, and a lot of the case for it rests on what it's expected to become — its eventual thickness, its end. Epochs are normally identified retrospectively, from their top. Does the procedure presuppose that, and does the Anthropocene break it?

The who-decides part. The marker is, in some sense, a message left for future readers of the rock record. But the word is mostly used by people living inside the period, as a self-description, and it's starting to do legal and political work. Does the institution presuppose that authority over a period name follows from expertise in reading the record? And if you thought the right judges were instead the people living in the period, or the ones who'll be affected by how it's dated, what would a ratification process built that way even look like?

Last one: the Iron Age, the Bronze Age, the Renaissance, the Industrial Revolution all get along fine without any marker in any rock, and the stratigraphers themselves have no problem subdividing the Holocene into ages by climate events. So what does ratification add? Is asking the golden spike to settle this a category error — pointing an instrument built for correlating rock layers at a question about a rupture in human history — or is that too quick?

Give me the load-bearing presuppositions, the ones that are optional, where the practice is underdetermined because several rival theories of what a period is would license the same procedure, and what would concretely change in the procedure if a rival theory were adopted. Skip citations and who-said-what, I've got the sourcing handled; I need the argument. And please flag anywhere my framing above has smuggled in an assumption I shouldn't be making.
