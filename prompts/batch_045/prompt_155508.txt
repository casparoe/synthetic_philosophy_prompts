so I keep coming back to that center-of-narrative-gravity idea (Dennett's, I think) — the self isn't a thing in there, its more like the abstract point where a body's stories converge. I've been going through cases by gut to see whether I actually believe it, and my verdicts dont obviously add up. Here they are:

- Total character overhaul over about two years — new guy explicitly disowns the old one, tears up the old diary. Still the same self, and the new story is the authoritative one about who he is now. Fine.
- A guy whose autobiographical memory is maybe 40% confabulated, but it hangs together as one story and he avows it completely. Self fully intact, and fully his. Fine.
- A kid raised in a compound where every detail of his life story was scripted by the adults, including memories of trips and beatings that never happened; he believes all of it and tells it fluently. I want to say his self is real but borrowed — thinner somehow, not quite his own. Which sits very badly with the last one, since that one was false too.
- Two sisters who've run a single joint persona their whole lives: one diary between them, one account, they answer to each other's names, we all the way down. Two selves, definately not one fused self. But there's only one narrative between them, so whatever principle I want can't just be one-narrative-one-self.
- Late-stage dementia, no story left, doesn't recognize the person in the mirror. I say the self is basically gone — and yet I'd still treat her as the same person for every moral purpose. I don't know if those two verdicts are allowed to come apart or if I'm confusing two questions.
- A man with dense aphasia who can produce no narrative at all but lights up with recognition when other people tell stories about him. Self intact. Which is odd right after the last one, since neither of them does any narrating.
- Outsourced narrative, near-future thing: an app logs everything and every morning reads you a five-minute coherent story of your life so far, which you endorse; switch it off and the story evaporates. Self intact, no problem at all. Except — that's a story authored entirely outside the person, and it's fine, while the compound kid's externally authored story felt stolen from him.
- Grafting case: surgeons transplant another woman's autobiographical memory set into you. You now narrate a childhood that was hers, right alongside yours, and it doesn't feel foreign at all. I don't think there are two selves in there, but I do think the self got contaminated in a way I can't spell out.
- The one I go back and forth on: a Parkinson's patient with a switchable stimulator. On: devout, shy, remembers her life one way. Off: loud, flamboyant, remembers it another way. Each state has full access to the others memories and completely disavows them. One self or two?

My tentative principle, which I keep retreating to: a self is a center of narrative gravity — not a real object, just the point where the stories about a body converge, and truth doesn't matter, only uptake by the person the stories are about. Test it against the list, tell me where it breaks, and repair it if you can rather than junking it. Be straight about which of my verdicts it can't save: if a clean version can't have both the confabulator and the compound kid, tell me which one I should drop and why that one. And give me its verdict on a case I haven't thought of, so I can see whether it or my gut is doing the work.
