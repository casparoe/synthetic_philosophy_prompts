I work in grantmaking at a small foundation, and over the last few years I've come to notice that my official view about long-run consequences and my actual practice have quietly come apart. Officially I accept the cluelessness worry: for almost anything we fund, the effects running out past a few decades are larger in expectation than the effects we can measure, and our evidence about their sign is close to worthless, so it looks like I should either be paralysed or should be funding whatever has the best story about the far future rather than whatever has the best near-term evidence. In practice I am neither paralysed nor a long-termist, and I'm fairly confident about most of the calls I make. I'd like to know what principle I am implicitly using, stated precisely enough that I can apply it to a case I haven't decided.

Here are the calls, as I actually make them. (a) Give now to an anti-malaria charity with unusually good near-term evidence, versus holding the money in an index fund and giving the (much larger) sum in thirty years: I give now, without much unease. (b) Call it the sealed-envelope program: its near-term effects are well measured and clearly good, but two long-run models of apparently equal credibility say, respectively, that its population-level effects make it astronomically good and catastrophically bad, and I can see no way to arbitrate between them: I fund it. (c) The empty box: a grant whose near-term effects are essentially unmeasurable and whose case rests entirely on a diffuse, untestable long-run story: I decline it — and I keep declining it even when I rescale the long-run story upward until the diffuse benefit is, on its own terms, enormous, which makes me suspect the size of the benefit isn't really what's doing the work. (d) The tail: a grant that might — maybe one in a thousand, maybe one in a million, nobody can say — avert a civilisational catastrophe, and otherwise does nothing measurable: I fund it, and I fund it in preference to (c), even though (c) and (d) are alike in having no measurable near-term effects and inscrutable ones further out. (e) Two grants with identical near-term and long-run epistemic profiles, except that one is a one-off and the other is the first of about two hundred similar grants we expect to make over the next decade, where if the long-run story is wrong we will have repeated the mistake two hundred times: I'm comfortable with the one-off and distinctly uneasy about the first of the series, even though the first grant's own effects are the same either way. (f) When two equally credible long-run models agree on the sign of an intervention's effect but differ by a factor of a thousand about its size, I act without anxiety; when they disagree about the sign, I feel I ought to abstain — but I also think abstaining is just another action with inscrutable long-run effects, so in the end I decide on near-term grounds anyway, and I can't say why that isn't simply giving up.

Two candidate principles have been floating around our office, and I'd like them tested against these verdicts rather than summarised. A colleague's is what he calls the corridor rule: when the long-run effects of your options are inscrutable, choose the option that best preserves the capacity of whoever comes later to notice the mistake and reverse course — buy information, stay reversible, don't burn the option to change your mind. My own is cruder: treat inscrutable long-run effects as having an expected value of zero, subtract a penalty for how much variance you're taking on, and decide on everything else. Tell me which of my six verdicts each gets wrong, whether either can be repaired into something that gets all or nearly all of them, and which verdict you'd overturn if you had to — I'd rather give up a verdict than accept a principle with a clause that exists only to save it. Then apply whatever you land on to the case I keep going back and forth on: an intervention whose near-term effects are large and well evidenced but which is effectively irreversible, where the long-run models disagree about the sign and I won't get a second chance. I don't want a survey of the literature or any citations; I want the principle, the clause-by-clause motivation from my cases, and an honest account of which of my verdicts no tidy principle can accommodate.
