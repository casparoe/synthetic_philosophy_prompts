My friend and I have been going round on Newcomb's problem for a couple of years, and the literature has been stuck on it for fifty-odd, so I'm assuming nobody is going to settle it tonight. She one-boxes, I two-box, we've both read all the standard stuff — dominance, why aintcha rich, causal versus evidential expected utility — so please don't just walk me through the two theories again. Neither of us has moved an inch.

Yesterday we tried to break the tie by tampering with the setup: suppose the predictor is 99% accurate overall, but it's errors aren't spread evenly — it's basically perfect at spotting one-boxers and almost useless on two-boxers, so if you two-box you usually find the million sitting there anyway. In that version she says the whole case for one-boxing was smuggled in by the accuracy spec; I say fine, then Newcomb was never testing decision theories at all, it was testing how you describe the predictor.

My working theory now is that the stalemate is fake: the one-boxer is really answering "what sort of agent should I be, what should I have made myself into before the prediction was made," and the two-boxer is answering "what should I do standing here with the boxes in front of me." Is that the whole of it, or is there a genuine disagreement underneath — about the causal structure of the case, about which conditional probabilities are the right ones to plug in, about which versions of the setup even count as data? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a formal result about when the act question and the disposition question come apart, clarity about which sense of "99% accurate" is the load-bearing one, or just agreeing to answer the two questions separately? I want a diagnosis with reasons, not another tour of the options.
