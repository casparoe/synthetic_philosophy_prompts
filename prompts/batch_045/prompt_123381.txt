I sit on the algorithmic accountability board of a public health insurance program, and we vote Friday on a vendor's proposal.

The vendor wants us to allow adverse coverage decisions to rest on what their team calls a latent veracity readout. Their argument is that written justifications are cheap rationalizations the model can generate for any verdict, so instead of asking the system for a reason, they run the case file through a few dozen paraphrases and reframings, keep whichever verdict survives all of them, and treat that invariant verdict as what the system actually knows. On files whose outcome was later settled on appeal, the readout beats the system's written output.

The case in front of us: a claimant's infusion therapy was denied on the strength of the readout, while every written explanation the system produced for that same file was coherent, checkable, and pointed the other way. Two further facts. On the handful of files where the written output was confidently wrong, the readout was wrong in the same direction. And our red team found that the readout shifts when the same clinical facts are simply reordered within the file.

Our rules require that a denial state the basis on which it was made; the vendor proposes a form letter naming the readout as that basis.

Draft the memo I will circulate before the vote as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no heading and no closing: item 1 the bottom line, whether to approve, reject, or approve with conditions, and if any pilot is permitted, for how long and subject to what check; items 2 through 4 the three considerations that should carry the most weight with the board, including whether what the system really knows is even well defined here given that the method can only be graded where we already know the answer; item 5 the strongest argument for the option you did not pick, and why it still loses. Use American English.
