A friend and I argued late into the evening about whether the Twin Earth cases really show that the content of a thought or utterance is fixed partly by the environment rather than by anything in the head. Her objection was that externalism destroys first-person authority: if what I mean depends on the microstructure of the liquid around me, then I cannot know what I mean without first doing chemistry. My reply was that she was running together knowing what one means with knowing what one's words refer to, and that the externalist is supposed to be able to — indeed needs to — make room for ordinary, uninvestigated knowledge of our own thoughts. Neither of us could make the other feel the pull of the other side, so I would like to see the consequences staged rather than argued.

Please write, in British English, a parody of a scheduled airline's Conditions of Carriage — numbered clauses, headings, and the flat, unapologetic register of the genre — for the first commercial carrier licensed to operate between Earth and Twin Earth, roughly 600 words. The comedy should come from getting the position right rather than from inventing a silly version of it: reference settled by the local environment at the point of utterance, speakers who are intrinsically unchanged and yet mean something different, and — this is the wrinkle I want exploited — the fact that the sublight crossing takes several years, so that a passenger's terms may be quietly re-anchored mid-journey with nothing whatever to mark the change from her point of view. The fine print should take substantive positions without ever naming the doctrine: a complaint about being served the local drink when one asked for water is not upheld, because the airline determines what was meant by the stuff at the arrival port and not by how things seemed in the cabin; a supplementary fare for the cabin in which the crew will humbly humour passengers who insist they have meant the same thing the whole way; a clause offering the reassurance that whatever a passenger has turned out to mean, she can always know a priori that she meant it. Close with a short model complaint letter from a slow-drift passenger, answered by the carrier in two sentences, such that the reader is left having to decide whether she has any grievance at all.
