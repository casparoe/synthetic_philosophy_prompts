I teach an upper-level seminar in philosophy of mind — 14 students, one 90-minute session, a projector and a room I can darken. Design an activity that makes them feel the force of the Orwellian/Stalinesque distinction about the timing of experience, staged on the color phi phenomenon: a disk flashed at one location, followed shortly by a differently coloured disk at a displaced location, timed so that nearly everyone reports a single moving disk whose colour changes mid-flight — apparently before the second disk has been presented. The distinction itself must not be named or explained up front; they should encounter it by trying to break it.

The skeleton I have in mind, which you should improve rather than follow slavishly: split the room into two teams and give each a plain-English version of one hypothesis. Team A: the journey was first experienced in one unchanging colour, and only afterwards was the record of it retrospectively rewritten. Team B: nothing was experienced until the visual system had settled on the journey as a whole, and the filled-in version is the very first thing ever experienced. Before any stimulus runs, each team writes down what its own hypothesis predicts for each trial type and wagers points on the result. Trial types: standard trials; catch trials interleaved unpredictably, in which the second flash is omitted or its colour is swapped, so that no report can be explained by learned expectation or anticipation; and a condition in which each student must signal, by keypress or raised hand, the instant the disk changes colour. Then run them. What they should walk into is that the two teams' advance predictions coincide for every measure the class can devise, and that every further measure they propose collapses in the same way.

Give me the materials and setup; the stimulus parameters together with how to pilot and tune them; the trial schedule and how many trials of each type; the rules and the payoff scheme for the wagers; a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes; a debrief that maps each moment of the activity onto a part of the distinction and supplies the labels only at the end; a frank list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it; and one honest note on which feature of the dispute the activity cannot stage, so that I know what I will have to argue for myself. Assume no internet in the room and build nothing around citations to experimental findings — whatever neural evidence matters I will supply in the debrief.

Begin with the activity itself: no preface and no commentary on how you approached the task.
