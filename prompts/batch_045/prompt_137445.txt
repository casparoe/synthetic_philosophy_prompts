I am an undergraduate in a political theory seminar this term, and our final assignment is a short position paper, a draft of which is due next week. Before I commit myself to it I would be grateful for an audit of the package of claims I want to defend, because I have a feeling that some of them are quietly working against the others and I cannot tell which. Here it is, more or less as I plan to write it.

(1) The left/right distinction is real. Every couple of decades it is declared obsolete — by technocracy, by the end of history, by whatever the current version of that announcement is — and on each occasion something recognizably left and something recognizably right re-forms within a few years. I want to treat that recurrence as evidence that the scheme tracks something, and not merely as evidence that we are creatures of habit.

(2) But its content is almost entirely plastic. Which question divides the sides rotates: at one point it is whether the state should be confessional or whether bishops should sit in the legislature; later it is whether steel and railways should be publicly owned; in our own period it is more likely to be who belongs to the nation or who may use which pronoun. The valence of institutional arrangements flips too. As I understand the history, in one era the people most eager to use central state power against local majorities were the ones demanding nationalization, while the language of states' rights belonged to the defenders of racial hierarchy; now the same language is used by states against municipalities, and by one side about abortion and by the other about immigration. So I hold that no particular policy is intrinsically left-wing.

(3) What I take to be constant is not a policy but an attitude toward inequality: the left treats the large inequalities of a society as made, contingent and in principle removable, the right treats them as natural, deserved, or at any rate not to be undone by collective action. This is roughly the egalitarian reading of the distinction that we were given in the Bobbio book, and I may well be simplifying it.

(4) The distinction is also not one dimension. Economic and cultural conservatism come apart constantly: there are many people who want a much larger welfare state, strong unions and a restrictive immigration regime, and many who want free markets and permissive social norms, and these people are not rare or confused, they are the median voter in several countries. Whatever the scheme is, it cannot be one axis.

(5) And most ordinary citizens are not ideological in any strict sense. If you ask the same person whether the government should guarantee everyone a job and whether the government should stop interfering in the economy, you frequently get yes to both. So the distinction lives, in the first instance, among parties, elites, intellectuals and activists, not in the beliefs of the mass public.

(6) It is also geographically parochial. The scheme was forged in a particular European argument, and across much of the world the cleavage that actually organizes politics is centre versus periphery, or religion, or ethnicity, or patronage, or anti-colonial sovereignty. When commentators force those onto a left/right map they usually end up saying that every serious party is on the left, which suggests the map is doing no work there.

(7) My own positive suggestion, the part I am least certain about and most attached to, is that what holds each side together is not a principle but a shared set of opponents. When I want to know where some new movement belongs, what I actually check is not its programme but who attacks it, which established interests it threatens, which party it is fighting. This test works surprisingly well, including for movements whose stated principles sit badly with their supposed side. It would also explain (2), since the programme rotates as the enemy rotates, and it would explain the instability of the coalitions in (4), since a negative coalition has nothing to hold it once the enemy stops being useful. My worry — and I genuinely cannot tell whether it is fatal — is that if I make this load-bearing I will have quietly given up (1) and (3). A distinction held together only by what it is against can in principle exchange its entire content while remaining, on my own account, the same distinction; and then it is unclear what the recurrence in (1) was supposed to be evidence for, or what role (3) is playing beyond describing whatever the left happens to believe this decade.

What I would like is the following, and I would rather have it as an audit than as an essay. First, which pairs among these are genuinely in tension, ordered from most to least severe, and for each one what exactly would have to give and what the cheapest revision would cost me. Second, which pairs only look like they are in tension, and why the appearance dissolves — I strongly suspect there is at least one, maybe between (2) and (3) or between (5) and (1), but I do not trust my own sense of which. Third, tell me which single claim is actually load-bearing and which is decorative, and specifically whether the package survives if (7) is dropped or demoted to a causal story about how coalitions happen to form, with (3) left in charge of content. Please be concrete about which numbered claim has to go or be qualified rather than telling me that the whole area is contested: I know it is contested, I have a draft due.
