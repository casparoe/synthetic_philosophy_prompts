I'm a PhD student in political philosophy, and I want to close the last session of a graduate seminar on algorithmic fairness with something other than another theorem statement. Please write me a five-stanza sestina — five stanzas of five lines, with sestina rotation (each stanza's end-words are the previous stanza's taken last, first, second-to-last, second, middle) — on the impossibility result for group fairness. Take as given: two groups that differ in prevalence (the base rate of the outcome being predicted), an imperfect classifier whose scores are collapsed into decisions at a threshold, and the result that calibration within groups cannot be had together with balance for the negative class and balance for the positive class. Use as your five end-words: calibrated, positive, negative, threshold, prevalence.

Let the rotation do the work: the constraints should change places and return without ever being jointly satisfiable, and by the third stanza the reader should notice that the trilemma bites only because the classifier errs — a perfect predictor would satisfy all of it — and that no such predictor is on offer. In the fifth stanza let two lines carry a second end-word internally, so that all five words crowd into the closing lines and the syntax or the music breaks there: the only way to keep calibration and both error-rate balances while prevalence differs is to set a different threshold per group, which offends a different fairness ideal (treating like cases alike, not sorting by group membership) rather than solving anything.

It should be exact enough that someone who has worked through the result would recognize it rather than see it name-dropped, and good enough as a poem that the rest of the room still hears something. Return the piece as exactly five numbered stanzas, with nothing before or after.
