I work on a tiny team that does mechanistic anomaly detection for a triage model deployed across 14 hospitals (it predicts who'll need a bed in the next six hours). At validation we characterized what we call the model's "route" for a handful of high-stakes behaviors — roughly, which components matter for the behavior and how they're hooked up — and every week the monitor surfaces cases where the prediction looks like it's arriving by a different route than the one we signed off on. The weekly meeting then deadlocks on the same question: is this the mechanism we already cleared, showing up again under distribution shift, or a genuinely new mechanism, in which case our old safety case doesn't cover it and we escalate. Two of us have criteria that basically never agree. The structuralist says: same mechanism iff the same components in the same organization are constitutively responsible for the behavior; her test is a transplant test — swap the corresponding subnetwork in from the reference system and see whether the behavior survives, and check that the same ablations break it. The other guy says parts are irrelevant, two behaviors share a mechanism iff they have the same response profile over a fixed battery of perturbations — panel-equivalence, with a tolerance — and anything outside tolerance is a new mechanism by definition. We've been doing this for two years and I have never once seen anyone move. The March incident is the template: a hospital migrated its admissions software, the model's high-risk scores started tracking a nursing-note timestamp field whose format had shifted, the transplant test came back "same components, same wiring, transplant preserves behavior — same mechanism, new input format," and the panel came back "response profile outside tolerance — new mechanism, escalate." Then in June we got the exact mirror image at two other sites: completely different components lit up, different ablation profile, and every single perturbation in the panel identical. They just trade those two cases back and forth.

My own guess is that this isn't a disagreement about what a mechanism is at all, and I want you to tell me whether I'm right. I think it's a disagreement about which interventions count as evidence — what the perturbation panel is, who gets to add to it, and how much it's allowed to grow — plus an asymmetry I can never get them to look at: a structural identity claim looks *verifiable* (you go look at the parts and the wiring and confirm it), whereas a counterfactual-profile identity claim can only ever survive attempts to break it, since you can't enumerate all interventions. So they're applying different standards of evidence to what is nominally the same identity claim. But there's a nastier version I keep circling and can't settle, which is specific to anomaly detection: the perturbations we actually care about are, by construction, the ones that aren't in the panel. The anomaly just is that something outside the reference class happened. So a criterion for "same mechanism" defined over a fixed panel seems to beg the question against the thing the label is for — if the relevant perturbation had been in the panel, it wouldn't have been an anomaly. That makes me suspect we're not even tracking the right object, and that what the escalations really turn on is something like "same response to the interventions we might actually deploy" or "same reason the fix that worked last time will keep working."

So: diagnose this for me, with reasons, and don't just survey positions on mechanism identity. Is it a verbal dispute wearing engineering clothes? A substantive fight about which cases count as data? A difference in standards of evidence — verifiability versus surviving falsification — dressed up as a difference about mechanisms? A hidden formal question in disguise, like whether there's a well-defined notion of mechanism identity *relative to* an intervention class and whether equivalence on a panel licenses any extrapolation off it? Or are we simply answering three different questions under one label (mechanism identity, mitigation-generality, and safety-case coverage), in which case the impasse is a sign the original question is ill-posed? And given whichever diagnosis you give, what would actually count as progress — a distinction we should adopt, a formal result, a change of question, an experiment we could run on our own system — or is the honest answer that we should expect no progress and stop asking the question at our weekly?
