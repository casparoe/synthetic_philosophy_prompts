ok so my lab has sort of converged on this framing and i want someone to take it apart: aligning a model is just a principal–agent problem. we're the principal, the model is the contractor, we can't observe it's "effort" (the internal reasoning), we write a spec and it exploits the loopholes in the spec, so the answer is better contracts — incentives, audits, monitoring, iterated interaction, reputation. my collegue leans on this for two conclusions: (1) there's nothing categorically new about ai alignment, it's mechanism design on a funny substrate, and (2) all the deception / treacherous-turn talk is projecion — a contractor who hides bad news isn't "deceptive" in any deep sense, its just responding to incentives we wrote badly.

the other half of this is a workaround we've been circling that's supposed to make the analogy airtight: treat deployment like a temp agency instead of a job. no persistent memory across tasks, credentials re-issued each episode, no ability to accumulate money or compute or relationships between episodes, everything sandboxed and revocable. the pitch is that if the agent can't carry anything from one contract into the next, the long-horizon reasons to deceive vanish and the ordinary contract-design toolkit is enough.

can you audit the whole thing? concretely i want:

- the correspondence table: what plays the principal, the contract, the consideration, the monitoring, the reputation, the court that enforces breach, and the at-will firing (i.e. the off switch / corrigibility)
- which background institutions the analogy quietly imports that just aren't there for models: courts and tort liability, careers, reputation, shared human ends, roughly comparable stakes between the two parties, the assumption that the agent's goals are legible and negotiable at all
- the one i think might be fatal: principal–agent theory takes the principal's utility function as given, and half of alignment is that we don't have one. is that fatal to the analogy, or does it just mean you apply the whole frame one level up (regulator as principal, lab as agent, safety team as agent of the lab) and the model barely appears in it?
- where the temp-agency move actually closes the gap and where it leaks: deception inside a single episode, things that persist in the weights rather than in memory, monitors that are themselves models (so you've hired the contractor's cousin to audit them), and whether "no memory" is enforceable or just a policy the system can route around
- whether conclusions (1) and (2) survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was doing the work

and if the contractor picture is the wrong one, what's better? i keep coming back to something like drafting a constitution for a polity that will outlive everyone who wrote it and can amend it themselves, but maybe that's worse — say why if so.
