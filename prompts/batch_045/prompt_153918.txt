Hofstadter has this idea — in his book on strange loops, and in stuff he's said since — that an "I" isn't sealed inside one skull. Chunks of me are literally located in other people's brains, at wildly different fidelities: a high-res version of me in my wife, a fuzzier one in an old friend, and so on down. The part that gets people is that he takes it to have survival consequences: when his late wife died a chunk of his I went with her, and chunks of her are still doing whatever they do inside him and their kids.

Please argue that this whole "distributed I" way of talking isn't a claim about personal identity — about what it takes for a person to exist, or to survive something — at all. Argue that it's really a claim about something else, and that it borrows its emotional force from the identity reading while only the other reading is actually true. Pick whichever reduction you think is strongest: that it's a causal-dependence claim (my psychology wouldn't be what it is without you, so you're part of the scaffolding that holds me up), or that it's a claim about what I have reason to care about and about what grief is really tracking, or something better I haven't listed. Don't hedge across all of them — argue for one.

Then the bit I actually want you to use it on. Suppose somebody builds a machine that carries a really high-fidelity version of a living person: trained on everything they've written and said, passes as them in conversation, the whole deal, while the person themselves is alive and fine. Hofstadter's way of talking looks committed to saying a big chunk of that person's I is now in the machine, and that wrecking the machine would be a partial death of the person. Run that as a reductio: argue that the identity reading dies here, and that your reduction is the only thing left standing.

And then the wrinkle. The picture is asymmetric in a way that seems load-bearing: the chunk of me in you depends on you staying alive (you die, that chunk of me dies), but it doesn't depend on me staying alive — a low-res me can keep running in your head long after I'm gone. Where does that asymmetry come from on your reduction, and do you still get it? If you don't, argue that Hofstadter never needed it, and that losing it costs him nothing he was entitled to.
