Putting together my notes for a reading group on Hofstadter's frame / outer / inner message distinction in GEB, and I want someone to poke at them before I present. Roughly where I've landed:

(a) Meaning isn't located in the object, in the signal, or in the receiver — it's a property of the whole loop: a maker, a medium, and a receiver who brings the right background.
(b) The frame message is the odd one out. It's the only level the object itself guarantees: you can be certain that some artifact dug up from a civilization you'll never decipher is a message, while having no idea what it says.
(c) The outer message is the objective bit. Once you've got the code, getting the signal out is mechanical — no intelligence required on the receiver's side.
(d) The inner message isn't pinned down by the outer one: same signal, receivers with different backgrounds, different legitimate reconstructions, and no single reading is privileged as the meaning.
(e) But it's not anything goes. A receiver who lacks the relevant background — someone who's never heard music, or a program that only sees word frequencies — gets a degenerate or empty inner message, and that's a failure rather than an alternative reading.
(f) Which is why I want to say that a translation of a poem can be complete at the outer level and empty at the inner one, and that this is a coherent thing to say rather then a paradox.

I genuinely can't tell which of these is doing to much work. Audit the set: which pairs actually collide, which only look like they do, and for each real collision what I'd have to give up and what it'd cost me. Rank the tensions by severity instead of flagging everything as equally bad, and tell me whether there's one revision that cleans up the most.
