Here it is as I have it:

"...if we conceive of a being whose faculties are so sharpened that he can follow every molecule in its course, such a being, whose attributes are as essentially finite as our own, would be able to do what is impossible to us. For we have seen that molecules in a vessel full of air at uniform temperature are moving with velocities by no means uniform, though the mean velocity of any great number of them, arbitrarily selected, is almost exactly uniform. Now let us suppose that such a vessel is divided into two portions, A and B, by a division in which there is a small hole, and that a being, who can see the individual molecules, opens and closes this hole, so as to allow only the swifter molecules to pass from A to B, and only the slower molecules to pass from B to A. He will thus, without expenditure of work, raise the temperature of B and lower that of A, in contradiction to the second law of thermodynamics."

I've read the standard modern account of where the compensation is supposed to come from, but I don't think I understand the passage on its own terms, and three things are bothering me in particular. First, the clause about the being's attributes being "as essentially finite as our own": is that merely defensive — warding off the reading on which an infinite being could simply do anything — or is it doing real work, marking out the second law as a law that holds only for agents who can manipulate matter in bulk and has no strict application to anything that can track individual molecules? Second, someone in my reading group insists the setup collapses before you get to any bookkeeping about information: at thermal equilibrium the cavity is full of blackbody radiation, there is no source cool enough to illuminate a single molecule against that background, so a being who "sees" molecules either cannot see them at all or must shine light on them, and the cost of doing so swamps anything the sorting gains. I can't tell whether that argument is decisive, orthogonal, or simply misses the point because Maxwell never says the demon sees by light at all.

Third, and this is the bit I care most about: I've built myself a toy model in which the demon never has to erase anything. Take a one-molecule engine — a box in contact with a bath at temperature T, a partition slipped in at the midpoint, the demon finds out which half the molecule is in and lets it expand isothermally against a piston, extracting the usual kT ln 2 of work per cycle. Now give the demon a memory consisting of a shift register of n bits: each cycle it writes the new result into the first cell and pushes everything along one place, so that the bit in the last cell simply falls off the end. That bit is never read out, never deliberately reset to a standard state, and never deliberately thermalised; the physical carrier of it (a particle in a bistable well, say, or a domain on a tape) just tips out of the register and becomes an ordinary uncontrolled degree of freedom of the surroundings. My supervisor says this is erasure under another description and that I owe kT ln 2 per cycle regardless. I say nothing has been compressed: resetting a bit means taking two logical states to one and dumping k ln 2 of entropy into the bath, whereas here the bit leaves the register with its state intact, so the entropy has merely moved into the environment rather than been destroyed. My simulation runs for thousands of cycles with the gas returning to its initial state each time and the register staying at n bits, and it shows net work extracted per cycle — so either I've found free energy or I've failed to count something I can't see, and I'd like to know precisely which and exactly where the missing entropy gets in.
