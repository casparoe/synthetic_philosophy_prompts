I belong to a small discussion group that meets at the public library once a fortnight and reads short selections from classic texts in translation, mainly with the help of free lecture series I find online. I have no academic background and have only been reading about this material on my own for a few months, so I may be relaying things imperfectly. Last month we spent two sessions on al-Ghazālī, an eleventh-century thinker, and specifically on his treatment of causation in the work usually translated as the Incoherence of the Philosophers. As I understood it, his claim was that when fire touches cotton and the cotton burns, observation never shows us more than that the burning happens *with* the contact; it never shows a necessary tie between the two. Created things, on his view, have no efficacy of their own, and the burning is brought about directly by God.

Two members of the group, Nadia and Peter, got into a long argument about what is actually at stake in that position, and they have asked me to help settle it. I cannot tell who has the better of it, and I have no horse in the race. I would be grateful if you would go through the exchange move by move and say who is right about which specific point, rather than just declaring a winner. Their exchange, as close as I can reconstruct it from my notes:

(1) Nadia opened by saying that the argument is essentially the one David Hume later made famous: experience gives us sequence and repetition, never a glimpse of a necessary connection, so the philosophers cannot prove that fire *must* burn cotton. On her reading, al-Ghazālī then fills the empty space with God because he needs something to stand where necessity used to stand, but nothing in the actual reasoning is theological. An atheist who accepts the Humean point loses nothing that matters, so the God part is decoration.

(2) Peter said this gets the dialectic backwards. The target is not some general claim that we cannot *know* about necessary connections; it is the Avicennan picture on which a cause brings about its effect by its very nature, so that even God could not produce the effect without the cause. The engine of al-Ghazālī's argument, Peter said, is the claim that a genuinely necessary connection between two created things would limit God's power and would make God something less than the only agent. The appeal to observation only shows that the philosophers have no proof; the positive thesis comes from theology. And it earns its keep: on the philosophers' picture a miracle would be impossible, whereas on occasionalism God remains free to create the burning without the fire, or the contact without the burning.

(3) Nadia replied that the usual way out of this is to say that God has a habit, a customary way of acting, and that this is why the world is regular. But then, she said, there are two possibilities and neither is comfortable. If God's habit is perfectly exceptionless, then occasionalism is just the laws of nature under another name and makes no difference to anything. If it is not exceptionless, then our confidence that the future will resemble the past is worse off than it is for Hume, because God could suspend the habit at any moment without any visible difference in the setup beforehand. She added that the standard reply she has seen, that God has also created in us the knowledge that He will not break His habit, only pushes the problem back: a belief produced directly by God could as easily be a belief in something false, unless we first assume God is trustworthy, and if God's trustworthiness is itself established by His habit, the reasoning goes in a circle.

(4) Peter answered both parts. First, he said, the difference between a habit and a law is not statistical but modal: on the philosophers' view God *could not* have made the cotton burn without the fire, and on occasionalism He could. That difference is what makes the possibility of miracles, and of petitionary prayer, coherent at all, which is hardly a minor addition for someone working inside that tradition. Second, he said he had come across a 1978 article by a scholar named Lenn Goodman, whose title asks whether al-Ghazālī denied causality at all; the article apparently argues that what he denies is necessary connection rather than causation as such. Peter's use of this was that if Nadia is right that only necessity is denied, then the entire burden of explaining why there is a stable pattern falls on God's will, which is precisely the theological part she called decorative.

(5) Nadia then proposed something she admitted she had made up herself, and which she named structural occasionalism. She asked us to separate three claims: that God alone *produces* the effect; that citing the fire *explains* nothing; and that we are not *licensed to predict* the burning from the contact. Her claim was that the argument at best supports the first, and that the second and third do not follow. Even if the fire has no power whatsoever, if God's creative activity has a stable structure, the same pattern every time, then fire burns cotton can be a perfectly good explanation and a perfectly good basis for prediction, understood as a description of the shape of God's creating rather than as a claim about the nature of fire. She extended the same treatment to human action, since on the face of it, if no created thing produces anything, then I raised my arm is as doubtful as the fire burned the cotton; she said the traditional doctrine of acquisition was the patch for this, and that her version handles it the same way, by treating my willing and my arm's rising as a pair within the divine pattern, which she said is all that ordinary talk of agency needs. As a test case for Peter, she offered this: a bystander moves the candle aside, so the fire never touches the cotton, and everyone afterwards says that if the fire had touched it, it would have burned. What, on the occasionalist picture, makes that sentence true?

(6) Peter answered that the structural version is not occasionalism but a regularity theory with a theological label, and that it inherits the counterfactual problem in a worse form. On his view the truth of that sentence about the candle becomes a fact about what God would have created in a circumstance that never obtained, that is, a fact about God's unactualized will, and it is quite unclear either that there is a determinate fact there or that we could ever have reason to think we knew it. So the structural version relocates the difficulty rather than answering it, and in the process it gives up the one thing occasionalism seemed to offer, which is an account of why there is a stable pattern at all rather than merely a description of the pattern. On human action, he added, the pairing story collapses the difference between deliberately raising one's arm and a muscle spasm, unless something is said about how the willing and the movement are connected, and he doubted that the materials al-Ghazālī actually has on hand, such as the will itself being created along with the act, supply that.

Where I would like your help: please rate each of these six moves as clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right, with a short justification in each case, and then say who you think is more right overall. I am also interested in three further things. First, is structural occasionalism, as Nadia describes it, actually coherent, and is it new or just a relabelling of a position philosophers already have on the shelf? Second, what should be said about the bystander-and-candle case, and does it favour one of them? Third, and most useful for me, what is both of them missing? In particular, I got the feeling that they were using the words cause and power in different senses without ever saying so, and I wonder whether the whole dispute turns on some distinction, perhaps between causation as production and causation as difference-making, that neither of them made explicit. If so, I would like to know how the argument looks once that distinction is on the table.
