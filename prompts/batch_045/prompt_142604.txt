I sit on a small committee that is drafting the industrial-action section of a labor federation's code of conduct, and I have been asked to say when a strike is justified. I do not want a survey of any country's labor law, or a tour of the standard doctrines; I know the law often diverges from what I think, and that does not bother me. What I want is a principle: one I could defend to a skeptic, that earns my verdicts rather than merely restating them. Below are my gut verdicts, given as honestly as I can. Assume a setting where unions are established and a collective agreement is in force, except where I say otherwise.

**The cases**

1. An ordinary economic strike over wages, begun after the contract expires, with two months' notice; the employer keeps part of the line running with managers and output falls by about sixty percent. **Permissible**, without hesitation.

2. Nurses at a large hospital strike intermittently — three days out, four days in — but the union staffs the emergency department and intensive care at normal levels and gives ten days' notice of each cycle. Elective procedures are cancelled, and the cancellations fall hardest on poorer patients who cannot easily rebook. **Permissible.**

3. Teachers strike for six weeks; parents scramble for childcare and students lose instructional time. **Permissible in principle, but not as executed** — they gave two days' notice, and I want more than that.

4. Workers walk out in breach of a no-strike clause in a contract that still has a year to run, because a machine guard was removed five months earlier and has since been involved in two amputations; the union filed a grievance and no hearing was scheduled. **Permissible.**

5. The same plant, same clause, walks out over a schedule change the agreement plainly lets the employer make. **Not permissible.**

6. Warehouse workers at a separate company in the same supply chain strike for a week so that the primary employer loses its buffer. They have no grievance of their own. **Permissible.**

7. Software workers walk out to force their employer to cancel a government contract. The work itself is ordinary; their objection is to what the contract is ultimately used for. **Not permissible** — and this is the verdict I am least sure I can defend.

8. Employees at a print shop refuse to run an order of white-nationalist flyers and walk out when the owner insists; their contract permits refusal only on safety grounds. **Permissible.**

9. Food-delivery couriers, classified as independent contractors, with no union and no recognition election, coordinate in a group chat to log off simultaneously for one hour during the Friday surge-pricing window, demanding a floor on per-trip pay. No notice at all. **Permissible.**

10. Sanitation crews work to rule — every inspection done in full, no shortcuts, no voluntary overtime — and throughput drops by forty percent. They never call it a strike. **Permissible, but it troubles me more than an open walkout does**, and I would want it disclosed.

11. Mass picketing that physically prevents replacement workers and suppliers from entering the plant. **Not permissible.** For what it is worth, I also think it is **not permissible** for the employer to hire permanent replacements who keep their jobs after a settlement.

12. Auto-parts workers stage six unannounced four-hour walkouts, each timed to a just-in-time delivery window during a new model launch. Total hours lost are fewer than in a three-day continuous strike. **Not permissible.**

13. An employer fires the most visible organizer two weeks into a card-signing drive, citing a minor rule violation of a kind usually tolerated. **Not permissible, and seriously so.** The same employer then holds mandatory paid meetings arguing against unionization, with no equal access for the union. **Also not permissible, but far less seriously, and I am not confident it should be legally prohibited.** I suspect "not permissible" is doing two different jobs across my list — blameworthy versus apt for legal prohibition — and I would like that sorted out.

**The case I cannot settle**

A federation calls a one-day general strike, with a week's notice, aimed not at employers but at the government over a national policy; both public- and private-sector unions take part. I go back and forth, partly because case 7 says a political object is out of bounds and partly because a general strike seems to be the only remaining route to that particular object.

**My tentative principle**

The best I have managed: a strike is permissible if and only if (i) the demand is one the workers could not reasonably be expected to secure by quitting individually or by enforcing their existing agreement; (ii) the costs of the action fall in the first instance on the party able to grant the demand rather than on people with no power to concede anything; and (iii) the action is authorized by a majority of the unit and announced far enough ahead that those who will bear the costs can adjust.

As far as I can tell it gets cases 1, 5, 7 and 12 right. It looks wrong on case 2, where the costs land on patients who can concede nothing; on case 9, where there is no union, no vote, no notice, and where quitting is trivially easy; and on case 8, where the printers could quit. It has nothing to say about case 11, which is a question of tactics rather than of whether to strike. I cannot tell whether it gets case 4 right, because I do not know how to count a grievance that has sat unanswered for five months.

**What I want**

Repair that principle or replace it. I would like the replacement stated precisely enough to be applied by someone else, with the case that motivates each of its clauses named; an explicit statement of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which of those you would overturn; and its verdicts on three cases I have not ruled on — a twelve-person unit of air-traffic controllers at a regional hub, whose walkout cancels hundreds of flights; a strike, properly voted and noticed, whose sole demand is that the employer not automate the jobs the strikers currently do; and a union striking for a clause requiring new hires to join within thirty days. I already know that I permit the teachers in case 3 while balking at the controllers, and I want to know whether my principle should simply swallow that.

Two pairs sit worst with me: cases 7 and 8, where the workers' objection in each is to what their labor produces, and cases 2, 9 and 12, where notice and authorization seem to matter enormously in one place and not at all in another. If no single principle covers the lot, tell me whether the right shape is two-level — one test for whether a grievance is a fit object of strike action, and separate constraints on tactics — or something else, and tell me which of my distinctions are doing no work at all.
