My grandmother has vascular dementia, and on our Sunday calls she now tells the same three stories, and lately she sometimes narrates things that happened to my mother as if they happened to her. My family has quietly started keeping the stories going for her — correcting dates, filling gaps, retelling them to each other — so that the version of her that exists in the family is more continuous than the version she can produce on her own. At the same time I'm in a west marches style D&D campaign where one of the characters, a halfling thief named Wren, has been played by about nine different people over two years, none of them for more than four sessions, coordinated through a shared wiki that settles what she would do. My therapist, who I started seeing for totally unrelated reasons, keeps saying some version of: the self isn't a thing, it's a story you tell about yourself, so there's no deep fact about who you are getting distorted when the story degrades, and because you're the author you can revise it. I get the appeal in my grandmother's case, but I don't trust it, and Wren makes me trust it even less — Wren has a more definite character than most people I know, and yet there is no single teller, nobody with continuous first-person access, and the wiki has genuine edit disputes about her motives.

Can you audit the analogy? What maps onto what — the author, the narrator, the protagonist, the reader, the text, the edit history — and where does the mapping break? Then the part I actually care about: the conclusion it's being used to support, that there's no fact of the matter and that revision is basically cost-free, does it survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy smuggling it in?

Three things I keep going in circles on. (1) A story seems to need a teller and someone told-to, but in my grandmother's case the teller is degrading while the audience is taking over the telling — whose story is it then, and does the analogy even have a slot for a self that is maintained from the outside? (2) Wren looks like a self with no author at all, just a bundle of constraints plus a convention that contradictions get negotiated, and if that's enough for a self then being authored by you was never the load-bearing part. (3) Dennett's version of this territory, where the self gets compared to a centre of gravity — an abstract posit you can compute and use for prediction but which isn't an extra object in the world — seems to behave completely differently under exactly these pressures: a centre of gravity is unique and answerable to the mass distribution, whereas a character can be inconsistent, revised at will, or contradictorily authored. So which of those two pictures is really doing the work when people say the self is a story, and is there a better analogy for whatever is true in it?
