I need a favour: I'm giving a talk in three weeks and I need someone to play the audience for me, because I know I'm going to get eaten.

Some background so you know who you're dealing with: I've got no academic background at all. I left school at seventeen, I work in the stores department of a hospital (inventory, stock codes, part numbers, chasing people about missing deliveries) and I muck about with a telescope at weekends. About two years ago I picked up a philosophy of language book in a charity shop and got completely stuck on Frege's puzzle, and I've been poking at it on my own since then, mostly on buses. I've ended up with a home-made account that I actually think is right, or at least is right about something, and the local philosophy group has let me have a slot.

Here's the talk, roughly. The claim is that the difference between saying the morning star is the morning star and saying the morning star is the evening star has nothing to do with meaning or with what the sentence says — both just say of one object that it is itself — and everything to do with checking. On my account, when you have a name for something, what you've really got is a routine for going and locating it: look in the western sky just after sunset, or look at the number stamped on the valve and match it in the catalogue, or watch for the white rump as it flies away, or just ask Dave. Two names differ, in the way that matters here, when they come with routines that could in principle deliver different things without either routine counting as broken — I call them independent routines. An identity sentence is informative exactly when its two sides hang off independent routines. And what it gives you when it is informative is not a new fact about the world at all, but a licence to merge your records: after you've got it, you may use either routine for either purpose. Then the bit I think people will really hate: once you know, going on running both routines side by side is not irrational and not a failure to put your knowledge together, it's just bookkeeping, because the routines cost different amounts, they fail in different ways, and one of them is no use at all in the morning.

The cases I'm building it on: the star one; two different part numbers that turn out to be the same valve; a bird I can pick out either by its white rump or by its dipping flight and only later found out was one species; and then the case I can't handle properly, where someone has a name whose only routine is ask someone else, or has no routine at all, like a name they've only ever heard in a story. And there's a collapse case I'm genuinely scared of: two names learned from the same person on the same occasion in the same way, where I have exactly one routine, and yet telling me they're one and the same still seems to land as news. I don't have an answer to that one.

The audience: it's a monthly group that meets in the back room of a pub, about twenty people, and from the meetings I've been to I can tell you who talks. There's Malcolm, who did a philosophy degree in the nineties and has never got over it; he will say the whole thing is just Frege's sense-reference distinction with the serial numbers filed off, and he'll bring up cases about a pianist who became a politician and about people who don't believe things they'd agree to. There's a retired software engineer called Pat who'll say that if learning an identity isn't acquiring a proposition then I need to say what on earth it is, and she'll want to know what my account does with two variables pointing at the same object. There's a poet who thinks the star example is about poetry and not logic and will want to know why I've drained all the wonder out of it. There's someone who comes for the mysticism and always asks whether we're just talking about words. And there's the chair, who keeps time and will at some point say, in a friendly way, so what — as in, if you're right, what changes. Assume nobody there is hostile, they're just interested, and assume they're all cleverer than me and that at least two of them have read quite a lot of this stuff.

What I want from you is a prep sheet for the question period, which is about twenty minutes. Give me the objections I'll actually get, in the order they'll probably come and roughly by how much damage they'd do, and each one written the way that particular person would actually say it, in their own words and with their own hobbies showing, not translated into tidy philosophical English — I want to recognise them when I hear them. For each, give me a reply I could say out loud in about ninety seconds, and be straight with me about where the reply is thin, because I'd rather know now than find out at the table. Tell me which one or two things I should just concede in the first two minutes before anyone opens their mouth, and tell me which single reply has to actually work or the whole talk is a waste of everyone's evening. And include the question I'm going to be quietly praying nobody asks, with an honest note about what I should do if they do.

Last thing on format: please write the whole thing as one continuous piece of prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just paragraph after paragraph the way you'd write an email, and number the objections inline in the text as you go, 1) 2) 3) and so on. I'm going to have my phone read it to me on the walk to the pub, so anything that only works on the page is no use to me. And please don't tell me to go and read anything — the library doesn't have it and I can't afford it, so whatever you give me has to be stuff you can say in your own words.
