I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I need someone to think in straight lines for a bit, because my characters keep arguing in circles and I genuinely cant tell which of them is right.

The book I'm drafting is three generations of one family and a house. Big shabby house on the edge of a town that went up in value mostly because the town did — nobody in the family did anything to earn that. Act two is basically the tax bill. There's a brother who stayed and looked after the mother for eleven years, a granddaughter who never met the grandmother and gets an exactly equal share, and a cousin who thinks the whole thing should just be sold.

The thing I keep circling is how the law actually handles a transfer at death. As I understand it (and if I've got some detail of real tax law wrong, just analyse the practice as I describe it — I'm not asking you to look anything up):

- the tax, where there is one, is charged on the dead person's estate, on the transferor's side, above a big exemption; the people who recieve the money generally don't pay tax on getting it, even though if the same sum had arrived a week earlier as salary they absolutely would have
- assets that have gone up in value get re-valued at the date of death, so gains that were never realised are never taxed at all — as if the appreciation happened to nobody
- but hand the same thing over while you're still alive and the clock matters: in some systems a gift only escapes if you survive a set number of years, and there are small annual allowances for passing money on early, so the rules seem to be watching the donor's intentions and not just the recipient's bank balance
- and the family home, or the family farm, gets reliefs that a portfolio of shares doesn't get

So: what does this bundle presuppose? I don't mean what's the best defence of it. I mean what it only makes sense under. Does it presuppose that a household is one economic unit rather than a set of separate persons? That the dead still own things, or that dying is a taxable event in a way that turning thirty isn't? That receiving money isn't income? That there's something special about a house or a farm as against a share certificate — or is that last bit just politics wearing a philosophical costume?

Please also be strict about which parts of the practice strictly need the theory and which parts several quite different theories would happily license. I want to know which arguments in my book are real disagreements about justice and which are just squabbling about rates.

Then the bit I'm actually stuck on. I'm toying with setting an alternate version in a made-up country that does it completely differently. There, nothing is taxed on the first passage of wealth — you can leave whatever you like to whoever you like, once. But every later passage of the same asset is taxed harder than the last: an asset that has already been inherited once is taxed steeply if it's inherited again, more steeply the third time, and the revenue goes into a fund that hands every eighteen-year-old a one-off cash stake. I've been calling it the second-passage rule. What does that presuppose instead — about how far an earner's claim over what they made survives them, about whether what's objectionable about dynastic wealth is its size at a moment or the number of hands it has passed through, about whether the right comparison class for equal opportunity is a lifetime or a generation?

And finally: which of my three characters does each arrangement quietly side with, and which arrangement gives me the fight that's really about something?
