I'm a novelist (two books out, third one dragging) and I've got a plotting problem that keeps curdling into a philosophy problem. There's a minor character in the new manuscript — a senior private secretary to a minister, late 50s, genuinely competent, the person who actually makes things happen. He never decides anything. What he does is take the option his minister has already half-chosen and make it administratively inevitable: he finds the precedent, drafts the memo so the awful choice reads as routine compliance, picks the euphemism that will survive a leak, and then quietly handles the fallout for years. He isn't cynical and he doesn't get promoted for it. A philosopher friend says this isn't the dirty-hands problem at all, because dirty hands is supposed to need an agent who chooses, who does wrong for the sake of the right, and who is then left guilty and blameworthy in a way that doesn't cancel out the fact that he did the right thing. On that picture my guy is just an accomplice, or a bureaucrat, or a case of moral luck about who ends up in the room. But I've started calling what he has 'delegated dirty hands' — the hands that get dirty are the ones that polish the glass, that launder a decision into something that can be done without anyone having to feel like they did it — and I want to know whether that's a real extension of the idea or whether I'm just misusing a technical term to make my character sound interesting.

So: I've been rereading Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day, because everyone told me it was the book about this, and I want it read properly through the dirty-hands lens rather than as wallpaper. Lord Darlington is the one with the political role, and he does seem to think he's acting for peace and honour, and he's destroyed by it. But Stevens is the one the novel is actually about, and his whole claim about himself is that a great butler doesn't have opinions — he serves, he doesn't choose, he takes his dignity from the quality of the service rather than the worth of the cause. So my questions: is the novel a case of dirty hands with no decision in it? Does the concept actually require the agonised moment, or does Ishiguro show a gap in it — that the morally interesting damage is done by the people who make the wrongdoing possible and presentable and then are left with nothing to feel guilty about, only a sense of a wasted life? And where does the book resist that reading honestly: is Stevens's failure really about politics at all, or is it about his father, and Miss Kenton, and a certain idea of dignity, so that pressing the political lens on it flattens the thing I actually like about it? I don't want a reading that turns the novel into an illustration of a thesis.

Concretely, what I want back: two or three scenes I'd point a reader to and what each one shows (I'm thinking the stuff with the Jewish maids early on, the conference at the hall, and the bit near the end on the pier, but tell me if I've got the wrong ones), a straight answer on whether my private secretary is a dirty-hands case or merely an accessory, and — this is the bit I care about most — whether dirty hands has to involve the agent's own sense of guilt. Could somebody have dirty hands while sincerely believing they have no hands at all? Because if the theory says guilt is essential and Stevens mostly feels loss, I'd rather know that than smooth it over. And does it matter for my guy whether he thinks the policy is genuinely right, thinks it's a necessary evil, or has stopped thinking about it entirely and just does the job well? I'd guess those three are different cases, but I can't say why.
