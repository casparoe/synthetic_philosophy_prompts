Setup, so it's self-contained: the proposal under discussion was "AI safety via debate." You have a question whose answer you can't verify yourself; two AI systems argue opposite sides; a human judge who can't check the answer directly picks the winner. The pitch is that this lets a weak human supervise a much smarter system, because each side is incentivized to point out where the other one goes wrong. The fight was about whether any of that survives once the debaters are smart enough to obfuscate.

(1) Priya: The whole thing dies on obfuscated arguments. A debater much smarter than the judge can win by producing reasoning the judge can't actually follow — buried premises, chains long enough that no individual step looks bad, a confident surface over a gap. And notice the direction of the effect: the smarter the systems get, the worse this gets, so the technique fails exactly in the regime we wanted it for.

(2) Sam: But both sides can do that, and the format is adversarial. If one side buries something, the other side's best move is to dig it up and say "premise 14 is doing all the work, and here's why it's false." Obfuscation only pays if the judge rewards apparent sophistication, and you can train judges to reward checkability instead of fluency.

(3) Priya: Digging it up isn't free. To show that a long argument is unsound you have to locate where it breaks, and locating that can be as hard as answering the original question. So you get a regress — check the argument, then check the critique, then check the critique of the critique. And there's an asymmetry on top of it: muddying is cheaper than clarifying. It's faster to write a misleading one-paragraph summary of a study than to explain what the summary distorts, so the dishonest side gets more moves per unit of judge attention.

(4) Sam: That's an argument against a passive judge, not against debate. Run it as a decomposition protocol: every claim has to be broken into sub-claims until each one is small enough for the judge to evaluate on its own, so nothing the judge ever has to assess is large. And if even the atomic claims are too hard for a human, you don't need a human at the bottom — run debate recursively, with weaker AI systems judging the sub-debates.

(5) Priya: Recursion just moves the bump in the rug. Now the sub-judges are the weak link and you have the same problem one level down, plus a new one: the thing you're actually placing trust in is a system nobody supervises, and you've hidden that behind a protocol diagram. Also, obfuscation isn't only complexity. It's presentation. True claims are frequently hedged, boring, and full of caveats; a false chain can be made to sound crisper and more decisive. In domains where the evidence is genuinely messy — long-run forecasting, macro stuff, most of philosophy — there is no clean fact that gets more robust the longer you cross-examine it. Cross-examination helps when the truth already has a shape the judge can recognize. It doesn't manufacture that shape.

(6) Sam: I'll grant you messy domains. But I think you're relying on a stronger claim than you need. The judge doesn't have to reach the truth, only to pick the better argument — and there's a real structural asymmetry favoring honesty, which is that for a false claim there's always a true counter-move available that survives scrutiny, whereas for a true claim there's no false move that survives indefinitely. So the honest side has more ground to stand on and the dishonest side runs out of ground.

(7) Priya: "Indefinitely" is doing all the work there. Judges have finite patience, finite attention, and finite working memory for a tree of claims. If the honest side wins only in the limit and the liar wins at twenty minutes, the liar wins.

(8) Sam: That's an engineering constraint, not a refutation. It says the judge needs scaffolding — notes, a lower branching factor, more time — not that the idea is broken. Courts and peer review are both degraded versions of this and both are clearly better than nothing, and nobody concludes from the existence of a bad lawyer that adversarial truth-seeking is incoherent.

For what it's worth, the reason it came up at all is concrete: our group is trying to decide whether to believe a contractor who says the house we rent needs about forty thousand dollars of foundation work. Priya said that's the obfuscated-argument problem in miniature, since none of us can evaluate his reasoning and none of us can afford to learn enough to. Sam said no, in the real world you get a second opinion, which is basically a second debater, and that works okay-ish. I have no idea who's closer to right, in either the small case or the big one.
