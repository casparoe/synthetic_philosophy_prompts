I am a high school senior, and next month I am presenting to my school's Academic Integrity Committee, which is rewriting its policy on AI tools in homework. The committee is four classroom teachers (history, mathematics, English, biology) who mainly want to know whether a submitted piece of work is genuinely the student's own; our AP Computer Science teacher, who is entirely comfortable treating cognition as computation and saying that the substrate does not matter; our librarian, who teaches information literacy and thinks almost exclusively in terms of source evaluation and attribution; the dean of academics, who cares above all about what is enforceable and about what precedent any rule sets; and two student representatives who want fewer rules and distrust any policy that cannot be applied consistently.

The thesis I am defending is that the policy should stop treating AI assistance as one thing. I want to argue that in a specifiable range of cases an AI tutor is not a substitute for a student's thinking but a part of it. I am reasoning by analogy with the well-known notebook case from the extended-mind literature, in which a man with a memory impairment carries a notebook everywhere, consults it automatically and without weighing it, and trusts what it says; if the notebook can count as part of his mind, I argue, then a device can count as part of mine.

The conditions usually offered in that literature — that the resource be reliably available, that its contents be automatically endorsed rather than deliberated over, and that it be easy to consult — strike me as too weak on their own, because a student who pastes an answer they cannot explain seems to satisfy all three. So I add two of my own. First, counterfactual sensitivity: a resource is part of your cognitive system only if, were it silently altered or removed, the difference would show up as a difference in what you believe and in what you would be willing to say or do, and not merely as a difference in what you hand in. Second, provenance transparency: you must be able to say, when asked, which parts of your answer came from the device and why you accepted them. Where both hold I call the case extension. Where the first fails but the device is still consulted automatically and trusted I call it borrowed cognition, which I treat as its own category rather than as cheating. Where neither holds it is outsourcing, which the policy can handle the way it already handles copying from a friend.

There is one consequence of my own view that I do not have a good answer to, and I would rather hear it from you than from the room: if the tutor really is part of my mind, then the company that retrains or silently updates the model between my sessions is in a position to change what I believe, and I cannot say why that is different from someone rewriting my notebook while I sleep.

What I would like is a Q&A prep sheet, as exactly five numbered items. Each item should be two sentences: the first is the objection as the person raising it would actually say it in that room, in their own vocabulary and from their own concerns, and the second is what I should say back, together with a note on where that reply is weakest. Please name the committee member raising each item, order the items from the most damaging objection to the least, and make the fifth item the question I am hoping nobody asks, wherever it would otherwise rank. No introduction, no conclusion, and nothing besides the five items.
