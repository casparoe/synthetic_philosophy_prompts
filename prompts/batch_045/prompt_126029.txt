**Slots, Not Persons: Filling-Relative Assessment for Identity-Affecting Choice** *(notes)*

**§1. Where I take the debate to be stuck.** In the familiar family of cases — a girl conceives at fourteen rather than waiting; a government depletes rather than conserves; prospective parents select for a child who will be deaf when they could have selected otherwise — the agent's act is a condition of the existence of the individual it affects. Whoever ends up existing has a life worth living and would not have existed had the agent acted otherwise. So the act cannot be wrong in virtue of making someone worse off, and yet it seems wrong.

There are two familiar packages. Keep the person-affecting restriction and accept that such acts are not wrong, unless the life produced is not worth living at all. Or abandon the restriction for an impersonal axiology that ranks outcomes by total or average welfare, and accept that the wrongness of such acts is of a piece with the wrongness of failing to add further happy people to the world. I want to claim that these packages share an assumption, which I will call *identity-determined assessment*: the moral significance of an outcome is a function of which individuals it contains, so that outcomes differing only in who exists are morally incomparable unless one reaches for an impersonal value. The first package holds the assumption and pays in counter-intuitive verdicts; the second abandons it and pays in implications elsewhere.

**§2. The proposal.** Deny the assumption while keeping the relational structure of wrongness. Distinguish the *occupants* of an act's effects from the *slots* those occupants fill. Slots are the positions that the agent's practical question was about: my first child, the patient who receives this kidney, a resident of this city in 2120, the child of the violinist I hire. Feasible options in identity-affecting cases differ not merely in who fills those slots but in how well they are filled. Proposal: an act is pro tanto wrong when, relative to the practical question the agent actually faced, it fills some slot worse than a feasible alternative fills the corresponding slot, and fills no slot better. Call this *filling-relative assessment*.

**§3. The complaint.** On this view the child of the girl who conceived at fourteen has a genuine complaint, but not the counterfactual one — not that she would have been better off had her mother waited. The complaint concerns occupancy: the slot you filled, your first child, you filled below the level that was open to you, and you did it knowingly. Wrongness becomes a relation between an agent, a slot her choice filled, and the alternatives she had. It does not require that the complainant would have existed under any alternative, only that she occupies a slot the agent was in a position to fill well. So the view keeps no wrong without a possible complainant while dropping no complainant without counterfactual improvement. That combination is the whole point of the paper.

**§4. Two claimed payoffs.** First, the view generates no verdicts in different-number cases. Where the options differ in how many slots there are, there is no counterpart matching to perform and filling-relative assessment is undefined. The procreation asymmetry and the repugnant conclusion are therefore outside its scope. I intend to present this as a virtue rather than a gap, on the grounds that these are separate problems needing separate resources, but I am aware it may look like an evasion. Second, the view appears to describe what large-scale institutional reasoning already does: cost-benefit analysis, risk regulation and demographic forecasting compare outcomes slot-wise, by reference to positions and statistical lives rather than to identified individuals, and feel no embarrassment about it.

**§5. The soft spot: matching.** Filling-relative assessment needs a counterpart relation between slots in distinct outcomes, and identity cannot supply one. My suggestion is that the relation is supplied by the practical question: the slots are those the agent's deliberation was already about, and the matching is fixed by what she was choosing between. I expect this to be where the view fails if it fails. If the practical question can be redrawn freely — individuate the slots by ordering their occupants by welfare, and match in that order — filling-relative assessment becomes a maximising impersonal view in disguise. I gesture at a constraint, that slots must be individuated independently of the welfare levels of their occupants on pain of begging the question, but I do not think I have shown that the constraint bites.

**§6. The soft spot: propagation.** Consider an orchestra. A conductor can appoint a competent first-chair violinist now, or hold further auditions and appoint a stronger player in five years. Either appointment determines where a person lives, whom she meets, and hence which children come to exist; so the decision fixes the filling of slots indefinitely far downstream, and filling-relative assessment is now comparing whole genealogical futures. If slots propagate without limit, every hiring decision becomes an occasion for optimising the welfare of the descendants of every candidate, and the view collapses back into impersonalism. If they do not propagate, I owe a principled cut-off, and I do not have one. My current line is that the slots that matter are those the agent could have foreseen being affected; but foresight is scalar, which would make the verdicts of the view come in degrees where I had hoped for something binary.

**What I would like from you.**

2. Attack §5. What, if anything, constrains the counterpart relation? Try to construct a case in which the practical question is genuinely indeterminate between two matchings and the verdict flips depending on which one is chosen.
3. Is the propagation problem in §6 fatal, or is there a non-ad-hoc cut-off? Does the orchestra case show the view is wrong, or only that it must become a view about foresight — and would that be a cost or an unexpected gain?
4. Is the silence claimed in §4 defensible? A view that falls silent exactly where the number of possible people varies may turn out to have no advantage over the views it was meant to replace.
