I spent most of last night arguing with a friend — a working analyst with little patience for foundations — who insisted that constructivism is just classical mathematics with the law of excluded middle deleted, and that there is therefore exactly one constructivist position, differing from orthodoxy by a single axiom. I told her the logical space is far larger than that, and that the real fault lines are not about excluded middle in general but about which restricted instances of it you are willing to swallow. I now have to make good on this claim at a reading group next week: about ten people, graduate students in logic and in philosophy, roughly half of whom know nothing about intuitionistic logic beyond the slogan. I want to do it with an instrument rather than a lecture.

Please write me a classification tree that sorts a person from a starting commitment to a named position in the foundations of mathematics. The tree must be built from the logical space rather than from a list of school names: first the diagnostic questions and the answer-combinations they allow, then the actual positions located in that space, with empty combinations noted and diagnosed.

Constraints on the nodes:

- Start from a commitment almost everyone in the room shares: that there are statements about computations or about numbers whose truth value nobody currently knows. Do not open with any question of the form "do you accept the law of excluded middle?" — that question sorts people by label, not by belief.
- Every branch node must be a single question, phrased as a concrete case or claim, that someone who has never heard of intuitionism could answer. No node may contain the name of a principle, a school, or a person; those belong in the answer key only.
- The distinctions I want doing the work: bivalence for a not-yet-settled universal statement about a search that has so far found nothing; bivalence for a statement about the result of a computation that is bounded but whose bound is astronomically large (say 10^60 steps) and will never in fact be carried out; whether a computable, bounded, increasing sequence of rationals with no computable limit nevertheless has a limit; whether a total function from the reals to the reals that sorts numbers into "below zero" and "not below zero" counts as a function at all; whether the double negation of "this program halts" licenses the assertion that it halts; whether every total function on the naturals is given by an algorithm; whether a definition that quantifies over a totality already containing the object being defined is legitimate; and whether the binary sequence that is constantly zero just in case an arbitrary statement is false may always be assumed to exist (if you think this last one earns a node, include it with a one-line gloss; if not, drop it and say why).
- Required concrete cases, reused across nodes: a program that has searched 10^12 steps for an odd perfect number and not halted; a program that by construction runs at most 10^60 steps and returns 0 or 1; the "sign of a real number" function above; a Specker-type increasing bounded sequence of rationals with no computable limit; and the claim that every nonempty set of natural numbers has a least element, where the set is given only by an undecidable predicate.

Deliverable:

1. The tree itself: seven to nine branch nodes rendered as an indented numbered list with go-to numbers (I will typeset it as a flowchart later), each node ending in "if yes go to N, if no go to M".
2. An answer key that walks each path, names the position at the leaf, gives in parentheses the principle that node actually turned on, and attaches one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment. The leaves must include: classical or platonist mathematics; Bishop-style neutral constructivism; Brouwerian intuitionism with the continuity principle; recursive constructivism with Church's thesis and Markov's principle; the variant that keeps Church's thesis but refuses Markov's principle; a predicative position of the Weyl/Feferman sort; and strict finitism.
3. A short closing section covering three things: the answer-combinations your tree allows that nobody occupies, each diagnosed as provably inconsistent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored; the pairs of leaves your questions cannot separate, flagged rather than papered over; and one cell I want you to adjudicate. During the argument I half-invented a view I cannot locate in the literature and want to know whether it is a real option: it grants bivalence to every statement about the output of a computation that some agent somewhere will in fact be able to carry out, withholds it from all others, and accepts that all total functions on the reals are continuous for functions given by choice sequences but not for functions given by a law. Place it in your tree, or tell me it is unstable and why.

Do not include bibliographies, reading lists, or historical asides about who held what: the instrument has to work for someone reasoning from the cases alone.
