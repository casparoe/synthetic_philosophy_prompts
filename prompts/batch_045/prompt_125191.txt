**On Being Second (draft)**

In 2016 I published a novel whose central device — a first-person narrator discovered, late, to be transcribing another person's manuscript, so that the voice we have trusted turns out to belong to no one — was, I thought, mine. Eighteen months later a novel appeared in Serbian built on the same device. Neither of us had read the other; the manuscripts overlapped in time. A reviewer wrote that this showed such ideas to be *in the air*. I want to know whether that sentence means anything, and if it does, whether it was good news or bad news for the two of us.

I think *was it inevitable?* is two questions wearing one coat. Call the first *latency*: if the actual maker had never existed — not delayed, simply absent — how long before someone else produced something the relevant field would count as the same result? Call the second *idiom-dependence*: how much of the subsequent shape of the field is owed to the particular form in which the result first arrived, as opposed to the result itself? Latency is a question about the supply of problems and instruments. Idiom-dependence is a question about what a thing is *for*. Nothing I can see forces the two to move together.

The calculus looks like a case of short latency. The problems — tangents, quadratures, rates of change — were public property, and several people in the same decades had instruments in their hands good enough to attack them; had either Newton or Leibniz not lived, we would not have waited a century for the equivalent. But it also looks to me like a case of high idiom-dependence: what later mathematicians could *do* with the result depended on whose notation became habitual, and a notation outlives the proof that was first written in it. I have a vague memory that the British attachment to Newton's fluxional notation left English mathematics behind for a generation or so. If that is folklore rather than history, say so, because I lean on it.

Natural selection seems to me to be the opposite mixture: short latency as a claim, long latency as a case. What made the *Origin* matter was not the bare thought that selection might do the work — others had grazed that — but the accumulated argument, and nobody else had the accumulated argument. On that reading, Wallace's arrival functioned less as a rival idea than as a deadline. If that is right, then a discovery can be nearly inevitable while the thing that made it a discovery was not inevitable at all, and any measure of latency that looks only at the claim will report inevitability exactly where there was none.

Now the part that is really mine. In science an independent second arrival is corroboration: it raises your confidence. In art an independent second arrival is a defect: it lowers the value of both objects, and it gets the later one suspected of theft. My novel and the Serbian novel did not confirm each other. We damaged each other slightly, and the reviewers forgave neither of us for being two. The reason, I think, is that a result can be had twice without being used up — two people can stand on the same theorem — whereas a work is partly constituted by being the only thing of its kind. Its value includes its not having been foreseeable. So inevitability is praise when said of a theorem and an insult when said of a poem.

From this I want a conclusion about whether it matters who. I do not think it matters for the reason the priority disputes suppose. Priority is fought over as though it were a medal for arriving first; it is more like a bookkeeping convention, a way of recording whom the field should hold responsible for the aftermath — whose idiom it must now write in, whose blind spots arrived packaged with the insight, whose successors will spend a generation cleaning up. For a novel the aftermath is nearly the whole object. A novel matters only by altering what later novels can do, and there is no content of a novel separable from the idiom it is written in. So the novelist's version of the inevitability question is not *would someone else have written this* but *would the same thing have become writable*.

Here is why I distrust all of the above. Once a thing exists, the route to it looks short: with the result in hand, everyone can see that the pieces were already on the table. I know this from my own trade. A finished novel looks to me like the only book I could possibly have written, and my drafts prove the opposite. If that foreshortening is real and unavoidable, then latency estimated after the fact is worthless, *it was in the air* is not an explanation but a genre of remark, and my second paragraph makes a distinction we can never apply. I cannot tell whether I have refuted the distinction or only its abuse, and that is the sentence I most want someone trained in philosophy to look at.
