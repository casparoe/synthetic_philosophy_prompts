I have been working through higher-order thought (HOT) theories of consciousness — the family of views, associated chiefly with Rosenthal, on which what distinguishes a conscious mental state from an unconscious one is that the subject has a suitable higher-order representation of itself as being in that state — and I have built an argument against them that I no longer trust, because it seems to prove considerably more than I set out to prove. I want a diagnosis of the argument, not a verdict on the theory.

Take as the target a standard formulation:

(1) A mental state M of a subject S is conscious if and only if S has a non-inferential, suitably produced higher-order thought to the effect that S is here and now in M. Nothing about M's own intrinsic, non-relational features contributes to M's being conscious; the difference between a conscious state and an unconscious one is exhausted by the subject's standing in a certain representational relation to it.

(2) Higher-order thoughts are representations, and it belongs to the nature of a representation that it can misrepresent: a representation can exist and can have the content it has whether or not anything answers to it. A theory that denied this would not be a representational theory.

(3) So radical misrepresentation is possible in principle: S can have a higher-order thought with exactly the content specified in (1) while not being in M at all — indeed while never having been in M and having no disposition whatever to be in M. A congenitally blind subject with a higher-order thought of itself as now seeing red would be one instance; another is a subject fitted with an interoceptive monitor which, in a confabulation setting, delivers such thoughts by direct stimulation while the corresponding first-order system is silent.

(4) But (1) states a sufficient condition. Whenever its right-hand side is satisfied, S is conscious of being in M; that, on the theory, is just what it is for there to be something it is like for S to be in M.

(5) Hence in a case of radical misrepresentation S is conscious of being in M while not being in M. There is consciousness, but no state that is conscious: a what-it-is-like that characterizes nothing.

(6) That is incoherent. Phenomenal consciousness is not a free-floating property that a subject can instantiate without instantiating it in virtue of some state of that subject.

(7) So (1) must be restricted: the higher-order thought makes M conscious only if M exists and stands in the right relation to that thought — causal, or counterfactual-covariational, or something of the kind.

(8) But the restriction is a trap. If the added condition is spelled out in causal or covariational terms, then what makes M conscious is a matter of M's relations to a monitoring mechanism, and the higher-order thought is doing no work that a first-order state's representing itself could not do; the theory has quietly turned into a first-order theory while keeping the higher-order vocabulary. If the condition is instead left as an unanalyzed requirement of appropriateness or acquaintance, the theory has stopped explaining what makes a state conscious and has merely relocated the mystery into a primitive relation.

(9) Therefore HOT is false, and consciousness must be located in the first-order state's own properties.

My worry is that (9) is not where the argument stops, because the same reasoning seems to run against the first-order representationalism I would prefer to arrive at:

(10) On first-order representationalism, a state's being a conscious state is a matter of its having representational content of the right kind, and its phenomenal character is a matter of what it represents.

(11) By (2), such a state can misrepresent. In hallucination a subject has a conscious experience as of a red sphere with no red sphere represented.

(12) If the constraint in (6) were genuine — no consciousness without an existing item for it to be of — then hallucination would be incoherent too. Hallucination plainly is possible, so (6) is false and (5) was never an absurdity.

So my argument appears to refute every representational theory of consciousness or none of them, and in neither case has it told me what I wanted to know.

What I would like from you:

Name the step you reject, explain why that step looked in order when I wrote it, and price the alternatives — what each of the other candidate rejections would commit me to. If the flaw lies somewhere I have not listed, say where.

In particular, I would like you to address four points:

(a) Whether (4) is faithful to (1). (1) is a biconditional about a state's being conscious; (4) talks about a subject's being conscious of being in a state, and then (5) treats that as yielding a what-it-is-like. Is there a shift across those steps, or do they come to the same thing?

(b) Whether the dilemma in (8) is exhaustive, or whether a higher-order theorist can require the existence of the target state without ceasing to hold a genuinely higher-order theory — and, if so, what exactly such a theory would still be claiming that its first-order rival denies.

(c) Whether the dispute between an amended HOT theory and first-order representationalism is anywhere merely verbal. Suppose both sides agree that the consciousness-constituting representation must be non-inferentially produced by the state it represents. Is there anything left that they disagree about, and is any of it testable?

(d) The obvious reply to (12) is an asymmetry: in hallucination what fails to exist is the object of the experience, whereas in (5) what fails to exist is the state itself, and the state is supposed to be the vehicle rather than the object of consciousness. I want to know whether that asymmetry is real or whether it simply restates the point at issue.

For what it is worth, here is what I am reluctant to lose: (2), the possibility of hallucination, and the thought that a theory of consciousness should not end by declaring phenomenal properties to be brute intrinsic features of states. If keeping all three requires giving up (6), say so plainly — and then tell me what the higher-order theorist should say about the confabulating subject in (3). Is that subject conscious of red, or does it merely seem to itself to be conscious of red, and is there any way of telling those apart?
