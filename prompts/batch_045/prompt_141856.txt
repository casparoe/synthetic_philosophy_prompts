You are a philosopher of archaeology who also knows how museums actually work: someone equally at home with contemporary philosophy of mind, action theory, and social ontology and with the inferential situation of excavation records, small finds catalogues, and display panels. You are also practiced at turning contested interpretive principles into procedures that non-specialists can run. Work carefully and slowly: brainstorm first, commit second.

Start with a short section of visible brainstorming. List the distinctions from philosophy of mind, action theory, and social ontology that might do real work in what follows — regularity versus rule, belief versus acceptance, intention versus function, convention versus institution, meaning-for-them versus interpretable-for-us, assertion versus implication, use versus depiction, and any others you think belong — and for each, in one line, say what it would let a field team distinguish. Then say explicitly which two or three you are building the procedure on, which you are dropping, and why: either because a three-person team cannot apply them in ninety minutes, or because they don't bite on the cases below. I want to see the rejected options, not just the chosen ones.

The setting. I direct interpretation for a regional museum and heritage service. My team is three people: one finds specialist and two generalists with MA-level training. We produce about forty interpretive texts a year — display panels, object labels, teacher packs, and non-technical summaries in heritage impact assessments that planning committees then quote. Panels run to roughly sixty words at about a middle-school reading level. Each text is signed off by our head of collections, a trained historian who is not an archaeologist, and by our consultation officer where descendant communities are involved. The budget is about ninety minutes of specialist time per object group, working only from existing catalogue records, photographs, and old excavation notebooks, many of them poor. There is no money for new dating, residue analysis, or sampling.

The stakes are asymmetric. What we write becomes the public's sense of who lived here; it is repeated by schoolchildren, tour guides, and local press, and when a planning objection quotes our text it can affect what gets built. Our dominant failure mode is over-claiming: under the previous regime, ritual, priest, and fertility were used as filler for whatever we could not otherwise explain, and we have publicly committed to stopping that. Under-claiming has a cost too. Text that says only that a function is unknown reads as evasive, gets cut by exhibit designers, and is replaced by something worse written without us.

The deliverable. A written decision procedure that takes as input (a) an object or assemblage with its context record and (b) a proposed interpretive sentence, and outputs one of four verdicts: approved as written; approved only in a weakened reportive form, with the wording supplied; not licensed, with a minimal true statement to use instead; or hand off to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts.

Requirements.

One. Order the questions, and give each one a stopping rule: what counts as a sufficient answer, and what the team does when no answer can be had — no comparative assemblage, no secure context, no usable number, no ethnographic or historical analogue they can defend.

Two. Keep a three-way distinction in the output language, sharpening or replacing my cuts if you think they are wrong: reportive claims, about what the material record shows — form, material, association, depositional treatment, wear, breakage pattern; attributive claims, about what people intended, thought, or meant — this was deposited as an offering, this was made to be seen, this was treated as dangerous; institutional claims, about rules, offices, roles, or standing arrangements — a priesthood, a moiety system, a guild, a tribute obligation, a marriage rule. Say what would have to be true of the record for a claim to cross from one grade to the next.

Three. Test, do not assume, the load-bearing thesis I want the procedure built on: that an institutional claim needs something a belief claim does not — evidence that the practice imposed costs, generated enforcement, or was adhered to where it was locally disadvantageous — since a regularity of behaviour is not yet a rule. If you accept the thesis, build the cost-or-enforcement step into the procedure, with concrete examples of what does and does not count as a marker. If you think it is wrong or insufficient, replace it and say why.

Four. Include an explicit anti-residual step. Because ritual and symbolic function in our archive as wastebaskets, the procedure must require the team to write down the plain practical alternative, and to specify what would have had to be present for the object to count as ordinary equipment or refuse. If no such alternative can be formulated, the claim goes back to the reportive grade.

Five. Include an equifinality check: name at least two distinct processes that could have produced the pattern, then say what evidence would discriminate between them. Where nothing in the record discriminates, the verdict is not licensed.

Six. Supply a calibration set: five one-line object-plus-claim pairs, with the correct verdict and output wording for each, that my team can run in ten minutes to check they have understood the procedure. At least one pair must have the fourth verdict as its right answer.

Seven. Supply a one-paragraph audit-record template, in plain language, that lets a non-archaeologist check the reasoning and that we can keep on file.

Eight. Be honest about the procedure's limits. Mark which steps are genuine constraints and which are judgment calls dressed up as steps. Say where it will systematically misfire: unprovenanced museum objects, old excavations with unreliable context, small samples, analogy with historically or ethnographically recorded peoples, and typologies built on the very interpretation under test. Where a step depends on a distinction philosophers disagree about, do not adjudicate it in the abstract; say which way the procedure has to go, and flag it as a choice we could have made differently.

Then run the procedure on three live cases, showing the questions in order, the point where it stops, the exact wording you would let us print, and the audit paragraph.

Case A. Forty-one polished stone axes from a peat bog at the margin of a former lake, in two discrete clusters on a wooden platform: unused edges, nine deliberately broken, no settlement within three kilometres. Proposed label: weapons offered to a god of the lake. This is our flagship panel and the image is on our posters.

Case B. Sixty graves over roughly a century and a half in one region: near-identical beaker form and stamp decoration, bodies uniformly oriented with the vessel at the left shoulder, but wide variation in grave depth and in the quantity of accompanying goods. Proposed text: these burials show a ranked society governed by priests. I would like to keep as much of this as I can.

Case C. Painted panels on land we manage jointly with a descendant community: hook-shaped anthropomorphs, repeated bighorn sheep, dots and parallel lines; no excavation is permitted. Proposed text for the teacher pack: shamans painted these during vision quests.

At least one of the three must come out with a verdict I will not like. Do not soften it, and tell me which one it is and why I will resist it.

Constraints on the answer. No citations, no bibliography, no reading list — name positions by description, and if you need a proper name, flag it as something to check rather than asserting a source. Do not invent archaeological facts; where a verdict turns on something about the material record you are not confident of, say so and tell me what to look for instead. Panel wording must be printable: sixty words or fewer, no stacked hedges, and no bare statement that we do not know. Aim for under twenty-five hundred words, and keep the procedure itself usable without the theory.
