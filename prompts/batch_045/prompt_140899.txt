Setup, so we're working from the same page (please just use what's below, nothing to look up). Three social outcomes: x = the prude reads the novel, y = the lewd reads the novel, z = nobody reads it. Prude ranks z > x > y: he'd rather the book be pulped, but if someone has to read it he'd rather it be him, reading it in disgust, than the lewd enjoying it. Lewd ranks x > y > z: he'd rather read it himself than have it pulped, but what he'd really enjoy is the prude being made to read it. Now two conditions:

- Minimal liberalism: each person is decisive over one pair in their own personal sphere — prude decides the pair {x, z} (whether he reads it or nobody does), lewd decides the pair {y, z}.
- Pareto: if everyone prefers one outcome to another, society does too.

Prude picks z over x, lewd picks y over z, and everybody prefers x to y. So x beats y beats z beats x. No acyclic social ordering can give you unrestricted domain, Pareto and minimal liberalism at once.

Here's my working list of escape routes. Some are the standard ones everybody mentions; the rest are things people have mailed me or sprung on me at conferences, and I honestly can't tell which of them are genuinely different moves and which are the same idea in a differnt coat.

R1. Restrict the domain. Filter out "nosy" or meddlesome preferences — the whole clash depends on each guy caring what the other one reads. If no one's ranking depends on anyone else's protected conduct, rights and Pareto play nice again.
R2. Demote or drop Pareto. Rights are lexical side-constraints, not one value to be traded off against efficiency; or keep Pareto only for pairs where no right is implicated.
R3. Wrong object. Rights don't attach to social outcomes at all — a right is a menu of acts you may perform, or a constraint on the procedure, so modeling it as "decisiveness over a pair of social alternatives" was the mistake from the start.
R4. Live with cycles. Social "preference" doesn't have to be an ordering; add a status quo and an agenda and the cycling is administrable, maybe even democratically healthy.
R5. The veto-budget move. Model a right not as pairwise decisiveness but as a veto on any social move that shrinks your protected option set below some floor. The conflict with Pareto then isn't an impossibility, it's a budget question: how many vetoes can a society carry before it can't act.
R6. The serialization move. Rights get respected one at a time, in sequence, and what's compared are whole histories of who chose when — not end-states. On that framing there's no single triple (x, y, z) to cycle over, and the paradox looks like an artifact of squeezing a temporal process into a static function.
R7. The two-level move. Rights belong to the constitutional stage where you pick the rules; Pareto belongs to the in-rules stage where you pick outcomes. The contradiction only shows up if you flatten a two-level setup into one social choice function, and the flattening is the thing that should be rejected.
R8. The constructed-sphere move. What counts as "personal" is already a social settlement, so "decisiveness over my own reading" presupposes a non-aggregative prior agreement about which pairs are anybody's business. The paradox is diagnostic rather than destructive: it tells you that rights need a background settlement, not that they're incoherent.

What I want: rank these by how much of the original puzzle each one actually dissolves, as opposed to quietly relocating it into some other doctrine where it will bite again. Be decisive — give me numbers, not "it depends what you mean by rights." If a route is really just another one wearing a hat, or rests on a mix-up, mark it as such rather than scoring it.
