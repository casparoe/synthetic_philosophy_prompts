I'm a novelist — three books out, none of which have made me any money — and I've let myself get talked into doing a short talk at a writers' festival in the spring on "writing consciousness," which in hindsight was a stupid thing to agree to. My editor thinks the whole philosophy angle is pretentious and that I should just talk about how I wrote the scene where Marlow drowns, and honestly she might be right. But the usual panel on free indirect style bores me to tears, and a friend who does cognitive science pointed me at Dennett's attack on the Cartesian theater and the multiple drafts model, and I started thinking that maybe the interesting question isn't how we write consciousness but whether the thing we think we're doing — transcribing a finished private sequence — is even coherent. So I've been scribbling. Below are my notes, closer to a skeleton for a 12-minute talk than to an actual draft, and they're a mess.

The Cartesian theater, as I understand it: the picture we're all secretly carrying, where consciousness is a show put on at some place in the brain, there's a stage where information "arrives" and gets stamped as conscious, and consequently there's one privileged order in which things really seemed to you. Dennett says the mistake isn't believing in a stream of consciousness, it's believing there's a *place* and a *moment* where each bit of content becomes official. Multiple drafts replaces that with parallel, continuously edited, partly finished versions running at once; what you get out depends on how and when you probe it, and there's no finish line at which a draft becomes *the* conscious version. His evidence is stuff like the color phi phenomenon: a blue dot at the top of the frame, then a red dot at the bottom, flashed in quick succession, and people report a single dot moving and changing color abruptly partway along its path — and they report the color change as having happened *before* the second dot was ever shown. So the content you end up with gets revised after the fact, and there's just no answer to "at exactly which moment did the change enter consciousness." Similar flavor with the cutaneous rabbit: taps near the wrist then near the elbow, in rapid succession, and what you feel is a series of taps hopping evenly up the forearm through places that were never touched.

The bit I got wrong at first, and which I think is the key to making the talk more than a party trick: "draft" implies a draft *of* something final. There is no final edition. "What it was like for me" is itself one more retrospection, not the reading off of a canonical record. And then the bit I actually care about as a novelist. Every third-person past-tense line like "she felt the cold at her wrist and then, absurdly, calm" presents itself as a transcript of a finished inner sequence, but on this picture it isn't a transcript of anything — it's a settlement imposed on competing partial versions, and the past tense is doing sneaky work by making the settlement look like it was always the record. Good novelists are editors, not stenographers.

Here's the new bit I want to put at the center of the talk, and the part I most want you to attack. Call it the narrative cutaneous rabbit. Take the rabbit as a model for *reading*. If you place two sensations close together in the prose but far apart in the scene — "the cold at her wrist, and the draught at the back of her neck" — readers report a continuous sweep of sensory attention across the space in between, a movement that is nowhere in the sentence. I want to claim this isn't merely "imagery"; it's the reader's own constructive process filling in, the same way the forearm fills in taps that never happened. And, crucially, the reader will deny having filled anything in, exactly as subjects in the rabbit experiments insist they felt those taps. So the reader's first-person authority over their own experience of a sentence is no better than the subject's. Practical upshot: retroactive revision. If content can be revised by later entries, then a sentence placed *after* a scene can change what the reader seems already to have experienced without contradicting a word of what came before — after a loud party scene, "the room had been quiet the whole time, she realised." Nothing earlier is falsified; the draft is just revised. That's not a gimmick, it's how the machinery works.

Two things I'm already nervous about, so don't just pile on there: (a) whether the drafts model is a claim about experience at all, or only about the timing and order of content — I might be help myself to a strong reading when Dennett only wanted a modest one; (b) whether the rabbit is a low-level sensory effect that has no business being dragged into a claim about how readers build a scene out of prose, and whether I'm quietly assuming reading is perceptual enough for the analogy to hold at all.

What I'd like from you: read the notes as a skeptical but sympathetic reader and tell me, point by point, (1) where I've got the philosophy and the illusion data wrong or over egged — I'd rather be corrected now than from the audience; (2) whether the narrative-rabbit idea is actually doing any work or is just a decorative metaphor with a science-y name, and whether the retroactive-revision technique really follows from the drafts picture or whether I could get it from any old constructivist story about memory; (3) whether this would be worth listening to for 12 minutes by people who write fiction, and what you'd cut or add, including whether the whole framing is a dud. Please don't be polite about it — if its a dud, say its a dud. And for each of your main verdicts, tell me how confident you are and what would change your mind: which argument, which empirical result, or which distinction I've missed would make you drop the narrative-rabbit claim, or concede it's stronger than you first thought, or tell me the drafts model doesn't license anything about narration at all. If the weakest link is somewhere I haven't noticed, that's the most useful thing you can tell me.
