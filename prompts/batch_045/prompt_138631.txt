The room is mostly ML people who think in expected-value, reward-model, and distribution-shift terms, plus two engineers who keep saying "isn't that just a guardrail with extra steps," and one visiting philosopher who's a hardline consequentialist and who already emailed me about some 19th-century utilitarian's doctrine of "esoteric morality" (I think it's Sidgwick) which I don't really no how to respond to.

My thesis: AI systems should be built to reason like deontologists — treat some action-types as off-limits without running the tradeoff — but purely as a commitment device, not because deontology is true. Two arguments. First, the delegation asymmetry: we hand tasks to AI exactly where our own moral uncertainty is highest and oversight is thinnest, and that's precisely where we should give the delegate the least discretion to recompute the tradeoff from scratch. Second, copy-correlation: a deployed model isn't one agent, it's a population of instances that can't coordinate with each other, so each instance maximizing expected value is the bad Nash equilibrium, while a shared side-constraint is the correlated equilibrium that no single instance would choose on its own. Conclusion: consequentialist reasoning at the design level, hard constraints at the object level.
