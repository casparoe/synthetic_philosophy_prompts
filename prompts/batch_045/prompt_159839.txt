Both paradoxes are generated by the same three principles. The first is the threshold principle, often called the Lockean thesis: it is rational to believe a proposition p whenever one's credence in p lies above some threshold t short of 1. I shall take t = 0.99. The second is closure: if it is rational to believe p and it is rational to believe q, it is rational to believe p and q, and similarly for any finite collection. The third is consistency: a jointly inconsistent set of beliefs is never rational. These three cannot all be true.

In a fair lottery of 1,000 tickets with exactly one winner, each ticket has a 0.999 chance of losing, so the threshold principle makes it rational to believe of each ticket that it will lose; closure then makes it rational to believe their conjunction, that is, that no ticket will win; but the rules of the lottery make it rational to believe that some ticket will win. The paradox is at bottom a result about the probability calculus: it shows that no coherent credence function can assign probability 0.999 to each of a thousand propositions whose conjunction has probability zero. The agent's degrees of belief are jointly incoherent, and that incoherence is what the word paradox registers.

The preface case, introduced by Kyburg alongside the lottery case, is the same argument in another setting. An author has good evidence for each of the many assertions in her book, and also knows that books of that length very rarely avoid error altogether. So she believes each assertion and believes as well that at least one of them is false. The same three principles then do the same work.

I think the two paradoxes are one argument. In the lottery, the belief that some ticket wins is supported by the rules of the lottery; in the preface, the belief that some assertion is false is supported by a base rate about error frequencies. These are merely two ways of arriving at a high credence in the negation of the conjunction of one's other beliefs, and once that is in place the mechanism is identical. So the preface paradox is the lottery paradox with the claims playing the part of the tickets, and any solution to one is a solution to the other; the only substantial question is which of the three principles to surrender.

One might instead try to make the threshold sensitive to how many propositions are in play, so as to block the thousandfold conjunction; but that makes whether one may believe p depend on how finely the space of alternatives is carved up rather than on one's evidence about p, so I set it aside. The threshold principle is what makes fallibilism intelligible, and the consistency requirement is not negotiable, so it is closure that must go. Little is lost: denying closure simply records that one may rationally believe p, rationally believe q, and yet withhold belief from their conjunction, which is unsurprising, since probability is not preserved under conjunction — a conjunction may be far less probable than any of its conjuncts.

The database case confirms that the two paradoxes are one. The audit plays precisely the role played by the rules of the lottery: it tells the consortium that some claim in the database is false. Each claim has been checked twice, so the consortium's credence in each is comfortably above 0.99, and it believes each of them; and 0.02% of 10,000 is two, so the consortium has overwhelming reason to accept that at least one of its claims is false. The case is therefore a lottery case, and it shows that the distinction some writers draw between the two paradoxes — that one rests on a certainty and the other on a mere generalisation — makes no difference to the argument. The same three principles produce the difficulty in both, and so one solution covers both.
