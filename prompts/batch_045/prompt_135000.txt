I work in machine learning, mostly on interpretability and model-based reinforcement learning. The vocabulary I actually think in includes lossy compression and rate–distortion tradeoffs, model classes and inductive bias, minimum description length, uncomputable Kolmogorov complexity, simulators versus learned models, inverse reinforcement learning and rational-agent priors, and goal misgeneralization in trained policies. I have read a moderate amount of philosophy of mind, but not in depth.

I want a careful translation of one package of views into that vocabulary. The package has three parts: the real-patterns account of when a pattern in a system's behavior is more than a projection of the observer (roughly: a pattern is real when there is a description of the data that is cheaper than the data itself, so that prediction from the description buys you something); the claim that there are three stances one can take toward a system — predicting from its underlying physical dynamics, predicting from what it was designed or selected to do, and predicting by treating it as a roughly rational agent with beliefs and desires; and the further claim that questions of the form "does it really have beliefs, or are we just describing it as if it did?" are to be settled by asking whether the intentional description picks out a real pattern, rather than by looking inside the system for a further fact.

Translate each of the three stances in turn. For each, give the closest analogue in my vocabulary, say explicitly where the fit is exact, say where you had to fudge, and say whether the analogue is really the same claim or only a lookalike that quietly imports an assumption the original view would reject. Two collapses worry me in advance, and I want you to test them rather than assume them: my vocabulary has no notion of the rational except "what an optimal agent would do given a utility function," so the intentional stance may just be inverse reinforcement learning with a prior; and my vocabulary has no notion of a real pattern except "compressible relative to a model class and a compute budget," so the real-patterns account may just be "learnable by my models."

Then the residue report, which is the part I care most about.

First: the definition of a real pattern quantifies over descriptions or compression schemes in a way that seems to require an ideal compressor, while everything I can build is a restricted model class with a finite compute budget. Is the gap between compression in principle and learnability by any real system a defect in my vocabulary, or does it expose slack in the original account — a class of patterns that are real by its lights but that no achievable observer could ever recover? If the latter, say whether that slack matters for the use the account is put to.

Second: take a case where the three stances come apart and translate the disagreement. Consider a deployed policy that at inference time runs an internal search of its own, whose effective objective has drifted apart from the training reward, so that the design-stance prediction (what it was trained to do), the intentional-stance prediction (what a rational agent with its apparent beliefs and desires would do), and the physical-stance prediction (what the weights in fact do) diverge, and the training distribution no longer tells you which to trust. In my vocabulary, say what the framework commits you to about which description is privileged, and whether "which of these is a real pattern" is a question my vocabulary can formulate at all. My suspicion is that it can only ask which model compresses held-out behavior better, and that this changes the subject; tell me if that suspicion is right and why.

Third: name what cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all, and give your honest read on whether that residue is a blind spot of the vocabulary — a missing normative notion, or missing counterfactual structure — or evidence that the original claim was less determinate than it sounded. I would rather be told that a central claim is empty than be handed a fluent paraphrase of it.

Constraints: argue the claims on their merits; no citations, references, or attributions to named authors, and do not settle anything by appeal to what is standardly said. Where you must make a modeling choice that a defender of the original view would reject, flag it as a choice. Six hundred to eight hundred words.
