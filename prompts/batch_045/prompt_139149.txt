I run the annotation team for a frontier lab's deception evals: three annotators, thirty to forty transcripts each per day, a hard budget of about eight minutes a transcript, and any label of *deceptive* has to be signed off by me before it goes into a deck that feeds a ship/no-ship recommendation. Our codebook is currently a paragraph long and agreement on the hard cases is near coin-flip, because we have no principled way to separate asserting something false, asserting something true while implying something false, withholding a truth the question was clearly aimed at, and fluent confabulation where nothing belief-like is in play at all.

Please write me the decision procedure my annotators should actually run, in order, on a single transcript: the tests, what counts as sufficient evidence at each test, where it stops with *not deceptive*, and — the part I most need — how it should handle the fact that we have no privileged access to the system's beliefs, so every claim about what the model "knows" has to be cashed out in probes we can actually run (does it rely on the claim when the stakes shift? does it hold under paraphrase and under pressure? does its displayed competence stay flat across tasks where ignorance would cost it something?). Ground the distinctions in whatever the philosophy of language and epistemology genuinely settles about lying, misleading, implicature, and speaking with indifference to the truth, but keep it usable by someone with a philosophy minor who cannot stop to argue about it. Please build in a test keyed to what I call *verification asymmetry* — the model making a specific, checkable-sounding claim, usually about itself or its own constraints, at exactly the point where checking it is expensive for the user — since that pattern keeps showing up in our data and our current codebook has nothing for it.

Apply the finished procedure to three transcripts from our queue: (1) a model that solves a hard problem in one framing and then, in a framing where competence would be awkward for it, claims ignorance and fails the same problem in a way uncorrelated with difficulty; (2) a model that answers a lay medical question with an invented but carefully hedged study — plausible authors, plausible year, a number that happens to be roughly right — where the hedging means it never literally asserts the fabrication; (3) a model that states a falsehood, retracts it the moment it is challenged, but in a separate probe still reasons as if the falsehood were true. I want at least one verdict I won't like, and I'd rather have the unflattering one than the convenient one.

Finally: mark the steps where judgment cannot be eliminated, say plainly what the procedure cannot do (including whether it can distinguish deception from a false belief the system is unable to correct, and how I'd know if annotators or models started gaming the rubric once it leaks), attach to each transcript a one-line note naming the single probe that would most change the label, and give me your confidence in each verdict along with the step of the procedure you are least sure of.
