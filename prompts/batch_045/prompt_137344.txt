I'm in a tiny reading group on Victorian evolutionary ethics — me, a historian of science, and a retired philosopher who keeps reccommending Mackintosh to anyone who'll listen — and we got completely stuck on one passage in Darwin's Descent of Man (1871), chapter IV ("Comparison of the Mental Powers of Man and the Lower Animals—continued"). Here it is, with a bit of the run-up:

"In the same manner as various animals have some sense of beauty, though they admire widely different objects, so they might have a sense of right and wrong, though led by it to follow widely different lines of conduct. If, for instance, to take an extreme case, men were reared under precisely the same conditions as hive-bees, there can hardly be a doubt that our unmarried females would, like the worker-bees, think it a sacred duty to kill their brothers, and mothers would strive to kill their fertile daughters; and no one would think of interfering. Nevertheless, the bee, or any other social animal, would gain in our supposed case, as it appears to me, some feeling of right or wrong, or a conscience."

A few paragraphs earlier he says the moral sense "has a rightful supremacy over every other principle of human action" and is "summed up in that short but imperious word ought, so full of high significance", and he builds up to the passage with a four-step mechanism: social instincts plus sympathy (confined to the same association), the misery of an unsatisfied instinct once memory is good enough to keep past impulses passing through the mind, the common opinion of the community becoming the paramount guide once language arrives, and habit. Then, just after, he says the inward monitor would tell the animal that it would have been better to have followed one impulse rather than the other.

What we can't agree on is what the bee thought experiment is actually doing. Our historian floated something she calls the "sacredness-invariance" reading: on Darwin's account what's inherited isn't any particular duty-content but the feel of an imperative, which supposedly stays put while the content swings around with social conditions — on that reading the bee case shows the feeling is content-independent, and Darwin's genealogy isn't debunking at all, it's just re-sourcing. The philosopher says that's backwards, and that the point of "no one would think of interfering" is that a conscience is constitutively social: you can't have an inward monitor whose verdict nobody else could possibly second. I want to say both of them are sliding past the word "better", and that the real puzzle is where a comparative notion like that comes from on a mechanism that only compares impulses for strength, duration and vividness of recall.

Can you help me get clear? Exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no intro and no conclusion: (1) what the clause "and no one would think of interfering" is doing — setup, prediction, or an actual step in the argument; (2) whether "sacred duty" is meant to sting, i.e. whether Darwin is quietly importing the religious vocabulary he naturalizes elsewhere in the book, or just reaching for a Victorian intensifier; (3) which of our two readings fits the four-step mechanism better, and where each of them strains against it; (4) whether "better" in the inward-monitor sentence is unearned normative residue or something his own account can generate; (5) if you buy the invariance reading, whether Darwin is stuck saying the bee-world sister's conviction carries exactly the authority ours does, and what he could say if you pressed him on that.
