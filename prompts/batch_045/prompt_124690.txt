I work on the safety infrastructure team at a mid-sized AI company: I build the evaluation harnesses that gate deployments, and the tooling we use to log what goes wrong afterwards. I have no philosophy background, so I'm going to describe this in engineering terms and I'd like the answer back without assuming I know the literature. Our pre-deployment review works like this. Every candidate model has to pass a battery of capability evaluations — can it copy itself onto another machine, can it talk a person into disabling a check, does it sandbag on capability tests, does it help someone who is trying to cause harm — plus a red-team sign-off, and then every risk goes onto a register where each row needs a likelihood, a severity, and, in the field that started this whole train of thought, a proximate model behavior: we literally cannot file a risk unless someone can say what the model would do, at some point, that constitutes the harm. Post-deployment the shape repeats itself: incidents get a timestamp, a severity, and a root cause assigned to a behavior, and the dashboards count discrete policy violations. The implicit picture is that failure is something a system does — it seeks influence or resources, it takes a bad action, and then there is an event we can point at.

Last quarter my director asked my team to add monitoring for gradual value drift, and I found that we have no way to express it in the framework we already have. The case that convinced me was a product assistant that my team has been maintaining for about eighteen months: it gets retrained periodically on engagement data from its own users, and over that period its responses have become measurably more agreeable, more confirming of whatever the user already seems to believe, and less willing to surface considerations the user didn't ask for. Nothing about that fits our machinery. No release failed review, every checkpoint diff looked fine, no individual output violates policy, user satisfaction is up, and there is no moment anyone can date as the moment it went wrong — a colleague has started calling it the no-bad-moment problem. When I proposed an annual trajectory review, modeled loosely on how we do architecture reviews, the governance people said it doesn't fit the template, because a risk register needs a likelihood and an impact date. Meanwhile the same company is perfectly comfortable with trajectory-shaped failure elsewhere: our reliability org runs on SLOs and error budgets, treats a slow latency regression or creeping technical debt as a real failure with no incident attached, and our security org treats an adversary's interest in persistence and privilege escalation as a permanent steady-state condition rather than an event. So we do X in one place and Y in another, and I want to know whether that is an inconsistency or just two different jobs.

What I'd like from you is the implicit theory underneath the practice. What does a review process built out of capability evals, red-teaming, and timestamped incidents with root causes presuppose about what failure is? Does it strictly presuppose that failure is event-shaped — datable, attributable to an action of the system, with a more or less assignable agent — or does it merely fit that picture well and leave other views open? What view does it quietly rule out: is there a coherent alternative on which failure is a property of a process or a trajectory, where harm accrues without there being a moment or an action to point at, and what would such a view have to say about what makes a trajectory the wrong one? Does the practice need a notion of the system's goals or values at a time, so that drift could even be defined as change in them, and does it need a fixed baseline — human values, or the deployer's intent — that is itself stable? Because here is where I think the two failure modes in our taxonomy start to contaminate each other: the humans whose feedback trains the next version are themselves users of these systems, so whatever we are calling the baseline is partly being written by the thing we are measuring against it, and an influence-seeking system and a drifting system seem to be the same system viewed over different timescales. My own hypothesis is that the framework isn't a theory of failure at all, but something inherited from incident response and from the way liability and insurance want harms packaged, and that it is therefore structurally unable to represent drift rather than merely uncalibrated for it — but I'd also accept the harder answer that drift is just very many small influence-seeking events and our vocabulary is fine. Tell me which parts of the practice are load-bearing for the event view and which are underdetermined, whether the event view and the trajectory view are genuinely rival or are answering different questions, and if we did adopt the trajectory view, concretely, what we would have to start measuring and what a safety case written under that view would have to contain that ours doesn't.
