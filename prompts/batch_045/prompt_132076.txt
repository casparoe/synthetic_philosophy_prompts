I've been reading the rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of fifteen years, and next month I'm giving a thirty-minute slot — twenty minutes of talk, ten of questions — at a London meetup whose regulars are mostly AI-safety-adjacent. The room will be roughly: three people who do interpretability or alignment work at a lab; two governance and policy types who spend their days on frontier-model regulation; a couple of effective altruists who read the same blogs I do; and one constitutional lawyer who came along because a friend dragged her. Assume the first group has Löb's theorem, mesa-optimisation and corrigibility as working vocabulary; that they think in terms of invariants, of what an optimiser would actually do rather than what could in principle happen, and that they are reflexively hostile to anything that looks like an a priori argument with no empirical bite. Assume most of them have played nomic at some point. Assume I am a software engineer with no graduate training in philosophy or law, and that I would much rather concede something early than be caught bluffing.

The subject is nomic, the self-amending parlour game in which the initial rules include a rule for changing the rules. I'm using a stripped-down variant I call Flat Nomic: eleven ordinary rules plus one amendment rule, A0, which says that any rule, including A0 itself, may be amended or repealed by a simple majority provided notice is given one turn in advance. No rule is immutable, there is no umpire standing outside the system, and the only fact of the matter about whether a move was legal is what the rules currently say.

The definitions on my slide are these. Reachable(S) is the set of rule-sets obtainable from S by finite sequences of legal amendments. S is trivial if Reachable(S) is every rule-set expressible in the language. S is frozen if Reachable(S) is {S} alone. And S is amendment-uniform if every rule, including the amendment rule, is amendable by exactly the same procedure, where that procedure screens only on who proposes, how many assent and when — never on what the amendment says.

My thesis has two parts. First, the no-corridor result: an amendment-uniform system is either trivial or degenerate, and the only degenerate cases work by starving the system of amendment-capacity — a per-player stock of amendment tokens, say — in which case what you get is not a principled corridor but a finite, unprincipled explosion, or a single point if the stock is one. There is no stable middle: you cannot have a system in which some but not all changes are legally available unless you introduce either an unamendable core or a content-restriction on amendment, and a content-restriction is itself just another mutable rule, so it buys you a corridor only until somebody amends it. The sketch: if the procedure screens only on proposer, assent and timing, and each of those conditions can be satisfied again afterwards, then I can iterate — first use A0 to replace A0 with a bald permission to enact anything by majority, then use that to enact any target rule-set — so the reachable set is everything; the only way to block the iteration is a condition that cannot be re-satisfied.

Second, the relocation claim: the two standard escape routes, an unamendable core or a two-tier procedure that makes the amendment rule harder to change than ordinary rules, do not restore safety but relocate it. Which tier a given move falls under is decided by no one outside the system, so the classification is made by the system's own current rules, and those are amendable; the higher law is higher only relative to a rule that can itself be changed. And this transfers: an AI system whose self-modification is supposed to be constrained by a written constitution has the same structure, because the constraint is only as good as the system's own classification of an internal change as a modification rather than as ordinary computation.
