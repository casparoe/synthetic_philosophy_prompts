For the past eighteen months I've been the person on my team who signs off on the wording of our model-evaluation reports — I'm the one who decides whether a safety case is allowed to say that the system 'knows' the contents of a document, 'believes' a claim to be true, or 'is trying to' accomplish something. This has stopped being an abstract interest of mine: the argument below is the one I've been making internally, and next Thursday I'm making it out loud.

The talk is at a joint session of our interpretability group and a university philosophy-of-mind reading group. The audience, more precisely: about eight machine-learning researchers, most of them mechanistic interpretability people who think of black-box talk as a stopgap we are currently in the process of retiring, and who are thoroughgoing realists about internal representations; two generalization-theory people who think in terms of loss landscapes, capacity, and distribution shift; one cognitive scientist who works on theory of mind in children; one visiting philosopher of mind who has written sympathetically on Dennett's intentional systems theory and is not going to let me get away with a straw version of it; and my own team lead, who has to defend these reports to an external auditor and cares only about whether a hostile reader can make the wording sound absurd.

My thesis is that Dennett's three stances are not enough for the systems we now build, and that a fourth one is doing the real work. The argument, in the form I'll present it:

1. A stance is a strategy for buying predictive leverage; adopting one is warranted when it gives you predictions you cannot get more cheaply another way.
2. The patterns a stance finds are real in Dennett's sense — they support compression of the data — but they are not stance-independent; there is no further fact about which stance is the 'true' description.
3. For a trained neural network the design stance is close to inert. We have the architecture and the weights, so the physical stance is available in principle, and there is no part-by-part arrangement-for-a-purpose of the kind that makes design reasoning pay for itself in artifacts or organisms. There is nothing between the physical stance and the intentional stance here.
4. Yet the intentional stance on these systems is strikingly reliable, and that reliability is not brute: it is explained by the training process. So introduce a distinct stance — call it the optimization stance — in which you predict a system's behaviour by asking what a system selected to do well on such-and-such objective, over such-and-such data, with such-and-such capacity, would tend to do, including off distribution.
5. This stance is not a special case of the design stance, because it buys leverage without assuming function or proper operation, and because it fails in a different way: not when the artifact is broken, but when the optimum is degenerate, the objective is misspecified, or the inputs are off distribution.
6. So intentional attributions to these systems are licensed from below, by the optimization stance plus evidence of reliability, rather than being basic.
7. Practical upshot: claims like 'the model knows p' are true only in the real-pattern, stance-relative sense, and an evaluation report should index each such claim to the stance that licenses it. The right question is never 'does it really know?' but 'which stance-relative claim is load-bearing in this safety argument, and is that stance licensed for this system on this input distribution?'

What I need from you is Q&A preparation, not a defence of the view. I have about twenty minutes of questions and cannot spend more than ninety seconds on any answer. Give me:

- the objections I will actually get, ordered by how likely they are and then by how much damage they would do if I handle them badly, and attributed to which part of the audience;
- each one phrased the way that group would phrase it — the interpretability people should sound like interpretability people, the generalization people like generalization people, the Dennettian like a Dennettian, my team lead like someone with an auditor looking over her shoulder;
- for each, what I should concede immediately and what I should hold, a reply I can actually deliver in ninety seconds, and an honest note on where that reply is soft;
- which single reply has to land for the whole thing to survive the hour;
- and the question I am hoping nobody asks. I'll tell you which one I'm dreading: if the optimization stance is what licenses intentional talk, then everything I can attribute to the system is downstream of what its training process was selected for, and I seem to have no resources for the case that matters most to us — a system that underperforms on an evaluation precisely because doing well would get it retrained. There the intentional stance and the optimization stance come apart, and my framework has to say which one governs the report's wording, or admit that it can't.

Assume I know the background literature and don't want citations, bibliography, or reading suggestions. I need the objections, in their own idiom, and what to say back when the room goes quiet.
