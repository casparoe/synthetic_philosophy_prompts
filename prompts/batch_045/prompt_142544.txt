Hey — I'm putting together the take-home final for my upper-level seminar on contemporary moral philosophy (mostly third-years, they've all had one ethics course) and I need one question on Scanlon's contractualism and the aggregation problem that can't be answered by writing out the reasonable-rejectability formula, noting that complaints get compared one person at a time rather than summed, and concluding "so contractualism can't aggregate, unlike utilitarianism." I get some version of that from every second paper and it never tells me whether the person actually understands the machinery. I want something that forces them to locate *where* the aggregation pressure really comes from, once you remember that contractualism assessess principles rather than individual acts, and that a principle applies to the same person across a lot of different situations.

So here's the twist I want the question built around — I was arguing about it with a friend who's writing on this stuff and we couldn't settle it. Stipulate the following, call it the Life-Profile Clause: when we ask whether a principle is reasonably rejectable for some given person, we assess that person's complaint not situation by situation but across the whole profile of situations in which the principle applies to them over a lifetime. Then give them a case where it does real work: an island ferry authority picks a safety standard under a principle that applies to everyone on the island. Cheap standard: 200,000 residents each pay about three dollars a year; twelve crew members face a roughly one-in-two-hundred annual chance of drowning. Expensive standard: no one pays, the crew risk drops to about one-in-two-thousand. Now add that the crews are picked by an annual lottery from the whole island population, so over a working life a given person is usually a payer and occasionally a crew member. Ask them what the Life-Profile Clause does to the aggregation objection here, and whether it helps or hurts: does it show that the anti-aggregation result was only ever an artefact of tallying complaints one situation at a time, or does it show that the utilitarian-looking answer is really being produced by the lottery and the reciprocity it sets up rather than by the rejection procedure itself? And then the part I care most about — what does the clause do for the people who only ever show up on the losing side of the profile (a resident who can't serve on a crew, a short-term visitor, someone born after the standard is set)? If it abandons them, is contractualism back where it started?
