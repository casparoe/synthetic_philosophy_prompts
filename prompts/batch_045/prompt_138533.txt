As a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, I’m familiar with the general shape of the algorithmic-fairness impossibility result — the kind of thing that gets cited whenever someone points out that a risk score seems to treat two groups differently — but I’m not a specialist, and I want to apply it to a specific institutional setup rather than just repeat the headline.

Here is the setup I care about. A lender uses a continuous risk score S, ranging from 0 to 1, that estimates the probability that an applicant would default on a loan; I’ll call applicants who truly would default the positive instances, as in the usual formulation. The lender’s decision rule has three bands. Applicants with a low score, below a lower threshold, are approved; applicants with a high score, above an upper threshold, are rejected; and applicants whose score falls in the middle band are asked for additional documentation and then reassessed, producing a final score S-prime that is used to make the ultimate decision. Motivated by this, I’ve defined a condition I’ll call reassessment balance: among applicants whose initial score fell in the middle band, the average final score S-prime is the same across the two groups, such as men and women, whose treatment we are comparing.

A friend who knows I’ve been following these debates sent me the following summary of the impossibility theorem and its relevance to my setup. I think he is trying to be fair to the result, but I suspect his application to the two-stage system is unfair somewhere; I’m just not sure where. He wrote:

‘The impossibility theorem for risk scores says that you can’t have it all. There are three properties people want. First, calibration within each group: if the model assigns a group of applicants a score of 0.7, then about 70% of them truly would default, and this is true separately for men and women. Second, balance for the positive class: among applicants who truly would default, the average score they receive is the same in each group. Third, balance for the negative class: among applicants who truly would not default, the average score they receive is the same in each group. The theorem shows that all three can hold at once only in two degenerate cases: either the groups have exactly the same base rate of default, or the score is perfectly predictive, assigning every defaulter a score of 1 and every non-defaulter a score of 0. So, in your two-stage lending system, if the initial score is calibrated, the theorem forces the average final score of reassessed applicants to differ between groups; you cannot make reassessment balance hold without giving up calibration. And because calibration is the only fairness criterion that ensures a score means the same thing for people in every group, the resulting disparity in final scores isn’t really discrimination — it’s just the mathematically unavoidable consequence of the groups having different default rates.’

I’m inclined to believe the statement of the three conditions and the degenerate cases is basically right, but the jump to my two-stage setup and the conclusion that the disparity isn’t discrimination both strike me as too quick. I don’t know enough to say exactly where he goes wrong.
