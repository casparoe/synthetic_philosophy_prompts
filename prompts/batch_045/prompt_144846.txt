I'm in a third-year module on perception and mind and we did Molyneux's question last week — the man blind from birth who's given sight, cube and sphere in front of him, and the question is whether he could tell which was which before touching them. Two people on my course, Nadia and Ollie, got into it in the library afterwards and went round in circles for about forty minutes, and I honestly couldnt tell who was winning, so I want someone to go through it properly.

Roughly how it went. (1) Nadia said yes: he already knows the cube has twelve equal edges, eight corners and flat faces meeting at right angles, while the sphere has one continuous surface with no corners anywhere, and sight presents exactly that structure, so the shape is the same thing specified in a different code — which, she said, is why blind people use the same shape vocabulary as everyone else and can read raised-line diagrams. (2) Ollie said that just begs the question: the shared structure is an abstraction, and an abstraction isn't something either experience literally contains, so two codes can encode the same thing and still be untranslatable without a key; besides, the man would first have to work out that visual patches are signs of surfaces at a distance rather than happenings in or on his eye, which is the Berkeley-style point. (3) Nadia replied that the shared structure must be doing some real work, or the connection couldn't be learned at all — and apparently people who've had sight restored after being blind from infancy pick up cross-modal matching within days — so if there were literally nothing in common the recovery would be magic. (4) Ollie said days isn't the first instant, which is what's being asked, and that those cases couldn't settle it anyway: any test takes time and learning begins the moment the bandages come off, and the patients have already had years of spatial language and of handling things, so fast learning is perfectly consistent with starting from nothing. (5) Nadia said that if every success gets written off as learning, the question becomes unanswerable in principle, so sharpen it: use two irregular lumps he's felt but never been given names for, test on a single first trial with nobody saying anything, and — her own addition — run a version where before the operation he's only ever explored shapes through a long-handled probe, so there's no feel of his own hand's posture to match and all he's got is the distal layout. (6) Ollie said the probe version just swaps in a different subject: someone who uses a stick to feel with has already learned to read a stream of contacts as a distal object, which is precisely the skill in dispute, so he isn't Molyneux's man any more; and the single-trial version quietly changes the question from whether seeing the cube now strikes him as the very shape he used to feel to whether he gets one forced choice right, and it's the former that anyone actually cares about. (7) Nadia said she had no idea what striking him as the same could amount to over and above sorting, predicting and acting — expecting the cube to feel sharp-edged when he grabs it, say — and that if amodal shape made no difference of that kind it was an idle extra. Ollie said that isn't idle, it's the whole point: the claim was always that shape is something experienced across the senses, not merely something behaviour tracks. Then they had to leave and I was none the wiser.

Two things they never got to that I keep turning over: whether putting the question to him out loud, asking him to point at the cube, already gives the game away, since he learned that word by touch while the tester is using it of something seen; and what would happen if his new visual field were rotated or spatially scrambled relative to the real layout — I'd have thought that case decides whether what transfers is the metric geometry or something more abstract, but I can't work out which way it cuts.

Can you adjudicate? Go move by move through (1) to (7): for each, say whether it's basically right, basically wrong, or right about one thing and wrong about another, and why; then say who's more right overall, and whether they're partly just answering different questions. Don't give me a reading list or tell me who to go and read, I've got the seminar handout — and lead with your overall verdict in the very first sentence, no preamble and no restating any of this back at me.
