I'm drafting the opening chapter of a thesis on laws of nature, and I've noticed that two analogies are doing more argumentative work in it than I can account for. I'd like them audited before I rely on either.

The first belongs to my supervisor, who is an anti-Humean: the laws of nature stand to events as the criminal law stands to conduct. A statute is not a summary of what people in fact do; it is a standard that their conduct answers to and can fail to meet. He concludes that nothing which is merely the best summary of the Humean mosaic could be a law, because summaries do not govern anything. The second is the one I reach for in reply: laws are like the contour lines on a topographic map, or like the grammar a field linguist reconstructs from a corpus — descriptions of a terrain or a practice, not standards that the terrain or the speakers answer to; nothing obeys them, and nothing could violate them. A third has been floated in our reading group: laws are like the physics engine of a video game — implemented in the machinery rather than read off the history of what happens on screen, enforced by the hardware, and violable, if at all, only by a glitch.

For each, make the correspondence explicit: what plays the role of the standard, the subject, the violation, the enforcer, and the legislator, if there is one. Then say where each mapping breaks, and — this is the part I care about — which of those disanalogies actually bear on the conclusion the analogy is being used to support and which are merely incidental. In particular, does governing require a legislator, or the possibility of non-compliance, or a genuinely normative ought, or can those be detached from one another? On that basis, which of the three analogies, if any, can carry an anti-Humean conclusion about what lawhood is, and which only seem to? If none of them can, say so, and say either what a better analogy would be or why this dispute is better conducted without one.

Finally, calibrate: state how confident you are in each verdict you give, and single out the one claim in your answer you are least sure of.
