I am giving the eulogy for my great-uncle, who volunteered at eighteen for a war that nearly everyone in the room now regards as unjust, and who spent fifty years neither defending the cause nor repudiating his part in it. He left instructions: I am not to say the cause was just, I am not to say he was wrong to fight, and I am not to shield him by pointing out that he was following orders or that he was only a boy — because, he wrote, being permitted to do a thing and being innocent in doing it are not the same thing, and the gap between them, which he called the unpaid difference, is owed something even after the permission has been granted and even after the war is long over. He wanted that gap named rather than closed. The room will include his surviving comrades, who still believe the war was just and will hear any concession as a betrayal, and his grandchildren, who regard the whole enterprise as criminal; two men from the army he fought against have travelled to be there, and he asked that I say something true to them as well — something that grants them whatever it is that combatants on opposite sides of a war are supposed to share, without pretending that this settles anything and without asking them to forgive him. Please write the eulogy, about five minutes long, for a room that is not made of philosophers and that cannot be allowed to leave consoled by a lie.
