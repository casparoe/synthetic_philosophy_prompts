Hi — I'm a philosophy professor who has somehow ended up chairing our university's academic conduct panel for the third year running. I've taught the Theory of Moral Sentiments maybe a dozen times, so please don't explain Smith back to me and don't give me a reading list or footnotes. What I want is a procedure.

The setup: each panel is me plus one faculty member from outside philosophy and one student, none of whom have read a word of moral philosophy. We sit twice a month during term, we get about forty-five minutes per case, and we have to produce a one-page written rationale that a dean who wasn't in the room can read on appeal. At the moment we basically argue until people get tired, and our outcomes are all over the place.

So: give me a checklist we can actually run in the room, in order, built out of Smith's impartial spectator, that gets us to three separate findings — whether the student's conduct was proper in the sense that the spectator can go along with the motives behind it, whether it warrants resentment or gratitude from the people affected (merit and demerit, in his sense), and if so what the response should be. Keep the first two findings separate; that distinction is the thing we lose most often, because we collapse everything into "how bad was it".

Two things I want you to add, because the book doesn't hand them to me. First, a rule about which spectator we're consulting. As far as I can tell there are three available: the one who knows only what was publicly observable at the time of the act, the one who also knows what the agent privately knew or had reason to know, and the one who additionally knows how it all turned out. These come apart constantly — someone acts on information that was reasonable and wrong, a bit of carelessness produces a huge mess, a huge betrayal produces almost none. Tell me which question each of the three gets to answer, and give me a rule for what we do when they disagree, including whether a panel should ever say out loud that we are resenting the outcome rather than the person and then decline to act on it.

Second, a calibration step. Smith builds the spectator inside us out of our accumulated experience of how other people have actually reacted to us, which means three people from the same department can construct a "spectator" that is just our own gut in a wig. I need a test we can run in two minutes that catches us doing that. My idea is something like: before stating any finding, each of us has to say, in the first person present tense, what the agent was feeling and what the person on the other end was feeling, and then check whether those two statements are merely mirrors of one another. Make that concrete and tell me what counts as failing it.

Then run the whole thing on three cases:

1. Two students in a class where discussing problems together is allowed but write-ups must be separate turn in near-identical proofs. Both say they worked them out together in the library and didn't think about it. Neither will say whose solution it mostly was.
2. A student who reports a roommate for cheating, and who has definately also just found out that the roommate is seeing their ex. Mixed motives, and the report is true.
3. The one I'm dreading: a first-year who was taught, explicitly and repeatedly, in a school system where the whole exam business worked by memorising and reproducing model answers, and who did exactly that on a take-home here. Everyone's instinct is that this is obviously a violation. My worry is that a spectator who really knew what she knew can't go along with any resentment at all — only with correction — and that the panel then has to write down something our dean will read as going soft. If that is where your procedure lands, say so plainly rather than softening it for me.

Finally, be straight about what the procedure can't do. I can think of at least three categories: where the real disagreement between the parties is about what happened rather than what it means, where the rule the student broke is itself the problem, and the empty gallery case — where the person harmed will never learn about any of this, because they've graduated, or the harm is diffuse, and there's no actual bystander around for the machinery to work on. And tell me which Smithian distinctions have to survive being translated into language a biologist and a nineteen-year-old can use, and which I should quietly let go of.
