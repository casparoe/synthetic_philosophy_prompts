I've just come out of a long argument with a friend about deflationary theories of truth, and I'd like a decision tree I can put in front of her the next time we meet, so that we can see whether we were disagreeing about one thing or about three. Her complaint was metaphysical: she thinks any view on which the biconditionals — snow is white is true if and only if snow is white, and so on for every substitutable sentence — more or less exhaust what there is to say about truth is simply declining to answer the question of what truth is, and that a view of that shape then owes us an explanation of why inquiry should aim at truth at all. My complaint was functional: I think the live question is what work the truth predicate does — blind endorsement, semantic ascent, the statement of a semantic theory, the setting of norms for assertion and belief — and whether a thin account can do all of that work. I suspect these are separable, and that most of our shouting was about the second while we both used vocabulary belonging to the first.

So: build me a decision tree, in the form of numbered branch nodes with explicit routing instructions, that starts from a commitment everyone in my reading group will grant — that some sentences are true and some are not, that we are not seriously proposing to stop using the word, and that whoever is right about truth, our ordinary practice of calling things true is not massively mistaken — and ends at named leaves. The group is six people: three philosophers, two machine-learning researchers, and one logician, none of them specialists on truth, so no node may contain the name of a position, the name of an author, or a technical term like equivalence schema or prosententialism; every node must be a case or a claim that a clever undergraduate with no exposure to the literature could answer, and to which a defender of the relevant view would recognizably answer in a particular way. Nothing may ask for readings, citations, or a bibliography; I don't want any.

The distinctions I care about, and which the tree should therefore branch on, are these: whether truth is a property at all and whether whatever it is has a substantial nature or is thin; what the truth predicate is for, and whether one thin account can do all the jobs or whether the jobs come apart; whether truth-aptness and the standard for truth are uniform across discourses or vary by subject matter; what a view owes us about paradoxical sentences containing their own truth predicate; and whether the value of true belief is something the theory explains or merely something it is compatible with. I want at least six and at most eight leaves: one substantive non-deflationary leaf of a correspondence sort, and a primitivist leaf as well if the tree can genuinely separate them; at least three distinct thin leaves of the minimalist, disquotational, and prosentential families — and if your tree cannot keep any of those three apart, say so rather than inventing a question to do it; one epistemic or anti-realist leaf of the coherentist or superassertibility sort; one pluralist leaf; and one eliminativist leaf on which there is no truth property to speak of. Add a pragmatist leaf only if the logical space has room for it without crowding.

Four cases must appear as nodes, because they are where the argument actually stalled. First, the case of a well-formed declarative sentence produced by a system that has no beliefs, no understanding, and no assertoric commitments — my friend said this forces you to locate truth in the proposition expressed, I said it drives a wedge between views that tie truth to endorsement and views that tie it to propositions or meanings, and we never resolved it. Second, the case of a community whose language has no truth predicate at all, though it has negation, quantification, and an ordinary practice of assertion: ask what, concretely, such speakers could not do, with the answer given in terms of their practical and inferential capacities rather than in terms of a theory. Third, blind endorsement: you endorse everything the Oracle says while knowing neither what it said nor how to enumerate it. Fourth, a necessary truth — something from arithmetic or logic — where the biconditionals seem, as my friend put it, to say nothing at all. Where a combination of answers your tree allows is unoccupied, because no one defends the view that sits there, say so and give me one sentence on why: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unclaimed.

Set out the tree, the leaves, the unoccupied cells, and the flagged inseparable pairs as continuous prose, the way you would write them in a long email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, only numbered nodes and routing sentences. And, in your own voice, tell me how much confidence you have in each node: which ones you take to be genuine fault lines and which you had to sharpen or invent to make a distinction bite, where you were extrapolating beyond views you can actually reconstruct, and how much of the taxonomy you would defend against a specialist. If you think my framing is confused — if the metaphysical and the functional questions cannot be pulled apart the way I have pulled them, or if one of my required cases rests on a distinction you take to be spurious — say that plainly and tell me what I should have asked for instead; I would rather be argued with than handed a polite tree.
