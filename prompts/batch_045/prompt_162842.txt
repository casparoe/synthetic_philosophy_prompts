ok so I just had a really long argument with my friend about whether beauty is objective, and I want to know how exposed the position I ended up holding actually is. Here's roughly where I landed (it's more my version than Hume's, honestly — though it grows out of his essay on the standard of taste):

Beauty isn't in the object the way shape is. It's a response-dependent property, and the "standard" for it is *constituted by* — not merely reliably detected by — the joint verdict of properly qualified critics judging under favorable conditions, where qualified means the usual five: delicacy of taste, developed by practice, sharpened by comparison, freed from prejudice, plus good sense. Call this the **verdict-fixed reading**. It contrasts with what I'll call the **critic-fixed reading**, where the qualified critics are just instruments that track a standard that exists independently of what they'd say. I went verdict-fixed because it seemed to buy a real kind of objectivity without me having to point to where in the object beauty lives, and my friend couldn't tell me where it lives either.

His two complaints, which I didn't have great answers to, were: (a) on my reading there's no sense in which an entire age's or culture's qualified critics can be *wrong* about beauty — the sentence "every competent judge in 1750 was mistaken about X" comes out false or empty (call it the no-error problem); and (b) the account goes silent for genuinely novel art, where there's no tradition of comparison yet to manufacture any qualified critics at all (the empty-jury problem).

So: what would count against the **verdict-fixed reading specifically** — not against response-dependence about beauty in general, and not against the critic-fixed reading? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to be explicit about which ones a defender of my view would *themselves* concede count against it, versus which ones only hit some sloppier version I shouldn't be attached to. Please cover:

- what my view predicts that the critic-fixed reading and full-blown aesthetic realism don't — the observable differences, so I can see what I'm actually risking;
- whether the no-error and empty-jury problems are defeaters or just costs I should bite, and what biting them commits me to;
- how much it should move me if we actually ran the empirical version of the test: assemble a jury that satisfies the five qualifications as well as we can operationalize them and their verdicts just don't converge, or converge only within a single training tradition. This is the thing I'd personally find most persuasive, fwiw.
- whether historical canon reversals — works that competent judges of their time rejected and that later got canonized, or the reverse — are already instances of that, or whether the view absorbs them;
- one more case: suppose a purely statistical model, trained only on low-level features of the works and with no training in the tradition, predicted the jury's verdicts better than individual critics predicted each other's. Would that be evidence that there's something in the object after all, or does my view just swallow it?;
- and finally an honest assessment of whether the reading has been insulated by "under favorable conditions" and "properly qualified" doing all the work — whether it's been qualified into unfalsifiability — and whether that's a problem for a view of this kind in a way it wouldn't be for, say, an ideal-observer theory in ethics.

Answer in American English, and keep it concrete: I'd rather have the cases spelled out than a tour of who said what. No need for citations or reading lists.
