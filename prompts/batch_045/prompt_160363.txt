I am building a taxonomy of persuasive influence, and I would like help understanding one passage from the opening chapter of Aristotle's Rhetoric that bears directly on it. The passage, in J. H. Freese's translation of Book I, chapter 1, runs as follows:

And yet they say nothing about enthymemes which are the body of proof, but chiefly devote their attention to matters outside the subject; for the arousing of prejudice, compassion, anger, and similar emotions has no connection with the matter in hand, but is directed only to the dicast. The result would be that, if all trials were now carried on as they are in some States, especially those that are well administered, there would be nothing left for the rhetorician to say. For all men either think that all the laws ought so to prescribe, or in fact carry out the principle and forbid speaking outside the subject, as in the court of Areopagus, and in this they are right. For it is wrong to warp the dicast's feelings, to arouse him to anger, jealousy or compassion, which would be like making the rule crooked which one intended to use.

The taxonomy I am working with distinguishes three ways in which a speaker A can bring an audience B to believe some proposition p.

1. Reason-transmitting influence: A conveys to B the considerations that are in fact A's reasons for believing p, and B's resulting belief depends on those considerations rather than merely following them.
2. Reason-counterfeiting influence: A produces in B states — vivid imagery, a felt sense of familiarity, indignation, urgency, deference — that play the functional role of reasons inside B's psychology without being reasons, and that move B toward p whether or not p is true.
3. Reason-preempting influence: A rearranges B's situation so that believing p becomes the path of least resistance, while leaving B's capacity to weigh reasons intact.

To sort legitimate persuasion from manipulation I have been using what I call the swap test: hold fixed the speaker's aims and techniques and the audience's psychology, but suppose that p is false, with the speaker's evidence for p gone along with it. If the very same technique would still have led B to believe p, the influence is truth-insensitive and therefore manipulative; if its efficacy depends on p's actually holding, or on the speaker's evidence tracking p, then it counts as persuasion. I keep two versions of the test in view, one indexed to the truth of p and one indexed to the speaker's evidence, because two cases trouble me: the speaker whose evidence is misleading but who transmits it in perfect good faith, and the speaker who deploys only truth-insensitive techniques yet deploys them exclusively on propositions he has independently verified.

Please help me with the following.

(a) A close reading of the passage. What exactly is Aristotle counting as outside the subject, and what work is the analogy of the crooked rule doing? Is his complaint about arousing the dicast's feelings that it causes belief in the wrong kind of way, that it is indifferent to how the facts stand, that it usurps a decision that belongs to the dicast rather than the litigant, or some combination of these? Please lay the options out and adjudicate between them as far as this passage allows, and flag anything in the wording that a modern reader is likely to misread.

(b) Consistency with the rest of the work. Aristotle goes on to give extended treatment to the emotions and to the character the speaker displays as means of persuasion, and he also argues that rhetoric is useful because the true and the just are by nature stronger than their opposites, so that when a decision goes wrongly the advocates are to blame and not the case itself. Does the passage quoted above sit comfortably with those commitments? If not, is the tension better resolved by reading this chapter as an attack on a certain kind of rhetorical handbook rather than on appeal to the emotions as such?

(c) Application to my taxonomy. On the principle stated in this passage, where would each of my three kinds of influence fall? In particular: does Aristotle's own later treatment of the emotions come out as reason-counterfeiting influence by the standard set here, and does the swap test capture what he objects to more cleanly than the three-way taxonomy does — or does the taxonomy catch something the swap test misses, such as the difference between bypassing an audience's reasons and merely making it easy for the audience not to bother with them?

(d) Which of my two versions of the swap test is nearer to Aristotle's own view, and does the passage give any reason to prefer a test indexed to the speaker's evidence over one indexed to the truth? I am especially interested in whether the claim that the true and the just are naturally the stronger would, if granted, make reason-counterfeiting influence gratuitous in a way that tells against my truth-indexed version.
