Each summer I help run a writing-and-mentoring program for incoming graduate teaching fellows at my university, and on Friday afternoons the handful of us who stay on campus sit in on an informal reading group on lying, deception, and related topics: mostly a linguist (Nadia) and a philosopher (Rowan), with me as the non-specialist who takes the notes. Two weeks ago the discussion ran away from us over a letter of recommendation. A senior scholar, Renée, had been asked directly by the chair of a fellowship committee whether her former student Dana's work was original and significant; Renée believes the work is derivative but did not want to go on record saying so, and she replied in writing that Dana had read everything in the literature and that she writes quickly and clearly, and left it there. The committee read the letter as an endorsement and ranked Dana highly. When Renée later said that every sentence she had written was true and that she had never claimed the work was original, the two regulars disagreed about whether that defense is worth anything, and they have asked me to referee before we meet again, which I honestly cannot do.

Their exchange went roughly as follows. (1) Nadia: Renée lied, or at any rate did what a liar does — what she meant was that Dana's work is strong, she intended the committee to come away believing that, and the literal truth of her sentences is a technicality; the distinction between what is said and what is implicated was drawn to explain how utterances convey more than they encode, not to serve as a moral exemption. (2) Rowan: On the contrary, it is exactly that distinction which does the work here. Grice's maxim of Quality is phrased in terms of saying — do not say what you believe to be false, do not say what you lack evidence for — and lying is a matter of assertion; Renée asserted only propositions she took to be true, so she misled without lying. Moreover the implicature is cancellable without contradiction: she could have added, in the same letter, that she did not think the work was original, which she could not have done had she written that it was. (3) Nadia: Two replies. First, Quality also has a supermaxim, make your contribution one that is true, and a contribution includes what you implicate, so by Grice's own standard she violates Quality as much as someone who says something false. Second, cancellability makes things worse for her rather than better: that she could have cancelled the implicature and instead chose the phrasing that licensed it is precisely the objectionable part — she wanted the committee's false belief while keeping a way out. (4) Rowan: The sub-maxims are about saying and the supermaxim is a summary of them, not an additional rule; and the maxims are constitutive of conversational rationality rather than a moral code, telling you what an utterance will be taken to convey given that others assume you are cooperating, not what you owe them. Anyway, if generating a false implicature were lying, then irony and understatement would be lying: of a colleague who stole my data I can say she has been a real friend, implicating the opposite of what I say, and nobody thinks I have lied. (5) Nadia: Irony is not a counterexample, because in irony the hearer is meant to see through it; what separates the two cases is uptake, not the boundary between saying and implicating — which is my point. And notice what Renée's case does to Grice's own account of speaker meaning: to mean something is to intend your audience to arrive at a belief through recognizing that very intention. Renée intended the committee to conclude that Dana's work was strong, and intended them to recognize that she was inviting that conclusion; she simply also wanted to be able to deny it afterward. By Grice's own criterion she meant something she believed to be false, and whether we file that under saying or implicating should not decide whether she lied. (6) Rowan: If that is the test, then the dispute is no longer about lying at all but about whether it is wrong to induce a false belief while protecting your own deniability — and if inducing false beliefs is the wrong-making feature, then ordinary underinformative replies are suspect too: someone asks how my talk went, I say the room was full, I have exploited Quantity to avoid saying it went badly, and I have not lied. (7) Nadia: In your case the hearer is left with no false belief, and if he forms one he was not invited to; Renée's letter is parasitic in a way yours is not, because it works only on the assumption, which the committee is entitled to make, that she is observing Quality — she was cashing in a reputation for truthfulness she was not willing to honor.

At that point an emeritus who drops in occasionally interrupted with a case that seemed to me to cut across both positions, and which nobody resolved: a mother telling her child, about a small cut, that he is not going to die. Everyone agrees she means that he is not going to die from the cut, and the question is whether that qualification belongs to what she said or only to what she implicated — because if what is said can be enriched that way, then Renée's sentences are not as plainly true as she claims (clearly relative to what standard? reading everything in which literature?), and the whole "I only said something true" defense may rest on a conception of what is said that people working on these questions do not all accept. So please adjudicate: go through the seven moves in order and rate each from clearly mistaken through partly right to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification apiece, then tell me who is more right overall and what, if anything, both of them are missing. I also want your own answers to three sub-questions: whether the saying/implicating line really falls where Rowan puts it in the recommendation-letter case; whether Gricean Quality governs what is implicated as well as what is said; and how much weight the "not going to die" style of example should carry, given that it bears on how much content what is said can absorb. Please write in American English, numbered by move but in continuous prose rather than fragments, and please do not give me citations, a bibliography, or suggestions for further reading — I am at a field station for the summer with unreliable internet and no library access, so anything I cannot follow from the argument itself is of no use to me, and we read the notes aloud at the next meeting.
