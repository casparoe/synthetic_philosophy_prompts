Draft the joint statement that could be signed, after a good-faith adversarial collaboration, by two parties to the species problem: a pluralist about the species category and a lineage monist.

The pluralist holds that there is no single correct account of what it is to be a species. The work that the notion of species does in evolutionary biology, ecology, and systematics divides among several distinct investigative questions — about reproductive boundaries, about independent evolutionary trajectory, about ecological role, about monophyly and diagnosability — and the taxa selected by the criteria that answer these questions sometimes cross-cut rather than nest. Hence the question whether a given group is a species is incomplete until an investigative question is supplied, and the question how many species a clade contains has no answer unrelativized to a concept. This is not conventionalism: each concept can carve at a real joint, and the taxa it picks out are real historical individuals.

The monist holds that the species category has one correct account: species taxa are segments of metapopulation lineages, populations evolving separately from others, and the familiar criteria — interfertility, fixed diagnostic character states, ecological distinctness, monophyly — are at best evidence for, and operational proxies of, that single underlying property. Where proxies conflict, at most one of them tracks what species are; the conflict marks the limits of our evidence rather than a multiplicity in nature. Species number in a clade is therefore determinate even when unknowable, and comparisons of diversity across clades are not merely comparisons of which convention was applied.

Both parties accept the distinction between the taxon question (is this particular group a species?) and the category question (what is it to be a species?), and both treat species taxa as individuals with historical origin-and-extinction conditions rather than as classes. Both grant that the rank of species carries no metaphysical privilege over the ranks above and below it.

Do not adjudicate. The deliverable is a document both could put their names to. Neither side may be portrayed in a form nobody would defend: the monist must be allowed to grant that cross-cutting taxa are real and that operational criteria are indispensable to practice, and the pluralist must be allowed to grant that in a given clade one criterion is usually the one worth using and that within-clade diversity comparisons can be scientifically meaningful. Where a claim would hold only under one side's favoured reformulation of the question, mark it as such rather than letting it into the agreed section.

Write the document in four parts.

1. Agreed claims, numbered, ten to fourteen of them, each substantive enough that denying it would be a real commitment, and each phrased so that neither signatory could say the wording has been tilted. They should cover at least: what the familiar criteria each actually test; why interbreeding criteria fail for asexual, uniparental, and reticulate groups; why diagnosability criteria fragment continuously varying lineages and are sensitive to sampling and to which loci are chosen; the circumstances under which different criteria yield nested, cross-cutting, or incommensurable groupings; and what taxonomy, conservation law, and biodiversity accounting respectively need from a species concept.

2. A disagreements table with these columns: the crux; a neutral restatement of the disagreement; the formulation the pluralist would sign; the formulation the monist would sign; and a verdict for that row on whether the dispute is verbal or substantive — and if verbal, which term is doing the work and what remains once that term is fixed. Include at least: whether the species category admits of one correct account or several legitimate ones; whether conflict among criteria is a symptom of evidential limitation or of genuine multiplicity; whether operational criteria constitute species status or merely indicate it; whether one and the same taxon can be a species under one concept and fail to be one under another while remaining the same individual, or whether changing the concept changes the referent; whether species number is a legitimate comparative measure across clades with different reproductive systems; and what identity over time a species taxon has in the absence of branching.

3. A tests-and-predictions table with these columns: the crux; the case or argument both parties accept as bearing on it; the pluralist's prediction, written as if the result were not yet in; the monist's prediction, likewise; what outcome would move the pluralist; and what outcome would move the monist. Use the three stipulated cases below, which are hypothetical and fully specified here, so the document needs no empirical lookup.

Case A — the Ashfall sedge complex. A genus of wind-pollinated sedges distributed in a ring around a montane basin. Stipulate: every adjacent pair of populations is interfertile in greenhouse crosses, with seed set declining continuously as genome-wide divergence rises and failing above roughly eleven percent; divergence rises continuously with distance around the ring; the two terminal forms meet in the south and do not interbreed in the field, the contact being secondary and dated to roughly nine thousand years ago, the ring itself having expanded from a single refugium roughly eighty thousand years ago; one terminal form is restricted to serpentine soils, flowers about six weeks earlier than the other, and is reciprocally monophyletic in the nuclear genome while rendering the other terminal form paraphyletic; and that serpentine form is the only member of the complex protected under a threatened-species statute. Ask how many species the complex contains, whether that question has a determinate answer, whether the answer differs if the question is asked of a single time slice rather than of the eighty-thousand-year history, and what a legal listing of the species should be taken to protect.

Case B — the kappa clade. Free-living bacteria, no sexual cycle, no usable morphology, genomes around four megabases. Recombination within each of two ecotypes runs at roughly twice the mutation rate; between them, at roughly one twentieth of it. Around eighteen percent of gene content per genome turns over per million years by transfer from outside the clade, while a core of genes is transmitted vertically. The ecotypes are separated by a sharp niche boundary and each undergoes periodic genome-wide selective sweeps that purge diversity. Ask whether the clade is one lineage or several, whether the ecotypes are species, and whether the monist's own criterion survives this case.

Case C — reticulation and deep time. A fern complex in which hybridization between two recognized species produces a fertile, reproductively isolated allopolyploid lineage that is also a novel ecological entity, while both parental species persist unchanged alongside it. Alongside it, a continuously cored marine sequence shows five million years of anagenetic morphological change without branching, wide enough in total that morphology-based practice would name three successive species, though variation at any single horizon is narrower than the total change. Ask whether speciation here adds to or subtracts from species number, and what the fossil record can in principle settle about lineage segmentation.

4. A closing paragraph, signed by both, stating what further work — empirical or conceptual — would actually settle something, what neither party should expect to be settled, and the single claim each most wants the other to concede. If part of the dispute turns out to be verbal, say plainly which part and what the residual substantive disagreement is under a stipulated single sense of the term; do not dissolve the whole dispute into words.

The document should run roughly twelve hundred to sixteen hundred words. After it, you may add at most two sentences of your own, clearly separated, noting where you think the collaboration is least stable.
