I'm a journalist — I write the science-and-ideas column for a mid-sized paper, and last year I got way too deep into cliodynamics for a feature: Turchin's secular cycles, structural-demographic theory, the whole population-pressure → popular immiseration → elite-overproduction → fiscal crunch → instability chain, Ibn Khaldun's asabiya, the 2010 letter to Nature, all of it. Long story short, the president of a small college about two hours from where I grew up read the piece and asked me to give the commencement address in May, mostly (I think) because she liked the line about how the same structural squeeze shows up in a rural county and in a hiring committee. Now I actually have to write the thing, and I'm stuck on it.

Here's the shape of the problem. It's a class of about 180 at a private college in a county whose biggest employer pulled out in the nineties; a lot of them are first-generation, a lot are local and will stay local, and my goddaughter is one of them — she's heading into a field with roughly a dozen applicants per opening, which is, as far as I can tell, a textbook case of credential inflation, too many trained people chasing too few slots. The room will be half families and local people (school board, a county commissioner, the guy who's money built the new career center and who made it in the industry that left) and half faculty, including several in the humanities who think the whole project of putting numbers on history is astrology with a database. And the president has asked me, very nicely, not to use the word decline from the podium, and would prefer I not tell a room full of parents that the country is in a disintegrative phase.

What I keep writing and deleting is this: the theory seems to rule out both of the things a commencement address is normally made of. It says the squeeze isn't a personal failure, so I can't do the bootstrap bit — work hard, pull yourself up, it'll work out — because on this view the bottleneck is structural and there genuinely aren't enough seats. But it also can't let me go fatalist and tell them the models say the decade is bad, both because that's a lousy thing to say to twenty-one-year-olds and because I wrote a whole section in my own piece on how contested this is: whether the roughly fifty-year oscillation is a real mechanism or curve-fitting on a noisy past, whether aggregate dynamics license anything at all about one person's prospects, whether history has general dynamics or is just contingency with a nice graph on it. And there's a third problem, which is that the view has no individuals in it anywhere — its variables are populations, elites, wages, state revenue, and there's no slot in the model for a person deciding something, which is awkward when the point of the speech is supposed to be that these particular people matter.

So please write the address. Eight to ten minutes, American English, plain enough that a grandparent in row twelve is with me the whole way and precise enough that the quantitative-social-science people in the faculty rows don't hear me get the mechanism wrong. I'd rather it not be a TED talk about resilience, no bending arcs of history, and it shouldn't leave the parents feeling like their town is a data point or the graduates feeling like statistics in somebody's secular cycle. What I want is for the theory to do real work shaping what can honestly be said to this room: something a cliodynamicist could actually stand behind about what a graduating class is for in a period like this, and that a twenty-one-year-old could take with her without being lied to. If your honest read is that I should have turned the invitation down, tell me that too — but I still need the speech.
