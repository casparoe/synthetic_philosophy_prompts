Next week Ive been dumped with a 50-minute guest slot in a graduate seminar on formal semantics — a dozen or so people, all fluent in first-order logic and lambda notation, and most of them came out of a semantics course last term where definite descriptions were taught as presupposition triggers: uniqueness plus familiarity, failure gives you a truth-value gap, and the gap projects through negation, belief verbs and conditionals. So they arrive already owning a theory that does what Russell's does and disagreeing with it about the failure cases, and my worry is that Russell lands as a historical curiosity filed next to something they think is strictly better. Don't explain the theory to me, I know it — what I want is the plan for explaining it. What has to come first and why that rather than the obvious opening move of putting the sentence about the present king of France being bald on the board and asking true, false, or neither; whether the example that carries the whole hour should be one where the description fails to denote or one where it denotes fine and the scope contrast is what bites, and what that example has to have and to lack in order to do the job; and where in the sequence the negation scope contrast goes relative to the three-clause analysis, because I keep changing my mind about whether the scope thing is the payoff or a footnote.

On the misconceptions side, here are the three I'm fairly sure of and I want to know where each gets defused, or whether I should just let it rip. One student writes compilers for a living and is going to say, early and loudly, that Russell's analysis is just a null check or an existence guard before you dereference — I want to know whether that's actually a decent on-ramp and the exact point where it starts to mislead them. Second, I think they'll read the analysis as a presupposition account with an extra step bolted on, i.e. as Russell denying the truth-value gap rather than denying that there's a presupposition to fail, so what has to already be on the board before that reading becomes available? Third, someone will bring up Donnellan-style cases where a speaker uses a description that doesn't fit the person they're talking about but the audience still knows who's meant, and I can't decide whether to head that off or recruit it. Then: what should I openly refuse to cover in fifty minutes and say out loud that I'm refusing — my instinct is the whole business of proper names as disguised descriptions, and incomplete descriptions like the one about the table, but tell me if I'm wrong — and give me one question for the last five minutes that would show they've got the analysis rather than just memorized the formula. I'd much rather they leave understanding why the scope ambiguity matters than leave able to recite the three clauses.
