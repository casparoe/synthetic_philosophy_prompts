Hey, I'm in a tiny reading group going through Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments, and I've been drafting an essay that I think has maybe one genuinely new idea in it and a lot of parts I can't tell anymore whether they're interesting or just me restating something standard in new vocabulary. I'd love your comments on it — especially on where it gets Smith wrong, and on which bits you'd cut.

The skeleton is this. First move: read the impartial spectator not as a criterion of rightness but as a specification of an audience. On the standard reading, the spectator's approval partly constitutes an action's being right; on my reading, the spectator is just a mechanism for generating a verdict — you imagine a well-informed, disinterested bystander, you simulate what they'd feel, and the output is your judgment. If that's all it is, then the content of Smithian morality is fixed by what sympathy can transmit, and sympathy (on his own account) only ever gives you a weaker copy of another person's feeling, built out of imagination from the outside. Two consequences I want to draw: an observability bias, since what's vividly imaginable from the outside is what gets weighed, so the framework should systematically overrate spectacular breaches of manners and underrate quiet, slow, invisible harms; and a publicity shape, since what the spectator can endorse is roughly what you could own in front of anyone, which I think explains why Smith slides so easily between moral judgment and mere propriety.

Second move, and the part I'm least sure about: use the cross-cultural white-lie case as a stress test. Take the familiar contrast — Dutch speech culture where bluntness is often neutral or even a form of respect, and Japanese speech culture where the same bluntness is cruelty and indirection is the kindness. Smith's spectator is described as well-informed, but well-informed about what? If the spectator knows the local conventions, the verdict just mirrors whatever etiquette happens to be in force, and the theory quietly goes relativist in a way that sits badly with Smith's own appeal to general rules of morality. If the spectator doesn't know them, it demands or forbids things no actual participant could do without becoming socially unreadable. I've been calling this the spectator's information problem, and I think it's the real crux rather than the usual worry that sentimentalism is just subjective.

Third move is my proposed fix, which is the part I most want attacked: split the spectator in two. A thick, local spectator who shares the conventions of the speech community and grades manner and propriety; and a thin spectator who knows only the brute facts — who suffers, how much, who intended what, who could have done otherwise — and grades the substance of the act. The rude-but-true remark is fine by the thick spectator where bluntness is the norm, and graded only thinly elsewhere; the soothing lie is graded locally for manner and thinly for whether anyone was actually better off. I keep worrying that this is either (a) just Kantian formalism with sentimentalist window dressing, or (b) empty, because a spectator stripped of all conventions of salience may have no verdicts left in it at all, or (c) fatal to the unity Smith wanted, since he seems to want propriety and merit to come out of one mechanism. I also gesture at an everyday datum: people who move between speech cultures describe a split between knowing something wasn't meant badly and still being stung, which I read as the two spectators disagreeing.

So, concretely, what I'd like: is the procedural reading of the spectator in the first move actually available to Smith, or does he need the spectator to be constitutive in a way my version quietly drops, and does his appeal to general rules block the relativism charge? Which premise is the most load-bearing and the weakest? Is the observability-bias criticism new or just the standard objection against sentimentalism reworded? And can you think of a case that would break the two-tier fix even if the rest of the essay survives — plus, bluntly, which of the three moves you'd drop if I had to cut one. As far as I remember Smith never gives white lies a proper treatment anywhere, so I'm working mostly from the sympathy chapters; tell me if that's wrong.
