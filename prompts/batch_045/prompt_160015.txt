hey — I'm a backend dev with no philosophy training (took one ethics class in undergrad, got a C, mostly remember the trolley thing). I'm writing the scoring module for a project that has to rank possible outcomes by how good or bad the experiences inside them are: hand it two outcome descriptions, get a verdict. The inputs are noisy — I get valence estimates with error bars, not exact numbers — and I can't tell in advance whether I'm comparing two lives, two populations, or one life against itself at different times. So the purpose is fixed: a rule good enough to ship as a comparator under uncertainty and measurement error, not a theory of what's right in the abstract.

Before I commit to an aggregation rule I want a spec. What does a theory of how pleasures and pains combine into one verdict have to deliver to count as usable for that? I want the requirements, not the provenance, so please don't give me a reading list or paper titles — I can't get them and they'd be noise.

What I'm after:

1. Ten or so requirements, each with the concrete case behind it. Where a requirement is really a property of the function, phrase it as a unit test I could write: input (two outcome descriptions), expected verdict or expected relation between verdicts. One line of pseudocode is fine if that's clearer than prose.
2. Mark each requirement non-negotiable versus negotiable for my purpose, and call out the ones that are really just one camp's taste dressed up as a requirement.
3. Tell me which subsets of the requirements can't all be satisfied together. I've got two suspicions already: (a) lexical priority for pain above some threshold versus not having a cliff where a sub-1% change in an input flips the verdict; (b) lexical priority versus deciding under uncertainty, where an arbitrarily small probability of threshold-crossing pain seems to have to dominate a certain large improvement. Find the collisions I haven't thought of, and say what has to give in each.
4. Name the single requirement that knocks out the most candidate theories, and what exactly it knocks out.

Cases I want covered somewhere in the list, since these are the ones that break the naive sum-the-valence version:

- chunking: splitting an hour of moderate pain into sixty one-minute slices, or merging sixty mild pleasures into one episode, shouldn't change the verdict just because I repartitioned the input. If your theory needs a smallest value-bearing unit, say what it is and why that isn't arbitrary.
- mixed valence: one experience that is pleasant and unpleasant at once — chili burn, a hard climb, grief you would not give up. Does the theory net it into one number or keep two axes, and what test separates those?
- non-hedonic valence: states that matter but aren't exactly pleasure or pain — boredom, exhaustion, flow, confusion. Is the theory supposed to score those or is that out of scope, and what breaks each way?
- the zero point: where do dreamless sleep, nonexistence, and plain neutrality sit, and must a theory say, or may it stay silent there?
- aggregation inside pain: do a million mild headaches ever amount to one severe pain, and is any threshold on intensity, on total, or on something else?
- range differences across minds: a mind whose worst possible pain is ten times the worst human pain, or one that can't feel pain at all. Absolute threshold or mind-relative one?
- dynamic consistency: does ranking whole lives give the same answer as choosing moment by moment?

Then a short scorecard — five lines, no more — for plain sum-the-valence hedonism, lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism, a two-axis view with pain lexicographically prior, and a weighted negative-leaning view. The list is the deliverable; the scorecard is just so I can check I read the list right.
