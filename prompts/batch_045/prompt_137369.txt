I'm a software engineer by training — twelve years of backend and distributed systems work, mostly on observability and reliability — with no formal philosophy background, though I've been reading about AI alignment on the side for a couple of years and following the eliciting-latent-knowledge (ELK) literature in particular. I've been offered a small six-month independent research grant (roughly ten hours a week, a couple of consumer GPUs, no collaborators) and I need a proposal for it. My half-formed idea, which I'd like you to turn into something fundable and actually doable: ELK is normally framed as a decoding problem — the model has already computed the answer somewhere in its activations, and the hard part is building a reporter that reads it out honestly. I think that framing is backwards in a way that matters, and that the interesting question is about the training distribution rather than the decoder. What we call latent knowledge looks to me like the same phenomenon as a quantity in a system that is observable in the control-theoretic sense on the training distribution but unobservable on the distribution where we need it: there are many internal states consistent with everything we can see, and the training signal never distinguished among them. So I want to propose an operational, testable criterion — call it shift-identifiability, or whatever better name you'd give it — on which a piece of knowledge counts as genuinely latent in a model just in case any decoder that recovers it (i) is trained only with the same supervision the model itself had, and (ii) still recovers it on a shifted distribution where the model's own observable behavior has gone degenerate, e.g. a case where the sensors have been tampered with or the event of interest is so rare that behavior is uninformative. The second half of my idea is the consequence I want drawn: if knowledge is latent in this sense, then no amount of post-hoc cleverness with probes and auditors can manufacture it, and the intervention has to move upstream to what the model was trained on and what it was trained to do. Elicitation becomes a property of the training regime, not of the readout.
