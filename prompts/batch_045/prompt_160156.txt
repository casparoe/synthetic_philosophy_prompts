I'm teaching my small seminar on moral ideals again in the spring (twelve people, seminar table, no screens) and we always jam in the same spot: everyone can rehearse Wolf's complaint about the moral saint, everyone can feel the pull of "but wouldn't the world be better with more people like that," and then the discussion just cycles. So I want to hand them something that sorts them instead.

What I want is a taxonomy of the positions, built as a decision tree — say six to eight splits, roughly ten leaves — where every branch node is a concrete case or claim a student can answer without knowing any of the labels, and every leaf is a named position (or "unnamed," with a note on why nobody lives there). Please build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names: start with the questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then drop the actual views into the cells that are occupied, and tell me which cells are empty and whether that's because the combination is incoherent, just unattractive, or merely unexplored.

The axes I care about, and that I want doing the work:

- Crowding-out vs. motive-contamination. Is the worry that moral concern eats up a fixed budget of time and energy (the cellist who could hand over her practice hours), or that a moral motive in the wrong place ruins the activity from the inside (the friend who visits you in hospital out of duty and thereby makes the visit worth less)? Those come apart and I definately want them split.
- Loss to the agent vs. loss to the world. Is what's missing from the saint's life a cost to her, or is it that a world where everyone is a saint has nobody writing string quartets — and if it's the latter, does the objection survive when only a few people are saints and everyone else specialises?
- Cost vs. defect. Is the saint's flatness a price she pays (blameless, regrettable), or is it the absence of a virtue — in which case "maximally morally good" is a misdescription, not a compliment with a catch? I want a node that tests this with something like whether her friend could reasonably resent or blame her, not just whether her life is poorer.
- One peak vs. a frontier. Is there a single optimum, or a set of incommensurable good lives that differ in how they mix moral and non-moral excellence? Say what happens to the whole debate if it's a frontier.
- First-personal vs. third-personal. Half the room wants to say the saint is the best person here and also that they'd neither be one nor marry one. Give me a node that presses whether that's incoherent, and if it isn't, the tree has to be able to land someone on both verdicts at once.
- Whether the saint may enjoy non-moral goods. Wholeheartedly? Only "lightly," holding them in a way she'd drop on demand? The gardener who loves her garden but can't shake the thought that she ought to be elsewhere — is she the ideal, or a fallen version of it?
- Whether the ideal you aim at can differ from the ideal you'd want realised — the regulative-ideal move, where someone aspires to sainthood while holding that a world of actual saints would be worse. If that's stable it deserves a leaf; if it's not, say where it breaks.

Leaves I want to be reachable: the no-upper-bound maximizer who denies there's any "too much morality" point; the reply that treats saintliness as one virtue among others, so a morality-saturated life isn't missing excellence; a threshold or moderate view on which morality is satisfied well short of everything; the alienation view (morality really is maximally demanding but isn't authoritative over how to live); the value-pluralist view; the tragic view (there is a real loss and you should be a saint anyway); and something like a perfectionist view on which non-moral excellence is lexically prior — plus at least two cells marked unnamed, with a one-line diagnosis each.

Also flag any pair of leaves your tree genuinely can't seperate, and tell me what further case would be needed. My guess is that the threshold/constraint view and "maximizing with sharply diminishing marginal moral value" behave identically on most mid-sized sacrifices, but tell me if I'm wrong. And give each leaf one sentence on the most awkward thing you have to swallow if you end up there.

Assume they've read the Wolf paper and one demandingness piece — don't restate either argument. Nested bullets or ascii, whatever prints on one page.
