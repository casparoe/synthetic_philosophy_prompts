I'm a third-year philosophy major taking a small seminar on social normativity, and we've spent the last three weeks on promising: a conventionalist reading of Hume, some contemporary expectation-based and rights-based accounts, and a bit on commitment devices and costly signaling. My study group has run into an annoying problem: we can all apply the same label to ourselves — "I'm basically a conventionalist," "I'm an expectation theorist" — and then answer concrete cases in completely incompatible ways, so I've concluded that the labels are doing almost no work and that we'd learn more from being sorted by our verdicts on cases. I'd like your help building a diagnostic instrument for our last session. Assume we have no library or database access in the room, so please don't route me to any text: work from the cases and the arguments themselves.

What I want is a decision tree that starts from a commitment nobody in the group disputes — that in an ordinary case (voluntary, uncoerced, clearly expressed, between adults, about something permissible) the promisor ends up with a reason to perform that they would not have had if they had stayed silent — and then leads a student, by their answers alone, to a named position. Build the tree out of the logical space first and only then drop the positions into it. Concretely, please produce:

1. A table of eight diagnostic questions, one per branch node, with the columns: case_id | the case in one sentence | the single distinction this case isolates | two or three answer options written as plain first-person verdicts a student could give | the branch each option routes to. No question may name a theory or a theorist, and no question may ask "do you think promises are X." These cases must all appear: (a) the secret promise — you promise a stranger under a pseudonym to do something they will never learn about and that costs them nothing if it goes undone, and then you don't do it; (b) the auto-generated promise — an assistant you configured (a calendar bot, or an LLM agent acting with your delegated authority) replies "I promise I'll be there" to an invitation you never actually read, and you don't show; (c) the released promise — the promisee sincerely says "I release you, don't do it," and you don't; (d) the trivial promise — you promise to touch your nose at noon in an empty room, nobody is watching, and noon arrives; (e) the promise to something that can't have expectations — your dead grandmother, your chatbot, a stranger's newborn; (f) the unknown third-party beneficiary — you promise B you'll pay C's tuition, and C never finds out; (g) a promise extracted by a threat from someone other than the promisee; (h) a promise whose content is set by a misinterpretation you could easily have prevented but didn't. I especially care about (b) and (e): I want nodes that force a view about whether promising needs a mental act of the promisor, whether an agent's utterance can bind the person who deployed it, and whether a promise can be directed at something incapable of being wronged.
2. A JSON object for the tree: nodes as {"id": string, "question": string, "yes": node_or_leaf_id, "no": node_or_leaf_id} and leaves as {"id": string, "provisional_name": string, "one_line_creed": string, "awkward_commitment": string, "held_by": string, where you write "unnamed" if you don't think anyone really defends it}. Six to eight leaves, and among them these must be locatable somewhere: a practice-dependent conventionalism; a non-conventionalist natural-duty view; an expectation- or assurance-based view; a rights-transfer or normative-power view; a view on which the obligation is really a duty of fidelity to oneself or of personal integrity; and a deflationary view on which promising adds no genuine reason and is only a costly signal or a prediction about one's own conduct.
3. A JSON array of at least three unoccupied cells in the logical space — combinations of answers allowed by your questions that no leaf fills — each as {"answer_path": string, "why_empty": one of "incoherent", "unattractive", "unexplored", "diagnosis": string}, with one sentence on why.

Then I want your own verdict, and I want it committed rather than surveying: tell me which single fork in the tree you think is genuinely load-bearing — the one where flipping your answer moves you to a different leaf — and which leaf your own answers put you in; say explicitly which two adjacent leaves your tree still cannot separate and what further case would separate them; and flag any place where you had to invent a distinction that nobody in the literature actually uses, since I'd rather know that than be handed a tidy tree that borrows its authority from a reading I can't check. Don't close with the observation that reasonable people disagree.
