I'm a junior, and I help set up for my school's math club, which last week held a joint meeting with the philosophy club. Afterward two seniors — I'll call them Maya and Jonah — got into an argument that neither of them would let go of, and since I was the only person still in the room stacking chairs, they asked me to referee. I genuinely couldn't tell who was winning. Here is the case and, as close as I can manage, the exchange.

The case: a machine in the physics department holds one ordinary coin. Tomorrow at noon it will flip that coin exactly once, display the result on a screen for ten seconds in an empty room, and then melt the coin and erase its own memory. Nobody will ever learn how it landed, and the machine will never be used again. The question is what, if anything, the sentence "the probability of heads is 0.5" means here, and whether it is true.

(1) Maya: There is no such thing as the probability of a single flip. Probability is relative frequency in a long run of repetitions. There will be no repetitions here, so that sentence is not true and not false. The true thing nearby is: in a long run of flips of coins like this one, about half land heads.

(2) Jonah: But you and I both have a perfectly definite expectation about tomorrow, and we would act on it — I would take either side of an even-money bet on heads. That expectation is well defined with no long run anywhere in sight. Call it my degree of confidence. Probability is a fact about the person doing the expecting, not about the coin.

(3) Maya: Then you have destroyed the difference between being right and merely being confident. Take someone who is cocksure about everything and sits at 0.9 on every guess he makes. On your account there is nothing wrong with him, and plainly there is. A probability that cannot be wrong is not a probability.

(4) Jonah: That is the caricature version. Degrees of belief have to fit together: if your confidences across a set of exclusive and exhaustive outcomes do not add up to one, then there is a package of bets, each of which you would regard as fair or better, that loses you money no matter what happens. So there is a real constraint here — coherence — and it is just the axioms of the probability calculus. What is wrong with the cocksure man is not that he is confident; it is that he is incoherent.

(5) Maya: I will grant that coherence is required. But it is obviously not sufficient, and that is the whole dispute. Coherence alone is just as happy with 0.83 on heads. So the number has to come from somewhere, and the only place it can come from is the setup itself — the coin's symmetry, the way it is launched, the air in the room. Call that a propensity: a physical, single-case chance belonging to this particular toss. Then the calculus is about those, and your coherence rule just says belief has to line up with them.

(6) Jonah: Now tell me what a propensity is. Either you define it by what it would produce — the frequency this same setup would yield if you repeated it — in which case it is frequentism in a costume, and it inherits your problem, since this setup will not be repeated and the coin gets melted. Or it is a primitive, irreducible tendency of this one toss, and then I want to know what would count as evidence that it is 0.5 rather than 0.83, if not watching a lot of flips, which takes you straight back to frequencies.

(7) Maya: I concede that I cannot say what the propensity is except in terms of the frequencies it would give. But I think you are in worse shape, and here is the case that shows it. Suppose we learn that the coin was drawn from a barrel in which roughly one coin in ten is bent, and bent coins come up heads about 70 percent of the time in long runs; which coins are bent was never written down. You will say your confidence is now 0.52. But that number is an average taken over a class, and you had to choose the class: the barrel, the physics department's coins, coins flipped indoors, coins flipped at all. Different classes give different numbers, and nothing in your account chooses between them. That is fatal, and it is your problem rather than mine.

(8) Jonah: I am not averaging over a class. I am responding to everything I happen to know, and 0.52 is what that evidence supports; tell me tomorrow that the coin is bent and I move to 0.7 without any fuss. And you should like that, because on your own new view nothing changed about the coin when I got the news — it had a propensity of 0.5 or one of 0.7 all along, and only my information moved. Which shows that probabilities about single cases track evidence, i.e. that they are credences.

(9) Maya: One last point, and I think it is the one that beats you. Suppose that before the flip a physicist we trust tells us: the chance of heads is 0.7. You would move to 0.7 no matter what you believed beforehand. That deference only makes sense if the chance is something in the world, distinct from your belief, which your belief is supposed to follow. So you need objective chances after all. And in our case there is no chance to be told about, which is exactly why I said at the start that there is nothing here to be true or false.

(10) Jonah: I will happily defer to chances when someone tells me about them. That only shows that chances earn their keep by moving belief, not that they are frequencies or primitive tendencies. In the one-shot case no one tells me anything, my 0.52 stands, and your propensity has to do its work in a world with no repetitions. Meanwhile my account at least tells me what to do tomorrow, and yours does not.

What I would like is a ruling on the arguments as they were actually made. For each of the ten numbered moves, please say whether it is right, partly right, or wrong, and give a sentence or two of justification; where a move concedes something important or quietly shifts the speaker's position, say so. Then tell me who, if anyone, is more right overall, and what both of them are missing. I can look up the standard -isms on my own, so please don't just sort them into textbook labels — I want to know who won which point.

One thing I noticed but did not say at the time, and I don't know whether it helps either of them: nobody asked whether the machine's flip is deterministic. If you launched the coin in exactly the same way, wouldn't it land the same way? If so, I couldn't tell what any of them should say about that.
