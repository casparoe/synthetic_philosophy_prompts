hey, I'm a software engineer (no philosophy background, sorry if this is a dumb framing) and I fell into the interpretability side of ML twitter and there's a debate there I think is basically already settled, but apparently smart people keep arguing about it, so I want to know what I'm missing.

The question is supposed to be whether a trained net "really" represents features, or whether we're just projecting our own concepts onto it. But here's my argument: the activations at some layer are just a vector in R^n. Give me any invertible matrix M and I can rewrite that vector in a different basis, and every single one of those bases yields a perfectly coherent story about which features are present and in what amounts. The math doesn't privilege any basis. So there's no fact of the matter about what the network represents, and the realists must be claiming something extra that the vector space just doesn't give them.

Like, take the famous demo where someone finds a direction that lights up on pictures of the Golden Gate Bridge. Fine. But I can rotate that direction a bit and get one that fires on bridges in general, or on the colour orange, or on San Francisco, and all of those are equally legitimate descriptions of the same activation vector. So the feature isn't in the network, it's in my choice of coordinates. And training doesn't break the tie either — training moves the vector around, it doesn't pick out a basis.

I also keep seeing people say sparse autoencoders settle it, which can't be right: an SAE finds one dictionary out of many that reconstructs the activations well, and low reconstruction error doesn't mean those are the features actually doing the work downstream.

So what is the realist claim that survives this? Is there a version of "the net represents X" where the non-uniqueness of the decomposition isn't fatal? Or is the real fight about something else entirely, like carrying information vs. doing causal work? I feel like I'm missing the actual crux and everyone is talking past each other.
