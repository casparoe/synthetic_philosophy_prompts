I write about science and ideas for a living, and I am trying to work out whether an argument I have been carrying around for a piece on the limits of inquiry is a result or a mistake. The core of it is the Fitch-style argument against the claim that every truth is knowable. The part I built myself is a generalisation of it, and the generalisation is what I distrust: it seems to refute things I am not prepared to give up.

Notation: *Kp* means that someone knows that *p* at some time; ◇ and □ are metaphysical possibility and necessity.

(1) Knowability: for every truth *p*, ◇*Kp*.
(2) Unknown truths: for some *q*, *q* is true and ¬*Kq* — no one, at any time, ever knows that *q*.
(3) Knowledge is factive: necessarily, if someone knows that *p* then *p*. And it distributes over conjunction: necessarily, if someone knows that *p* and *r*, then they know that *p*.
(4) Let *q* be as in (2), and let *p\** be the proposition *q* ∧ ¬*Kq*. By (2), *p\** is true; so by (1), ◇*Kp\**.
(5) But *Kp\** is impossible. Suppose it holds: by distribution, *Kq* and *K*¬*Kq*; by factivity applied to the second, ¬*Kq*. So *Kq* and ¬*Kq*. Hence □¬*Kp\** and ¬◇*Kp\**, contradicting (4).
(6) So (1) and (2) cannot both be true. Since (2) is about as firm as anything — no one will ever know how many individual insects died in a particular field in a particular week last summer, or the exact number of grains of sand on a particular beach at midnight on some date in 1650 — the standard verdict is that (1) fails: some truths are unknowable even in principle.

The generalisation. Nothing in (4)–(5) used anything specific to knowledge. Let *O* be any operator that is factive and distributes over conjunction. The same three lines show that *for every truth p, ◇Op* is incompatible with *for some q, q* ∧ ¬*Oq* — that is, any unrestricted *in principle* claim for a factive operator collapses into the corresponding *in fact* claim. Take *O* to be *some observer measures that*: if every physical fact is in principle measurable, then every physical fact has been measured. Take *O* to be *some inquirer proves that, in a sound system*: if every truth is in principle provable, then every truth has been proved. Take *O* to be *someone regrets that*: if every truth could be regretted, every truth is regretted.

That batch is why I suspect my own construction. The claim I would least like to surrender is not (1) but its scientific cousin: no physical fact is such that no possible observation could even bear on it. That is roughly what separates a testable hypothesis from metaphysics, and I lean on it constantly. Yet the generalisation says the creed entails that every physical fact has been observed, which is flatly false.

I have three candidate diagnoses and cannot tell which is right, or whether any is. First, the generalisation slides: propositions like *no one ever measures that q* are not themselves candidates for measurement, so they fall outside the range of the operator even though they fall inside the range of *every truth* in (1). Second, the argument is valid and I should accept that no factive operator admits an unrestricted in-principle version — giving up the observability creed along with knowability. Third, something is wrong with how (1) quantifies: perhaps it should be read as a schema over a language that cannot express ¬*Kq*, or perhaps the *someone* in (1) ranges over merely possible inquirers while the *no one* in (2) ranges over actual ones, in which case *Kp\** is not contradictory and (5) never gets started.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would like to keep (2), the factivity and distribution of knowledge, and the observability creed; I would sooner give up (1) than any of those — but not if giving it up means restricting it in a way that is ad hoc, or in a way that invites a revenge instance in the style of the liar paradox. Please price the main options: restricting knowability to some privileged class of propositions; denying factivity at the modal strength the argument needs; denying distribution; restricting the domain of propositional quantification; weakening the logic so that the argument yields something like *no truth is unknown* rather than *every truth is known*; and denying (2). Then tell me whether the generalisation is a genuine result or rests on an equivocation — and note that whatever fix I adopt has to work uniformly for the core argument and for the generalisation, which I suspect pull in different directions.
