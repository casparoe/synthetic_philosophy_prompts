I sit on the board of Riverbend Metro Transit (fictional name, obviously — mid-sized city, about 14 stations, two of them interchange). We're finally replacing the 1970s turnstiles, and last night's meeting ran past 10pm without a vote, so I've been asked to circulate a written opinion before the next one. Two of the three bids are simple: one vendor sells fixed half-height gates (open sightlines, easy to hop, basically an honour system with random inspections), another sells full-height revolving "security" gates (hard to evade, but a nightmare for wheelchairs, prams, bikes, and anyone carrying something bulky). The third bid is the one we're stuck on. Same physical hardware either way, but it can be installed at different heights and, crucially, it has a software "enforcement mode" — low, medium, or high — that can be flipped remotely from the vendor's dashboard. The vendor's pitch is that this is "policy-neutral hardware," future-proofed so that an elected board can revisit the question later instead of being stuck with whatever concrete we poured. The catch: every mode change carries a per-change licence fee, changes need five business days' notice, the audit log of who changed what is only viewable by the vendor's account holders rather than by us, and the unit ships with high as the factory default. Our ops director also quietly mentioned that in the two other systems he's seen install it, nobody ever actually changes the mode after go-live, because the fee has to come out of somebody's maintenance budget — so the default quietly becomes permanent. A friend of mine who did science-and-technology studies in grad school keeps sending me links about that old 1980 Winner essay on whether artifacts have politics (the one with the low overpasses on the Long Island parkways) and about the social-constructivist reply that the same hardware is politically indeterminate until it's embedded in a practice, and I get the gist but I can't tell how either position is supposed to cash out in a procurement decision where the interesting variable isn't the artefact but the switch.

What I want from you is the memo itself, addressed to the board, with a real recommendation at the top — approve the configurable bid, approve it with conditions, or reject it and buy the fixed gates — plus the reasoning, and I'd like the reasoning to actually run in both directions rather than just decorating a decision I'd have made anyway. Concretely: take Winner's two ways an artefact can be political (arrangements that quietly settle a distributive question, versus technologies that only work within a particular kind of social order) and tell me which one, if either, is doing work here, given that our hardware is deliberately designed not to settle anything. Then take the flexibility argument seriously and press on it: if the politics of a gate lives in the mode rather than the metal, doesn't the politics just move up a level, into the factory default, the price of switching, who holds the credentials to switch, and who can see the log? Is a design that makes its own politics reversible and chargeable more or less political than one that bakes it in — and is "we can always revisit it later" a genuine democratic gain or just a way of making a contested choice nobody has to vote on? I also want the case to be used as a test of the theory and not only an application of it: does a reconfigurable artefact with a paid, logged, vendor-mediated switch look like an anomaly for Winner's framing (which assumes fairly fixed infrastructure), or does it confirm it, or does it just show the whole framing was silent about the meta-level all along? Please engage the strongest version of the losing side — for us that's the pragmatist view that the board has to bolt something to the floor, that a reversible decision beats a poured-concrete one, and that rejecting the flexible bid probably means buying whatever fixed high gates are cheapest — and say honestly if the memo's recommendation would change were the default shipped as low instead of high.

Please write it in British English, as a proper memo I could forward — heading block, recommendation up front, then the substance — about 900 to 1,100 words, plain prose rather than bullet soup, though numbered conditions at the end are welcome. Don't cite anything or point me at reading; I can't access the web from this account and I need the argument, not sources. One more thing: I want a fallback line in the memo for if the vendor refuses to move on the default or the audit log, since our counsel thinks they will dig in, so please say what we should do in that case rather than leaving it hanging. And I'd rather you didn't recieve... sorry, I mean, I'd rather you didn't just restate the overpass story at me; use it only if it genuinely clarfiies something about gates.
