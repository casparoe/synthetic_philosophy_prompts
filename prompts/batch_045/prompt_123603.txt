My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether a neuron-by-neuron replacement procedurecould get a person onto a synthetic substrate, and I came away with an argument whose conclusion I cannot accept but whose steps I cannot prise apart.

(1) If a procedure is such that at no step is there any difference, from the subject's own point of view, in what they experience or in what they would say about it, then the procedure preserves that subject's consciousness.

(2) In gradual replacement, each biological neuron is swapped for a device that emits exactly the signals the original neuron would have emitted in response to the signals that in fact arrive at it. Every such swap is therefore undetectable from the inside, and so is the sequence of them.

(3) So the fully synthetic successor is conscious, and is me.

(4) Nothing in (1) or (2) says how the substitute produces its signals, or that the swaps be spread out in time. So consider a transcript substitute: before the procedure we simulate my brain in fine detail for the next eighty years, record for each neuron the train of signals it emits, and install, in place of each neuron, a player keyed to the signals that in fact arrive. Because the body goes on behaving exactly as recorded, the world goes on responding exactly as recorded, so the transcript never runs off the end of its track; and every swap is undetectable from the inside for precisely the reason given in (2).

(5) So the same argument shows that a brain made entirely of transcript players is conscious — and, for the same reason, that replacing my whole brain with such a system in one step would be too, since that also produces no difference the subject could notice.

(6) But (5) takes away the point of gradual replacement, which was supposed to be the slow route around the discontinuity worry. If a brain of players is conscious, gradualism buys nothing over instant replacement; if it is not, then (1) and (2) cannot be what makes (3) true.

My friend's way out is to accept the transcript brain as conscious and say I should never have expected otherwise. I would rather keep (3). But then I owe a premise that separates the prosthetic from the player, and the only difference I can locate is that the prosthetic would also do the right thing for signals it never actually receives — a counterfactual fact, which no premise about what the subject can notice seems able to deliver. And any finite list of counterfactual cases I care to name can simply be recorded into the transcript as well.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Specifically: can (1) be strengthened so that it supports (3) while ceasing to support the one-step transcript case, and what does such a strengthening commit you to about how many counterfactuals a substitute must respect and who does the respecting? And if instead you take my friend's way out, what do you owe — is there any remaining way for that view to deny consciousness to a system that only looks its responses up, or is that just a prejudice about hardware I should give up?
