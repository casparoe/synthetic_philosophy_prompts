I'm a journalist working on a piece about motivated reasoning, and two of my sources got into an argument over dinner that I could not referee. Please write your answer in American English.

Marisol, who does philosophy, opened with the standard worry: self-deception as literally described is impossible. To deceive yourself into believing not-p you have to know p, and to succeed you have to not know p, at the same time and about the same proposition. That isn't a weird mental state, it's just a contradiction. The intention version is worse: a plan to bring it about that you believe not-p while knowing p is a plan whose success destroys the knowledge the plan requires you to have. So the cases we point at are really wishful thinking, or weakness of will, or plain error, and the word *deception* is doing all the work.

Dave, a clinical psychology guy, said that argument proves too much. Ordinary interpersonal deception doesn't require the deceiver to hold contradictory beliefs either: the liar believes p and gets the victim to believe not-p, and nobody thinks the liar must therefore be confused. What deception needs is a deceiver role and a victim role, and self-deception is just the special case where both roles are filled by one person. You don't need a homunculus for that. There's plenty of ordinary machinery: selective attention, selectively gathering evidence, stopping the search early. On his picture the self-deceiver doesn't believe both p and not-p; she believes not-p, and the truth is present in her only in the thin dispositional sense that she would revert to believing p if the motivation lapsed. That is a real state, and it deserves the name.

Marisol came back: if that's all it is, you've changed the subject. You've described motivated error, which is everywhere, and there's nothing specially deceptive about it, and nothing that explains why we blame the self-deceiver in a way we don't blame someone who was simply misled by another person. Worse, on your account the agent doesn't have the truth in view at all — the truth is just causally nearby. But the classic cases are supposed to involve someone who almost sees it, who has to keep looking away.

Dave then pointed at something I kept running into in my own reporting: people who arrange not to find things out. They tell their assistant never to put certain emails in the summary. They install a blocker so they can't check a page or a result. They know there is something bad in there, they engineer it so they never learn the specifics, and they end up with no false belief at all — just a carefully maintained blank. He called it managed ignorance. If that counts as self-deception, the concept needs neither contradictory belief nor a false belief; what it needs is a plan the agent executes against her own information-gathering. And it's clearly blameworthy in the way you said was missing.

Marisol said managed ignorance is real but is the mirror image of what we started with, not a solution to it. In the classic case the agent ends up with a confident positive belief that is false; he thinks she's faithful. Your avoider ends up with nothing. And a gap can't do that work: someone who avoids the scan isn't thereby believing she's healthy, and if she walks around acting as though she is, that's an extra step you haven't accounted for. Anyway, she said, your avoider knows exactly what she is doing — she is the least self-deceived person in the story.

Dave's last move: that's too quick, because she also has to keep the avoidance itself out of view. If she noticed that she was engineering the blank, the blank would tell her what she doesn't want to know. So managed ignorance needs a second-order version of itself, and now we're back to something that looks like hiding something from yourself.

I've got two thousand words due and I still can't tell who won. Can you go through this move by move and say who is right about what, rather than just who wins overall? Roughly rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, flag anywhere one of them is attacking a claim the other didn't actually make, and tell me what both of them are missing — including whether managed ignorance is the same phenomenon as the classic cases, a different one, or a version of one hiding inside the other.
