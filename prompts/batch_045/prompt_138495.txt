I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've been asked to run a breakout session at a health journalism conference this autumn: 75 minutes, a hotel meeting room with six round tables, and about twenty participants — mostly medical and science writers, plus four or five practicing clinicians who drifted in. No projector, no laptops. I can print handouts and I will have a flip chart.

The session is about what makes something a disease. I do not want to open with the distinction between naturalist and normativist accounts of health and disease, and I do not want anyone in the room to hear either word until the final ten minutes. I want them to collide with the disagreement instead of being told about it. So: design me an activity — a staged deliberation, a committee simulation, a role-play, whatever fits — in which participants have to decide, case by case, whether a candidate condition goes onto an official list of diseases, and then discover that they cannot state the rule they have been applying.

Constraints. The cases should be invented rather than drawn from real diagnostic controversies: I want control over the variables, and I do not want the hour consumed by people relitigating the science of conditions they have actually reported on. What I need is a set of cases that quietly cross-cuts the two dimensions the philosophical argument turns on — whether a condition is a departure from normal biological functioning, and whether it is bad for the person who has it — so that some cases are dysfunctional but harmless, some are deeply distressing but biologically unremarkable, some are both, and at least one is a problem only because of the environment its bearer lives in, or only because of the label itself. I also want one twist: partway through, something about a case changes and the group has to vote again, and the flip — or the stubborn refusal to flip — is where the useful material is. Give the decisions concrete and small stakes: a fixed research budget, a coverage ruling, a diagnostic code that unlocks or withholds something. Handouts should carry no philosophical vocabulary at all.

Please give me: the setup and the handout text itself; the rules, roles, and a timeline with minute marks; how the second vote is run; and a debrief for the last ten minutes that maps specific moments in the room onto each of the two competing pictures — including the point at which participants find that their own verdicts are inconsistent across matched pairs of cases. I want them to leave holding two things: that "it is simply a biological fact" and "it is simply a value judgment" both become hard to say with a straight face once you have had to decide, and a sense of what actually rides on the choice — who has the authority to classify, who gets treatment and who gets a name for their suffering. Be candid about what will predictably go wrong: my participants are professional skeptics, they will interrogate the facts on the cards, and they will ask what the real study showed. Tell me how to absorb that without breaking the frame. Also tell me where I should flag that I might quote them, since this may end up in print.

Finally, your honest read on the limits of the exercise: which feature of the disagreement no committee role-play will make anyone feel, and which I will simply have to say out loud at the end.

Please write the whole thing in American English.
