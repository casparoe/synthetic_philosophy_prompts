I am a doctoral student in philosophy of biology. I have constructed an argument against multi-level selection that I have come to think proves far too much, and I would be grateful for a diagnosis of where it breaks.

The argument runs as follows.

(1) A trait T is an adaptation *for* entities at level L only if T was produced by selection among entities at level L. If it was produced by selection at some other level, then at L it is a byproduct.

(2) Where selection acts at several levels at once, only the level with the greatest response to selection produces adaptations. Responses at the other levels are swamped, and show up at those levels as byproducts.

(3) The response to selection at a level, over a fixed period, is proportional to the number of reproductive events that entities at that level undergo in that period: units that turn over more often accumulate more evolutionary change.

(4) For every level above that of the gene there is a level below it that turns over faster. Cells divide more often than the bodies containing them reproduce; bodies reproduce more often than the groups containing them fission and go extinct; and so on up.

(5) Therefore no level above that of the gene is ever a unit of selection. Every appearance of design above the gene — the vertebrate eye, the mammalian heart, the sterile worker, the cooperative colony — is a gene-level adaptation or a byproduct of one.

(6) Applied to a multicellular body: the cell lineages within it turn over far faster than the body does, so the body is not a unit of selection, and its organisation is a byproduct of selection among cell lineages.

(7) It follows that the mechanisms which hold a body together — a single-cell bottleneck at conception, a sequestered germline, fair meiosis, the policing or programmed death of cheater cell lines — are not adaptations for the body either, since by (5) nothing above the gene is an adaptation.

(8) But those mechanisms are the standard explanation of why bodies, genomes and multicellular life are stable at all: they are exactly what prevents the faster lower level from winning. And (6) predicts what we do not observe — heritable variation among cell lineages routinely overriding the organisation of the body, cancer unopposed, multicellularity never having got off the ground.

What embarrasses me is that I built (1) through (5) in order to say something principled about why the multi-level selectionists cannot have organism-level design and group-level design both, and it turns out to say that organisms are not real either.

Two things I cannot place. First: a collaborator in synthetic biology evolves engineered microbial consortia in which two strains cross-feed obligately, and she imposes a single-cell bottleneck at each transfer, so that within any consortium there is no heritable variation at all while between consortia there is a great deal. Productivity at the consortium level responds to her selection at once. I have been dismissing this as a laboratory artifact, on the grounds that a bottleneck alters variances and not rates, but I keep returning to it. Second: if I imagine a lineage in which the higher-level entity turns over faster than its parts — colonies that bud off new colonies many times a day while the cells composing them divide once a season — the argument reverses, and returns colonies as the units and cells as byproducts. That makes me suspect the conclusion was never about levels at all but about some quantity I have not isolated.

I would most like to keep (1) and (2). Step (3) has always struck me as the kind of empirical generalization that does no conceptual work, and my supervisor says much the same. But when I remove (3) I do not get my argument back: I lose the ability to say why a gene's-eye description is ever the correct one, and I seem to slide into the pluralism I set out to refute.

Which step do you reject? Explain why the step looked safe, and price each of the alternatives for me — including the alternative of accepting the argument and its conclusion about organisms.
