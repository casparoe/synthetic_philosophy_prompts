I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I work on software supply-chain security, where provenance is treated as a first-class object: a byte-identical binary with no signed attestation is worthless to us, while the very same bytes with a valid signature and a proper bill of materials are trusted. I also help run a small artist-run space, and for about a year now a friend and I have argued about whether my professional intuition transfers to artworks. She holds what I would call the experience-first view: a work's value is exhausted by what a competent, well-informed viewer can get from it under good viewing conditions, so history and provenance matter only evidentially, as a signal of what you are about to experience. (She also likes the argument that if two works cannot be told apart by looking, then you simply have to look harder — and that the difference you eventually learn to see is a genuine perceptual difference, not a piece of extra information bolted onto the experience. I have never been able to work out whether that argument helps her or helps me.) I hold the provenance-first view: part of what is valuable in a work consists in its standing in the right causal-historical relation to the artist's own act, so that a perfect substitute, however indistinguishable, is a different and lesser thing. We have agreed to stop arguing and to write the adversarial-collaboration document we should have written a year ago. Please draft it for us.

Format it as follows. These are the cruxes I think matter, though please say plainly if any of them is a red herring, if two are really one, or if there is a better one I have missed:

- The silent swap. The original is destroyed in a fire and replaced overnight with an atom-for-atom duplicate; the switch is never discovered. Has anything of value been lost, and if so, lost to whom?
- De-attribution and re-attribution. A painting from the master's own hand is catalogued for decades as a studio copy and prized only as a curiosity; it is then re-attributed, and its price, its placement in the museum and the attention viewers give it all jump, with no physical change whatever. Is that jump a response to a real property that was there all along, or is it a change in belief that changes what people look for and hence what they experience?
- The ageing forgery. A forgery made in the 1930s is so accomplished that it reshaped how the master's late period was understood. It is now a century old, painted by a real person with real skill, and hangs in a museum labelled as the work of an imitator. Can authenticity accrue with time — can an object acquire, through its own subsequent history, some of what it originally lacked?
- The authorised multiple. A sculptor makes a mould and authorises twelve lifetime casts; all twelve come equally from her hand and none is a copy of another. What, if anything, is lost across the set, and does the answer tell against my view or against hers?
- The signed edition. A video work is issued as five cryptographically signed copies of one identical file; the files are bit-identical and the only difference between number one and number five is a key and a certificate. Please treat this as a real crux rather than a curiosity, because it is the case I actually reason from: if provenance here is nothing but a signature, is that a degenerate or merely legalistic case of provenance, or is all provenance like this underneath?
