I'm a senior at a public high school and I work around fifteen hours a week at a grocery store, which is how I ended up in an argument in my economics class that nobody in the room, including the teacher, seemed to know how to finish. We were discussing whether a country should cap how many hours an adult can be asked to work. Someone brought up France's thirty-five-hour week, someone else brought up karoshi — the Japanese phenomenon of people dying from overwork — and my teacher pointed out that we already have a patchwork of rules about long hours and that the pieces don't obviously add up. From what I can gather, the pattern is roughly this: if I work more than forty hours in a week, my employer has to pay me one and a half times my normal rate for the extra time; my mother, who is paid a fixed annual salary at a hospital and is described as exempt, gets nothing extra however many hours she puts in, and recently did several weeks of sixty; my brother drives a delivery truck and is required to keep an electronic log that legally forces him to stop driving after a certain number of hours, and commercial airline pilots have rest requirements of the same general kind. So extra hours are expensive for some workers, free for others, and flatly illegal for a third group. On top of that, we let an adult consent to work as many hours as they like for money, but we will not let that same adult consent to work for less than a legally set wage, so we don't entirely trust consent in the labor market either.

What I want to know is what view of work, rest, and the person doing the working this arrangement presupposes, and whether the parts of it presuppose the same thing. Some candidate thoughts I can't sort out: that long hours are chiefly a cost to the worker and that making them expensive is therefore enough; that what the law really protects is third parties who never accepted the risk, which would explain the truck and plane rules but not mine; that hours are a good measure of what some workers give and a bad measure of what others give, so salaried people are being paid for a role or a level of responsibility rather than for time; that what makes my mother's sixty-hour weeks acceptable is simply that she agreed to them; or something older and less economic, like the idea that diligence is a virtue and idleness a kind of moral failure, which a podcast I listened to blamed on the Protestant work ethic.

For each of these I'd like to know whether the practice actually needs it or merely sits comfortably beside it, and where the pattern is underdetermined enough that several quite different theories would produce the same laws. I'd also like to be told which parts are genuinely in tension with each other rather than merely looking that way — the overtime premium and the salaried exemption, or the way we override consent on wages but defer to it on hours — and what would have to change if we took seriously the thought behind both the thirty-five-hour week and the word karoshi: that there is some number of hours past which a person is being damaged, whether or not they agreed to it. Someone in class also said the UN's human-rights declaration apparently lists rest and leisure among human rights; if that's right, I'd like to know whether it belongs in this picture at all, or whether it is doing something completely different from anything the rules above are doing.
