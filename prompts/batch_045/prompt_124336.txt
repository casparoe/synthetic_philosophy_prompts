i'm in a study group for my third year metaphysics seminar and I wrote up this argument for our session on the special composition question, mostly to wind up my friend who's a die-hard commonsense realist about tables and chairs, and now I can't find the hole in it — partly because I'm not sure I want to. Here's what I've got:

(1) Something earns a place in our ontology only if believing in it lets us explain or predict something we couldn't explain or predict without it — no causally idle posits.
(2) For any composite thing, its causal powers are fixed by the causal powers of its parts together with their arrangement: there's no difference in what the whole can do without some difference in the parts or in how they're arranged.
(3) So the causal work done by a composite is already done by its parts, and the composite itself contributes nothing.
(4) So no composite earns a place in the ontology: there are no composite objects, only whatever is causally basic (simples, if there are any).
(5) Nothing in (1) to (4) turns on how big the composite is or on how attached we are to it, so the argument generalizes: a proton's powers are fixed by its quarks, a person's by the particles making them up, a belief's by the neural goings-on given that the physical domain is causally closed, a nation's economy by the people and firms in it — so protons, people, beliefs and economies all fail (1) in exactly the way tables do.
(6) And if there's no mereological bedrock, if it's proper parts all the way down — the gunky scenario nobody has actually ruled out — then nothing at all satisfies (1), and there is nothing at all.
(7) I can live without tables. I can't live without people, and I definately can't live with there being nothing, so something in there has to go.

My friend says the argument is fine and that I should just read (4) as saying only fundamental things exist, so protons and people "exist" in the everyday sense while dropping out of the serious inventory — which feels like changing the subject, but I can't say why. If I have to pick, I'd rather keep (2) than (1): (2) looks to me like just a fact about how the world hangs together, whereas (1) is a methodological rule I invented because I wanted it. But I also don't want to give up (1), because it's the only thing I have against the view that any old scattered bunch of things composes something. So: which step do you reject, why did that step look fine, and what does each of the other ways out cost me? Please end with one last line on its own: Verdict: <the step or transition you reject, like (3) or (2)->(3)>.
