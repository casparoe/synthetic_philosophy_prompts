I am setting the take-home final for an upper-level seminar — fourteen third- and fourth-year students — on naming, dispute, and the drawing of distinctions in Warring States thought. The final block of the course is the 'white horse' chapter of the Gongsun Longzi. I need one essay question on it: ninety minutes, hard cap of 1,200 words, marked out of 20. The difficulty I am having is that the topic invites exactly two stock answers — that the sentence is merely ambiguous between a claim about non-identity and a claim about class membership, and that the whole thing is sophistry — and both can be produced by a student who has not thought about the argument at all. I want a question that a student can only answer well by doing something with the argument.

Assume candidates have the chapter open in front of them but have not memorised it, so the stem should itself lay out the materials they must work with, in your own words: the opening claim that 'horse' designates shape and 'white' designates colour, and that what designates colour does not designate shape; the advocate's argument about what may be delivered — that if one seeks a horse, yellow and black horses may be brought, while if one seeks a white horse they may not, that what is sought would then be the same, and that the acceptable and the unacceptable are mutually opposed; the objector's reminder that having a white horse is having a horse, together with whatever the advocate must concede in reply; and the closing material on whether whiteness and horse can be separated from a white horse.

To block the stock answers, the question should build in three stipulated comparison cases the students have not seen. First: a wooden horse is not a horse — everyone in the seminar will grant that the sentence is true there, on any reading. Second: a white horse is not a horse, which is true on one reading and obviously false on another. Third: imagine a language in which the phrase 'white horse' is replaced by a single unanalysable word applied to exactly the white horses, a word containing no part that means horse and no part that means white; ask whether the thesis could still be asserted in that language and whether the argument about what may be delivered would still go through in it. The point of the set is to force the question of whether the delivery argument can distinguish the second case from the first — whether it can tell a modifier that excludes from a modifier that merely specifies — and, if it cannot, what else in the chapter would have to carry the weight, and whether that other material yields a thesis anyone should have denied.

Please give me:

1. The question as it will appear on the paper, in a handout version of no more than 130 words, with the three cases stated crisply enough to be used under exam conditions.
2. A model answer of roughly 900 to 1,100 words. It should take a definite position on what the delivery argument establishes, on what work the shape-and-colour material and the separability material are doing once the delivery argument is demoted, and on what, if anything, is left of the thesis once everything merely verbal has been set aside. No secondary literature, no citations.
3. A marking scheme of five items at four marks each. For each item, name the specific move an answer must make to earn the marks, and add one line on the mistake that typically loses them — the plausible-sounding thing a candidate writes instead. Give the thresholds that separate an A from a B and a B from a C, and say which items an A answer must have secured.
4. A short closing note, under 150 words, on how you would tell a genuinely good answer from a merely fluent one here, and on what you would expect to go wrong for the strongest students in the room.
