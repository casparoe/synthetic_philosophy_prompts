I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy training, and I've been reading about the string theory / multiverse argument as a hobby. I want to know what kind of argument it actually is, because from the outside it looks like nobody has moved in about twenty years.

Here's what I keep seeing both sides do. One side points at facts about the research community rather than facts about the world: a lot of very good people spent decades trying and failed to build a rival framework that does the same jobs; the framework keeps turning out to already contain the structures you'd want (gravity-ish stuff, gauge-ish stuff) rather than having them bolted on afterwards; the programme keeps generating mathematics that other people then find useful. They treat this as some kind of support for the theory. The other side says none of that touches the world, it's at best a reason to keep funding people, and that the one part that could be checked — how often, historically, has a theory that seemed to have no alternatives turned out to be right? — is a record we can't read, because the cases where it went the other way are exactly the ones nobody writes up.

Also, the same observation gets cited by both sides: "it's striking how many of the things we need just fall out of this" versus "a framework loose enough to give you a huge number of different universes isn't striking, it's just unfalsifiable".

My hypothesis is that this is two questions sharing one name — "is this probably true?" and "is this worth working on, given that we can't test it?" — and that everybody answers whichever one they feel like. But I also suspect there's a real factual disagreement buried inside it, about how crowded the space of possible theories is and how likely the community was to have found an alternative if one existed. In my head it's a prior thing: to be impressed that nobody found a rival, you need to know how likely it was that someone would have found one if it existed, and I've never seen anyone put a number on that, or even say what the number ranges over.

So: verbal, substantive, two different questions under one heading, or a hidden empirical question that nobody can actually get at? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a physics result, a way of formalising the argument so the sides at least disagree about a number, a change of question, or honestly nothing? I'd rather have a diagnosis with reasons than a survey of who said what.
