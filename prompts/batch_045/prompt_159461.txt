Please take the role of a professional political philosopher who also knows the empirical literature on legislatures and party systems, and treat what follows as a consistency audit rather than an opinion piece. Before you answer, brainstorm: write out every candidate tension you can find among the commitments below, including the ones you will later reject, and only then rank the ones that survive. I want to see the reasoning, not just the verdicts.

I have just come out of a very long argument with a friend — the first two hours on whether "left" and "right" pick out anything real, the rest on two hypothetical cases that neither of us could settle — and I have the uneasy sense that the things I said over those hours do not all fit together. Here is the set, stated as neutrally as I can.

(1) Minimal realism. The distinction does real explanatory work. Knowing that A is on the left and B is on the right lets you predict, before you know anything else about them, who will govern with whom, whose voters will defect, which policies get bundled into a single package, and whom each side treats as its principal enemy. I do not know what more "being real" could require of a political distinction than that.

(2) Anti-essentialism about the bonding. What holds each side together is not a shared principle, and there is no proposition such that accepting it puts you on the left. Each side is a historically transmitted bloc: an alliance held together by overlapping enemies, inherited electorates, party organisation and funding, and the brute fact that these people can actually govern together. Call it the bloc view.

(3) A substantive centre of gravity anyway. Left is the side that treats inequalities as presumptively standing in need of justification — as contingent, removable, and requiring a defence — while right is the side that treats at least some inequalities as presumptively justified, or as simply part of how things are. I mean this as a claim about typical content, not about essence.

(4) On the evidence. The familiar result that a single dimension accounts for most of the variance in legislators' roll-call votes, across a wide range of assemblies and periods, is evidence of something structural. I am not willing to write it off as an artifact of how the data are coded or of what happens to get put to a vote.

(5) A verdict on a case. Libertarianism — anti-paternalist, pro-market, and also anti-clerical, permissive on speech, drugs and sexual conduct — is a position on the right. It is not a third thing that synthesises or transcends left and right, and it is not "left on culture and right on economics." I hold this even in countries where its bearers are socially liberal and where the cultural left is their only available ally on speech and lifestyle questions.

(6) Extension beyond politics. I am happy to call non-political things left or right — a style of literary criticism, a theology, a way of writing history — and I do not experience this as a metaphor.

(7) A method I notice myself using, call it position-anchoring. To locate a movement, ask which existing bloc it can realistically govern with, whose voters it inherited, and who praises and blames it; do not read its manifesto, because manifestos get written after the location has already settled. I also think this method gives the right answer in a case I invented to break it. The Swap Case: a country in which, over roughly thirty years, the two blocs exchange their economic platforms more or less wholesale — the party that stood for public ownership and central planning now campaigns for privatisation and deregulation, while its rival now defends the welfare state and industrial policy — while continuing to call themselves, and to be called by voters, by the same labels, and while continuing to govern with the same minor parties. My intuition is that in the Swap Case the blocs keep their locations: the party that was on the left still is, and the labels are not being misapplied.

(8) A second case, if you want a tie-breaker. The Refused Movement: a new movement that wants state-led industrial policy, closed borders and a traditional family policy, and that is refused by both existing blocs. My intuition is that it has no location yet — it acquires one only when it joins a bloc or is taken in by one — not that it sits in the centre or that it is a mix.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture. Which of these genuinely pull against each other, and which only look as though they do? Rank the real tensions by severity — by how much has to be surrendered and how much damage the surrender does to the rest of the set — rather than presenting them as a flat list of equal worries. For each real tension, say exactly what would have to go: the whole commitment, or only the reading I have put on it. Then tell me which single revision does the most work, in the sense of leaving me with the largest surviving part of what I actually care about. Where a tension dissolves on inspection, state in a sentence or two the distinction that dissolves it, so that I can judge whether I have really been let off or merely talked out of it.

Three constraints. First, no surveys of the literature and no names, titles or dates: reconstruct any position you need rather than attributing it, and argue rather than cite. Second, mark explicitly where your answer depends on an empirical claim about how party systems behave as opposed to a conceptual claim about the distinction, and say what you would need to check to be confident there. Third, I am not attached in advance to any particular item on the list, so do not assume that the ones I stated most confidently are the ones I would defend hardest.
