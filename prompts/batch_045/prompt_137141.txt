I'm a reporter covering science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about a mid-sized city that's been quietly experimenting with prediction markets that don't just forecast outcomes but actually pick between policies. I got hold of the minutes of the board that has to sign off on every such proposal (I'll call it the Decision Market Review Board). What I need from you is a prediction about the proposal sitting in front of them right now, but honestly I'm more interested in the rule they're running: the one they'd write down if you asked them, and the one that actually fits all seven of their rulings so far. Skip citations, links, and further reading entirely — I've read the futarchy literature, I just need the reasoning. Please answer in American English.

Setup, so we're on the same page: in every case the mechanism is the conditional one, where traders buy contracts that pay out only if a given option is adopted and the settlement metric comes in above a target, and where the trade is voided and everyone's money returned if that option isn't adopted. Roughly: elected officials set the goal, the market picks the means.

Their record:

1. Approved. Choosing between two snowplow routing algorithms. Settled on average emergency response time over the following winter, measured off fleet GPS data. No stake caps, small liquidity subsidy.
2. Rejected. Whether to build a minor-league ballpark. Settled on a twenty-year local GDP index. Board's stated worry: the settlement date is way past the life of the market, the market will be thin, and a few interested parties can take huge positions.
3. Approved with conditions. Which of two ballot formats to use for participatory budgeting. Settled on turnout plus a post-hoc satisfaction survey. Conditions were a market-making subsidy, a $500 cap per trader, public disclosure of positions above $250, and something they invented called a confidence gate: the council only has to follow the market if the winning option's price clears 65 cents and total volume clears a floor.
4. Rejected. Whether to close a low-enrollment elementary school. Settled on a neighborhood well-being index that the same city office proposing the closure would have designed and computed.
5. Approved. Which of two vendors to run the 311 chatbot. Settled on blinded expert ratings of resolved tickets plus cost per resolution, judged by an outside panel, about eighteen months out.
6. Rejected as binding, but a non-binding shadow market approved instead. Whether to adopt a citywide rent stabilization ordinance, settled on rent burden and housing starts five years out. The market may run and publish prices; the council decides anyway.
7. Approved. Whether to move trash collection to a compressed four-day schedule. Settled on recycling tonnage, complaint rate, and cost at one year. Some residents hate the new schedule and some workers lose their preferred shifts.

The proposal on the table now: a conditional market to choose between building the full twelve-mile protected bike lane network in two years, or phasing it over six years starting with a pilot corridor. The settlement metric was drafted by a citizens assembly over three weekends rather than by the city, and it's a blend of bike commute mode share and the rate of serious cyclist injuries; it will be measured by the regional metropolitan planning organization, which is independent of the department that would build the lanes. Accounts are limited to city residents and locally based businesses, non-transferable, capped at $1,000, with disclosed positions, a subsidized market maker, and the same confidence gate as in case 3. The novel bit: a split-horizon settlement, where 40 percent of each payout settles on eighteen-month data and 60 percent on five-year data. The board has never ruled on a split-horizon settlement before. On the losing side, roughly forty businesses on two corridors lose on-street parking, and one bridge loses a traffic lane.

So: what do they do? Give me the candidate rules that fit all seven earlier verdicts, say what each one predicts here, and give me your best guess with the reason you'd bet on it. Which precedent is the closest analogue? And if their record genuinely underdetermines it, tell me what principle they'd be committing to with each choice, plus the one further case you'd want to see to tell the surviving rules apart.
