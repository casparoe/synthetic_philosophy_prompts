I'm an undergraduate taking a philosophy seminar on collective memory and political identity, and over the past few weeks I've become attached to a thesis about lost-cause myths that I now need to stress-test before I write it up. I'd be grateful for a careful map of what would actually count against it.

Here is the thesis, as precisely as I can state it. A lost cause stabilizes into a durable national myth only when the defeat itself can be represented as something the community *chose*: when the loss can be redescribed as declining the terms on which survival or victory was available, and therefore as an act of fidelity to a value that matters more than winning, rather than as something that merely happened to the community. The emotional engine of these myths, on my account, is the conversion of a passive injury into an authored act — the losers did not lose, they elected. That is what makes the Confederacy's Lost Cause, the Kosovo legend, and the Masada story recognizably the same kind of thing despite their differences: the first recasts secession and defeat as constitutional fidelity against overwhelming odds, the second turns a catastrophic battle into a deliberate preference for one kind of kingdom over another, and the third turns the end of a siege into a death the enemy could not inflict.

A consequence I like, and that I take to be a genuine virtue, is that this reverses the usual emphasis on the content of the cause. What keeps the myth alive is a formal feature — the chosen-ness of the loss — not whether the original cause was defensible. That is meant to explain something I find otherwise puzzling: that people can stay attached to a myth long after they have stopped endorsing what the losers were fighting for.

Three things worry me, and I'd rather you engage with them than restate my view back to me.

First, my tutor raised a class of narratives that redeem a defeat by attributing it to betrayal at home rather than to the enemy's strength. These seem to put an agent back into the story and to restore the honour of the defeated, but not through anything the community chose or can plausibly take pride in. I can't tell whether this is a counterexample, a third category I need, or my own thesis in disguise.

Second, I'm unsure how to classify Dunkirk. It looks like a defeat turned into a celebration, but it was a defeat inside a war the community went on to win, and its myth seems to be about rescue and endurance rather than about a loss that had to be made meaningful. If a loss must be unredeemed by any later victory to do the work my thesis assigns it, Dunkirk may not be a lost-cause myth at all — and I don't know whether that is a problem for me or a prediction I should embrace.

Third, as far as I can tell from seminar discussion, the earliest surviving narrative of Masada was written by someone who had gone over to the winning side, which makes it a strange specimen: it may be the victors who first told it. I don't know whether that should unsettle a thesis built on what the defeated community does with its own losses.

What I want from you:

1. The considerations that should move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, including ones already visible in the cases above.
2. For each, whether a sympathetic defender of my thesis would concede it as counting against them or would dismiss it as resting on a rival framing — and where exactly the line between those runs.
3. An honest verdict on how much real exposure the thesis has. In particular: if, in principle, any defeat can be narratively redescribed as chosen, does my claim still explain anything, or has it been qualified into unfalsifiability? And if it has, say plainly whether that is fatal for a thesis of this kind or an acceptable feature of an interpretive claim.
4. What the thesis predicts that its main rivals do not, stated so that I could tell the predictions apart.
5. What would move me most, personally, is a case in which the chosen-loss framing of a myth was publicly dismantled and the myth either died or mutated in a direction my account does not predict. I can't check whether that has happened from where I'm writing, so please tell me how much weight such a finding would carry if it were true, and what would make it merely awkward rather than decisive.

Please don't give me reading suggestions, bibliographies, or citations — I'm drafting this offline and I want the arguments themselves rather than pointers to sources. Roughly 1,000 to 1,300 words, American English, with the defeaters in a ranked list and a short final section on the falsifiability question.
