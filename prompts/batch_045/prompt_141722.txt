i keep going back and forth on this and my advisor is useless on it, so: why has the methodological individualism vs holism thing never actually gone anywhere? I'm a sociology PhD student, I do quantitative and agent-based work on residential segregation and neighborhood effects, and in my world the debate shows up in a very specific form. Every couple of years someone publishes a paper saying a "neighborhood effect" or a "context effect" isn't established until you've got a microfoundation for it, and then someone replies that the macro variable isn't a sum of individual properties anyway so the demand is beside the point, and then nothing happens. People gesture at the same classic arguments, nobody's position moves, both sides write as if they won decades ago. One of my committee members just keeps writing "what's the mechanism" in the margin of my chapter on network composition effects and I have no idea which of these arguments he thinks he's making.

My hypothesis is that it isn't one disagreement, and that this is most of why it looks frozen. I think there are at least four claims wearing the same label:

1. an ontological one: whether there are social entities or structures with causal powers that aren't just individuals and their relations;
2. an explanatory one: whether an explanation is acceptable or complete only if it specifies the individual-level mechanism that produces the pattern;
3. an evidential or inferential one: whether a macro-level regularity is evidence of anything at all, or whether it might just be an artifact of aggregation, selection or the ecological-inference problem, i.e. a description that could be produced by ten different micro stories;
4. a heuristic or division-of-labour one: where it's most tractable to look for models, which is basically an engineering question with no true answer.

And separately, I suspect a lot of the heat comes from an unmarked distinction about the shape of the macro variable. Call a macro variable aggregative if it's fixed by the distribution of individual-level properties (mean income, share with a degree) and relational otherwise: it depends on who is connected to or separated from whom, on the arrangement and not just the list. Segregation indices, network density and centralization, variance, whether a cluster crosses a threshold, whether a norm has a tipping point. My sense is that the individualist's "just build it up from the micro level" demand is cheap for aggregative variables and weirdly expensive for relational ones, because you can hold every individual's intrinsic properties fixed and change the macro variable by rearranging people, and you can hold the macro variable fixed while the sign of every individual contribution flips. So the "nothing over and above" slogan is doing totally different work in the two cases. If that's right it's a distinction that isn't in any of the slogans, and I want to know whether it survives inspection or collapses as soon as you push on it.

Then the diagnosis. For each of the four: is it verbal, substantive, or a difference about standards of evidence? Which ones are the same question asked at different levels of description? Is there a hidden empirical question doing all the work — about how often relational variables actually matter, or about whether macro-level relations are stable enough to support counterfactuals under intervention? Are the ontological and explanatory claims logically independent, so that you could be a holist about explanation and an individualist about what exists, meaning a century of argument is people talking past a cross-cutting distinction? Or is the whole thing bedrock temperament about what a good explanation looks like, which neither side can argue for?

And then, given whatever you diagnose, what would count as progress? I don't want a verdict on who's right, I want to know what would end this, and I want you to be willing to say that nothing would. Some candidates I keep turning over: a case where a macro model has explicit microfoundations, the microfoundations are empirically false, and the macro predictions keep working — is that a win for the holist or just a useful fiction; some result about when relational macro variables can or can't be recovered from individual-level data; or just giving up on "what exists" in favour of "which model supports the intervention I care about". Is any of that progress, or is this an ill-posed question where the right move is to dissolve it — and if so what does the dissolved version look like for someone who has to pick a modeling strategy on Monday morning?

Please don't give me a literature review or a reading list, I can assemble one myself. I want the structure of the disagreement, with reasons, and which of my four (or my aggregative/relational thing) is doing the real work.
