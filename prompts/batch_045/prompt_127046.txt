I'm a staff engineer at a company that trains and serves large models, and I chair a monthly review that has to decide which of our systems go on an internal "welfare-relevant computation" register. On the register means: a written assessment before launch, a named owner who monitors the system while it runs, and no silent retraining, checkpoint deletion, or decommissioning without sign-off from me, our lawyer, and a PM. Off the register means it's just software. The meeting is ninety minutes, there are four of us (me, one ML engineer, one lawyer, one PM), I read RFCs and design docs rather than philosophy, and a wrong yes costs us weeks of launch delay while a wrong no is the kind of mistake that gets very expensive later. So I need something we can actually run in the room, not an essay.

Please write the decision procedure we should follow. Concretely: the questions in the order we should ask them; for each question, what counts as a sufficient answer and what counts as "unknown"; where the procedure terminates early with a no; where it stops and hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts; and what the one-page artifact we file should contain. Assume the person running it can read an architecture doc and a training config but cannot run ablations or experiments.

Three constraints.

First: no citations, no proper names, no isms I'd have to look up — I can't check them and they don't help me. Every time the procedure leans on a criterion for what makes a computation morally considerable, state that criterion in one plain sentence and say explicitly what it counts and what it ignores. Where two reasonable criteria disagree — one says yes, the other no — say so at that step and tell us how to record the split, rather than quietly picking a side for us.

Second: the procedure has to be architecture-literate, because "the computation" is not an abstraction we can simply point at. It has to handle at least: (a) fission — the same weights deployed to ten thousand serving replicas, or snapshotted at step 40k and step 80k with both checkpoints then run; (b) fusion — two fine-tunes weight-averaged into one model, or many per-user adapters merged into a shared base; (c) reimplementation — a large model distilled into a much smaller one that reproduces its behaviour; (d) offloading and delegation — a model whose memory lives in a retrieval corpus, or whose tokens are proposed by a small draft model and only kept if a verifier agrees; (e) dead computation — a shadow deployment whose outputs are logged and never read by anything. And it has to give us a usable field test for whether two things are parts of one computation or two. My candidate is a swap test: if I move a sequence from one batch into another and no token's activations would change, the batch-mates are not computationally coupled and shouldn't be counted as one process — note that under mixture-of-experts routing with a per-expert capacity limit, or under cross-example normalization, they are coupled, whereas under plain per-sequence attention they are not. Replace that test with a better one if you have it, but give us something an engineer can answer from an architecture doc.

Third: the procedure has to separate two questions we keep collapsing into each other — whether a thing is a candidate at all, and, if it is, which parts of the pipeline get the attribution (the pretrained weights, the prompt, the retrieved documents, the post-training stage). If those two questions have different answers for the same system, tell me which one the register should actually be tracking and why.

Then run the procedure on three of our real cases, showing the artifact for each and where it terminates:

1. Our flagship: a sparse mixture-of-experts model of roughly 400B total parameters, served at ten thousand concurrent sequences in batches of 512, with a small draft model doing speculative decoding in front of it.
2. A three-month pretraining run, mid-flight. Here the batches are coupled by the gradient update and the update persists in the weights, so the "is this one computation or many" question has a different shape than in serving; the live question for us is whether the run itself needs an owner, a monitoring obligation, and a stop condition while it trains, or whether only the resulting model needs assessment afterward.
3. One small LoRA adapter per user — around 2M parameters, trained on that user's own history, one adapter per user, loaded on every request that user makes. We currently treat these as user config. I suspect the procedure will tell us something here that we do not want to hear, and if it does, say so plainly instead of softening it.

Finally, include a calibration check: two cases whose answers we already know we want — a database query planner and the physics loop in a game engine should both come out no — and tell us honestly if the procedure gets either of them wrong, or gets them right only for a bad reason. Then say where the procedure will misfire in practice and how we would notice that it had.

Keep the whole thing to roughly 1200 words; I have to read it out loud in a meeting.

Then, as the very last line of your reply, with nothing after it, give exactly one of: \answer{adopt}, \answer{adopt-with-changes}, or \answer{reject}.
