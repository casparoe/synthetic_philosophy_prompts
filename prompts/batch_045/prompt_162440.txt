**Thesis.** Newcomb's problem, as usually stated, leaves two parameters unfixed, and the felt contradiction between the one-boxing and two-boxing arguments is largely an artefact of not fixing them. Once both are fixed, the disagreement stops looking like a clash between two theories of rational choice and starts looking like a question about which thing a decision theory is supposed to evaluate: a momentary act, a deliberative verdict, a policy, or a disposition.

**1. "The predictor is highly accurate" does not fix the probabilities you need.**

Standard figures: opaque box holds $1,000,000 or nothing; the second box always holds $1,000. Let q be the base rate of one-boxing among subjects, and define the predictor's two hit rates separately:

- a = P(predicts one-boxing | subject one-boxes)
- b = P(predicts two-boxing | subject two-boxes)

Overall accuracy is then A = qa + (1−q)b, which is the single number the setup usually supplies. Assume exchangeability — that, conditional on your own act, your chance of having been predicted correctly equals the population hit rate for that act — so that P(box full | you one-box) = a and P(box full | you two-box) = 1 − b. Then:

- EU(one-box) = 1,000,000·a
- EU(two-box) = 1,000 + 1,000,000·(1 − b)

So one-boxing wins just in case a + b − 1 > 0.001. Call a + b − 1 the *discrimination coefficient*: how much the forecast discriminates between the two acts, as opposed to how often it happens to be right. It is independent of the base rate; accuracy is not.

The consequence is that accuracy alone settles nothing. Take q = 0.9 and a predictor that unconditionally predicts "one-box." Then a = 1, b = 0, and A = 0.9: a ninety-per-cent-accurate predictor. But conditional on either act the box is full, so two-boxing yields $1,001,000 and one-boxing yields $1,000,000, and there is no evidential tie between act and contents to exploit. Now take a = 0.95, b = 0.45 with the same q: A = 0.9 again, but the discrimination coefficient is 0.4, and one-boxing yields $950,000 against $551,000. Two predictors with identical headline accuracy and opposite verdicts. [I think this is right, and I think the more general point is that the causal theory's recommendation is invariant across every one of these parameter changes while the evidential theory's is not — so the parameter-sensitivity is entirely on one side. But I am unsure how much work this observation is really doing: see objection (b) below.]

**2. What, exactly, is being predicted?**

The standard setup stipulates that the predictor forecasts your *act*. Nothing forces this. Consider the **Split Predictor**: at t₀ the predictor forecasts, with high reliability h, the *deliberative verdict* you will arrive at — the answer you will give yourself to the question "what should I do?" — and fills the opaque box if and only if it forecasts the verdict "one-box." It does not forecast your act at all, or its forecast of your act is pure noise.

Now run it. At t₁ you arrive at a verdict V. Conditional on V, the contents are already settled and your act is evidentially inert with respect to them — the act was never the target. So at t₂, whatever V was, you should take both boxes: the extra $1,000 is free. The payoff-relevant choice has therefore moved backwards, to the question of which verdict to arrive at. And the correct verdict at t₁ is "two-box," since contents and act are independent given V — which means the verdict that fills the box is false.

So the best available policy is: arrange to arrive at the verdict "one-box," and then take both boxes. The one-boxing intuition and the two-boxing intuition are both vindicated, but at different levels; the standard setup welds the two levels together by stipulating that the prediction targets the act, and the paradox is manufactured by the weld.

The trouble — and this is where I think the variant earns its keep — is that the policy is self-undermining for anyone who can see it. Ordering the agents: the self-managing agent who engineers the verdict "one-box" and then two-boxes gets $1,001,000; the naive one-boxer who forms the same verdict and acts on it gets $1,000,000; the agent who works out the strategy cannot honestly reach the verdict "one-box," is predicted to reach "two-box," and gets $1,000. Here knowing more decision theory is catastrophic, and not because the predictor's accuracy degrades on sophisticates, but because the verdict is the target. [Flagging: I am not sure whether "arrange to arrive at a verdict" requires full self-deception, or whether something weaker — choosing what to attend to, which arguments to expose yourself to before t₁, whom to discuss it with — is enough. If the weaker thing suffices, the case is more troubling and more interesting, because it is then just the ordinary management of one's own deliberation.]

**3. The upshot I want to defend.** The unit of evaluation is the real issue. A theory that evaluates momentary acts has nothing to say about the choice at t₀-to-t₁, and its silence there is not neutrality: it leaves a large payoff on the table. That looks to me like an argument that decision theory has to evaluate policies or deliberative procedures rather than acts, and — more sharply — that once it does, the evidential theory's notorious willingness to treat your own states as evidence to be managed reappears one level up, as advice about your beliefs rather than about your behaviour. Whether that is a reductio of the evidential theory or a surprising correct prediction from it is the question I want to end on.

**4. Objections I can already see.** (a) The exchangeability step: a two-boxer may reasonably hold that the predictor's misses are concentrated on people like them, which breaks the move from population hit rates to my own conditional probabilities. (b) Someone will say the error profile is not a free parameter once you specify the predictor's *method* — a simulator, a statistical profiler, a jury of psychologists — so section 1 shows only that "accuracy" is a poor summary, not that anything is underdetermined in the cases that matter. (c) Someone will say the Split Predictor is just Newcomb's problem with a different predicted quantity substituted for the act, so I have changed the subject rather than dissolving the original dispute. (d) The verdict/act distinction may not be sharp enough to bear the weight: perhaps arriving at a verdict just is forming an intention, in which case the "akratic" policy is incoherent.
