What I'm arguing, compressed:

(1) Unification. Goodhart's law — the point that a statistical regularity stops holding once you put pressure on it for control purposes — and the older program-evaluation slogan that the more a quantitative indicator gets used for social decision-making, the more pressure it creates to corrupt it, are both instances of one conflict. A single number is being asked to do two jobs at once: be a *target*, something agents are rewarded or punished for moving, and be a *diagnostic*, something we read as evidence about the thing we actually care about. The two jobs pull in opposite directions, because a diagnostic is only trustworthy if it isn't being steered, and a target is only effective if it is.

(2) Three failure modes, not two. The usual taxonomy gives you correlation decay (the world shifts under the proxy, so the old correlation breaks) and effort substitution (agents redirect activity toward the proxy at the expense of the target). I add a third, which is the part that's mine: *reflexive drift*. For a certain class of targets — quality of care, student learning, rehabilitation, "good science" — the content of the target is partly fixed by the practices through which it gets administered. Push the proxy and you don't just come apart from the target, you drag the target along with you. Your yardstick moves, so you can't even say the proxy got worse. Example I use: a hospital system graded on thirty-day readmission, where "managing a patient safely at home" slowly gets reconstituted as whatever keeps the thirty-day number down; and a school accountability regime where "learning" gets reconstituted as test-taking literacy.

(3) The risky bit, which is also the bit the talk needs. Call it the fidelity penalty. The intuitive rule is: pick the proxy that correlates most tightly with the target today. I want to say that's wrong as a general rule, because a tighter fit tells an agent more precisely where the cheap lever is, so it collapses faster — and with reflexive targets, a sharper proxy reshapes the target faster too. So proxy choice is an intertemporal problem, not a fit problem, and there are cases where you should deliberately pick a blunter proxy, rotate it on a fixed schedule, and re-specify it through a panel that sees case narratives but never sees the previous numbers.

(4) Where it ends up: a trilemma. For a target of the reflexive kind, a publicly announced metric that carries real accountability can't simultaneously be stable enough to compare across periods, responsive enough to still track the target after agents adapt, and non-distorting. Pick two. The cost of buying responsiveness is that you lose comparability, because you keep having to redefine the thing.
