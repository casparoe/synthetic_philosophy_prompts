I'm a novelist — literary stuff with one foot in SF — and I'm about 40k words into a book that hangs entirely off a quantum suicide setup, and I keep drafting scenes that my beta readers, and my mate who did physics at Imperial, tell me are cheating. So what I want isn't a physics lesson, it's a craft procedure: something I can run on any scene I've written in about ten minutes, in the vocabulary I actually work in — point of view, whose head we're in, free indirect style, what the narrator is licensed to know, what the reader can infer from what's on the page, dramatic irony, offstage action — that tells me whether the scene is honest to the premise or whether I've quietly smuggled in a bit of magic. Constraints: no equations, no branching-tree diagrams (I tried drawing one and it made everything worse), it has to work for both my present-tense first-person chapters and the third-person-limited ones, and it should end in either a rewrite instruction or an explicit "this one's a judgment call, you decide" rather than in a shrug. I cant afford many more dead chapters.

Where I've got to, which you should correct if I've mangled it: as I understand it, every outcome of the device happens somewhere, but you only ever have experiences in the outcomes where there's still a you around to have them, so from the inside you apparently survive everything while from outside you're just dead in nearly all of it. The snags I keep hitting and want the procedure to handle, in roughly the order I hit them: the *grain* problem, since dying isn't instantaneous and I'm not sure there is a single clean outcome in which "she survives" — what do I do with a scene where the character is clinically gone for four minutes and comes back, is that a branch or a smear? The *mangled-branch* problem, since the outcomes where you walk away from a shotgun in the face are outcomes where you walk away ruined, and my character can't be untouched by chapter nine — how much of that do I owe the reader, and at what point does it stop being a book anyone finishes? The *witness* rule, since every other character in the book sees her die — what am I allowed to dramatise and what can I only report, and can I ever write from the daughter's point of view at all, or is that a different novel? The *accumulation* problem, since from the inside she can't know she's in the freak sequence of outcomes but the reader has to feel the improbability mounting, and I don't know how to get that onto the page without handing the narrator knowledge she couldn't have. And the *other-people* problem, since at one point she tries to drag someone through with her, and I genuinely can't tell whether the premise lets her.

Then run it on three scenes I've got drafted, and give me the third answer even though I don't want it: (a) a present-tense first-person chapter where she presses the button for the hundredth time and nothing happens; (b) a third-person chapter from the daughter's point of view the following morning, identifying the body; (c) my ending, which I love and which I suspect is exactly the one that breaks the rules — she's ninety-odd, she dies in bed with the daughter holding her hand, and the last two pages are inside her head as it goes dark. Tell me precisely what (c) is getting wrong and what the cheapest fix is that keeps the feeling of it. And be honest about where the procedure runs out: mark the points that are just artistic calls, and separately the points where it runs out because the philosophy and the physics don't settle it either — I'm thinking of the business of whether the point of view tracks how many outcomes there are or how thick they are (amplitude? my mate used a word I've forgotten), and whether there's some floor below which the surviving sliver is too thin to be anybody at all. Don't pad it out with a reading list or names of papers, I've read the pop-science accounts and I'm not going to read the papers — I just want to stop writing scenes that don't work.
