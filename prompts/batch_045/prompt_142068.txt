a pop-sci book i picked up at the airport has a short chapter on Hofstadter's MU puzzle, and I can't tell how much of it to trust. here's roughly what it says (paraphrasing, but pretty close to the original):

You start with the string MI and there are four rules: if a string ends in I you can add a U to the end; you can double everything after the M; three I's in a row can be replaced by a U; two U's in a row can be deleted. The question is whether you can ever reach MU, and the answer is no, because of an invariant: the number of I's in any string you can produce is never divisible by three. Doubling can't get you there, and the other rules either leave the I-count alone or take three away, so MU, which has no I's at all, would need the count to reach zero — and zero is a multiple of three. This, the author says, is a miniature of Gödel's incompleteness theorem: the puzzle shows the MIU system is incomplete, and what Gödel did was essentially find the same kind of unreachable string inside arithmetic, which is why no formal system can ever capture all of mathematics. Hofstadter's moral is that the only way to see any of this is to jump out of the system — meaning you have to stop playing by the rules and help yourself to a new rule that lets you produce MU, which is exactly what mathematicians do when they bolt a new axiom onto a theory that can't prove something. And that's the real lesson: mechanical rule-following can never be enough, because understanding consists in being able to step outside whatever rules you were handed, and that's not something a machine running fixed rules could do.
