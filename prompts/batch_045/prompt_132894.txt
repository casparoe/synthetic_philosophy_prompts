I sit on the executive committee of a branch of a general union, and I would like a decision procedure we can actually run.

The setting: our branch covers roughly seven hundred directly employed members across three parcel distribution depots, plus — and this is the part that keeps causing arguments — around two hundred self-employed couriers who work the same routes, on the same handheld platform, against the same performance targets, but who are engaged through a separate services contract with an intermediary. Several times a year we are asked to authorise some form of industrial action: overtime bans, work-to-rule, short intermittent stoppages, all-out walkouts, refusals to cross another union's picket line. We have about forty minutes at a branch meeting, our members are not philosophers, and any decision has to survive two further stages: a ballot of the affected members and a sign-off from regional office, who will not sign off on anything they think is disproportionate. We cannot commission fresh advice for each decision, and I would rather the procedure did not rest on the details of any particular jurisdiction's labour law — treat statutory permissions and restrictions as a parameter we fill in locally, and give me the moral structure underneath, the part that would still oblige us if the law changed.

The rough rule we have been using is: we may withdraw what the employer is paying for; we may not prevent other people from working; we may not aim at anyone who cannot give us what we want. I know that rule is too thin, and I will say why below.

What I want is a procedure: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the points at which the procedure returns a no and stops; the points at which it hands the question to a vote because it is a question about what we value rather than about facts; and the points at which it has no opinion at all. For each step, give me one line on which justification of the right to organise that step leans on — the freedom-of-association line, the coercion objection, the anti-domination or countervailing-power line, or something else — so I can see which parts of the procedure would collapse if we rejected one of those justifications. Please also mark the steps that are judgement calls dressed up as procedure; I would rather know which they are than be reassured.

Two difficulties I want the procedure to handle explicitly, because they are where our current rule runs out. First, addressee: a strike seems to require somebody who can hear it and respond, but many of the decisions that matter to our members — routing, shift allocation, deactivation thresholds — are made by an automated scheduler, tuned by a product team that none of us can name and that has no employment relationship with us at all. Who, if anyone, is the legitimate addressee of pressure there, and does it matter that the local depot manager can only pass the pressure on? Second, aggregation: if members stop for two hours at a time, twice a week, for ten weeks, is that one strike or twenty, and is proportionality assessed against each stoppage, against the running total, or against the threat of the series continuing? Our rule gives no answer, and the answer decides whether intermittent action is the mildest available step or the most coercive one.

Then run the finished procedure on four live proposals, showing your working:

1. An overtime and voluntary-extra-shift ban at the two largest depots, properly balloted and notified, in response to a roster change that moves start times by forty minutes on two weeks' notice, which the employer says is permitted under a general flexibility clause. The financial impact is small and the disruption is mostly absorbed by the employer's own planning.

2. An intermittent stoppage of two hours, announced twenty minutes in advance, twice weekly, in support of cleaners at the same site who are being dismissed and re-engaged through a subcontractor on worse terms. They are in a different union. Our members have no complaint against our own employer, the subcontractor is not our employer, and the stoppages are timed to the evening sort so that most of the cost falls on our employer rather than on the party to the dispute.

3. An immediate walkout at one site after a serious near-miss involving a reversing vehicle: no ballot, no notice, and the yard to stay closed until an independent safety audit is commissioned. Our own safety representatives think the risk is real and continuing, and the members would vote for it on the spot. I expect the procedure to tell me something I will find hard to sell at the branch, and I want to know whether that verdict is correct or whether it exposes a defect in the procedure.

4. A settlement offer of a six per cent increase bundled with a three-year no-strike clause. Tell me whether the procedure even has jurisdiction over this, or whether waiving the right to strike for a period is a values question for the members, and if so what the members should be asked.

Finally, tell me honestly what the procedure cannot settle. In particular: can any version of it tell us, in advance, whether our members' simple refusal to volunteer for extra shifts is industrial action at all, given that the extra shifts were never contractually owed? And what may we do, under the procedure, about the thirty or so members who say they intend to keep working, and about the couriers who are not members and to whom none of our rules apply? Please do not give me reading lists, case citations or legal surveys — I need the argument, in British English, in a form I can take into a meeting.
