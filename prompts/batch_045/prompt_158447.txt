I have been writing a paper on the two main cosmological arguments and I built what looked like a clean dilemma against both. Now I think the dilemma proves too much, and I cannot locate the move that did the work. I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict on the arguments themselves.

Set-up. Let E be the principle that does the motivating work in both arguments: no fact about what exists is without explanation. The kalam version runs a temporal restriction of E (whatever begins to exist has a cause of its beginning). The contingency version runs E in a form that looks unrestricted (whatever exists contingently has the explanation of its existence in something that does not exist contingently). Say "the argument" for whichever is in play; my objection seems to hit both.

(1) E cannot be unrestricted. If every fact without exception required an explanation, the chain of explanations would never terminate, and terminating is the entire point of the argument. So E must be restricted: only contingent facts require explanation, while a necessary being is self-explanatory — its existence is explained by its own nature and needs nothing outside it. Call this restriction R.

(2) God's creating — the fact that God wills there to be a world of this character, rather than no world or a different one — is contingent. Both arguments need it to be: the contingency version needs the world to be contingent, and the kalam version needs a first moment that need not have been. So R applies to it: it stands in need of explanation.

(3) An explanation of a contingent fact either necessitates what it explains or it does not. Call the demand that it must necessitate N.

(4) Take the N horn. The explanation of God's creating cannot be another contingent fact, since E forbids contingent facts as ultimate explainers, so it must be God's nature, which is necessary. A necessary explainer that necessitates its explainee makes the explainee necessary. So the world exists necessarily. Then the contingency argument's own premise — that the world is contingent — is false, and the kalam's picture of a universe that begins to exist and is caused from outside itself becomes unstable, since what an eternal necessary cause necessarily produces is eternal. Worse, N generalises: if every explained fact is necessitated by its explanation and every chain bottoms out in the necessary, then nothing at all is contingent and modality collapses.

(5) So take the other horn: deny N. The explanation of God's creating is non-necessitating. God's reasons incline without compelling, and the divine will settles the matter.

(6) But a non-necessitating explanation does not remove the bruteness that E was introduced to remove. The question why there is this world rather than none, or rather than some other one, now receives an answer that leaves the selection itself unexplained. If that is tolerable here, it is hard to see why it was intolerable when the naturalist said the fundamental physical state simply obtains. Either the argument never had a principle strong enough to get going, or it has one it must abandon in order to stop.

(7) Generalise: nothing in (2) through (6) was specifically theological. Run the same dilemma on any free agent's choice and you get that free choices are either necessitated, so there is no freedom, or brute, so E fails for a large and familiar class of contingent facts. The same structure also tells against non-theistic terminating explanations: a necessarily existent physical ground together with an indeterministic law faces the dilemma in exactly the shape described in (4) and (6).

(8) So the chain appears to yield two conclusions I did not want: that no cosmological argument of either shape can terminate in a free necessary being, and that agency and a strong principle of sufficient reason are incompatible.

There are three claims I would rather keep than the argument: that contingent facts require explanation in some robust sense (E, restricted by R); that God creates freely; and that the created world is genuinely contingent. If forced, I would sooner drop the argument than any of the three. The two places where I keep stalling are (2), since I am unsure whether divine creating counts as a contingent fact at all once God is taken to be simple and eternal, and (4), where the inference from a necessary explainer to a necessary explainee may be doing more than I noticed.

Which step do you reject, and why did it look innocent? I would like each of the plausible rejections priced against the three commitments above. In particular: is the distinction I need a distinction about the strength of explanation — a necessitating ground versus a reason that makes a fact intelligible without necessitating it — or a distinction about the object of explanation, between why there is anything at all and why this rather than that? If it is the latter, can the contingency argument survive in a form that still reaches a personal cause, or does it degrade into a bare demand for some necessary ground?
