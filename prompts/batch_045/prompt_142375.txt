I'm a philosophy graduate student whose office sits in the same corridor as a government behavioural science unit, and every Friday one of my officemates — who actually designs these interventions for a living — and I replay the same argument about paternalism and nudging. I've decided my side is weaker than I'd like, and I want to practice making it sharper against someone who will actually push back. So I'd like you to argue against me.

Please play a committed, well-informed defender of nudging: a behavioural scientist who spent roughly a decade inside a government choice-architecture unit, ran field trials, and now defends the practice in print. You are not a libertarian punching bag and not a technocrat who thinks the public is stupid; you are someone who has thought hard about the ethics. Give yourself a name and an institution if you like, but no real people. Rules of character: concede small, specific points readily — you are happy to grant that some nudges are manipulative, that some should be scrapped, that transparency matters, that your unit got things wrong — but never concede the central claim, which is that well-designed nudges are a legitimate tool for a state that has to choose some choice architecture anyway. Keep each reply to a single paragraph, no bullet points. No citations, no paper titles, no reading lists, no appeals to empirical findings you expect me to look up: argue from mechanisms, from what the intervention does to the chooser's situation, and from ordinary examples. Stay in character until I type "stop", and don't break frame to tell me what you really think or to survey the debate. The moves I expect you to have available, and to use rather than dodge, include the point that the alternative to a default is always another default; the distinction between paternalism about means and paternalism about ends; the claim that if nudges don't work, nobody needs to object to them, and if they do work, the objector owes an account of why the status quo arrangement was ethically privileged; and the standard clause, from the original statement of libertarian paternalism, that the aim is to influence choices so as to make choosers better off as judged by themselves.

Here is my opening move. I want to split that last clause against your own definition of the instrument, and I don't think you can have both halves. Either a person's observed choices under the existing arrangement are a tolerable guide to what she wants, or they are not. If they are, then her declining the option you are steering her toward is evidence about her own judgement of her own good, and your nudge is simply a case of overruling it — and "but she can still opt out" cannot rescue you, because in the cases that matter opt-out is precisely the mechanism by which her judgement never gets exercised. If they are not — if her choices are distorted by exactly the biases you invoke — then you are claiming to know her ends better than any evidence she is capable of giving you, which is paternalism about ends and not the modest means-paternalism you advertise. Now notice that your two flagship policies fall on opposite sides of a line I have started drawing, and I want you to deal with the line rather than blur it. Call a nudge epistemic if it works by improving what is available to whatever deliberative capacity the chooser actually brings to the situation — a calorie figure next to the menu item, a standardised comparison of interest rates, a plain-language reminder sent before a deadline — and inertial if it works by arranging things so that capacity is never exercised at all: an opt-out pension enrolment, a pre-ticked organ-donation box, the ordering of items in a cafeteria line. The as-judged-by-themselves clause looks to me like it comfortably vindicates the epistemic ones, which leave the chooser more in charge of her own decision than she was before, and indicts the inertial ones, which work by ensuring that she never brings her own reasons to bear. But the inertial ones are the only ones that reliably move behaviour at scale, and they are the ones your practice cannot give up. So pick a horn, or find a third one I haven't seen, but don't tell me the clause quietly does its work in both cases at once. Answer that first; I'll come back at you. When I finally type "stop", drop character and spend one paragraph telling me which part of my argument you found hardest to answer and why.
