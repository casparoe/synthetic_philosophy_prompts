Here's the situation. Next week I'm presenting at a joint philosophy–economics workshop, 30 minutes plus maybe 10 for questions, and the Q&A is definately the part I'm worried about.

What I'm defending. Harsanyi's aggregation theorem: suppose each person i has preferences over lotteries satisfying the vNM axioms, so each has a utility function u_i unique up to positive affine transformation; suppose the social ordering over lotteries also satisfies the vNM axioms, so it too has a utility representation U, unique up to positive affine transformation; and suppose Pareto in the ex ante form — if every i prefers lottery p to lottery q, then society does too. Then, assuming the u_i aren't affinely dependent on each other, U = sum of lambda_i times u_i, with every lambda_i nonnegative, up to an affine transformation of U. My line is that this is a measurement theorem, not a moral one. The lambdas are exchange rates between interpersonal units of utility, not a distributive verdict; so the theorem can't be read as a defence of utilitarianism (or of average utilitarianism via Harsanyi's own impartial-observer move, where you imagine an equal chance of being anybody), because that reading conflates a unit convention with a moral weighting. All the distributive content sits in the choice of units. So I propose one, which I call unit-stake calibration: for a given decision problem with feasible set F, rescale each person's utility so that the gap between her best and worst attainable option in F is 1, then set all the lambdas equal. On that convention the theorem hands you an additive rule that behaves prioritarian in exactly the cases people argue about — someone whose utility range is compressed (severe disability, restricted opportunity, adapted expectations) gets her gains counted at full unit value — while all three axioms stay intact. And I want to argue that the menu-relativity of the weights that falls out of this is a feature rather than a bug: weights track stakes, and stakes are a property of the decision problem you're actually in, not of the person in the abstract.
