My friend Dale and I walk together most mornings, and for about a year we've been arguing about whether a thing's design can be political in itself — whether the object is doing the deciding, or whether it's always the people behind it. He has ruled on a run of cases, and I've come to think he is applying some rule he has never said out loud, because his reasons sound different from case to case.

Where he has said yes — the design itself is political:

1. The low concrete overpasses on the old beach parkway: a sedan clears them, a bus cannot. "It decides who gets to the beach, and nobody has to say a word."
2. A mechanical tomato harvester that only pays for itself across a big acreage. "Nobody passed a law; the machine did the consolidating."
3. The armrests bolted across the middle of the bus-shelter bench. "Somebody meant it, and it works without anybody showing up."
4. The grocery cart whose wheel seizes the moment you push it past the lot. "It keeps a property line with no guard."
5. The one-piece desk-and-chair units bolted to the floor in rows at the elementary school. "Nobody can rearrange them, so the room decides."

Where he has said no:

6. A heavy cast-iron skillet he cannot lift one-handed. "Just a bad fit for me."
7. The QWERTY keyboard. "An accident that stuck; nobody's winning now."
8. The county rule that curbside trash has to go out in clear bags. "That's a law somebody enforces; the bag isn't doing anything."

The new one: the water authority has just put steel gates at the two footpath entrances to the reservoir loop. They are wide, and a timer swings them shut at dusk and open at dawn — nobody throws a switch, nobody rules on anybody, and they went up to stop people dumping mattresses out of pickup trucks after dark, not to do anything to people on foot. There is another way in, an old dirt track open at all hours, but it is steep and adds twenty minutes; in practice the gates are the only entrance anyone with a stroller, a walker, or a bicycle will use.

Will Dale call the gates political? I would rather have the rule than the answer, but I want both. Please reply as a single JSON object:

- "rules": an array of three objects, each with "label" (a short name for the rule), "statement" (one sentence stating the principle he might be running), "fits" (which numbered verdicts it explains), "strains" (which ones it explains badly or not at all), "call" ("political", "not political", or "depends") and "confidence" (a number from 0 to 1).
- "best_guess": an object with "call", "rule_relied_on" (the matching label), and "reason" (two or three sentences on why that rule fits his track record better than the others do).
- "tiebreaker": an object with "artifact" (two or three sentences describing one further case I could put to him), "separates" (which two rules it pulls apart), and "if_yes" and "if_no" (one sentence each on what his answer would tell us).
