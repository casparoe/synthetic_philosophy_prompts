I've been going down a rabbit hole on my own for the past few weeks (no philosophy classes since school, so please keep things plain) about that puzzle where a decision changes *which particular people* end up existing. The version that got stuck in my head: a government picks an energy policy that's a bit worse for future people, but if they'd picked the greener one, different couples would have met, different kids would have been born — so the actual future people can't complain they were harmed, because the alternative was them never existing at all, and their lives are perfectly fine. I gather this is called the non-identity problem and that it's supposed to make philosophers uncomfortable rather than smug, but I can't stop thinking about how *convenient* it is for anyone in power, and I want to make fun of that.

So could you write me a parody — a public information leaflet in FAQ form, maybe 600 to 700 words, numbered questions, in the voice of a very calm, very modern government communications office? Call the body something like the Office for Intergenerational Fairness, and it's publishing something like "Answers for Concerned Citizens" after announcing that it will no longer be accepting complaints from anyone whose existence depended on the policy being complained about. The voice should be that soothing corporate-government tone you get on tax letters and rail delay notices — we understand this may raise questions, we have a dedicated helpline — and it should never break character or wink at the reader.

What I really care about is that the jokes come from taking the reasoning seriously and following it, not from inventing silly stuff about it. So: give the office a bureaucratic threshold test for whether a life counts as worth living, with a form or a certificate, since the defense only works when the life isn't so bad that nonexistence would have been better for that person; have one question about the case where the same number of people are born either way but they're completely different people (a medical programme, an emissions rule), which should be handled as an even cleaner win for the office; have the office point out, as a feature rather than a bug, that the bigger and longer-lasting a policy is, the more future people are barred from complaining, so its own hands are cleanest exactly where the stakes are highest; and have it answer one letter from a citizen saying "but I'm worse off than I would have been" by explaining, patiently, that there is no one who would have been. I'd also like at least one moment where the office is technically correct and quietly monstrous — maybe about compensation, or about why the same reasoning has now been extended to complaints from existing staff. If you can end on something that shows the argument starting to eat itself, do that. Please don't tack on a paragraph explaining what's wrong with the view or have anyone step out of character to say so; if it's deadpan enough, the reader should feel the recoil on their own.
