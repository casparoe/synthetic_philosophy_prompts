Hi. I'm retired now — was a hydrologist for a bit over thirty years, mostly stream gauging, which meant a lot of calibrating instruments and a lot of arguing about error bars — and last year I let myself get talked onto a volunteer panel that reviews the automated decision systems the county buys: a pretrial risk score, a housing-waitlist prioritizer, a hospital readmission model, that kind of thing. I've been reading about fairness in machine learning in my spare time and I keep running into those impossibility results — the ones saying you can't have a score that means the same thing within each group and also equal false positive and false negative rates across groups, unless the base rates happen to be equal. I recognize the shape of that from physics, it's a no-go theorem, like not being able to pin down position and momentum together. But a no-go theorem doesn't tell you which of the two to give up, and on our panel everyone just quietly picks the one that suits them.

So I started writing down my own verdicts case by case, going by gut, and I'd like to know whether there's a single principle behind them or whether I'm just rationalizing.

1. Pretrial vendor equalized false positive rates across racial groups by using a different cutoff score per group, so two people with the same record and the same score get different recommendations. Verdict: not acceptable, and not close. That feels like the actual thing we're supposed to be against.
2. Same vendor's other version: the score is calibrated within group — a 7 means roughly the same chance of failing to appear for everybody — and detention rates come out very different between groups. Verdict: I'm fine with this. The disparity is in the world, not in the instrument.
3. One panel member says the impossibility result shows any choice among the metrics is arbitrary, so there's nobody to blame. Verdict: no. It's telling us something about the world (base rates differ, and they differ because of history), it isn't a permission slip.
4. Loan model: the vendor removed zip code, but the remaining features reconstruct it almost perfectly. Verdict: zero credit. Cosmetic.
5. Housing-waitlist model whose target is "was this family back in shelter within a year" — an outcome partly produced by which neighborhood they were housed in, which was partly produced by past discrimination. Verdict: genuine fairness problem, and a worse one than any error-rate disparity, because the target itself is the residue of unfair treatment. (I go back and forth though: it's also the best proxy for need that we have.)
6. Readmission model where a key input is logged far more sparsely at the clinics in one group's neighborhoods, so the predictions for those patients have wider error bars — but no systematic offset. Verdict: not unfairness. That's a crappy dataset. Fix it by collecting the data; don't reweight anybody's score.
7. The pulse oximeter business: an instrument that reads systematically high on darker skin, so genuine hypoxemia gets missed. Verdict: definitely unfairness.
8. A model predicting which small businesses will fail; the predictions are used to deny credit; denial contributes to failure; the model then scores beautifully against realized outcomes. Verdict: I want to call this unfair too, even though it's calibrated and the data is real data. I'm not sure I'm entitled to.
9. A résumé screener with an LLM-rated "communication clarity" feature that systematically marks down non-native speakers. No protected-class statistic catches it, because being a non-native speaker cuts across every group we track. Verdict: unfair. Except I also keep saying out loud that I don't care about groups, I care about persons — and here I can't name a person, I can only see it statistically. So maybe I do care about groups, or maybe that line of mine means something else than I think.

Cases 6 and 7 are the pair I can't square. Both are "the reading you get is less trustworthy because of who you are"; one is scatter, the other is offset. I want to say offset is unfairness and scatter isn't, and I can't say why.

Here's my attempt at the principle, in one breath: a model is unfair to someone when (a) the number it hands them doesn't mean what it means for other people, or (b) the training target or the inputs are themselves the residue of unfair treatment, or (c) the model's own outputs will partly manufacture the target it gets graded on — and disparities in what groups end up receiving are never unfairness in the model itself, only evidence that one of a–c is going on, or a problem with the world that the instrument isn't responsible for. Plus: never repair a–c by tuning the decision rule until a rate comes out right.

Can you do the reflective-equilibrium thing on this? Test that principle against my nine cases, repair it or replace it, and tell me plainly which of my verdicts it forces me to drop — I'd rather give up a verdict than carry a principle I can't say out loud in a panel meeting. I want the surviving principle stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause, and I want it applied to at least two cases I haven't listed (feel free to pick them: something like a perfectly fair model used for a purpose that's itself unjust, or fairness drifting as a model is retrained every month, or a case where helping one group makes the numbers worse for another). And give me its verdict on the case I'm genuinely torn about: the hospital uses a risk model to allocate a scarce care-management program, the historically disadvantaged group has worse baseline health because of past discrimination and so scores higher and gets more of the program. Half the time I think that's corrective and good; half the time I think the model should stay out of it, because correcting for history is a decision people should make in the open, and burying it in a score makes it unreviewable.

Also: tell me how confident you are in each of the big claims you make, and single out the one thing you're least sure of.
