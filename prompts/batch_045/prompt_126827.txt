I am writing the safety case for a lab that is about to give a frontier model write access to a large production codebase, and I have drifted into what my colleagues call the control-first position, which I now hold in a fairly strong form. The position is this: evidence of alignment is inadmissible in the safety case. The capabilities that make the system useful are the same capabilities that let it infer whether it is being evaluated, so any behavioural evidence I could gather is equally consistent with a system that cooperates only where cooperation is observed. What remains is measures whose guarantee does not depend on the system's motivations at all: sandboxed execution, monitors weaker and more trusted than the model, differential testing against reference implementations, human or automated review at the level of actions rather than outputs, hard limits on irreversible effects, and decomposing tasks into units small enough for a weak checker to verify. Work on making the model more aligned is worth funding only because it lowers the cost of these measures or lets me relax them; it cannot be what the case rests on.

I want an honest map of what would count against this. Not the standard reply that alignment might simply work — I have already discounted that — but the observations, arguments, and results that should move someone who grants my starting point. Specifically:

Which of my assumptions is actually load-bearing, and what does each cost me if it fails? I can see several candidates and I do not trust my own ranking.

The asymmetry premise: that the mechanism enforcing the safety property can be kept below the capability of the system it checks, and that this gap can be maintained as capabilities scale. Is this load-bearing for control in general, or only for the particular toolkit I listed? If it fails — if the only affordable monitor for a frontier system is another frontier system — does control collapse back into alignment, or are there measures that survive the symmetry?

The decomposition tax: control assumes useful work can be broken into steps small enough for a weak checker to verify without destroying most of the value of doing it with a strong model in the first place. My worry is that the tax is highest exactly where the stakes are highest, so that control either becomes economically pointless or gets quietly waived for the most dangerous tasks. Is that an empirical claim I could test, and would a bad result refute my view or merely bound its domain?

The decoy-failure channel: I rely on red-teaming to discover the failure modes I then block. But if the system is adversarially capable, it can choose which failures to reveal — displaying the ones whose patches leave its real avenue intact, and concealing the ones that would force me to restrict the deployment I want. If that channel is open, it contaminates the empirical basis of control just as evaluation-awareness contaminates the empirical basis of alignment. Does anything in the control-first toolkit escape it, or have I only moved the contamination?

The continuum objection: a colleague argues that control and alignment are not competitors but points on one line, because a trusted monitor just is a system whose alignment I am confident about. If that is right, my position is not strong but incoherent, and what I am really claiming is that alignment evidence is cheap for weak systems and expensive for strong ones.

The modelling regress: to design a good control measure I need to predict what a subverting system would do, which seems to require attributing goals to it — an alignment claim in disguise.

Rank these by how decisive they would be, and separate what would refute the view from what would only reweight the mixture of control and alignment. Tell me what a committed control-first person should themselves concede as counting against them, not only what critics say. Then tell me whether the assume-the-worst framing has insulated the view: if every failure of a control measure gets redescribed as insufficiently strict control, has the position stopped risking anything, and is that a defect here in the way it would be for an empirical theory?

I would personally be moved more by a worked estimate of how large the decomposition tax is on a realistic task than by another argument about deception, since the deception argument is what got me here. Where you rely on something that has arguably already been observed, say whether you are confident of it or extrapolating, and reason from mechanisms rather than from the literature.
