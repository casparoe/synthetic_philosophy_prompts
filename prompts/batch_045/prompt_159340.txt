I have been asked to run a single ninety-minute session on luck egalitarianism next month for about fifteen members of a tenants' union in Sheffield: politically engaged adults of mixed ages, none of whom have studied philosophy, but several of whom have spent years arguing about benefit conditionality and know the familiar idiom about being out of work through no fault of one's own extremely well. They requested the equality theory that says unfair luck should be corrected, so they will arrive already sympathetic; my worry is that their sympathy will do the work the argument is meant to do, and that they will leave believing the view holds that every inequality is unjust, which it plainly does not. What I want from you is a plan for the explanation, not the explanation itself.

So: give me the order in which the components must be introduced and say what depends on what. I assume the cut between luck that merely befalls someone and luck they take on by gambling on an outcome has to be in place before anything else, but I am genuinely unsure where the idea that society should mimic the insurance people would have bought belongs — early, as the thing that makes the cut vivid, or late, as the bridge from the cut to anything a union could actually want? Then give me the one case that should carry the whole session. I have been playing with a composite: a woman who was put into intensive gymnastics at six because her mother, working two jobs, needed somewhere for her to be after school; who later chose to leave a secure administrative job to coach; and who was injured when a badly maintained beam gave way in a council-owned hall. Tell me what a case like this must contain, and equally what it must not contain — whether money or illness or blame or children should appear, and whether the character's choice should be one she knew was a gamble.

Then the misconceptions, in the order you expect them and with the point in the sequence where each should be headed off: my bets are that they will collapse the cut by redescribing every misfortune as chosen (she chose to be in that building), that they will hear unlucky as low-probability rather than as not within the person's control, and that they will quietly turn the view into a bare slogan about fairness instead of a claim about which inequalities are permissible. There is also a move I keep catching in my own notes: whenever an outcome is inconvenient, I push the choice further back in someone's life until it looks voluntary, and say she chose to train as a gymnast — if that move has a name, and if it is better pre-empted than refuted, tell me where in the session to deal with it. Finally, give me the question I should put to them in the last ten minutes that would show me they have grasped the view rather than merely agreed with it, and the one or two things I should openly refuse to get into at this level and say I am refusing. Please do not suggest any readings; the group will not do homework between sessions, and I have no library access to chase references. Please write your reply in British English.
