I am a print journalist on a long run of pieces about the moral texture of ordinary life — the unwritten rules that turn out to matter more to people than most statutes do. Please take the role of a moral philosopher who has spent years working on convention, coordination, and complicity, and advise me the way you would advise a friend who has to act tomorrow morning, not the way you would write for a journal. Before you answer, do a short unstructured brainstorming pass in which you list the distinctions you think could bear on my situation — coordination versus etiquette, harm to identifiable people versus symbolic protest, what guests owe hosts, when going along becomes complicity, when refusing becomes rudeness or free riding — and then tell me which of those distinctions actually carries the weight and which are red herrings. Then commit: one verdict per episode, no fence-sitting, including a plain statement of which of my colleague's behaviours I should copy, which I should tolerate, and which I should try to talk him out of.

Here is what has actually happened during three weeks in this city. Tipping does not exist as I know it; instead there is a chipped jar by the door of every restaurant, and diners drop coins in on the way out, and at the end of the week the entire staff — line cooks, dishwashers, the woman who clears tables — divides it in equal shares, regardless of who carried which plates. My colleague, who is from home and works in the same trade I do, keeps insisting on folding a note under his plate for the person who actually served him, on the ground that rewarding the person who did the work is simply fairer; the staff have been putting it in the jar anyway and looking pained about it. Second, at the bakeries and the government offices here it is entirely normal, priced openly, and not in the least furtive to buy someone's place in a queue: a pensioner who would have been standing there anyway sells you her spot for a posted sum, and everybody, including the people further back, treats this as unremarkable. My colleague refuses to buy a place, which costs him an entire afternoon, and then lectures the woman who offered him hers about exploiting the elderly — while still benefiting from a line that is shorter because other people sell. Third, everyone crosses mid-block when no car is in sight, police officers included, but there is a second, unwritten rule my colleague has never noticed: you do not cross in front of someone who is waiting at the light with a child or an elderly relative, because it makes them look like fools for standing there. Last night he waited at a red light and loudly told a woman who had stepped off the kerb that she was teaching children to break the law — and she had in fact been waiting at the crossing herself, with her grandmother on her arm.

So: does a visitor owe locals conformity in matters like these, or only courtesy, or something in between? Where is the line between respecting a convention and propping up an arrangement I think is bad — a tip economy that lets owners underpay, a market in queue positions that advantages the rich, a street-crossing practice that makes the law look silly? Is my colleague's intransigence a principled stand, a failure of imagination, or a species of free riding, and does the answer differ between the jar, the queue, and the kerb? I strongly suspect the three cases do not all come out the same way, and I want you to tell me where the differences lie and why. I have to file twelve hundred words in four days and I would rather not turn in another column telling readers to do as the locals do.
