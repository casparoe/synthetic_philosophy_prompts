For about three years now I've been the person who writes the discussion questions for a large online reading group on the brain and agency — mostly schoolteachers, two retired clinicians, a handful of engineers — and across something like forty sessions I've watched one shape of argument recur in two quite different bodies of work, and I cannot find a name for it that I'm satisfied with. The older case: volunteers watch a moving spot on a clock face, flex a finger whenever the impulse takes them, and afterwards report where the spot was when they first felt the urge to move; a slow electrical buildup over the motor regions turns out to begin a few hundred milliseconds before that reported moment. The reassurance that reliably follows is that what was recorded was only the buildup toward the movement and not the deciding itself — that the participants could still have vetoed the movement at the last instant, and that the veto, not the urge, is what agency really consists in. The newer case: volunteers in a scanner choose between two buttons, and the choice can be predicted from activity in frontoparietal cortex several seconds before they say they had decided. The reassurance this time is that what was decoded was only a covert preparation or attention-shift toward one option, not the endorsement of it — that agency consists in the endorsement, in the yes, in identifying with the option rather than merely drifting toward it. What struck me, setting the two side by side, is that in each case the item that survives the result is another time-stamped, threshold-crossing, perfectly measurable event of exactly the sort the next experiment can also precede; and when the next experiment does precede it, the identical move is made again, to the next thinner item. The retreat is not to something hidden in a gap in current knowledge: the veto and the endorsement have both since been measured. What changes is not whether we know about the item but what the item is said to be.

I've had two suggestions from a friend who does moral philosophy, and neither of them fits. The God-of-the-gaps style label doesn't fit, because the thing being retreated to is not unexplained — it is fully measured, fully natural, and the appearance of a gap only opens up after the redescription. Motte-and-bailey doesn't fit either: this isn't a strong claim and a weak claim sharing a slogan, it's a single grammatical claim — the agent decides, the outcome is up to the agent — whose referent is quietly swapped while the surrounding argument, including the confidence that the empirical findings leave agency untouched, stays exactly as it was. There's a third feature I care about most and can't place: the machinery runs in both directions. The group's resident hard determinist relocates in reverse, redescribing each later candidate as a readout, a side-effect, or a mere feeling of deciding produced by the earlier one. So the two of them differ only in where they stop, and where each stops looks to me like temperament rather than evidence. My working label is job-preserving redescription, with a three-part criterion: a term carrying normative or explanatory weight keeps that weight; its referent is reassigned in response to an empirical result; and the reassignment is not constrained in advance, since nothing in either theory says where it must stop. I'm not certain the third condition is doing real work, or whether a version of the phenomenon without it would still be the same phenomenon.

So: is there an established concept for this, or something close enough that my label is redundant? I'm not after a bare name — I want the carving: what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie. I'm also not asking for references, citations, or further reading; I can't get at journals from where I am, and a list of names wouldn't tell me whether the carving is right. Please structure the reply in four named sections. "Nearest candidates", about 300 words: the two or three established concepts that come closest, each stated accurately enough that I could recognise it if I met it again. "Where each fits and where it doesn't", about 350 words: run my two cases against each candidate in turn, say which one fits best, and say explicitly which of them, if any, captures the fact that the relocation runs in both directions. "Verdict, with a name if there isn't one", about 200 words: state plainly whether an established term exists; if it doesn't, coin one, give a one-sentence definition, and say what the definition has to exclude so that it doesn't swallow every case of a concept being clarified. "The three test cases", about 300 words: apply your definition to (a) a theorist who fixes the location of agency in advance — say, in the late endorsement stage — and who states beforehand that if the endorsement turns out to be predictable too, then there is no agency, so that the relocation is no longer a response to data; (b) the apparently same shape outside this debate, where each proposed neural correlate of consciousness gets demoted to a precondition or a readout once a better one appears, and where in AI interpretability each internal variable identified as the model's reason is demoted to a mere feature once someone probes it; and (c) the first-person version, that in ordinary deliberation I can always redescribe I chose as the choosing happened in me. For each, say whether it is an instance of the phenomenon or something else, and why.
