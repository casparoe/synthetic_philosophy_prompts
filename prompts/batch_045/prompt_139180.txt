I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for about a decade, so I came to Parfit's teletransporter argument secondhand — the fission case, the claim that identity is not what matters, the thought that survival comes in degrees — and I had always filed it under intuition pumps with no cash value. Then two things happened in my own family that make me think the question is not academic at all. Last winter my uncle had a brain hemorrhage and was declared dead by neurological criteria while he was still warm, still on a ventilator, still with a beating heart; the hospital asked whether we would donate his organs, and we said yes. A year earlier my grandmother had died with end-stage Alzheimer's, at a point where there was almost nothing left of the person — no memory, no recognisable character, no capacity to form or act on the simplest intention — and yet everyone from the hospice nurse to the probate court treated her as unambiguously alive and as the very same person whose decades-old advance directive still bound what could be done to her body. Same legal system, same hospitals, two apparently flatly different answers about what has to continue for the person to still be there, and I have been going in circles trying to say precisely where the inconsistency lies.

Here is the cluster of practices I mean, as concretely as I can put it. Death in most of the United States is defined by a statute descended from a uniform model act that most states adopted in the early 1980s: a physician may pronounce death either on the basis of irreversible loss of circulatory and respiratory function or on the basis of irreversible loss of all functions of the entire brain, brainstem included, and the two routes are treated as interchangeable routes to the same fact, though jurisdictions differ over whether the whole brain or only the brainstem must have stopped. Transplantation runs on the dead donor rule, the rule that organs are not to be taken from someone who is alive, and yet a large share of donors are brain-dead patients with beating hearts and ventilated, warm bodies who are wheeled into an operating theatre and opened up while looking, to any layperson, alive. Where donation instead follows the circulatory route, the wait after the heart stops is a matter of minutes, and the bioethics literature openly describes the standard as permanence rather than irreversibility — the patient will not be resuscitated because we have decided not to, not because nobody could. Meanwhile, down the same corridor, a patient in a persistent vegetative state or with end-stage dementia is a living person with full legal standing, whose prior instructions and property rights are still in force, and from whom taking a kidney would be murder.

I also notice that the people who take this question most literally in practice have quietly settled on an answer: the cryonics organisations charge substantially less to preserve just the head than to preserve the whole body, treat their legally dead clients as patients with continuing interests and continuing contracts, and some of them propose dodging the copy problem altogether by replacing the brain a small region at a time with functionally equivalent hardware, so that there is never a moment of discontinuity and never a second copy to argue with the first. So here is what I want. What view of what matters in survival does this whole apparatus — the neurological criterion, the dead donor rule, the treatment of the demented and the vegetative as still present — actually presuppose? Is it the psychological-continuity view; or something thinner, like the claim that the substrate of consciousness sits in this organ; or only a bright line that several incompatible metaphysics could equally live with? Which rival views does the practice strictly rule out, as opposed to merely sitting badly with — does it really rule out the view that what matters is the continuing life of the human organism, and does it really rule out a demanding psychological-continuity view, given that such a view would make my grandmother both dead and available as an organ source? Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, such that two or three theories would license exactly the same behaviour, and where is it instead doing genuine metaphysical work that an administrator could not quietly drop? And then take the three rivals one at a time — the organism view, a strict psychological-continuity view, and a view on which survival is degreed with no further fact — and tell me what exactly would have to change in the practice if each were the one we adopted: which declarations would become impermissible, which would become mandatory, and which current distinctions would collapse. Please do not give me a survey of the literature, a reading list, or citations; I want the implicit theory reconstructed, the parts that are substantive separated from the parts that are administrative convenience, and a straight verdict on whether the whole thing is coherent.
