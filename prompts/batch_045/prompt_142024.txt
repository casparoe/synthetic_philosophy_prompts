I'm a fifth-year PhD student in philosophy and I'm giving a forty-minute talk next Thursday at a one-day workshop on well-being, choice, and policy that a public policy school is hosting. My working title is Utility Is an Evidential Policy, Not a Quantity. The position has three parts. First, I distinguish a thin reading of the consumer theorist's utility function from a thick one: on the thin reading, which I take to be what the ordinal revolution actually delivered, a utility function is nothing but a numerical representation of a choice relation and carries no commitment to utility being a feeling, a hedonic state, or a person's good; on the thick reading it is the thing that makes someone's life go better for them, the quantity a benevolent regulator should maximize. Second, I argue that revealed preference is best read not as a definition of utility but as a thesis about the evidential situation of the observer: observed choice is the only evidence there is, and utility talk that outruns it is idle. From there I claim that the standard objections to revealed preference borrow their force from the thin reading while landing on the thick one, and that once you separate the two they show only that choices don't pin down welfare, which is what my reading predicts anyway. Third, I offer a constructive protocol I call menu-invariant welfare inference. Say that a variation in the way a choice is presented (display order, defaults, framing, the presence of a decoy, time of day) is welfare-irrelevant when there is no independent reason to think it changes how well off the chooser ends up. Call a pair of options welfare-comparable on the evidence just in case their ranking is invariant across all the variations the theory counts as welfare-irrelevant. Where the invariance test passes, a regulator may read a welfare ranking off the choices; where it fails, the right output is not a best guess but a recorded incompleteness. I run one example through the whole talk: a city transit agency's mobile ticketing app lists fare products in a fixed order and pre-selects the middle one. In the agency's pilot data nearly two-thirds of riders end up with the pre-selected product; in a small A/B test, reversing the display order flipped the ranking between the two main products; but administrative ride records show almost identical subsequent travel in both arms, and essentially nobody switched back or complained. I want to say the agency is licensed to infer nothing about which product serves riders better, and should record the pair as welfare-incomparable until it finds a presentation variation that stabilizes the ranking. My claim to novelty is that this beats both the naively behaviorist reading, which takes the choice as welfare, and the paternalist reading, which substitutes the expert's or the survey's judgment for the chooser's.

The room will be about forty people, and I'd rather be over-prepared than surprised. Roughly twelve are applied micro and behavioral economists who estimate discrete-choice models for a living and read utility as a latent index plus an error term; they will care about identification, about what my protocol does to out-of-sample prediction, and about whether an invariance test is even implementable with finite, noisy data. Four or five are philosophers of economics friendly to the objection that revealed preference emptied preference of content and left economists with a notion that cannot do the explanatory work their own theory assigns it; they will want to know why my thin reading isn't that same emptiness with a friendlier name. Two or three work in the choice-theoretic welfare economics tradition that separates welfare-relevant from welfare-irrelevant ancillary conditions and gives axiomatic conditions under which observed choices can be used for welfare analysis, and I expect them to say I have reinvented their framework minus the axioms, or minus the parts that make it usable. There is a neuroeconomist who will say her lab measures utility directly as the firing rate of value-coding neurons, and who thinks the workshop's central question is empirically settled. There is one axiomatic choice theorist who cares about consistency conditions and Afriat-style rationalizability and who will want my proposal stated as a theorem or not at all. And chairing my session is a senior theorist in the Samuelsonian vein who regards the turn back to welfare as a relapse into pre-1938 hedonism; I suspect he will agree with me for reasons I don't want to be associated with, which is a problem of its own.

Finally, give me the one question I am hoping nobody asks, together with an honest assessment of what happens to the talk if they do.
