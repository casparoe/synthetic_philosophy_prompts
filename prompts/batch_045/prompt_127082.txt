I'm a backend engineer at a company of about three hundred people, and after one too many arguments at dinner with friends — most of whom have studied more philosophy than I have — I've realized that my gut verdicts about deception don't line up with any simple rule, so I want to hand over the raw verdicts and see what principle, if any, actually collects them. Here they are, mostly from work. Faking a browser-identifying header so a scraper can read a public page: fine. Answering a vendor's automated security questionnaire falsely, knowing the filled-in form goes to a human auditor at a customer: not fine — which already bothers me, because the act looks the same as the last one. Writing a changelog line that is literally accurate but phrased so that the tool that generates our customer-facing release notes will strip the hedge and emit a sentence I know to be false: not fine, even though the very same line written two years ago, before that tool existed, was fine. Telling my manager "the migration finished" when it did finish but silently dropped two hundred records that I'm going to repair before anyone looks: not fine. Telling my on-call manager "no revenue impact" when the entire admin panel is down, after he has told me a dozen times to spare him details and only report revenue impact: fine, and I don't feel guilty afterward. Watching a teammate post a wrong root-cause diagnosis in a public channel and saying nothing: not fine, but clearly less bad than posting it myself. Burying the account-deletion link four clicks deep, with every label on the path accurate: not fine. Defaulting new signups to the privacy-preserving option so that most people end up there: fine, even though it steers just as much as the buried link does. Shipping a progress bar that animates smoothly to one hundred percent regardless of actual progress: not fine, and here I can't even name who was lied to or what false claim was asserted, since nobody said anything. Telling my aunt I love the sweater: fine. Telling my partner I've already left the office while I'm still at my desk finishing up: not fine — and since I think it obviously would be fine to tell a stranger from a data broker that I'm not the decision maker, plain falsity can't be what's doing the work.

The best I've managed on my own is something like: what matters isn't the literal truth of the sentence but whether I'm making someone's evidence worse on a question they have a claim to a true answer to, and whether they would have agreed in advance to be steered in that way. But I don't trust that formulation, and it seems to give the wrong answer somewhere. So: test it against my cases, repair it or replace it with something more precise, and tell me which clause is doing the work in which case — and be honest about which of my verdicts a defensible principle has to overturn, because I'd rather drop a verdict than keep a contrived rule. I'd especially like to know what the resulting principle predicts for cases I haven't listed: an interface pattern that steers people with no statement or representation of any kind, the progress-bar sort of case where there may be no speaker at all, a changelog line fed into a summarizer run by someone else rather than by us, and deliberately feeding a false premise to an AI assistant that then acts on it. I'd also like the "less bad than" comparisons accounted for, not just the binary verdicts. One case I genuinely go back and forth on: telling a customer that a fix will be in Thursday's release when I haven't finished it and only expect it to land, where I know from experience that hedging with "I'm aiming for Thursday, it may slip" reliably triggers an escalation on their side that leaves them worse off than if I'd said nothing. Please don't give me a reading list or citations — I'm not going to follow them up; I want the principle, stated precisely enough that I could actually apply it next week.
