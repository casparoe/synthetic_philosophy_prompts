hey — I'm TA-ing a second-year grad seminar on contractualism and I need a handout for next weeks session on the aggregation problem. What I want from you is a decision tree / flowchart (or a "which position are you actually in" quiz with a scoring key, whichever fits better) that takes someone from the starting commitment "morality is about what no one could reasonably reject, and rejectability is driven by the complaints individuals would have" to a named leaf. No lit review, no reading list, no citations — I'll hand out the readings myself.

Setup so we agree on the problem: because contractualism compares complaints rather than summing them, it looks like it can't let a million tiny complaints outweigh one big one, but it also looks crazy to say you should save one stranger's finger and let a million people get migraines. Everything interesting is in how a view gets out of that.

Here's what I want the tree to do. Each branch node is one question, phrased as a concrete case a student can answer cold, without knowing which position it leads to — no node should mention a position's name or use the word aggregation. Each leaf gets a name (or "unnamed" if nobody actually holds it) and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first the questions and the answer combinations they allow, then the real positions located in that space, then a list of the empty cells with a one-line guess at why nobody is there (incoherent, just unattractive, or merely unwritten?). Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really separate instead of pretending it can.

The distinctions I most want doing the branching work (add any I've missed):

- numbers as pure tie-breaker (they only matter when the strongest single complaints are exactly equal) vs numbers that can outweigh vs numbers that can never matter at all;
- aggregation across persons vs aggregation within a single life — and then whether the relevant unit is the person, the person-at-a-time, or the life as a whole;
- whether an aggregate complaint has to be somebody's complaint — i.e. is the many-people fact something each person gets to complain about, or a complaint the group has, or not a complaint at all so numbers must come in through some separate principle sitting next to contractualism?;
- the shape of the growth: linear in N, or dampened so that the thousandth complaint adds less than the tenth, or a salience threshold below which a harm generates no complaint at all;
- ex ante vs ex post: cases where you can't identify who'll be hurt and each person just gets a small risk;
- the pairwise sweep: compare each person's situation under each option without summing magnitudes, then count — majority? size of differences? something else? — and whether that really avoids aggregating or just smuggles it back in;
- whether the view is symmetric: do numbers work the same way on the benefit side as on the harm side, or does a complaint-driven framework only aggregate harms?

Cases that have to appear somewhere in the tree: one big harm to one person vs a small harm to each of very many; one person who suffers many small harms at the same time vs a different person with one medium harm; many small harms spread over one long life vs one big harm to someone else; a policy that adds a tiny unidentified risk to millions; a harm spread so thin that nobody notices or remembers it; and a case where 60% are slightly better off under option A and 40% are much better off under B.

Aim for about eight branch questions and nine to twelve leaves.
