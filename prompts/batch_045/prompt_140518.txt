I'm TAing a philosophy of game theory seminar this term and I keep falling back on the same move: iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies, but with mixed strategies allowed as dominators, is the one bit of game-theoretic rationality I'm actually confident in. No equilibrium concept, no common prior, no claims about what anyone believes beyond that they're rational and that this is somehow common — and behind it there's an actual argument, the no-lose one: if some hedge over your own actions beats your action against every opponent profile there is, you can't lose by switching. I've started saying out loud that it's the assumption-light core that everything else in the subject should be measured against. I want to know what would count against that.

Here's what got me worried. Two players, player 1 has rows A, B, C, player 2 has columns X, Y, Z. Player 2 just wants to guess player 1's row: X pays 2 against A, Y pays 2 against B, Z pays 2 against C, everything else 0. Player 1's payoffs, in one numbering (entries are against X, Y, Z): A = 2, 0, 1; B = 0, 2, 1; C = 1.5, 1.5, 0. Now take a second numbering that's just an increasing relabeling of player 1's payoffs — 0 < 1 < 1.5 < 2 becomes 0 < 1 < 1.5 < 10: A = 10, 0, 1; B = 0, 10, 1; C = 1.5, 1.5, 0. Both numberings represent the same ordinal ranking of the nine outcomes. But: in the first, C isn't dominated by any mix of A and B, since you'd need weight on A above 3/4 to beat it in column X and below 1/4 to beat it in column Y; and Z is fine too, since no mix of X and Y beats it at row C; so nothing is ever eliminated. In the second, half A half B gives 5, 5, 1 against C's 1.5, 1.5, 0, so C goes; and once C is gone Z gives 0, 0 against the surviving rows, any nondegenerate mix of X and Y beats that, so Z goes too and you end up with A,B times X,Y. Same ordinal game, totally different surviving set.

My hypothesis is that the mixed step isn't assumption-light at all — that it presupposes expected utility, or at least cardinal payoff scales, in a way pure-strategy dominance doesn't, and that the no-lose argument is then only as strong as EU is. But I want the whole map, not just confirmation:

- Does the relabeling thing actually damage the position, or is there a standard reply (something about what a strategic-form game is defined to include, and about which transformations the procedure is invariant under) that disposes of it?
- Separately: what I half remember is that with three or more players, being strictly dominated by a mixture lines up with never being a best response to a *correlated* belief about the others rather than an independent one, so the surviving set is bigger than what common belief in rationality plus independent beliefs would give you. If that's right, does the epistemic story I keep telling either overgenerate or quietly assume something I haven't declared?
- If a player has a set of priors over the opponents and evaluates by the worst case rather than a single expected value, does dominance by a mixture have any grip left?
- On the positive side: does the procedure predict anything that could be false, or is it insulated by being sold as normative rather than predictive — and is that insulation legitimate here or is it a dodge?

Rank whatever you find from fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit about which ones a defender of my line would concede count against them rather than only what a critic would say. I already discount the experimental people only do two or three rounds of iterated reasoning stuff, so don't lead with it.
