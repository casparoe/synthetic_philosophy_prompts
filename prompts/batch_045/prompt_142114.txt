hey — I'm in a constructed-language group online and we keep hitting the same wall and I want to know what people even mean when they say what they say.

One member has built a language, call it Tevari, whose grammar has no way whatsoever to mark counterfactuals: no conditional mood, no irrealis, no "would have", not even a word for "if". Everything comes out flat and indicative-looking. His claim is that this means Tevari speakers literally cannot think counterfactually — that "what would have happened if..." isnt just awkward to say, it's unavailable as a thought. When I pushed back he said, language shapes thought, that's like the basic result, and someone else said, no, it shapes but doesnt determine, and within about four messages we were at the color-word example and the Hopi thing that supposedly got debunked in the 80s, and nothing moved, same as every time.

I'm not asking you to adjudicate his conlang. I want the slogan taken apart. Every time I try to pin down "language shapes thought" it slides between something obviously true and something obviously nuts, and I can never catch it in the act.

So: could you lay out a menu of the precise theses that sentence might be expressing, ordered roughly from trivial to wild, with a verdict on each — platitude, defensible and substantive, untestable, or false? A few things I especially want separated:

- "Thought" seems to be doing five or six different jobs at once. There's what you can consciously put into words, what you notice without being prompted, what's fast versus effortful, what you could learn to do with practice, what a prelinguistic infant or a chimp does, and what you can hold in mind as the format of a piece of reasoning. Does a version of the claim have to pick one of these, and does the plausibility ranking change depending which one it picks?

- Is there a reading where the claim is about habit or salience instead of capacity — grammar forcing you to rehearse a distinction hundreds of times a day until it becomes automatic — and if so, is it a real empirical claim or one of those things that's true in a way nothing could contradict?

- The strongest reading, that some thought is flatly unavailable to a speaker, looks unfalsifiable to me: any speaker who succeeds at the task gets explained away as contaminated by a second language. Am I right that that reading is empty, or is there a sharper formulation of it that has actual teeth?

And the bit I actually care about: which item on your menu is the one my friend needs for his Tevari claim to work, and is that the reading anyone serious defends, or is he quoting the boring one and relying on the crazy one?
