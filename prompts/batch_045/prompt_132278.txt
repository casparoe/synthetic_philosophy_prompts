So I was covering office hours for a colleague and one of our senior majors swore she had a knockdown solution to the combination problem for panpsychism. I wrote up what she said when I got home, and now I can’t get it out of my head because I think it proves to much — it seems to entail that a pile of sand has a unified conscious experience. I’m not asking for references or a reading list, and please don’t just say that’s the combination problem and stop; I want the step where it goes wrong and what rejecting it costs me. Here is the reconstruction, in the form I gave it to my seminar.

(1) Your total conscious experience right now is a single unified conscious field, and yet it plainly has many distinct phenomenal parts: the look of the screen, the hum of the fridge, the itch on your ankle, the nagging thought about dinner, and so on. So, in this ordinary case, many distinct experiences are combined into one unified experience.

(2) If many distinct experiences can be combined into one unified experience here, then the relation that does the combining — call it C — must be a perfectly general metaphysical relation. It can’t be a relation that only ever holds between experiences that happen to belong to one biological brain, because that restriction would be ad hoc, with no basis in the nature of experience itself.

(3) The only candidates for C are phenomenal fusion (the sort of thing people call subject-summing) or quality combination / mental chemistry: either the micro-subjects and their micro-experiences literally fuse into one macro-subject with one macro-experience, or the micro-experiences stay distinct but their qualities are blended or co-instantiated so as to yield one unified macro-quality for a single subject. Either way, the experiences combine in virtue of standing in the right formal arrangement — roughly, being the experiences of parts of a single, appropriately integrated physical system (or, if you like, a single mereological fusion).

(4) But lots of things besides brains are single physical systems, or single mereological fusions, whose fundamental parts are fundamental physical entities with micro-experiences: the pile of sand on my office floor, the Eiffel Tower, and the scattered object composed of my brain plus the Eiffel Tower are all, in the relevant formal sense, unified wholes made of experiential parts.

(5) Therefore, by C, the experiences of the parts of the sand pile, of the Eiffel Tower, and of the me-plus-Eiffel-Tower fusion are also combined into single unified macro-experiences. The sand pile, the tower, and the fusion are each subjects of a unified conscious experience.

(6) But that is absurd: a pile of sand does not have a unified conscious experience, the Eiffel Tower isn’t a subject, and the mereological fusion of me and the Eiffel Tower is definately not a conscious subject either. Socks, towers, and human-tower fusions are not subjects of experience; that much I take as Moorean.

I can’t accept (5), but every premise on the way to it looked fine to me when I wrote it up, and I can’t locate the slip. I’m most attached to (1) and to the Moorean refusal in (6); I’d sooner revise my metaphysics of composition, or my account of what C is, than give those up. Which premise or step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? If you reject (2), tell me what I lose — do I have to say that biological brains are metaphysically special, and is that worse than the sand-pile result? If you reject (3), say whether any version of the combination story survives: was I wrong to think fusion and quality combination are the only options, and is there some fourth thing C could be? If you reject the move from (4) to (5), or you think (4) is where the trouble starts, tell me what principled restriction I’m allowed to impose on C and why that restriction isn’t just as ad hoc as the brain-only restriction I threw out in (2). Don’t just tell me the conclusion is false; price the alternatives.
