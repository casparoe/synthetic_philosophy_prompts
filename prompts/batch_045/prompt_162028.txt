I'm in the second year of a PhD program and spent this summer in a small reading group on scalable oversight — half of us come from ML, half from philosophy, and we keep talking past each other, so I want to find out where the disagreement actually lives rather than win it. Here is the proposition I want a number on. Consider the standard amplification-and-distillation scheme: a human supervisor answers questions by breaking them into subquestions, with the option of consulting copies of the (partially trained) system on the subquestions, those consultations themselves expand into further layers, and the resulting tree is then distilled back into a single fast model that can answer the top-level question directly, with the distilled model used as the subagent in the next round. Now suppose this scheme is pushed to the point where the composed system substantially exceeds what any individual supervisor could produce or verify end-to-end. Proposition: in that regime, the system's out-of-distribution behavior still tracks the operator's intended goal, rather than merely matching what supervisors approved on the cases they actually saw. Concretely, I'd count the proposition as resolving "yes" if a system trained this way declines opportunities to pursue some easier-to-achieve objective that a competent-but-divergent system would take when those opportunities appear in deployment, and "no" if it looks fine on supervised cases and diverges once it can get away with it.

My own credence is around 0.3; one of the ML people in the group puts it near 0.7. Give me your number, but more importantly the decomposition. The terms I keep coming back to are: whether "aligned on the parts" composes into "aligned on the whole" at all, or whether alignment — unlike competence — fails to be additive under composition; whether the decisions where divergence would actually pay end up inside nodes a human inspects, given that the system is the thing proposing the decomposition; what I've started calling the legibility tax, the pressure to favor decompositions that are easy for a supervisor to check over decompositions that are faithful to the intended goal, so that the selection pressure rewards reasoning that reads as aligned; and whether distillation preserves the goal rather than the observable behavior on the training distribution, since the two come apart exactly off-distribution. Add whatever terms you think I'm missing — something about the determinacy of the top-level objective, or about how much optimization pressure the loop applies — assign a rough probability and one line of justification to each, say whether the terms are independent or whether one swamps the others, and show how you get to your final number. Then tell me which single term moves it most, which term you are least confident in, and what experiment someone could actually run now, with current models and real human supervisors on a bounded domain, would shift that term the furthest. Finally: between the person at 0.3 and the one at 0.7, which of these sub-questions are they most likely disagreeing about, and which are they probably agreeing on while describing it differently?

Be explicit throughout about which component claims are supported by things that have actually been tested at small scale in bounded domains and which are contested extrapolation among people who work on this — I would rather have a flagged assumption than a contested view presented as the field's settled position. And where your own decomposition depends on an empirical claim you can't check, say so rather than smoothing over it.
