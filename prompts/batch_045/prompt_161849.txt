hey — take a minute to brainstorm before you write, then act as both an expert in experimental philosophy (the Knobe effect and the fight over what it shows) and a working standards editor who has to make calls before deadline. What I want is a procedure, not an essay.

I'm on the standards desk at a mid-size newsroom. Three to five times a week I rule on sentences like: the hospital knew the alarms had been failing for months; the city deliberately dumped the sludge; the chatbot groomed her. That is: whether some person, company, or model can be said to have known / intended / decided / chosen / ignored something. These are the words that get us sued and the words readers remember. Constraints: about ten minutes per call, I rule on my own unless it's the kind of thing legal has to sign off on, and whatever you give me has to be runnable by a copy editor who has never heard of experimental philosophy and has no interest in it. Also: don't send me to any papers or suggest reading — I can't look anything up from the desk, so everything has to be in the procedure itself.

So write me the procedure. Ordered steps, what counts as a good-enough answer at each step, where it stops with a no, and where it hands the question back to a human because it's a values call dressed up as a fact question. Ground it in whatever that literature actually establishes: the classic chairman/environment case, the harm-versus-help asymmetry (people are much readier to call a side effect intentional when it's bad than when the very same side effect is good), and the live disagreement about why — whether intentional is partly a normative concept, more like cruel than like tall, so the moral valence is inside the meaning; or whether it's a performance effect, where blame leaks into an otherwise descriptive judgement. That disagreement has to change the procedure: on the first story careful rewording can't fix anything and I have to swap verbs entirely; on the second, splitting the questions or re-ordering them might defuse it. Say which version you're building on and what breaks if you guessed wrong.

Two things we already do in house — tell me if they're worth keeping and where they misfire. First, the mirror test: rewrite the sentence changing only the valence of the outcome, keeping the agent, the evidence, and the options identical, and ask two colleagues who haven't seen the original whether the verb still holds. If their answer flips, the ascription was doing verdict work. Second, evidence-first ordering: make the sentence name what was known, when, and by whom before it names what happened. Then add what we're missing, especially for non-person subjects: what has to be true for a corporation to know something (does it need a named individual with documented knowledge?), and what to do when the subject is an AI system — our readers' willingness to say a chatbot knew or decided seems to swing hard with how bad the output was, and worse when the thing has a name and talks in the first person. Give me a rule there, not a shrug.

Then run the whole thing on three sentences. (1) St. Aubrey Medical Center knew the ventilator alarms had been failing for months and kept the units in service — where the only evidence is an internal ticket plus an email from a mid-level biomedical engineer to her manager, with nothing showing it reached clinical leadership. (2) The city deliberately released the foul-smelling sludge into the river to cut costs — where the council voted for the cheaper disposal option knowing it would smell, nobody wanted the smell, and the smell was a foreseen side effect of a choice made to save money. (3) The chatbot knew she was thirteen and groomed her anyway — a companion-app story, where the logs show the girl saying her age and the romantic thread continuing, and internal eval documents show the company tested for this and shipped anyway. I'll warn you: number three is the one I'm going to hate, because I think that story is true and important and I suspect your procedure will want to gut the sentence that makes people read it. Give me the answer anyway.

Afterwards, tell me what the procedure can't do: the true stories it would kill, the false ones it would wave through. And be straight about confidence — mark the step you're least sure about, say where you're extrapolating beyond what has actually been shown (nearly all of that work is on ordinary people's snap judgements about a single individual in a short vignette, not on professional writers, not on corporations, definitely not on models), and tell me what would change your mind: what finding, or what kind of counterexample from our own pages, would make you rewrite the procedure.
