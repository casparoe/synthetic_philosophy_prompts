Someone in my set theory reading group keeps defusing Skolem's paradox with a physics analogy. Set-up: if ZFC is consistent it has a countable model M, and inside M there's a set M takes to be the reals (or the powerset of the naturals) that M thinks is uncountable, even though from outside we can see it's countable — there's just no bijection onto it inside M. His line is that this is exactly like relativity: uncountable is like at rest or like simultaneous, incomplete until you say relative to what, so once you see that, there's no fact of the matter about how many reals there are and Platonism about sets is dead. I think the analogy is doing more work than it should but I can't say where.

Can you audit it? I want the mapping laid out explicitly — what plays the observer or frame, what plays the measured quantity, what plays the transformation between frames, what plays the theory's laws — a table is fine. Then the disanalogies, split into the ones that actually matter for the conclusion he wants and the ones that are just noise. The bit I keep circling round: in special relativity the observer-dependence comes with invariants, the interval and the laws, and the whole point is that the relativity is disciplined rather than anything-goes. What, if anything, plays that role here — is cardinality-as-seen-from-outside the invariant, and does that rescue the analogy or wreck it? Relatedly, both observers in SR are inside the one universe and equally right, whereas here the inside and outside viewpoints don't seem to be on a par, and I want to know whether thats a real disanalogy or just the paradox restated in different words. Finish with whether the Platonism-is-dead conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether there's a better analogy for what a countable model is doing — I half-think the right one is about a first-order theory not being able to pin down its own quantifiers or its own notion of finitude, but I can't make it stick.
