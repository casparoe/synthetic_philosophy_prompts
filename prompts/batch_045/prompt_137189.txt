I've just finished a two-year term on the clinical ethics committee of my hospital trust, where we rewrote the guidance for allocating the last intensive-care bed when more than one patient needs it, and I argued successfully for a position I now want stress-tested before I go on defending it elsewhere.

The position: numbers carry no weight of their own — nobody's claim to be saved is strengthened by the fact that others would be saved alongside them — but numbers do have a derivative weight, because when we cannot save everyone the only defensible way to respect each person's equal claim is to allocate chances rather than lives, and those chances should be divided so that the larger group's chance of being rescued is proportional to its size: five patients get a five-in-six chance, the lone patient a one-in-six. So save the many in expectation, but never with certainty, and never by adding up lives.

Tell me what would count against this. I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with a clear line between what a defender of the view should concede as counting against it and what only a critic would assert. In particular please consider whether the step from equal claims to proportional chances quietly reintroduces the aggregation it was supposed to avoid; whether "equal claim" in fact supports giving each individual the same chance (a coin flip) rather than weighting by group size; what the view implies when chances cannot be randomised credibly, or when the same decision-maker faces the choice repeatedly; and whether anything survives once my usual qualifications are added — be blunt if you think it has been hedged into something unfalsifiable, and say whether that matters here. Finally, say what would change your own ranking, and where you think it is most likely to be wrong.

Open with one sentence giving your overall verdict, then the ranking and the reasoning.
