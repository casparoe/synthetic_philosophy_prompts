I'd be grateful for your help with a piece I'm writing, and I should say at the outset that I know you can't look anything up: everything below is invented, or from what you already know, and I'm not asking for sources, citations or quotations from real documents.

Our daughter was born in November. Since then, thinking about what we owe people who do not exist yet has stopped being a parlour game for me — it now has a date on it, roughly 2085, which is when she'll be my age. I write a fortnightly column for a small magazine, and the editor has asked me for something on climate and our duties to future generations. I agreed on condition that I don't have to write the column everyone already knows — the one about the grandchildren and the borrowed planet. I'd rather do it as a spoof.

The piece: an official leaflet of the kind that comes through the letterbox with a freepost envelope, announcing a fictional statutory scheme — call it the Future Generations (Discharge of Duty) Scheme — under which every adult household receives an annual statement of its residual intergenerational liability and may discharge that liability through any of the routes set out in a published tariff. I'd like the invented mechanics taken seriously and made internally consistent, because the jokes should come from the design rather than from the adjectives.

Three features I want built in:

Liability is computed in units of future welfare — quality-adjusted life-years at risk, say — from the household's emissions, and then discounted to present value at a rate the leaflet describes as consistent with the growth-adjusted return on capital, so that harm further away costs less to discharge. Put real numbers in the tariff and show the arithmetic; I used to work in pensions valuation, so I know how much quiet work a discount rate can do, and I'd like a policy economist to laugh rather than wince.

The tariff should offer, in one flat voice: direct emissions reduction; funding adaptation; and payment into a Compensation Fund. The Fund's trustees are legally bound to compensate only identifiable future persons who can show they were made worse off by the emissions in question. Since the Scheme's own eligibility rules require a claimant who would have existed anyway and would have been better off, and since our choices about energy and family size partly determine which people come to exist at all, the Fund has never located a single eligible claimant. Its unspent balance is now very large and is invested, on the trustees' standard advice, in a global index tracker.

The cheapest route, and therefore the one the Scheme's guidance recommends: a Non-Procreation Credit. A household that certifies it will have no children, or no more children, discharges its entire liability, on the reasoning that it has created nobody who could be exposed to the harm and that nothing else on the tariff avoids harm at lower cost. I want this recommended in exactly the tone used for loft insulation, with a worked example showing that a couple intending two children owes roughly what a household flying long-haul every year owes, and that both are better advised to take the credit.

In passing, without ever naming them, the leaflet should let a reader see: the thought that future people will be richer than us and so less deserving of our money; the difference between failing to benefit someone and harming them; and the worry that the Scheme turns a duty into a purchase. None of these may be stated as objections — they should be visible only as the shape of the thing.

Voice and constraints: deadpan throughout, no winking, no nudging, no "critics might say". It should read as satire to a general reader while being funny in a specific way to someone who knows the discounting and population-ethics literatures. No named individuals, living or dead; no real organisations; no quotations from any real text; invented department, form numbers, helpline and all. About 700–800 words, laid out as a short numbered FAQ with the tariff as a table.

Then, separately and in about 150 words, a note for me rather than for the page: where the satire takes liberties. Which joke rests on a contested reading of the philosophy rather than a fair one? Which one overstates what anyone actually holds? I'd particularly like to know what a defender of discounting, or someone who thinks the non-identity point dissolves rather than deepens the duty, would say the leaflet misrepresents — and what would have to be true for you to rewrite it so that the joke went the other way.
