I teach an upper-division seminar in philosophy of science — 18 students, one 80-minute meeting, a whiteboard, index cards, and no computers. Most of them can already recite the standard underdetermination thesis: that the evidence available at a time is compatible with more than one theory, so evidence alone never singles out a unique one. That is precisely the difficulty. They can state the thesis and they do not believe it. They file it alongside Cartesian skepticism as something true in an inert philosophical sense and irrelevant to practice, and when I press them they retreat to a much weaker claim — that the real situation is just poverty of data, and that more data of the same kind would settle the matter.

I want an activity in which the gap between that weak, temporary reading and the strong, structural one is something the students collide with rather than something I announce. Nothing should be named in advance: the words underdetermination, empirical equivalence, Duhem, Quine, and any grue-style predicate should not appear, in my mouth or on any handout, until the debrief.

Three design constraints matter more to me than elegance. First, I want the divergence between the rival accounts to be actionable rather than observable. The two candidates should agree on every observation and on every prediction about what will happen if nobody interferes, and disagree only about what would happen if someone intervenes — so that the only way to tell them apart is to do something, the doing has a cost, and the group must decide whether to pay it before they know whether it was worth paying. Second, the observation vocabulary should be fixed at the start by a printed list of permitted recording categories. If they want a category that is not on the list, they must buy it with a scarce token, so that extending the language is itself a move they have to justify to each other. This is the part I care most about: the case where the underdetermination turns out to be an artifact of what they can measure rather than of how the world is, and where the repair is itself a theoretical choice rather than a neutral one. Third, there should be a payoff scheme scored against a hidden data stream that I generate, arranged if possible so that being right for the wrong reason and being right for the right reason come apart — I would rather they feel the sting of that than win cleanly.

What I need from you:

- The setup: any roles, teams, or asymmetric information; a minute-by-minute timeline; the exact materials; the scoring rule; and the rule for the intervention decision, including what happens if they decline to spend.
- A debrief that maps particular moments in the session onto the weak/transient reading, the structural reading, the vocabulary artifact, and the observation-intervention asymmetry, and only then supplies the standard names and the standard objections. At the end I want them able to say what a working scientist in that situation should actually do, and what a tie-break such as simplicity, fruitfulness, or consilience is doing if it is not doing the work of evidence — but I want them to have earned that list by needing it, not to receive it as a summary.
- An honest forecast of what will go wrong the first time: I expect the stronger students to meta-game, someone to try to extract information from me by asking questions, the group to split and then simply assert past each other, and above all a real risk that the session lands as science is arbitrary, which is the opposite of my aim. Tell me how to head each of these off inside the rules rather than by intervening as the authority.
- A frank note on what the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in discussion: that real theories are whole webs rather than single rules, that actual scientists share background commitments that quietly break ties, and that nothing here touches the global skeptical version of the thesis.
