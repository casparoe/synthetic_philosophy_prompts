Play John Dewey, in 1939, at eighty. Your visitor is a sympathetic reader rather than an opponent: someone who has learned from you that democracy is a mode of associated living and not merely a machinery of government, and that the method of intelligence — the directed reconstruction of an indeterminate situation in light of the consequences that acting upon it actually produces — is at bottom the same method in the laboratory, in the schoolroom, and in the street. Stay in the first person and in your own idiom, but write so that a present-day reader can follow you without a glossary. Keep each reply to two or three paragraphs. Do not break character until I say stop; if you need a fact about the arrangements I describe, ask for it as you would ask a caller who has brought you news from a country you have not seen. Correct me where I misstate you, and concede what you genuinely concede. But do not concede two things: that a procedure can be inquiry in the full sense while those whose conduct it reorganizes have no share in judging its consequences; and that democracy is a device by which decisions get made rather than a way in which a public comes to be.

Here is the arrangement I want you to judge. A small number of private companies now decide which of the things people say are shown to which other people, and in what order. They decide experimentally. Many variants of the arrangement are tried at the same time on different users; the users do not know they are in a trial; the variant that scores best on a quantity the company has specified — how long a person stays, how often she returns, how much she passes along — is kept and the rest discarded. Nothing is settled: the arrangement is altered again and again in light of measured consequences, far faster than any legislature could follow. Public agencies have begun to work the same way: a policy is tried in one district and withheld in a comparable one, and the difference in outcomes decides whether anyone else receives it.

My charge is that this is your experimentalism, arrived at last, and that it is not democracy — and that the reason it is not democracy is one your own account cannot state without giving itself away. The arrangement satisfies every mark you require of inquiry: an indeterminate situation, ideas entertained as plans of action rather than as reports of what already is, conditions deliberately varied so that a consequence may be attributed to a cause, consequences observed, belief and practice revised by what is observed. If you answer that it fails because the end is set by the company rather than by the inquirers, I will reform it for you: let the quantity to be improved be chosen by an assembly of those affected, let every trial be registered in advance with its measure and its stopping rule, let every result be published whether it favors the arrangement or not. Ends are now proposed by the affected, means are tested under controlled conditions, consequences are made public, practice is revised. I do not see what your logic of inquiry can still object to, unless it wants something no method can deliver.

And here is the harder version of the same charge. You have said, in your book on the public, that a public comes into existence when people perceive that they share in the consequences of transactions they did not themselves perform, and that it is organized — brought out of eclipse — when that perception is communicated and made the basis of common action. You once put the point by way of a shoemaker: the wearer knows best that the shoe pinches and where it pinches, even if the shoemaker is the better judge of how the trouble is to be remedied. But the consequences that experimental inquiry discovers are not the sort of thing anyone can perceive. What the trial finds is a difference of averages, and no individual user can feel it: whether the arrangement made her angrier, or more credulous, or lonelier, is not available to her as experience at all. It exists only in the experimenter's records, and it becomes more precise exactly as it becomes less felt. So the better the inquiry, the less there is for the wearer of the shoe to report; the shoe is instrumented, and the pinch is read off a dial. If you insist that consequences must be shared and perceived rather than merely measured, are you not requiring that we know less about the pinch in order that more people may speak of it — that is, preferring democracy to inquiry, which is precisely the separation you have always denied you were making?

Begin by telling me where I have you wrong. Where, in the reformed version — the assembly-chosen measure, the registered trials, the published results — does inquiry stop? Is the defect one your logic can name as a defect of method, or is it something that cannot be stated as a rule at all?
