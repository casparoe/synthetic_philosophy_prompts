I convene a final-year option, "Learning Theory and Epistemology", taught jointly by the philosophy and computer science departments, and I am setting the exam. I want one question — 45 minutes, about 800 words, 25 marks — on the relation between the no-free-lunch results for black-box search and Hume's problem of induction.

What the students have already had, so you can pitch it: the NFL identity for finite black-box search (fixed budget of m distinct queries, no resampling, performance averaged uniformly over a class of objective functions that is closed under every permutation of the search space), the parallel result for supervised learning, and Hume's problem as a regress about the justification of any principle saying that the unobserved will resemble the observed. They have also had the standard move of saying that NFL formalises Hume, and the standard rejoinder that real problems have structure so NFL is irrelevant to practice.

The crux I want the question to turn on is a two-part handout note I am giving them, and which you should restate in exam-paper form so the question is self-contained:

Lemma 1. The averaging identity depends on permutation-closure, not on the size of the space. If the class of objectives is closed under a group of permutations of the domain that has two orbits — say it permutes freely within each of two blocks but never maps a point of one block into the other — then the identity can fail, and there are such classes on which one search algorithm beats another on the uniform average over the class.

Lemma 2. Take a Boolean objective on a domain of n points. Of the 2-to-the-n functions, at most a fraction 2-to-the-minus-k can be singled out by a description more than k bits shorter than the trivial one. So a uniform distribution over the whole class is not a neutral or assumption-free standpoint: it is, with probability at least 1 minus 2-to-the-minus-k, a commitment to an objective that is incompressible and noise-like.

The question must force candidates to take a stand on whether the restriction on the space of possible problems that NFL shows to be necessary is the same kind of commitment as the uniformity principle Hume worries about, and on what follows if it is not. It should require them to use both lemmas, and it should be genuinely open: a candidate who argues that NFL is simply irrelevant to the problem of induction, and one who argues that it sharpens or vindicates a Humean conclusion, must both be able to take a first if they engage with the lemmas and with the relevant distinctions. It must not be answerable by any of these three stock moves, each of which I want actively screened for: (i) NFL shows all algorithms are equivalent, so it is just Hume made mathematical; (ii) NFL is irrelevant because actual problems have structure, full stop; (iii) the reply that one simply adopts a prior, with no attention to whether the choice of prior inherits the regress. The paper is closed-book and I want argument rather than scholarship, so the question should ask for no citations and no verbatim quotation from primary texts.
