I'm helping to organize a two-day seminar on causation in historical explanation, and I want the second day to open not with a paper but with a piece of short fiction — about 1,800 words, meant to be read aloud in a dozen minutes — that makes the problem of overdetermination and multi-causal explanation something the audience has to feel before they can argue about it. I'd be grateful if you would write it.

The setup I have in mind is below. Rename everything freely; keep the structure.

A compact among five river cities — call it the Kestrel Accord — has held for thirty years and collapses inside a single week. In this world, causation is an institutional matter: a Bureau of Removals keeps ledgers in which clerks, working from the archive, record for each documented factor whether the Accord would still have fallen had that factor been absent. The Bureau has a written procedure, and the procedure contains a convention: when a factor is removed, everything else is held to its actual course except where the removal itself would have altered it, and the clerks are forbidden from tracing those alterations backwards.

By the Bureau's ledgers, four things are on record:

- a grain convoy that failed to arrive, after which the riverside districts rioted and the council never reconvened;
- the assassination of the one mediator whose personal authority could have held the council together, carried out by a first shooter who fired while a second shooter waited in the crowd and never fired;
- the mutiny of a river garrison whose commanders had made their own separate decision to march on the capital;
- a procedural rule, written into the Accord at its founding and never noticed since, requiring unanimous ratification of any emergency measure.

The ledgers say: remove any one of the first three and the Accord still falls; remove the unanimity rule and it does not fall, whatever else happens. A separate finding, disputed within the Bureau, concerns a fifth item — a dispatch revealing that one city had secretly rearmed, which would have wrecked the Accord on its own but arrived two days after the council had already dissolved.

A tribunal now has to name a single cause of the collapse, because reparations and a treason indictment turn on the naming.

What I want the story to make legible, without ever lecturing: that a factor can fail the but-for test and still plainly be a cause, both where the causes are symmetrically redundant and where one of them merely got there first and pre-empted the other; that a different test — being a non-redundant element of some larger set of conditions that together were enough — restores all of them as causes and arguably restores too much; that a cause is always an answer to a question with a contrast built into it, so that why the Accord fell when it did, why it fell rather than held, and why it fell this way rather than by quiet lapse can have different answers from the same facts; that the rule nobody noticed is necessary but explanatory only for someone with a certain interest, in the way that oxygen is necessary for the fire and is not what the investigator names; and that every one of these verdicts sits on top of the Bureau's convention about holding the background fixed, so that a clerk who is willing to trace the consequences of a removal backwards through the archive can reverse a finding without any new evidence at all.

Two constraints on how you do it. First, the Bureau should be a human institution with clerks, habits, and a rulebook, not a machine that delivers truth; its findings should be the source of the trouble rather than the resolution of it, and at least one finding should turn out to rest on the convention rather than on the archive. Second, no one in the story should say words like overdetermination, sufficient set, or contrast class — they should argue in their own idioms: procedural objection, indictment, pamphlet, precedent, the insulted pride of a clerk whose ledger is being overridden. The prose should be plain and concrete, with dates, weather,Documents I can picture.

End with the tribunal's decision actually handed down, and with the cost of that decision visible. I don't want the story to settle which account of causation is correct; I want a reader to come out of it unable to treat the tribunal's question as one the causal facts alone could have answered, and also unable to treat it as merely a matter of words.
