so I'm writing a novel — near-future thing, a research ship in orbit and a drilling company breathing down everyone's neck — and I've written myself into a corner. Two of my characters have been stuck in a room together for eleven months and are supposed to hand the Classification Board one joint memo. One of them is a metabolism-first person, in the autopoiesis / self-maintaining-chemical-system tradition: life is something a thing does to keep itself going, and a virus isn't alive because it never runs its own show. The other is an information-first person in the NASA-working-definition tradition: life is a system that can undergo Darwinian evolution, metabolism is just plumbing, what matters is heritable variation plus differential success. The stakes aren't academic: if the Board says alive, the vent field gets protected and the drilling permit dies; if it says not alive, the company moves in next week.

I need the memo they'd actually produce after a good-faith year of arguing, not a tidy encyclopedia entry. Give me the claims both of them can sign; the exact places they still differ, in wording each side would accept for its own view; the cruxes — the sub-questions where settling them would move at least one of them; and for each crux the test or case both agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. And if any part of their fight turns out to be about words rather than the world, say which part and why.

The material on the table is all invented by me, so please work with my cases rather than reaching for real ones (and no citations or further reading — I'm not putting footnotes in a novel):

- the plume filaments: strands that grow inside the mineral chimneys of a vent field. They hold an internal chemistry far from equilibrium, they repair themselves, and they copy short sequence-patterns with heritable variation, some variants leaving more copies than others. But the copying only ever happens against the chimney's mineral surface; move a strand off the vent and it keeps its chemistry indefinitely but never copies again.
- the Kestrel agent: virus-like, no metabolism of its own, inert on its own — but inside a host cell it directs the whole metabolism, and it evolves fast.
- the Ridley reactor: a lab setup, no cells and no membranes, just a chip-fed vat that drips in pre-activated building blocks. Ten years in, it has thrown up catalytic functions nobody designed and nobody predicted.
- the cinder field: an old dried-up vent where the same chemistry still creeps along, a thousand times slower, and structures keep accumulating over centuries. Nothing there has copied anything in a long, long while.

Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table, then the cruxes with predictions. Keep the register flat enough that two exhausted scientists would sign it, and add a couple of lines at the end on where each of them privately thinks the other one caved. Oh, and if you think one of my four cases is doing no real work — that it definately just reduplicates another — say so and drop it rather than padding.
