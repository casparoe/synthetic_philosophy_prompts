I'm in a tiny reading group — four of us, one of whom actually does metaphysics and the rest of us are hangers-on — and we spent last term on the special composition question: for any material objects, the xs, when do they compose something? It's got under my skin for a dumb personal reason. Our viola player moved to Lisbon in March, and when I said "the quartet is over," Ana (the metaphysics one) said something like "the quartet was never a thing in the first place, so nothing was lost," and I got properly upset about it in a way I couldn't defend. So I wrote the thing below. It's meant to be a real argument and not a mood piece, and I want comments before I inflict it on the group.

One ground rule: I know there are people who've written whole books on this, but I'm not asking for a reading list or for names and dates — I can't get to a library until September and none of us would read them anyway. If the view below already exists in some form, describe it in you're own words so I can see how close I am, but don't cite anything. Also, "it depends what you mean by composition" is not a comment, it's the thing I'm trying to get past.

---

**What were we asking?**

**1.** The three families of answer are familiar: the xs never compose anything (nihilism), they always do (universalism), or they do only under some further condition (restrictivism — van Inwagen's being the famous case, roughly that the xs compose something iff their activity constitutes a life, or there is only one of them). Every answer has somebody who finds it obviously false, and the dialectic seems to cycle. I want to suggest why: the question is missing a parameter.

**2. Indexing.** Composition gets invoked to do several different jobs: to pick out the subjects of action and experience, to pick out the units of causal explanation, and to license singular reference and counting. Nothing guarantees that one relation does all three well. So replace "do the xs compose something?" with "do the xs compose something that φs?" — composition-for-action, composition-for-causation, composition-for-counting. The special composition question as standardly posed assumes job-neutrality, and the cycling is a symptom of that assumption rather than of the difficulty of the cases.

**3. Loops.** The substantive bit, for the action/experience job: the xs compose an agent iff there is some quantity V such that (i) the states of the xs each make a difference to V, (ii) V makes a difference back to the states of the xs, and (iii) the whole arrangement tends to keep V within a range — the xs are wired into a closed feedback loop around a single regulated quantity. Call it loop-composition. Organisms are the paradigm: temperature, blood chemistry. When the loop opens, the composite goes out of existence. That's a place where I think my view beats the life-based one: on the life view, a body in cardiac arrest is an awkward borderline, whereas on mine the answer is clean — the loop is open, the thing is gone, and if it closes again then something new begins.
And a playing string quartet, I say, is a case: there is one regulated quantity, ensemble tempo and balance; every player's output shifts it; every player hears it and adjusts. Four people warming up in separate rooms are not a quartet. That's what I wanted to say to Ana. The quartet existed while we rehearsed and played — not never, and not always either, but only while the loop was closed.

**4. The artifact problem, which I don't know what to do with.** My bicycle in the hall has no feedback loop. Strictly, on my view, there is no bicycle there — only parts arranged bicycle-wise. That's within spitting distance of nihilism, which I don't want and which Ana will use to wave the whole thing away. Options: (a) bite the bullet, most artifacts aren't objects; (b) bolt on a second, non-loop condition for artifacts, something about rigid mutual support plus a designer's intention; (c) give up on answering SCQ in general and say I only ever offered an answer for the sub-question that matters — subjects of action and experience, i.e. animals, persons, and some groups. I lean towards (c), but (c) makes the essay smaller, and I suspect it also means the complaint in §1 is doing all the real work and §3 is decoration.

**5. What it costs.** Composition becomes intermittent, extrinsic, and vague: how much feedback is enough, how much of a player's sound has to reach the others, whether a thermostat composes an object together with the furnace. I want to say the vagueness is a feature, because it's the same vagueness we already live with about when a committee is functioning. But I can see that this relocates the problem rather than answering it, unless I say something about why vagueness in this spot is benign.
