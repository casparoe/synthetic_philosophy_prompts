Hi — I'm on the grants committee of a tiny foundation that funds community groups (a few hundred thousand a year, all local), and we've had the same argument for about two years now. Half of us think we should only fund groups that deliver some service anyone can see the point of, the other half think that's exactly the wrong question and that what we're really doing is keeping forms of life going. We keep using words like neutrality and autonomy and then noticing we mean different things. I want to hand out a quiz at our next away day that sorts us by what we'd actually do with an application, not by which label we'd pick.

So: write me a "which position are you actually running this foundation on?" quiz. Ten questions, three answer options each. Every question has to be a concrete application on the table — the applicant, what they want money for, and the three ways we could respond — and it has to be phrased so someone with no philosophy background can answer it without any clue which theory it's a trap for. No question that amounts to "do you think the state should be neutral?" Please also give me a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto positions, plus a short note on which two or three questions are doing nearly all the sorting work and which are basically decoration.

The cases I want used, because these are the ones we actually fought over:

- the county historical society — i.e. the majority culture applying for the same money, on the same terms
- a language with maybe eight hundred speakers left, every one of whom already speaks the majority language fluently, asking for money to keep the language alive at all
- a religious group whose food bank requires volunteers to sit through a service
- a group asking for money for something with no measurable outcome whatsoever, which its members describe as the thing that makes their lives make sense
- a people split by a border, with a rich half and a poor half, where the poor half wants us to treat both halves as one community
- a voluntary non-cultural association — a workers' co-op with a very strict internal code, say — that can expel members by majority vote
- a community that wants to educate its own children entirely within its own comprehensive doctrine
- a group that says in so many words that it can't explain to outsiders what the money is for, you'd have to be one of us

Don't start from a list of names. Start from the logical space: lay out the questions and the combinations of answers they actually allow, then locate the real positions in that space, then tell me which combinations are unoccupied and whether that's because they're incoherent, just unattractive, or merely untried. Leaves should include at least: strict antiperfectionist neutrality, liberal culturalism (culture as a fairness issue / as something individuals need), perfectionist liberalism, civic republicanism, recognition-based communitarianism, the thick localist neo-Aristotelian option, toleration-for-reasons-of-fallibility, and the leaf for someone who thinks the whole framing of the question is the problem. Name each leaf where there's a name, write "unnamed" where there isn't — I know the authors already, so no reading lists or citations, I just want the map.

Two more things. First, flag any pair of leaves my ten questions can't actually pull apart, and say whether the difference is only in justification (i.e. they'd fund the same things anyway) or whether there's an eleventh question that would split them. Second, most of us will answer inconsistently — what should I do with a board member whose answers don't land cleanly anywhere? Is there a principled way to say what they "really" hold, or should the key just report the region of the space they're scattered across? And give me one line per leaf on what that position has the hardest time saying out loud in a room like ours.
