I spent a little over thirty years as an experimental atmospheric chemist, most of it on aerosol-cloud interactions, where the honest summary of nearly every result I ever published was that the effect was real but the error bars were humiliating. Since retiring I have been auditing a graduate seminar in normative ethics, and I find I keep reverting to my old habits: when a discussion turns on some quantity nobody can measure, I want to know whether the uncertainty is the ordinary kind, where noise is roughly symmetric and cancels in expectation, or the nasty kind, where you know your own evidence-gathering is skewed in a direction you cannot identify. The seminar recently stalled on exactly this distinction while discussing consequentialist cluelessness, the worry that an act's long-run effects lie so far beyond our forecasting powers that expected-value reasoning over the long run is not merely noisy but unavailable. Several of us thought the standard reassurance, that long-run effects are high-variance but unbiased and therefore wash out so that one may simply act on near-term effects, handles only the first kind of uncertainty and silently assumes away the second. I would like a case that makes that gap visible.

Please construct a decision problem of a single paragraph, set in an ordinary institutional or land-management context, with no science fiction, no existential-risk-scale payoffs and no exotic devices, containing two options, call them A and B, built to the following specifications. The near-term effects, which the agent can forecast with reasonable confidence, should slightly favor A. Both options must also have long-run effects that are large relative to the near-term ones, say one to three orders of magnitude larger, while remaining entirely mundane, and the mechanism that makes them large must be specified concretely as lock-in, path dependence, a durable standard, or a self-reproducing institution rather than merely stipulated. For A, the long-run effects should be merely high-variance: a spread the agent has decent grounds to treat as roughly symmetric around zero. For B, the long-run effects should be genuinely clueless in the strong sense: near-certain to be large in magnitude, but with a sign about which the agent has no well-founded credence, and, this is the part I care most about, the agent must know that their evidence about the sign is systematically skewed, because only successes get recorded or because the channels they can observe happen to be the flattering ones, so that their mild felt optimism that B is for the better carries no evidential weight and the long-run expectation of B is indeterminate rather than zero. The case must also not be a Pascal's mugging: the large magnitude has to come from near-certain amplification, not from a vanishing probability of an astronomical payoff. The point of all this is that the usual dissolution must fail, so it should not be possible to defuse the case by observing that long-run uncertainty is symmetric and cancels.

Then do the check. Derive, from the credences you have built into the scenario rather than by announcement, three verdicts: first, that long-run expected-value maximization using the agent's unadjusted available credences favors B; second, that a window-restricted consequentialism counting only effects the agent can forecast favors A; and third, that a decision rule for indeterminate probabilities also favors A, though for a reason that still attends to the long run rather than simply discarding it, and here you should say which rule you are using and what the alternatives do or do not deliver. Finish by naming the single feature of the case that is doing the work, saying what happens to all three verdicts if that feature is removed and B's spread becomes merely symmetric, and explaining how a defender of the first verdict would argue that the case has been rigged.

At the very end, as the last line of your reply with nothing after it, give the three verdicts in exactly this form: \answer{long-run EV: X; window-restricted: Y; imprecise-credence rule: Z}, where each of X, Y and Z is A, B, or indifferent.
