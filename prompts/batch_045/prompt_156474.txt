I have no mathematical training beyond school level, and I've been reading a popular book on infinity — one of those volumes about the strangest ideas in mathematics — whose central chapter I can't tell whether to trust. I've started arguing with it in the margins, and I'd like to know whether my objections are standard ones or just naive.

The chapter runs roughly as follows. Before Cantor, the received view from Aristotle onward was that infinity is only ever potential: you can always add one more, but no completed infinite collection exists. Cantor's diagonal argument overturned this by showing that the infinity of the real numbers is strictly larger than the infinity of the counting numbers, so there are at least two sizes of infinity and infinity has to be treated as a completed, actual thing. Taking actual infinities seriously then produces absurdities, of which Hilbert's Hotel is the famous one: a hotel with infinitely many rooms can take in a new guest even when every room is occupied, by moving each guest up one room, and can even absorb infinitely many buses each carrying infinitely many passengers. Hilbert, described as a finitist, is presented as using this to argue that completed infinities are not merely physically impossible but incoherent, since a whole cannot be the same size as one of its proper parts, and so the hotel is a contradiction rather than a curiosity. Mathematics is said to have gone the other way and accepted the actual infinite anyway on the strength of Cantor's hierarchy — countable infinity, then the continuum, then larger and larger infinities, with nothing in between the countable and the continuum — and the chapter concludes that although mathematicians can reason about infinite sets consistently, arguments like Hilbert's Hotel show that no infinite collection can exist in the real world.

One thing I keep coming back to is that the hotel seems to me to be doing two different jobs at once. Read one way, moving each guest up a room is just a fact about there being a pairing between the guests and the rooms: nobody moves, nothing happens in time, and the point is only that a certain rearrangement is possible in principle. Read another way, it is an operation the manager performs, and then it seems to smuggle in something extra, because infinitely many coordinated moves squeezed into a finite time looks like a separate problem from whether infinite totalities can exist — the chapter's own story about guests shuffling down corridors is what makes it vivid, and that vividness may be doing the arguing. Relatedly, I suspect that every room is occupied has stopped carrying the consequence I expect from the finite case, where full plainly means no more guests can be accommodated, and that this slide is doing real work in the chapter.

Could you go through the chapter's claims one at a time and tell me which a mathematician would accept as stated, which are loose but harmless popularizations, and which are simply wrong, including anything I have repeated uncritically above? I especially want to know whether the conclusion about the real world survives once the loose claims are tightened, and whether my distinction between the static and the operational reading of the hotel is one that is actually drawn. Please answer in continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
