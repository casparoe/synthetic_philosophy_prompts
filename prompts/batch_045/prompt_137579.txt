I was handed leadership of a small replication unit attached to a psychology department six months ago, shortly after a journalist quoted one of our "failed to replicate" verdicts about a finding its original authors had built careers on and I found I could not defend the wording. We run about four pre-registered multi-site replications a year and issue a short verdict on each that other researchers rely on, and our internal disagreements have convinced me we need a written procedure rather than argument case by case: two of us read a non-significant result with a tight interval as evidence of absence, two read the same numbers as "this design could never have detected the original effect anyway" and want to call it uninformative, and we have no agreed handling of a significant result in the opposite direction, or of a point estimate that is a third of the original's.

So I want a decision procedure we can run in a fixed order on each completed replication, taking design and results to one of a small set of verdicts, with an explicit account of what to do when we cannot get the numbers we would want — we usually have neither the original raw data nor the original authors' cooperation. Two features of our setup make this harder than the standard treatment of the question. First, we no longer report a single Bayes factor; before seeing the replication data we elicit a predicted-effect distribution from three sources — the original authors, a skeptic we appoint, and a small meta-analysis of comparable studies — and report all three. The procedure has to say what the verdict is when these disagree about whether the data support the effect, and whether such disagreement should itself be published as an outcome rather than settled by the procedure. Second, we treat "did this replication actually test the original claim" as separate from "what do the numbers say," and I am unsure where it belongs in the sequence and what would count as a sufficient yes.

Be committed: make the actual choices — the verdict labels, the order of operations, whatever thresholds you think belong — and give your reasons, including where you think our three-source policy is a mistake and where you would overrule the way replications are usually reported. Mark the steps that stay judgment calls no formalism removes. Then run the finished procedure on two of our cases: a replication landing at roughly a third of the original effect size, with an interval excluding zero but excluding the original value too, and a non-significant result whose interval comfortably includes both zero and the original effect. I suspect I will dislike at least one of the verdicts; give it anyway.
