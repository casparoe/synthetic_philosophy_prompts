I work in a nonpartisan bill-drafting office for a mid-sized state legislature, and I keep having the same argument with one of the attorneys who reviews our language. Any time I point out that a literal reading of something we drafted will land somewhere nobody in the building wanted, he shuts it down with "the legislature said what it meant and meant what it said."

Most recent instance: a tenant notice-requirement provision keyed to "residential building," which given the definition section in our code doesn't reach the converted-motel and single-room-occupancy units that are a big share of what the bill was actually aimed at, and that nobody in 2019 thought about at all. His position is that we shouldn't try to clean that up in the implementing guidance, or by tacking a purpose clause onto the enacted text, because the words are the law and a future legislature can fix it if it cares.

I'm pretty sure the line is smuggling in several different claims at once, and I want to take it apart before our next meeting. Can you lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses it might be expressing — and for each one say whether it's basically a platitude, whether it's substantive and defensible, whether it's probably false, and whether it actually *gets him* his conclusion in the SRO case?

Some of the seams I think matter, but please add ones I'm not seeing:

- Who the reader is supposed to be. "What it said" comes apart depending on whether the imagined reader is a member of the public holding only the statute, a lawyer with the whole code and the case law, a judge with the committee record, or the staffer who actually typed the words. A theory could be optimizing for one reader's competence rather than another's, and that seems like a choice the slogan hides.
- What "meant" refers to. Something like the actual communicative intentions of the people who voted, versus a public-meaning construct that gets called intent, versus the purpose the bill was trying to serve. And at what grain: a hundred-odd members with different reasons, a sponsor, a committee, or whoever wrote the draft.
- How much drafting competence the second half assumes. "Meant what it said" reads to me like an empirical claim — that omissions are deliberate and silence is meaningful. I can tell you that in our office terms are often left ambiguous on purpose because the coalition couldn't agree, and that a lot of language is lifted near-verbatim from model bills written by advocacy groups, so "the legislature said" is already shaky. Which of the readings collapse if that is what really happens?
- Whether it's a claim about meaning at all, or a claim about authority — i.e. that even if we knew perfectly well what the enactors wanted, only the enacted words may be enforced, for rule-of-law or fair-notice reasons — or a claim about which branch is allowed to fix mistakes. Those would support his conclusion very differently.

Last thing: for each reading, tell me what it would imply for how I should be drafting in the first place — whether I should be fighting harder for findings-and-purpose sections, defining terms of art tightly, or writing the ambiguity in explicitly. And don't just pick your favourite reading and run with it; I want the menu, including the trivial ones, and a guess at which reading a guy like him is actually reaching for.
