I have no formal training in philosophy and I've been arguing about this with a friend after work. I came across Kavka's toxin puzzle secondhand: as I've seen it stated, a stranger with a reliable way of reading your mind offers you a large sum if, at midnight tonight, you genuinely form the intention to drink at noon tomorrow a vial that will make you miserably ill for a day and do you no lasting harm. The money is paid for the intention alone; you never have to drink. My friend says that's impossible, and he gives this argument, which I think proves too much.

(1) To form an intention to do A is to settle on doing A for what you now take to be reasons to do A.
(2) You cannot now settle on A for reasons you know will not be reasons at the moment of acting: an intention is a commitment carried forward, and it has to be a commitment you expect still to have force when the time comes.
(3) In the toxin case the money is earned at midnight. By noon tomorrow the only reason to drink has been spent, and there is a positive reason not to — a day of misery.
(4) So at midnight you cannot form the intention to drink at noon. Resolving, visualizing, saying to yourself that you'll do it — none of that is an intention, because you know in advance you'll drop it.
(5) Nothing in (1)–(4) depends on the toxin, the money, or the stranger. They depend only on this: you now know that when the moment to act arrives you will have no reason to act and some reason not to.
(6) But that is the ordinary shape of a great deal of real intending. I intend to drive my brother to the airport at 4 a.m. I intend to go to the gym straight after work. I intend to keep a promise I already expect to regret. In each case I know that when the hour comes my reasons will have thinned to almost nothing and the reasons against will look large.
(7) If (4) were right, none of those would be intentions either. They plainly are.
(8) Therefore (1) or (2) is false: either intending is not settling-on-reasons at all, or you can intend to act for reasons you know will not survive until the act.

If I have to give something up I would rather keep (3), (4) and (7) — those feel like the data — and give up something in (1) or (2). My friend says that if you give those up you lose the ability to explain why the stranger's offer is any harder to take than an ordinary bet, which is the whole point of the puzzle.

So: which step do you reject, or is the argument valid and one of the premises has to go? If you reject (5) as a bad generalization, say exactly what the toxin case has that the 4 a.m. airport run lacks, and whether that difference is a difference in degree or in kind. Then apply your diagnosis to a variant I invented: everything is as before, except that you know the money is transferred the instant the intention is formed and you know you are free to drop the intention one second later — call it the flash-intention version. Does that make the puzzle easier or harder, and does the step you told me to reject still do the same work there? End by saying, briefly, what if anything the puzzle shows about intention that a non-philosopher like me would otherwise miss.
