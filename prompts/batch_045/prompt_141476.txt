The students have all had the standard version: strict preferences over m ≥ 3 alternatives, a deterministic social choice function defined on all profiles; if it is strategy-proof and its range contains at least three alternatives, then some voter is a dictator (her top always wins). I dont want any citations or history in it — nobody's looking anything up in the exam.

Here's the hook I want the question built around. A student last year emailed me claiming he'd refuted the theorem with plurality rule: fix once and for all a tie-breaking order on the alternatives, everyone names her favourite, most votes wins. His argument: plurality is onto, nondictatorial, there are three or more alternatives — and yet no voter can ever make her own favourite win by lying, because her ballot already gives her favourite as much as it possibly can, and lying can only move her vote somewhere else. So, he says, here's a rule that does everything the theorem says is impossible.

What I actually want to test is whether they can pinpoint the move he's making: he has swapped strategy-proofness for a weaker property I'll call favourite-proofness — no voter can raise the probability that her most-preferred alternative is selected — and then applied a theorem whose antecedent is the real thing. It isn't a counterexample, it's a silently weakened premise. And then I want them to show the weakening buys nothing: favourite-proofness leaves entirely unprotected exactly the voters who matter in real elections, the ones who've worked out their favourite has no chance and are choosing between their second and third choices.
