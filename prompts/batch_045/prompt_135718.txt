I'm the philosophy person on the board of a small land trust — the rest of the board is a retired field biologist, a real-estate lawyer, and two farmers — and two or three times a year we have to decide whether some population we're being asked to protect, buy an easement on, or hand back to the state program is really a distinct species, or "just" a population of something common. The money is small (low six figures at most) but the commitments are basically permanent, and we have to produce a one-page rationale the state natural heritage program will accept. We can't afford new sequencing, so we're always working from whatever conflicting papers someone else published, read by people who are not taxonomists, in one afternoon.

What I want is a procedure we can run, not a theory. Concretely: the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to hand the decision back to us because the question is really about what we're protecting things *for* rather than about what's out there. Please ground it in the metaphysical stuff I keep half-remembering — the idea that species are historical individuals (lineages) rather than classes with essences, so that membership isn't a matter of how different an organism looks but of which lineage it's part of; and the fact that there are several legitimate species concepts that genuinely come apart, so "how different is different enough" has no theory-neutral answer.

Two mistakes we keep making: treating some percentage of genetic divergence as a threshold, and treating "they can still interbreed" as decisive. Tell us what to do instead.

Apply it to three cases. (1) Two streamside salamander lineages, ~2% divergence in mitochondrial DNA, one narrow hybrid zone, no obvious ecological difference. (2) A plant on a serpentine outcrop that looks and flowers differently from the widespread form but is genetically almost identical to it. (3) The one I'll hate: our flagship, a named mountain population that genomics now puts nested inside a widespread, unlisted species — we've raised money on it for years.

Finally, be blunt about what the procedure can't do, and where we we should expect it to misfire.
