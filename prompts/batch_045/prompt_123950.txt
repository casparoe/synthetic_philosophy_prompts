So I need a favor, and I'm asking you rather than a friend because everyone I'd normally ask is going to be in the room and would tell Hal first. I'm one of ninety people picked by lot last spring for the Cascade Water and Land Use Assembly — eighteen-month single term, nobody can serve twice, nobody who's held elected office in the last ten years is eligible, nothing we pass becomes law unless the legislature takes an up-or-down vote on it within sixty days without amending it. The pool was drawn from the voter file plus, weirdly, from people who hadn't voted in either of the last two general elections. That last part was Hal's idea: he was chair of the committee that wrote the statute, and he made it a condition of supporting the budget that year. Half his caucus thought it was a retirement vanity project. I testified against the non-voter thing at a hearing and said a lottery should mirror the electorate, and that drawing from people who don't vote selects for people who won't show up. He said — he was nice about it — that the people who vote in every single election are the most sorted, most certain, most already-claimed people in the state, and that not voting was the only cheap signal available of someone who hadn't been claimed yet. I thought that was clever and wrong. Six months in, our attendance is running in the nineties and the non-voters are if anything the ones who show up having actually read the binder. So I owe him something and I don't know how to say it in a toast without making the toast about me being wrong.

The occasion: retirement dinner for State Senator Hal Brenneman, thirty-four years in the legislature, seventy-one years old, Methodist, not a roast guy. About two hundred people in a hotel ballroom — his four kids and nine grandkids, a lot of former staffers who are now lobbyists, the governor, the committee chair who took over from him and quietly opposed the statute, two reporters who will absolutely print a line out of context, and maybe a dozen people from my assembly cohort. Roughly half the room knows what sortition is; the other half thinks the assembly is some kind of civic club. He's the reason I'm in the room giving this — I got picked by lot a second time to be the one who speaks, which he finds hilarious.

What I want from you: a five-minute toast, so under seven hundred words. Constraints that I think are the hard part. It can't say we'll miss his expertise or his institutional memory, because the entire premise of the thing he built is that expertise isn't the relevant credential. It can't say we need more politicians like Hal, which is what a normal toast at a normal retirement dinner says, because his own statute is designed to make thirty-four-year careers much rarer. It can't turn into a lecture on sortition — no jargon, no explaining the mechanism, no miniature civics lesson, and if it needs an image for what a lottery is, one image only. And the honest tension I keep circling: it took somebody with thirty-four years of accrued power and no re-election left to give any of it away, which is at once the best defense of career politicians anyone could make and the strongest case against them, and I want that in there without saying "term limits," which in front of this room just reads as a partisan slogan. It should land as affection and make the grandkids laugh once. Also, please: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — continuous prose, just the toast as it would be spoken, and then a couple of sentences after it if you want to flag a choice you made.
