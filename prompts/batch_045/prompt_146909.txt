I'm a teaching assistant for an undergraduate political theory course, and last week we ran a session on the boundary problem: the difficulty that any democratic procedure must take for granted a set of people whose decision counts as the decision of the group, while the identity of that set is itself one of the things supposedly up for democratic decision. The session went fine over the familiar ground. The students quickly saw that *let the people decide* is unhelpful when what is disputed is precisely who the people are, and we spent most of the hour on the standard contrast between a constituency defined by affectedness and one defined by subjection to coercion, together with the standard complaints that the first is wildly over-inclusive and the second leaves out everyone who is harmed without being governed. What I mishandled was a harder variant that a student raised in office hours afterwards, and I'd like help with the concept rather than with the syllabus: please don't give me readings, authors, or a literature map, just the conceptual structure.

Her variant goes as follows. Suppose a polity adopts what I'll call a *variable franchise*: instead of a standing electorate, the constituency is reconstituted for each decision, and includes exactly those persons whose interests are affected by that decision above some specified threshold, with the determination made by a standing commission applying an affectedness test. I want four things explained. First, agenda-dependence: whether some group counts as affected seems to shift with which options are placed on the table and how the question is framed, so that a decision to build upstream, a decision not to build, and a decision framed as water-rights rather than flood-control each appear to enfranchise a different set of people. The composition of the deciders therefore seems to depend on choices that the deciders themselves make. Is this the same circularity as the familiar one about who decides who decides, or a different one, and does it have a different moral? Second, the move from a scalar to a binary: affectedness admits of degree while the franchise normally assigns equal votes, so the threshold looks arbitrary, and resisting that arbitrariness by giving the more-affected more weight seems to break political equality. Is the arbitrariness of the cut a real objection to affectedness as a criterion of inclusion, or is it benign? Third, the diachronic worry: if the constituency is reassembled issue by issue, there may be no persisting group that governs itself over time, and it becomes unclear why someone should regard a decision they lost as one they nonetheless made. Does the idea of self-government actually require a continuing demos, or is that an aesthetic preference for continuity? Fourth, the generality worry: every account of legitimate authority, including non-democratic ones, needs to say whose authority and over whom, so does the boundary problem give a distinctive objection to democracy, or is it a problem democracy inherits rather than one it creates?

If you can, rank the four worries by how damaging you take them to be, say which of them the variable franchise is meant to solve and whether it does, and flag anything important that all four of them are missing.
