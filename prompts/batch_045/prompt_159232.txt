So I've got two friends who've been arguing about the absent-minded driver for something like two years, and I want to force them to produce something other than another round of the same three moves. Write the joint statement they could both sign.

Setup, so we're all using the same numbers: a driver leaves a bar and hits a highway with two indistinguishable intersections. He can't tell them apart and doesn't remember passing the first. Exiting at the first pays 0 (bad motel), exiting at the second pays 4 (home), going straight at both pays 1 (sleeping in the car at the end of the road). He picks a continuation probability p. Ex ante that's 4p − 3p², topped out at p = 2/3 with value 4/3. But at an intersection, if he infers his location from his own plan he gets 3/5 on being at the first one, and re-optimizing from there points to continuing with something like p = 1/3 (roughly half, depending on how you handle the belief), which looks better by his lights at the intersection (~1.8 vs ~1.6) while averaging 1 per trip ex ante — i.e. worse by the only number that actually aggregates. If you get different numbers than mine, redo the arithmetic and use yours, but say you did.

The two camps, as neutrally as I can put them:

- The Planners: the unit of choice is a strategy chosen once, at the bar; the answer is the ex-ante optimum (continue with 2/3); what happens at the intersection is the execution of a plan, not a fresh decision, since he learns nothing there; the pull toward 1/3 is an artifact of aggregating two visits wrongly, and the fact that he'd talk himself out of the plan is a fact about his psychology, not a reason.
- The Sequentialists: the unit of choice is the action at the intersection; the answer has to be something the driver would ratify when he's actually there, with the belief he actually has there, and the ex-ante number isn't available to an agent who can't commit — so the answer is a fixed point of some kind, not the ex-ante optimum.

Deliverable: a numbered agreed-claims section (things both can sign: about the arithmetic, about what counts as a decision situation, about what the perfect-recall version of the case gives, about what a commitment device would do), then a disagreements table with columns for the question, the Planner's phrasing of their own view, the Sequentialist's phrasing of theirs, whether it's a genuine crux or merely verbal, a concrete case or computation both agree bears on it, and each side's prediction about that case, written down in advance.

Cruxes I want covered, at minimum: (1) which evaluation point is normative; (2) what the driver's self-locating belief at the intersection is — the 1/(1+p) you get from your own plan, a flat 1/2, or something undefined — and whether betting behavior at the intersection (bets offered at each intersection separately vs one bet at the bar) settles it; (3) whether the intersection-relative expected payoff double-counts the path on which he visits both; (4) whether the residual dispute is really about commitment and self-control rather than about belief or aggregation; (5) whether imperfect recall is a coherent decision problem at all or a pathology that shows up only at zero information.

Cases I want used as tests, with predictions attached: the fleet version (a thousand fresh amnesiac drivers, or one driver doing it a thousand nights; realized average 4/3 vs 1 — both sides agree on the arithmetic, so the disagreement has to be about its force); the dimmer-switch version, where a lamp at each intersection is a noisy signal of which intersection it is, with signal quality dialed continuously from 0 to 1 — does the Sequentialist recommendation approach the Planner's smoothly as the signal improves, or is there a jump, and what would each side count as a surprise; the three-intersection version, as a stress test of whether each method generalizes and whether the Sequentialist's fixed point still exists and is unique; and the co-driver version, where a passenger with perfect recall and no stake in the outcome can be handed the wheel — both sides agree this changes the answer, so the fight is about what it shows.

Two more things: say explicitly which parts of their dispute you think are merely verbal (I suspect at least one is about "what should he do" meaning different things — advice to a deliberating agent vs a verdict on outcomes — and one is about "belief" as betting odds vs as input to action); and don't let this collapse into causal vs evidential decision theory, since both camps can be card-carrying causalists. Finally, for each camp, write down what would actually make them switch, not what their opponents say should.
