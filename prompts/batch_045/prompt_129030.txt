I spent my career in sensory neurophysiology and have come to philosophy of mind only since retiring, so I expect I am misreading something. My understanding of heterophenomenology is this: the experimenter records what subjects say about their experience but suspends judgment on whether the items described really have the properties ascribed to them; the reports are treated as texts out of which one constructs the subject's world, which is then to be correlated with third-person data. My difficulty comes from a small auditory detection study I still help with. On catch trials, where no tone is presented, a few listeners report a fully externalized tone — out in the room rather than in the head — and two of them, run on the same apparatus in the same session, describe it in incompatible ways: one as a steady tone at a fixed place, the other as a warbling tone that moved. If the stimulus log already establishes that there was no tone, and if the method declines to credit what reports say, I cannot see what the reports add: should they not be discarded as verbal noise, with the button presses explained on their own? And if the method cannot tell me which of the two listeners was right, what is it a method for? I would be grateful for an account of where my reading goes wrong, and of what a heterophenomenologist would in fact do with reports of this kind.
