so I've ended up somewhere weird on this virtual objects / virtual harms business and I want to know how exposed the position actually is. My view, in short: virtual objects are real, genuinely and not as a metaphor, but virtual harms are almost never harms *to* the virtual thing — they're setbacks to a person's projects, and the avatar or the item is just where the setback lands. What pushed me there is what I call the answer-back test: a sword in an online game answers back. It resists, it degrades with use, it can be stolen, and what happens to it depends in fine-grained detail on what I do, whereas a sword in a novel does exactly nothing no matter what I do. So the novel's sword is a description and the game's sword is a thing, and I think that's a real metaphysical difference, not a difference in how attached people get. But notice the answer-back test only earns me the object. It doesn't earn me a bearer of harm. When someone deletes my sword I'm not harmed *for the sword's sake* any more than I'm harmed for my job's sake when I'm fired — though the firing can wreck me just as thoroughly.

What I want from you is what would count against this, ranked fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to separate what a defender of my view should concede from what only critics say. Some of the levers I've already poked at, tell me which of them actually bite:

- The copy problem. Most virtual items can be duplicated at near-zero cost, and there's no settled fact of the matter whether the item in the backup and the item after the restore are the same item. If you don't get an object without a criterion of identity for it, maybe I never had objects at all — but then I seem to be committed to saying a symphony isn't an object either, which I don't want.
- The sealed-world case: single-player, procedurally generated, never streamed, no community, no one will ever know the item existed. If social uptake is doing the real work in my argument, my view should go quiet here, and I'm not sure it does.
- The price-tag lever. If I say reality comes from uptake and uptake comes from other people, then turning on real-money trading should make the very same bit-pattern *more real*. That feels ridiculous to me, but I can't say why.
- The recomputed item: an item that is regenerated from a seed every frame and never stored anywhere. Indistinguishable to the player from a stored one. Does my view have to say its metaphysical status flips on an implementation detail?
- Server shutdown and drift into fiction: the world dies, ten years later it's a wiki entry and some memories. Does my view predict objects quietly converting into fictional objects over time, and is that a feature or a collapse?

Also, mabe more importantly: is this view even risky? If "harm" just means setback to a project, then anything anyone cares about counts and nothing could ever count against me — so tell me honestly whether I've qualified it into unfalsifiability, and whether that's fatal or fine for a view of this kind. Say too what it predicts that its nearest rivals don't (the full realists who think avatars can be harmed themselves, and the error theorists who think virtual objects are just props in a fiction), and which rival I'm in most danger of collapsing into.

The case I'd personally find most moving, if you can make it work against me: an AI-driven character in a game that no player controls and no other player ever encounters, which gets deleted. I have no player whose project is set back, no community whose uptake confers reality — and yet my gut says something bad happened there. Tell me whether that gut is evidence against my view or just a confusion I should get over, and what I'd have to give up to accommodate it.
