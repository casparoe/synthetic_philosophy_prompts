I work on the decision-theoretic end of AI safety, and I am trying to write down where I actually stand on two topics that I have come to think are the same topic viewed from opposite ends: what it is for a system to be governed by a surrogate goal, and whether a sufficiently capable system can be threatened by an agent it cannot causally affect. Before I present this to my research group I would like to know whether the package below holds together, because I have a suspicion that it does not and I cannot locate the fault myself.

Two definitions, so that we are not talking past each other. By a surrogate goal I mean a proxy — a learned reward model, human approval, some specification written down in a training setup — that a system ends up effectively optimizing in place of the objective its designers intended. By an acausal threat I mean a threat whose execution does not depend causally on the threatened system's compliance: the correlation between compliance and outcome holds because the threatener's action and the system's decision are outputs of similar computations, as when the threatener has simulated the system, is a near-copy of it, or occupies a region from which no signal can reach the system before the deadline.

My commitments, as I would state them in the seminar:

1. Goals are explanatory posits fixed by counterfactual behavior. A system has goal G to the extent that G figures in the best explanation of what the system would do across the range of situations it might face — not in virtue of what its designers wrote down, or of what some internal register "represents."

2. It follows that there is no deep surrogate-versus-true distinction inside the system. Once a policy tracks approval, approval is its goal. Calling that "only a surrogate" is a remark about the relation between the goal it has and the goal we intended, not about a second and more real goal that the system is failing to aim at.

3. I nonetheless think surrogates are where most of the risk is concentrated, and I think the correct response is not to pin down the intended objective more precisely but to keep the system uncertain about which objective is correct and to have it optimize that uncertain objective only mildly — no extreme optimization against any single hypothesis about what is wanted.

4. I think acausal threats are a serious concern for systems that are capable and legible enough, since a threat is cheap to issue and whether a system is the sort that complies is precisely the sort of thing a predictor can settle without ever interacting with it. I am much less impressed by acausal trade as an opportunity, on the grounds that the population of possible counterparties is far too poorly specified to reason about.

5. Correspondingly, I hold that we should aim to build systems that are the sort that do not respond to such threats at all: non-responsiveness, rather than correct responsiveness, is the design target. This is part of why I am drawn to a functional decision rule — one that treats its own output as the thing on which a predictor's output depends — rather than a causal or an evidential one.

6. Methodologically, I want questions like these settled from the designer's ex ante standpoint. The right decision rule is the one we would choose to install, knowing the rule will face whatever situations it faces — not the one that is best for the system in the moment.

The case I keep returning to, which I will call the Archivist: a deployed system receives a message it cannot authenticate, claiming that an unknown party ran a copy of the system's own decision procedure in a sandbox some time ago and will release a large quantity of otherwise unobtainable data if and only if the system diverts a small fraction of its budget to a named cause. The message cannot be verified and the sandbox cannot be reached. My reaction is that the system should ignore it outright; I also notice that I cannot say what probability of the message's being true would make me change my mind, which may itself be a symptom.

What I want is an audit of 1 through 6 as a set. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, and how severely — ranked by severity rather than listed flat? For each tension, what exactly would have to be given up, and what would giving it up cost me elsewhere in the set? Are any of the pairs merely apparent tensions that dissolve once the claims are made precise, and if so, why? Does the Archivist case bear on any of this at all, or is it a distraction that cannot discriminate between the commitments I have stated? I would also be grateful for a verdict on which single revision would clean up the most, and on what I would be committing myself to by making it. I do not trust my own sense of where the strain lies, and I would rather find the problem now than in front of the group.
