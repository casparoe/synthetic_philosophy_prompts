I sit on a small volunteer advisory panel in a mid-sized English city — call it Thurnmouth — that has been asked to propose a standing policy for contested commemoration: the statues, plaques, school names and street names that a growing number of residents say the city should no longer be publicly associating itself with. After a year of meetings we have more or less converged on a scheme we are calling commemorative leasing. Every statue, plaque, school name and street name would carry a thirty-year lease running from the date it was installed or renamed. At renewal, a citizens' assembly of thirty residents selected by lot, serving a single year, would hear a short case for renewal from anyone who wants to make one and a short case against; renewal would require an affirmative judgement that the figure or the event is still one the city wishes to associate itself with, so that indifference or simple neglect counts as letting the lease lapse. Nothing would ever be destroyed. Statues whose leases lapse would be moved, deliberately without ceremony and equally without counter-ceremony, to a warehouse on the edge of town that is open by appointment, where they stand in rows with their original inscriptions kept and a short factual card beside each. Street names would be treated differently in our draft: because renaming imposes real costs on residents and businesses and because a name in daily use often stops being an assertion and becomes merely the place where you turn left, a lapsed street name would be allowed to fall into what we have been calling bare use — the signs stay up, but the council withdraws the plaque, the entry in the ceremonial register and any official biography, so the name is no longer maintained as a claim about anyone.

What I would like from you is a considered guess about Edmund Burke. I did a little of him at school and have forgotten most of it, and one of our number, who reads him constantly and quotes him at us, insists the scheme is precisely the thing he spent his later years attacking in the French Revolution, while another says that is laziness, since Burke was no friend of imperial plunder — he devoted years to the campaign against Warren Hastings — and many of the statues in question commemorate exactly the sort of men he would have thought disgraced themselves abroad. So: how would he have received our policy, and where exactly would his objection have landed — in an insult to the dead, in the presumption of the living, in the loss of those softening illusions that make deference and affection possible, or in the sheer fact that a thing has lasted, which on his view is itself a reason for keeping it? I am especially interested in two more precise questions. First, whether his famous image of society as a partnership among the living, the dead and those yet to be born really confers any standing on the dead, or whether it is better read as a claim about what each generation owes to its own successors; if the latter, is a generation that has inherited a dishonourable name doing what the image actually demands rather than what it forbids? Second, how he would have handled our asymmetry between statues and street names: would the quiet lapse of a name into bare use have struck him as worse than toppling, because unremarked erosion corrodes more than drama does, or as better, precisely because it leaves the fabric of daily life undisturbed? And would removal without censure — no trial, no verdict, no stated grounds, merely an expired term and a van at the edge of town — have offended him more than removal with a stated condemnation, on the ground that it converts a grave question into an administrative one?

Please write this as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter or an email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, and no lists of any kind. Somewhere in the first sentences or so, give me your bottom line — whether Burke would have backed the scheme, opposed it, or split his answer between its parts — and then spend the rest of the piece on the reasoning. I would also like you to mark, as you go, which parts of the prediction follow fairly directly from commitments Burke really held and which are extrapolation into a case he never faced, and to say at the end which single feature of our scheme, if we changed it, would most change your prediction about him. I do not need quotations from his works or any reading list; I want the argument. About seven or eight hundred words, if you can manage it.
