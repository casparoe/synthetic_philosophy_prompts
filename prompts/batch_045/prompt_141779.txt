My friend and I spent most of last evening arguing about a case, and the argument kept circling back to a maxim he repeated as if it closed the subject: no wrong is done to one who consents — volenti non fit iniuria. The case we began with was this: a company runs a paid medical study in a town where the payment amounts to several months' wages. The consent form describes a serious side effect as rare, when the sponsor's own data put it closer to one in fifty. Participants are told they may withdraw at any time, though withdrawal forfeits the payment. One participant reads the form, does not really grasp what one in fifty means, signs, and is badly harmed by exactly that side effect. He says she consented, so no wrong was done to her. I say that cannot be right, or at any rate cannot be right just like that: the risk she was told about is not the risk she ran, the money is not much of an option to refuse, and she may not have been in a position to understand what she was agreeing to. He replies that I am confusing the question whether the consent was valid with the question what consent does, and that the maxim concerns only the second. But he then used it to conclude that nobody needs to look into how the consent was obtained, and that conclusion looks to me as if it needs the first question after all.

So I want the disambiguation. Please set out, as distinct theses, the different precise claims that maxim could be making — among them, at least:

1. the reading on which it is a tautology about the word consent, and can therefore do no work in our argument at all;
2. the reading on which consent is a normative power that waives or transfers a claim, so that the maxim silently presupposes validity rather than saying anything about it;
3. the reading on which consent is only evidence that no setback occurred, so that the maxim is really a claim about welfare;
4. the reading on which no wrong is done means no wrong of the kind that grounds a complaint or a remedy, rather than nothing wrong happened;
5. the reading on which consent must be consent under a description, so that deception about the nature, the magnitude, or the identity of what is consented to severs the link;
6. the reading on which the maxim requires a theory of voluntariness and therefore a baseline — consent against acquiescence, take-it-or-leave-it offers, threats, and the question whether that baseline is moralized or not;
7. the reading on which only a competent party can consent at all, together with the harder question whether someone whose preferences have themselves been shaped by the arrangement she is consenting to counts as competent in the relevant sense;

and any reading I have missed, including the strongest available version of the one he was probably reaching for.

For each, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or straightforwardly false; whether it is compatible with the thought that consent can be undermined by deception, by pressure, or by incompetence; and — this is the part I actually care about — whether it supports the conclusion he wanted, namely that in a case like this one the way the consent was obtained is nobody's business.

Two moves I kept groping for and could not state precisely, if you can place them on the menu: I wanted to distinguish consenting to a gamble from consenting to the outcome the gamble produces, and I wanted to say that even if consent removes the complaint about the harm, it does not remove the complaint about having been lied to. Tell me where each belongs, what precise form it has to take to be defensible, and whether accepting either of them leaves the maxim idle in precisely the sort of case people cite it in.

A numbered menu I can take back to him, one thesis at a time, with a verdict and a sentence on what it does to our case, would be ideal.
