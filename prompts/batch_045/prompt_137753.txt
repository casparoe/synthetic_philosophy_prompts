The student's answer (about 620 words):

Goodman's puzzle begins with the predicate 'grue'. Something is grue if and only if it is observed before a fixed future time t and green, or not observed before t and blue. 'Bleen' is defined in the parallel way, with blue and green interchanged. Every green emerald observed before t is therefore a confirming instance of both all emeralds are green and all emeralds are grue, since such an emerald satisfies both. The two hypotheses nevertheless disagree: the first predicts that emeralds first observed after t will be green, the second that they will be blue. Our evidence cannot adjudicate between them.

This is not simply Hume's problem. Hume asked what justifies induction at all — why we should expect the unobserved to resemble the observed. Goodman's question has a different shape. It grants, for the sake of argument, that some inductive inferences are in good standing, and asks which ones. Even a fully satisfactory answer to Hume would not tell us why all emeralds are green rather than all emeralds are grue is the hypothesis our evidence supports. The new riddle is thus the problem of distinguishing projectible from unprojectible hypotheses, and it survives any solution to the old one. Goodman's own point of view is the familiar one that generalizations divide into lawlike and accidental ones: all copper conducts electricity can be confirmed by particular pieces of copper, whereas all the men in this room are third sons cannot, and the new riddle is the question of which predicates can appear in lawlike generalizations.

Goodman's answer appeals to entrenchment. 'Green' is projectible and 'grue' is not because 'green' is much better entrenched: it has figured in vastly more successful predictions than 'grue' has, and that superior track record is what licenses projecting it. In effect, Goodman's view is that 'green' earns its projectibility by tracking a genuine natural kind, a real similarity among green things, whereas 'grue' carves things up arbitrarily and answers to no such kind. That is why the grue hypothesis, though compatible with everything we have observed, is not really confirmed by that evidence.

Two consequences follow. First, confirmation is relative to a vocabulary: since 'grue' and 'green' are interdefinable — grue can be defined using green and blue together with a condition on observation time, and green can equally be defined using grue and bleen — there is no objective fact of the matter about which hypothesis the evidence supports. Had we grown up projecting grue and bleen, the grue hypothesis would have been every bit as well confirmed for us as the green hypothesis is for us. Goodman's lesson is therefore skepticism by another route: no prediction about the unobserved is objectively better supported than its deviant rival, and the appearance of a difference is only the shadow of our linguistic habits.

The grell variant adds nothing. 'Grell' is defined by whether an emerald has been chemically assayed rather than by a time, so whether an emerald is grell depends on something we do. But a hypothesis about our own behavior is disqualified as a rival prediction, since predictions concern what the world will do, not what we will do. And in any case we may simply decline to assay emeralds, in which case the grell hypothesis never yields a determinate prediction at all. The variant therefore shows that Goodman's problem is an artefact of his using a temporal predicate, and that once the temporal element is removed the difficulty disappears.
