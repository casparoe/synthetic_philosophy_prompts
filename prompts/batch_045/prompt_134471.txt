I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of biology — 16 students, one 75-minute session, a room with movable chairs, a whiteboard, and no reliable AV, so anything you design should run on printed cards and a stack of tokens. Last time I taught this material as a straight lecture on the etiological (selected-effect) versus causal-role accounts of biological function, the students left convinced that the disagreement was merely verbal: two definitions of one word, interesting only to people who like definitions. What I want instead is for them to *do* something in which the two accounts issue conflicting instructions and they have to pick one before they know there are two. So: design a runnable in-class activity, with no lecture up front and no mention of either account by name until the debrief.

The frame I have in mind is a role-play I've sketched but can't get to work: students are a Certification Board on a terraforming station that maintains a small menagerie of engineered and imported organisms under a fixed maintenance budget. Each part of each organism must be certified CRITICAL, OPTIONAL, or JUNK, and certification determines which parts get the station's limited repair credits when systems degrade; the organism's survival across a few simulated seasons then determines each Board's score. The twist I want exploited is that the organisms come in two kinds — "legacy" lineages with an unbroken four-billion-year selection history, and "assembled" organisms synthesized from a sequence library a few decades ago, with no selection history at all — and that each Board is issued a different "capacity ticket" naming the one system-capacity it is charged with protecting (oxygen uptake, thermoregulation, predator avoidance, reproductive signalling, and so on). Please give me: the materials (a dossier of organism parts, with cases constructed so that history and present causal contribution come apart — e.g. a chamber that was retained for buoyancy and now does gas exchange, a structure that was selected for display and now kills its bearer under a new predator, a vestigial part, a by-product that has become load-bearing, a duplicated part where only one copy has a history); how the groups are constituted and what each knows and doesn't know; the rules and payoff scheme, using tokens rather than money; the timing of any reveals, including a reveal late in the session that a Board's organism has a physically identical twin of the other kind, and a "handover" stage in which the organism is moved to a changed environment and the Board may revise one certification; and the scoring, including any wager you'd have them place on whether their verdicts will change once they learn their organism's provenance. Make the comparison across Boards do real work: same organism under different capacity tickets, and same ticket over legacy versus assembled organisms.

Then give me the debrief: how to move from what actually happened in the room to the two accounts, mapping specific moments and specific parts onto the etiological and causal-role verdicts, and where to let the students name the pattern themselves before you supply the vocabulary. I want them to come away seeing that the accounts are not two definitions of one word but two answers to different questions — one tied to what a part is *supposed* to do and to the possibility of malfunction, the other to how a system's present capacities decompose — and that the tension between them survives even when everyone agrees on all the local facts. Also tell me, honestly, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this: students who collapse function into usefulness-to-us, a Board that refuses its odd capacity ticket and certifies for survival instead, someone who declares the whole thing obviously a matter of both and wants to go home. And be frank about which feature of the dispute the activity cannot reproduce and which I will have to supply myself in the last ten minutes — I suspect the budget framing quietly stacks the deck toward one of the two accounts, and I'd like to know where, and whether to correct for it or make it part of the lesson.
