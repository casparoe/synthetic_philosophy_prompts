I've got a history-department colloquium in three weeks — 25 minutes of paper, then 40 minutes of Q&A, which is the part that actually scares me — and I'm presenting the opening chapter of a book project on germs, climate and volcanoes as historical actors. What I want from you is a prep sheet, not a defense.

The thesis, briefly: non-human causes deserve to be written into narrative as actors, but with a thinner, non-intentional kind of agency than the human sort — what I've been calling menu-setting agency. An epidemic or an eruption doesn't pick the outcome; it decides which options are on the menu to begin with and what each of them costs, and it does that on a different clock than the decisions run on. That last bit is the part I care most about: because the effects arrive out of phase with the decisions, the people living through it (and so the archives) systematically credit the wrong thing. I've been calling that credit-scrambling. My guard against determinism is a double counterfactual rule: I only promote something to actor status if (a) removing it changes what happens and (b) some different human arrangement would also have changed what happens. Insensitive to human choice = determinism, I don't want it. Insensitive to the germ = backdrop, demote it. Two cases carry the argument: Napoleon's expedition to Saint-Domingue, gutted by yellow fever, and Tambora in 1815 producing the crop failures, food riots and migration of 1816.

The room, pretty precisely: a senior cultural historian of the early republic who came up through the linguistic turn and thinks agency needs intentionality and documents, and that anything you can't source is just you; two environmental historians who are on my side in principle but hate the guns-germs-and-steel framing and will want to know who gets off the hook when a volcano is an actor; a physical climate scientist on a joint appointment who does event attribution and will say I can't hang a single harvest on a single eruption without a counterfactual ensemble, and that a global cooling of well under a degree seems too small to bear the weight I'm putting on it; one grad student who reads actor-network theory and will ask why, if microbes get agency, sugar and rats and the soil don't, and what my stopping rule is; and an economic historian who'll want identification — where's the natural experiment, isn't the timing endogenous. The chair is a historian of science who will definitely ask one question about whether "actor" is doing causal work or literary work.

So: give me the objections in roughly the order they'll come, with who raises each, phrased the way that person would phrase it, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are. For each, a two-minute reply, plus one line on where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede in the first two minutes before anyone asks anything, what I should refuse to give up, and which two people in the room are likely to gang up on the same point. Then include the one question I'm hoping nobody asks — and be honest about whether that's the "what does agency buy you that independent variable doesn't" question or the "you have rebuilt environmental determinism with better manners" one, and which of the two I actually can't answer.
