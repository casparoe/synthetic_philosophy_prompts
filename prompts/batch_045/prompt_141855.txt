Bit of background so you know where I'm coming from: I'm in my early seventies, retired a few years ago after a career in experimental physical chemistry — spectroscopy mostly, which meant a lot of my working life was spent arguing about whether a signal was real or an artefact of the instrument. Since retiring I've been going to a small philosophy reading group at the library, four of us, all ex-science. We did a session last month on the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness — the ones showing that you can't satisfy the usual list of group-fairness criteria simultaneously once the groups differ in their base rates — and I came away both delighted and unsatisfied. Delighted because it has exactly the shape of a no-go theorem in physics: the interesting move isn't picking a winner, it's asking which assumption you're prepared to drop. Unsatisfied because everything we read was organised as here are the criteria, here's the theorem, here's a clever fix, and I still can't see the shape of the space of positions.

So: I want to make a one-page handout for the group — a decision tree / taxonomy of positions on what to do when your score can't satisfy all the criteria at once. Start everyone from one shared commitment we can all grant: there is a model outputting a score, two groups, different base rates, and the score is used to allocate something that matters (parole, a transplant slot, a loan, a follow-up screening). Then branch.

What I want, concretely:

1. Build it from the logical space, not from a list of names. Give me the branching questions and the combinations of answers they allow first; only then place the actual positions into that space.
2. Every branch node should be a question my group can answer without knowing where it leads — 'do you accept X about this case?', never 'are you a Y-ist?'. Use concrete cases wherever you can: the parole board, the hospital waiting list, the audit queue.
3. Leaves I definitely want: fairness through blindness; keep the score calibrated and live with unequal error rates; equalize the error rates and accept that the score means different things in the two groups; use randomization or a lottery so criteria hold in expectation; scrap the score and change the intervention instead (the it's-the-world-not-the-model response); treat the whole thing as a measurement failure about what the target variable even is; and the meta-position that the impossibility results are really a reductio of the criteria themselves. Merge or add as you see fit, but say what you're doing and why.
4. One case I want every leaf forced to answer: the very same score is used to decide how much scrutiny someone gets — more audits, more police attention, more follow-up tests — rather than who receives a benefit. I have a feeling that flips some positions and leaves others completely untouched, and I want to see which is which.
5. My own hobby horse, and I'd rather you placed it than humoured it. In spectroscopy, if your error budget is dominated by the instrument rather than the sample, post-processing the numbers is theatre; the honest move is to say the measurement can't yet support the decision and go get a better instrument. I've been calling the analogue here instrumental sufficiency: before asking whether a score is fair, ask whether its residual error is mostly noise in how the outcome was recorded, and if it is, the right response is refusal to deploy — not reweighting, not a cleverer criterion. Is that a genuine extra leaf, or does it collapse into the construct-validity one? If it collapses, say so and tell me what I'm missing.
6. For each leaf: one line on what that position has to swallow, and one line on what kind of empirical result would actually count as evidence against it.
7. Flag the empty regions: answer combinations your questions permit that nobody actually defends. For each, say whether it's incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored and unmarketed.
8. Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really separate, rather than quietly pretending the final question did the work.

Size and style: about six or seven branch questions, eight to twelve leaves, one page if you can manage it. Keep notation light and define any criterion name in half a line the first time you use it — we're scientifically literate but none of us are statisticians, and two of the group will just stop reading if a formula shows up.

And please, no reading lists, no who-proved-what-in-what-year, no citations: we've got no library access worth speaking of and honestly I cant tell a seminal paper from a footnote anymore. If you think the whole framing is wrong — if a taxonomy is just the wrong instrument for this debate — say that in a sentence or two at the top, and then build it anyway. Its been nagging at me for weeks and I want to see it on paper.
