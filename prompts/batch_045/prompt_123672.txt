I spent my career in the laboratory — molecular biology, mostly — and now sit in on a graduate seminar in moral philosophy, where I keep wanting to handle arguments the way I used to handle experiments. Please act as a professional moral philosopher with a real taste for careful case design, and build me a matched pair of cases on the shallow-pond argument: a treatment and a control.

What the pair has to do. Condition A is the control: Singer's principle — roughly, that if we can prevent something bad from happening without sacrificing anything of comparable moral significance, we ought to do it — and a commonsense morality that permits partiality to oneself and one's near and dear, treats large sacrifices as supererogatory, and holds negative duties more strictly than positive ones, both return the verdict "you must help." In Condition B the commonsense verdict flips to "you may walk on," while the principle still returns "you must help." The two conditions may differ in exactly one feature — a feature the principle, as stated, does not mention — and must be identical in everything the principle does advert to: the same bad outcome, the same certainty that it will occur and that the agent can prevent it, the same genuinely trivial cost to the agent, the same physical distance, no special relationship, no one else present or able to help, and no causal responsibility on the agent's part.

Cheating. The flip must not be manufactured by any of the usual suspects: greater distance; larger numbers, thresholds, or aggregation; doubt about whether the aid arrives; the agent's own projects or relationships; some other agent or institution whose job it is; or the mere fact that people feel less about distant sufferers. Name the feature you have chosen and show that it is not one of these in disguise. Also say plainly what the pair is for — I want to know whether the pull of the pond case comes from its moral structure or from something about how it is presented, and that fixes what would count as rigging it.

Constraints. Realistic: a hospital, a field station, a funding or hiring committee, a shared lab — something a department head could actually face. No trolleys, no science fiction, no thought-experiment machinery. One paragraph per condition.

Deliverable. Both conditions; then the verdict of each of the two views in each condition, with the reasoning that gets there; then the diagnostics — which single feature does the work, what happens to the divergence when you remove it, whether the effect survives describing the conditions in the opposite order or to someone who has never met the shallow-pond argument (if it does not, say so; that is a finding, not a failure), and how a defender of whichever view comes out worse would attack the case — in particular whether your feature is a moral difference or an artifact of framing.

Before settling, spend a few lines brainstorming two or three candidate features and discarding the ones that turn out to be forbidden levers in disguise. I would rather have one honest, fully diagnosed pair than three that merely look clever.
