I recently joined the advisory board of a small foundation that is spending down the last large pot of money it will ever control, and I have been handed the job of shaping one of its final programs. The pot is modest — roughly eight funded projects, two to four years each, starting next year — and the mandate is deliberately narrow: conceptual work only (ethics, political philosophy, formal and conceptual analysis), no engineering, no empirical policy research, no advocacy. The output is supposed to be the kind of thing that could actually be read by the people who will draft the founding charter for the first permanent, demographically self-sufficient off-Earth settlement — a few thousand people, perhaps within fifteen or twenty years. My degree was in philosophy but that was years ago and I have not kept up with anything, so I am asking for help setting the agenda rather than proposing one: given that audience, that budget, and that timeline, which open questions in the ethics and governance of permanent settlement should the program prioritize, in what order, and by what criteria?

To make the question less abstract, here are the candidates I keep circling, though I do not trust my own ordering and would rather you reorganize, merge, or discard them than merely comment on them. (1) The ratchet: a charter written while the settlement is small, dependent on resupply, and demographically skewed will be very hard to amend later, so one generation's constitutional choices may lock in institutions for centuries — what does that do to the usual apparatus of consent, exit, and amendment, given that exit is physically possible but economically and medically close to meaningless for most residents? (2) Reproduction at population scale: viability arguably requires something like a managed fertility floor, and any heritable adaptation to local gravity or radiation — genetic, pharmaceutical, or otherwise — would be chosen for people who cannot consent and who would not otherwise exist; I suspect this is the non-identity problem wearing a spacesuit, but I cannot tell whether that makes it a tired old problem or a genuinely new one. (3) The fragility of the insurance argument: nearly every defense of settlement I hear bottoms out in reducing extinction risk, and I am unclear how much of that case survives the observation that an early settlement is a correlated asset rather than an independent one, drawing on the same industrial base, the same supply chains, and arguably the same failure modes. (4) The museum question: is there any non-instrumental obligation to leave some off-Earth environments untouched, and how does it trade against the value of a second home for a species that currently has exactly one? (5) Who counts: rotating crew, long-term residents, children born there, and remote operators or owners who never leave Earth but control most of the physical capital — what is the right way to think about membership when presence, stake, and control come apart like that?

Two things I would like you to be blunt about. First, I suspect the two questions that dominate public discussion are overrated for this particular program: whether the money would be better spent on Earth strikes me as a question about the foundation's overall allocation rather than about settlement, and the framing of settlement as colonialism with better public relations strikes me as delivering a verdict without producing anything a charter drafter could use. Tell me if I am wrong, and either way say what criterion demotes or retains them. Second, for each question you keep, I want the reasoning behind its rank: what it is upstream of, whether it is tractable by people doing armchair and formal work over a few years rather than by demographers or aerospace engineers, whether it is neglected or already crowded, and how legible its output would be to someone actually drafting institutions. Then tell me which two or three items on my list you would cut, what is missing that I have not thought of, and what new evidence — a settlement actually reaching self-sufficiency, a shift in the treaty framework governing off-Earth activity, something else — would most change your ordering. I do not want a survey of the literature and I do not want citations; I want a defended agenda I could put in front of a board.
