I hold a position I want stress-tested rather than defended. Call it the calibration argument about intuitions, and let it have two steps.

The first step is the demographic-variation argument in its familiar form. Across several families of cases that analytic philosophy has leaned on heavily — intuitions about reference for proper names, knowledge attributions in Gettier-style cases, ascriptions of intentional action in the side-effect cases — subjects' answers shift systematically with cultural and linguistic background, with socioeconomic position, and with other variables that no one on any side of those debates takes to be relevant to whether the claim in question is true. If intuitions were even moderately reliable indicators of the property they are supposed to indicate, this pattern would be surprising. And since the method that consults them has no independent track record against which to score them, the pattern is best explained as unreliability.

The second step is my own addition, and it is where I expect the trouble. I think the first step generalizes further than its usual defenders take it, in two directions. First, variation of the same shape appears across within-subject and presentation-level variables: the order in which cases are encountered, whether a case is presented verbally or in writing, which cases surround it, framings that look immaterial, and features of the questioner. Second, I think the experimenters are inside the scope of their own argument: the judgments that a subject understood the case as intended, that a translation preserved the intended reading, and that the data show variation in intuition rather than variation in what was being asked — all of these rest on the experimenters' own case-level intuitions. So my actual position is not that demographics debunk intuitions. It is that intuitions are uncalibrated instruments and that nobody in this neighborhood has an error model for them.

Tell me what would count against this. I'd like, in particular:

1. Defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with the ranking defended: which observations, arguments, or formal results should actually move me, and which should I be licensed to shrug off?
2. An answer to the in-kind question. Is there a principled distinction between demographic variation and the other kinds I lumped in with it — order effects, presentation format, framing, questioner effects? If there is one, state it sharply enough that I could test it. If there isn't, say so and say what that costs me. My suspicion is that defenders of the first step rely on such a distinction without stating it, and I want to know whether it can be made out.
3. The measurement-model escape, which I expect to be the strongest objection and the one I'd personally find most persuasive if it can be made concrete. A noisy or biased instrument is still usable if you can characterize its error: run it against known quantities, model the drift, correct in aggregate. What would a working error model for philosophical intuitions have to look like, what would we need in order to build one, and how much of the first step's sting could it draw? If the calibration framing turns out to help my opponents more than it helps me, I want that said plainly.
4. What each side would concede. I don't want only critics' objections. Which of these defeaters would someone who accepts the first step but not the second grant as genuinely counting against them? And, honestly, is there a version of my position that survives every defeater you can construct — a retreat to something like intuitions being defeasible evidence pending calibration?
5. An exposure assessment. Has this argument been qualified into unfalsifiability? If the second step is doing most of the work, is that because it is right or because it is vague? And if it has become hard to test, is that a defect in a methodological argument of this kind, or the appropriate posture for one?

Please argue from the cases and from the structure of the argument rather than from the literature: assume no access to it, and so no study names, no bibliographies, nothing that turns on who found what or when. Where a premise rests on an empirical claim, say what the claim is and how exposed it is to being false. One thing I will not count as a defeater, unless you can say why it isn't merely dialectical: the observation that we have nothing better to go on than intuitions anyway.
