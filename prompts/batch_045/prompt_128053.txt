I write about medicine and ideas, and I am finishing a piece on a case I cannot stop turning over. At an academic hospital I will leave unnamed, support was withdrawn from a patient with a catastrophic brain injury; her heart stopped; after the interval its protocol specifies — long enough, everyone agrees, that the heart would not have restarted on its own — she was declared dead by circulatory criteria, and two kidneys and a liver were recovered. Her family is suing. Their expert, a neurointensivist, walked me through an argument that I have since reconstructed, numbered below so you can point at a step. Every step looks sound to me. The conclusion looks like something nobody could believe. I would be grateful for a diagnosis.

One thing I am setting aside: the family's lawyers are mainly arguing that the withdrawal rather than the injury caused her death, and that a hospital which withdraws support and then recovers organs brings about what it foresees. Assume, as the hospital argues, that withdrawal merely stopped doing something that was never going to work, and that the injury is what killed her. My argument does not depend on that dispute.

(1) At the moment of the declaration her blood had stopped moving, but the oxygen in it had been loaded minutes earlier, her cells were still respiring, and — the part I keep coming back to — had a resuscitation team walked in and started compressions at that moment, there is a real chance her circulation would have returned, and a non-trivial chance that her brain would have been perfused again in time to matter.

(2) The reason no team walked in is that a decision had been made that no team would walk in: a valid order not to resuscitate, a decision to withdraw, a protocol that keeps the recovery team out of the room until after the declaration. Had those decisions gone the other way, she would not have been dead at the moment she was declared dead.

(3) So the criterion actually applied was not irreversibility — her circulation was not beyond restoration — but permanence: it would not be restored, because no one was going to restore it.

(4) Permanence is not a property of a body alone. It is a property of a body together with a set of decisions and the policies that license them. The same heart in the same state is permanently arrested in a unit where support is being withdrawn and not permanently arrested in an emergency department where it is being worked.

(5) But whether a person is dead cannot depend on what anyone has decided, or on which unit they are lying in. Two bodies in identical states cannot be differently dead. Death is either going on in a person or it is not.

(6) So, if (5) is right, the declaration was false, and every recovery in a donation-after-circulatory-determination-of-death program is a recovery of organs from someone who was not dead — which makes those programs, in the only sense that matters to the families I have interviewed, programs of killing.

(7) The repair is obvious: go back to irreversibility. A person is dead when the function in question cannot be restored, not merely when it will not be restored.

(8) But "cannot" is hostage to what medicine can do, and that boundary has moved repeatedly — defibrillation, cardiopulmonary resuscitation, extracorporeal support, protocols that return people from long arrests in cold water. The peg for my piece is a reperfusion protocol — a supposition for now, though the researchers I interview say something like it is a decade off — that would restore, in a human brain an hour after circulatory arrest, integrated activity of the kind our tests treat as the activity of a living brain. If it ever works, then the patients declared dead by neurological criteria this year were not dead either, or at any rate not irreversibly so: what killed them was not the destruction of their brains but the poverty of their era's technology, which is not a fact about them at all. And the same then follows for every death ever declared: each becomes revisable by later invention.

(9) So neither criterion can do the work. Permanence makes death a matter of policy; irreversibility makes it a matter of what happens to be technically possible, and retroactively at that. There is no fact of the matter about when anyone dies — only local decisions about when to stop, described in biological language.

What makes me suspect a bad step rather than merely a premise I dislike is that the argument proves too much. Nothing in it turns on organ donation, or on circulatory criteria as opposed to neurological ones. Run it on brain death, and the judgment that a destroyed brain will not recover also rests on a view about what is worth attempting. Run it on an unattended death at home, where no one attempts resuscitation, and the same reasoning goes through. If the argument is sound, no death has ever been declared for a reason.

So: which step do you reject, and why did it look like a platitude when I wrote it down? I want a verdict rather than a survey. Name the step, explain what it was trading on, and tell me what rejecting it commits me to — including what it commits me to about the donation programs, which is the sentence in my piece that I am most afraid of getting wrong. If you think the argument is valid and I should simply give up a premise, tell me which, and tell me what else I lose with it. Given the choice, I would rather keep the claim that these programs are not programs of killing than the claim that death is a purely biological event — but I would like to know the price of each, and whether I can have both, and if I can, which of my steps was the counterfeit. Please skip the literature: I cannot follow citations, and a list of who has said what would not settle this. What I need is the seam.
