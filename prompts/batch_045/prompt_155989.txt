I'm in a small final-year seminar on self-knowledge, I'm meant to be writing a term paper on exactly this, and I keep circling back to a move my tutor made in the first session and has leaned on ever since. She presented self-deception as literally the same phenomenon as deceiving another person, with the two roles occupied inside a single head: one part of the agent stands to the rest of the agent as deceiver to deceived. She then used that mapping to argue for a dilemma — either the mind really is partitioned, with a subsystem that retains the true belief, forms the intention to plant a false one, and works on the rest of the agent, or else "self-deception" is only a façon de parler for wishful thinking, denial, or selective attention, and nothing literally deceptive is going on at all. What bothers me is that the dilemma seems to be carried entirely by the analogy: if the mapping is loose in the right places, both horns may turn out to be optional, and I can't tell whether she has an argument that survives independently of the picture.

The features I suspect the analogy quietly imports are these: that the deceiver's true belief is stable and stays available to him throughout; that deceiver and victim occupy different evidential positions, so the victim's false belief needs no motive to explain it; that there is a single intention formed before the deception and then executed on a comparatively passive target; and that success is a product state, the victim simply ending up with the false belief. In the one-person case all four look as though they would have to be manufactured by the very process that exploits them, which is roughly why people separate a "static" puzzle — how can one mind hold that p and that not-p at once? — from a "dynamic" one — how could the deceiver's plan succeed when the victim is itself and must not catch on? When I said this in seminar I floated a rival analogy: self-deception is less like lying to a friend than like what a defence attorney does when she would rather not know whether her client did it. She plants no false belief; she declines to commission the one test that would settle the question, reweights the evidence she already has, and can sincerely report that she does not know. My tutor's reply was that this is merely motivated ignorance rather than deception, and so doesn't touch her argument.

I'd like to test that reply against a case I invented. A composer learns that a review of her new opera has appeared in a journal she subscribes to. She never opens it, and this is not pretend; when a friend offers to summarise it she changes the subject; over about six months she begins citing the silence of the press as mild evidence that the piece went down well, and by the end she believes and says that it got a good press. Asked at month three whether she thinks the review was hostile, she says truthfully that she has no idea, and if you finally made her read it she would be surprised and distressed. Please audit the interpersonal analogy against this case: give me an explicit table of correspondences — what plays the deceiver, the victim, the true belief, the false belief, the intention, the lie, the discovery — then sort the disanalogies into those that bear on my tutor's dilemma and those that are merely cosmetic, and say plainly what the analogy imports that the single-agent case lacks. Then say whether the dilemma survives: does the analogy really force partition-or-nothing, or is there a third option it is structurally unable to see, something like the "twisted" case in which a false belief is the by-product of motivated bias rather than the object of an intention? If my attorney analogy is the better one, say what it predicts about the composer that the interpersonal one does not — is she self-deceived at month three as well as at month six; does her distress on finally reading the review show a retained true belief, or only rapid updating from new evidence? And flag anywhere the disagreement between my tutor and me is really verbal: about "deception", about "intention", or about how much a state has to behave like a belief in order to count as one.
