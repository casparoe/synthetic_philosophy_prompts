I would like to know what a very ordinary academic practice commits us to about causation, because the more I press on it the less I can see how its parts fit together.

Start with the house style of causal explanation in history as it is actually taught and examined. In the standard essay — why did a general European war break out in 1914? — a strong answer names several distinct causes: the alliance system, the naval and land armaments competition, the timetables baked into mobilisation plans, imperial rivalry, Balkan nationalism, domestic pressure on particular governments. A candidate who names one cause well is marked down for narrowness. Breadth is rewarded. And nowhere in the rubric is the student asked what would have happened had any one of these been absent; the counterfactual gesture, where it appears at all, is ornament.

Now set that beside a second feature of the same discipline: counterfactual history is a pariah genre. The "what if" book is reviewed as entertainment rather than scholarship, and graduate training steers students away from it. This looks inconsistent to me, and I say that as someone who has spent years around people who draw causal diagrams, score forecasts, and reach for the do-operator whenever the word "cause" appears. Asking why the war came in 1914 looks like asking for the difference-makers, and the only machinery I know for cashing out "difference-maker" is counterfactual. So either the two practices rest on different conceptions of causation, or the taboo is not really about causation at all.

The case I care about is where they come apart sharply. Plenty of serious accounts of 1914 are, in effect, overdetermination stories: two or three processes running in parallel, any one of which, had it run alone, would plausibly have produced a general war within a few years. If that is the right shape of the story, then on a strict but-for view none of the items on the essay list is a cause, and a rubric that demands several causes cannot be read as asking for necessary conditions. It looks instead as if it is quietly presupposing something like an INUS-style account: a factor that is not sufficient alone and not necessary overall, but is a non-redundant part of some sufficient combination.

The device I have been using to make this precise, and which is where I stop being able to see my way, is a kind of causal bookkeeping. For each candidate cause I try to assign a weight by asking how much of the outcome's probability rides on it: how far the probability of war drops if I intervene to remove that factor alone, and how far it rises if I add that factor alone to an otherwise quiet baseline. Then I demand that the weights across causes add up to something coherent — that causal weight behave like a quantity that is conserved and shared out, the way the essay's structure implicitly assumes when it divides its marks evenly between causes.

This works cleanly when causes are independent contributors pushing on a threshold, and it breaks in a specific way under overdetermination: each cause looks like a full cause on its own, the weights sum to more than the event, and there is no non-arbitrary way to renormalise them without importing a theory I have not stated. I want to know whether that breakage is a bug in my device or a discovery about the concept.

So, concretely:

1. Reconstruct the implicit theory of causation behind the multi-cause essay rubric. What must a cause be for "name several, and be penalised for naming one" to be a well-posed demand? Does the practice strictly require an INUS-style account, or would a difference-making account, a mechanistic account, or a pluralist one license it equally well?

2. What does the taboo on counterfactual history presuppose? Distinguish the metaphysical reading (there are no facts about what never happened) from the professional-evidential reading (historians may assert only what their evidence disciplines, and counterfactuals are not answerable to evidence). If it is the latter, is the taboo actually in tension with the rubric at all, or does the appearance of inconsistency come from conflating the two?

3. Under genuine overdetermination, is there a fact of the matter about how much each cause contributed, or is causal weight underdetermined by everything that happened? If the latter, what would a rubric — or a piece of historical writing — that took overdetermination seriously have to change? Would it have to stop asking "why" in the singular, and would it have to stop allocating credit and blame in the way that commemoration, centenary journalism, and the search for the man who "started" the war all do?

4. Finally, what would history look like if written under a thoroughly interventionist or probabilistic view: a history of propensities and distributions of outcomes rather than of the one sequence that obtained? Would the question "why did the war come in 1914?" survive as a well-formed question, or would it dissolve into a set of claims about conditional probabilities, and does anything we use history for — assigning responsibility, drawing lessons, deciding what to commemorate — depend on the question surviving?
