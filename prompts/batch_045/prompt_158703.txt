I should say at the outset that I have no academic background — I work in freight logistics and do most of my reading on the train — so if the tangle I've got into has a settled name in the literature, I neither know it nor need to know it. What I want is the argument, not the reading. Please don't suggest books, papers or authors, and don't cite anyone: I can go and find those myself, and I'd rather the piece stand on reasoning than on who is meant to have said what.

The question I keep coming back to is whether the shift from foraging to farming was a mistake. I mean the original Neolithic transitions, wherever they happened independently, and not modern agriculture as it happens to be run now.

My brother-in-law and I have been arguing about this for about two years and we have reached the stage of repeating ourselves. He is the optimist about farming; I am the sceptic. Neither of us is a fool, and I'd like a dialogue that grants that. So: write me one, around 900 words, between two people who have been arguing about whether the Neolithic transition was a mistake. Both should be intelligent and well-informed in the way a determined non-specialist can be, and neither should be there to be knocked over. They should genuinely answer each other: when one of them makes a point, the other should either meet it head-on or concede the part that works and explain what is left standing. I'd like each of them to give ground at least once on something they asserted early on, and I'd like at least one moment where they catch themselves using the same word to mean two different things. Please keep technical vocabulary out of it — if a character reaches for a technical move, they should explain it in plain terms as they go. British English throughout.

Here is where we've got stuck, and these are the moves I want made and then answered:

The affluence point. The case for the foragers is roughly that the working week was short, the diet varied, mobility kept infectious disease and waste in check, and there was little to store, defend or inherit — so the usual story of farming as progress has to explain why anyone would take up a form of life that was longer on hours, shorter on stature and narrower on food.

The ratchet. My brother-in-law's reply is that none of this touches the point, because the transition was a one-way door: once a valley supports ten times the people on grain, you cannot go back to foraging without most of those people dying, and nobody at any stage was ever in a position to choose the whole path at once. My complaint is that this sounds less like a defence of farming than like a description of a trap. I want them to fight over whether irreversibility excuses the outcome or makes it worse, and over whether a transition no one could have chosen as a whole can be called a mistake at all.

The wrinkle I can't get past. As far as I can see, almost everyone alive now exists only because farming happened; the specific people who would have existed on the forager path are not us. So when I ask "was it a mistake?", I can't identify who it was a mistake for — the ones who paid for it are long dead and were not us, and we cannot compare our lot against not existing. My brother-in-law says this dissolves the question entirely. I say it doesn't, but I can't say why cleanly. I want this argument made properly on both sides: the strongest version of the thought that the question has lost its subject, and the strongest reply that it survives anyway — whether by moving to what the people at the time had reason to do, or to some impersonal judgement about what came after, or somewhere else I haven't thought of. If one of them wants to raise the domesticates — that a handful of species did spectacularly well out of it, and that how you count depends on whether you are counting individuals, populations or species — fine, but it shouldn't be used to dodge the question about human lives.

Finally, after the dialogue, add a short note of no more than 200 words in your own voice: which of the two you think has the better of the exchange, the single consideration that would most plausibly lead you to change that verdict, and where each character's argument is most likely to fail. If there is any empirical claim in the piece you are not reasonably confident about — about skeletons, hours worked, population densities — say so there rather than quietly asserting it.
