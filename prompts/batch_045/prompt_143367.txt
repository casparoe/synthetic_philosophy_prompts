Hey — I need a Q&A prep sheet, and I need it for the room I'm actually walking into on Thursday, not for the version of this argument that lives on philosophy twitter.

The setup: I'm a 4th-year PhD student, and I'm giving a 25-minute talk plus 20 minutes of questions at a hospital neuroethics grand rounds. Not a philosophy department. The room is going to be roughly:

- a systems neuroscientist who builds cortical and hippocampal prosthetics, and who will want to know what a "functionally equivalent substitute" cashes out in — spikes, local field potentials, whatever — and will not accept an answer at the level of "the same functional role"
- a pediatric neurosurgeon who has taken out hemispheres in kids and watched them recover language, and who thinks philosophers massively overrate how much tissue you need for a person
- one visiting philosopher of mind, a biological naturalist about consciousness: she thinks the actual causal-mechanical goo of neurons matters, not just what they compute. She is definately not going to be moved by an argument that is functionalist all the way down, and she has heard the standard moves.
- the IRB chair / clinical ethicist, who will ask about consent, and about who exactly the person at the end of the procedure is
- a dozen neurology residents and nurses who will ask the blunt versions of everything and who do not care about my distinctions

My thesis: gradual replacement survives the usual objections. More exactly: if you replace neural tissue bit by bit with something that preserves the local functional role, there is no fact of the matter about the moment at which consciousness is lost, so the "the upload won't be conscious" objection never gets a location for the loss — and an objection with no location isn't an objection. On top of that I'm pushing two things that aren't in the usual version:

(1) The sleep gap. We already let consciousness go out and come back every night and count the person who wakes up as the same subject. So my objector owes me a story about why "a substitution plus a night's gap" is worse than "a night's gap." If their view survives ordinary sleep it should survive my procedure; if it doesn't survive sleep, it was never about replacement.

(2) Diagonal replacement. Don't do one neuron per second while the subject talks. Do the swapping only during natural unconsciousness: each night, during slow-wave sleep, swap in the next tranche. Then no waking moment ever contains a substitution event, there is no moment at which the subject could notice a change, and the fading/dancing-qualia style objection has nothing to bite on — the gradualness isn't even experienced, which I think is the part people haven't dealt with.
