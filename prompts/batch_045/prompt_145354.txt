I adapt historical material for the screen, and there is a distinction that everyone I work with appears to observe and that none of us can articulate. I would like to know whether it has an established name, and if it does not, I would like a term and a definition precise enough to be argued with. I am not asking for sources or further reading — I want the shape of the thing, its boundaries, and an account of what would show that I have packed two separate phenomena together or that there is nothing there at all.

Three instances.

A biopic of a twentieth-century statesman ends with a scene for which there is no record and no witness: he is alone with his wife and says aloud that he traded his children for his career. The line is invented. Reviewers, and people who knew him, treated it as an intrusion, and I agreed with them. Yet the same film contains an invented aide who says of him, in a crowded room, that he traded his children for his career, and that scene drew no objection whatever. Both scenes place the same invented content in the film. Audiences believe neither of them, since audiences know what a biopic is. The difference seems to be only that in the first the dead man is made to say it in his own voice.

A drama about an enslaved woman whose own words have almost entirely not survived has her read, in voiceover, letters she never wrote. Here the same device — putting unsaid first-personal speech into a real person's mouth — reads not as an intrusion but as a kind of restitution, and critics who would object to the statesman's scene praise this one.

A production takes a public speech the subject really gave, quotes it accurately, and restages it as a private confession to one listener. It reads as more invasive than plain invention, despite being, word for word, true.

What I take this not to be: deception, since no one acquires a false belief; damage to reputation, since the invented speech is often flattering; a breach of any contract implied by the words based on a true story; and the general question of whether the dead can be wronged at all — assume for the sake of the argument that something can be owed in respect of them, and tell me which specific thing this is.

My own attempt: call it voice-conscription, the wrong of enlisting a real person's first-personal voice to carry a reading of their life that they did not author, and distinguish it from interpretive preemption, where the wrong would be that a vivid dramatization crowds out the person's own surviving self-interpretation and leaves no room for it to be heard. I cannot tell which of these is doing the work, or whether both are.

So: is there an established concept for this, and if the nearest candidates fit only imperfectly, where exactly does each one diverge from what I have described? If nothing established fits, give me a name, a definition, and boundary cases. Tell me which variable — authorship of self-interpretation, intimacy, preemption, or something I have not separated out — the intuition actually tracks, and what would settle that. Tell me under what conditions it vanishes: very remote subjects, explicitly labelled fiction, flattering content, subjects who themselves fictionalized the people around them, or the older historiographical practice of a historian composing the speeches he attributes to his protagonists. And tell me why the restitution case inverts it: whether the difference is who controls the archive, whether the silence was itself an injustice, or whether the device is simply a different device there and my description has conflated them.
