I'm in Year 12 doing my EPQ on whether falling birth rates are actually a problem — you know, governments handing out baby bonuses, "demographic collapse" headlines, the whole pronatalist thing. There's a little reading group attached to it run by a PhD student at the univeristy who's really into population ethics, and I've now sat through about six sessions where the same fight breaks out every single time and nobody budges even slightly. One side says: if you could choose between a smaller population of very happy people and a much bigger population of people whose lives are only just barely worth living, the second is better as long as the total amount of happiness is higher, even though almost nobody in it is doing especially well. The other side says that's mad — adding people can't improve things unless it's good for somebody, and there's an asymmetry here: we have a strong reason not to create a child who'd be miserable, but no comparable reason to create one who'd be happy. Everyone has known everyone else's best arguments since the 1980s and nothing has moved at all, which is what I find weird.

My hypothesis is that the fight isn't about population at all — it's a bedrock clash about whether morality is about how good the world is or about what happens to people, and neither side can argue for its starting point, so the "debate" is just two gut feelings wearing arguments. But then someone in our group proposed a view we've been calling "Room for One More", which is meant to dodge the whole mess: a new person's life counts as making the outcome better only if, once they're grown up and fully informed, they'd rather go on living than never have existed at all. We argued for an hour about whether that question gets asked from inside the life or from before it, and I genuinely can't tell whether this is a third position or just one of the two sides in a costume — the PhD student said it's "the asymmetry with extra steps" and then wouldn't explain what he meant. Also bugging me: the real-world case for more babies is never that total happiness goes up, it's pensions, carers, who looks after old people, whether a culture survives — which makes me suspect the philosophy and the politics are answering completely different questions under one heading.

So please diagnose it properly: is this verbal, a clash of unarguable starting intuitions, two different questions in a trenchcoat, or a real substantive disagreement that's just hard? Where does "Room for One More" actually sit, and does it stay standing or collapse into one of the two camps? I'd rather you committed to one diagnosis and argued for it than lined up the options and left me to pick — and tell me how confident you are, what piece of evidence or argument would change your mind, and which bit you're mostly guessing at.
