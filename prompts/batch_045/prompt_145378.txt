I sit in on a graduate reading group that has spent the term on Scanlon's contractualism: the view, roughly, that an act is wrong when it would be disallowed by any set of principles for the general regulation of behaviour that no one could reasonably reject, and that whether a principle is rejectable is settled by comparing the complaints it would give particular individuals rather than by totalling benefits and burdens across people. At the last session, two of the regulars argued past one another for the better part of an hour. I have to write up the session notes and would rather adjudicate the argument properly than summarize it, so I am setting it out as faithfully as I can.

They started from the standard worry: because the test looks at complaints one person at a time, it seems unable to add up many small burdens. It therefore appears to tell us to inflict an hour of severe nausea on each of two million people rather than break one stranger's arm, and, by the same structural feature, it cannot see why five lives saved is better than one.

1. Marta said insensitivity to numbers is the point of the view rather than a defect. Justification here is person-relative: what we owe someone is a justification addressed to her, and telling her she must bear a broken arm because two million others would otherwise suffer less is no answer to her at all. The transplant case is the payoff — we do not kill one to save five — and any view that reaches the nausea verdict by summing will somewhere sacrifice an individual to the aggregate. She added that Ellis's confidence about the nausea case is itself suspicious: the individual burden is tiny, the victim is unidentified, and the intuition has exactly the shape of the utilitarian verdict he is supposed to be arguing for rather than assuming.

2. Ellis replied that Marta is conflating two things the contractualist needs to separate: refusing to sum burdens across persons, and refusing to count how many persons a principle burdens. The first may be defensible; the second is not, and the rejectability test as stated can only do the second, since the strength of a complaint is a function of what happens to the person who has it, so adding further complainants cannot strengthen anyone's complaint. That is also why the view cannot say that five deaths are worse than one. Marta wants the transplant verdict, but she is deriving it from the same source that produces the nausea verdict, and she has offered no principled way to keep the first and discard the second. If she wants the transplant result, she should get it from a tie-breaking rule or from the distinctive wrong of killing.

3. Marta conceded that the nausea verdict has some pull, and said contractualism has two internal resources. First, numbers enter through risk: where a principle distributes a burden by lottery, each person's complaint is only as strong as her probability-weighted prospect, and enlarging the pool dilutes each complaint, so the view can register how many people are affected without summing anything. Second, burdens that recur within a single life accumulate inside that person's own complaint. On this reading contractualism is numbers-blind only where burdens are certain and borne by distinct people, which is precisely where, she says, numbers should not count.

4. Ellis said the risk move forces a choice of standpoint and that neither horn is comfortable. Consider a maintenance crew of a thousand keeping a hazardous generator running. Policy S gives each worker a one-in-a-hundred chance of a disabling hand injury, so about ten injuries are expected. Policy C names ten workers who will certainly be injured and spares the rest. Set aside any preference for certainty as such: the expected number of injuries is the same and everyone, ex ante, prefers S. Assess complaints ex post and the two policies are indistinguishable, since the injured have complaints of identical strength under both — so contractualism cannot explain why we must spread the risk, which looks like a plain duty. Assess them ex ante and contractualism has quietly taken on expected-value reasoning over a population, which is the aggregative apparatus it was meant to replace; at that point it is ex ante prioritarianism with a story about justification bolted on. So counting-through-risk is either idle or a surrender.

5. Marta gave two replies. First, the ex ante standpoint is not a concession but the natural home of the view: what gets assessed is a principle, and a principle is the sort of thing one accepts or rejects before knowing where the losses land. Second, the duty to spread need not come from expected numbers at all. Each worker can reasonably reject a principle that assigns certainty to a named few while others bear nothing, because it singles her out; that is a complaint about how she is being treated relative to others, not an aggregate of anyone's injuries. She called this the singling-out complaint, and said it is individual in form, so contractualism gets the duty to randomize for free.

6. Ellis answered that the singling-out complaint is numbers in disguise: its entire force depends on how many others are spared. If the crew were ten and all ten had to be injured, there would be nothing to single out and no complaint; if it were ten with one to be injured, the complaint has the same shape. What Marta calls singling out is just the minimization of total harm described from the position of the loser. Worse, the ex ante move has a further cost, which he called the after-the-draw objection: once the lottery is drawn and the ten injured are known, Marta's view seems committed to saying that the policy is still not reasonably rejectable, so that we may go ahead against people whose injuries we now know to be certain — and that is exactly the kind of sacrifice contractualism was built to forbid.

7. Marta said the after-the-draw objection confuses which principle we may act on with what we owe those who end up badly off under it. Compensation, aid and accommodation are governed by further principles, and keeping the two apart is a virtue: it lets us act on principles everyone can accept while still having something to say to the injured. She then suggested the whole dispute may be misdescribed. Perhaps the rejectability test was never meant as a decision procedure for cases of this shape at all, but as a model of the relation of mutual recognition among persons; if so, insensitivity to numbers is the correct output rather than a defect, and Ellis's objection presupposes the decision-procedure reading.

8. Ellis granted that the aggregation objection by itself does not refute contractualism, but said that is the wrong thing to grant. If the test is a model of mutual recognition rather than a criterion of wrongness, then it cannot deliver verdicts on institutional cases like the crew's at all, and Marta should say so plainly instead of reaching for the decision-procedure reading when the nausea case is on the table and the recognition reading when the exposure case is.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the eight moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two justifying each rating, judging the arguments as actually made rather than as they could have been made. Then say who is more right overall and on which point, and finally say what both parties are missing — in particular whether the duty to spread risk in the exposure case is as clear as Ellis assumes, what it would really cost contractualism to take numbers into account where burdens are certain, and whether the ex ante / ex post distinction is doing the work both of them take it to do. No citations or reading suggestions, please.
