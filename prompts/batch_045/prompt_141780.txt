I am writing a novel set some decades after the arrival of commercial scan-and-rebuild travel, and I need help drafting an artefact that exists inside the world of the book: the Continuance Statement, a questionnaire that the carrier requires every traveller to complete before their first journey.

The background, so that the constraints make sense. Travel works by destructive scan: the traveller's body is disassembled at the origin station and a numerically distinct body is assembled at the destination from local feedstock. Two fares exist. Under the Single-Copy Guarantee, the scan is certified as destructive and exactly one recipient is ever built from it. Under the cheaper Branching Fare, the traveller is merely sedated, survives the journey intact, and a second body is built at the destination which is later released alongside the original; the company also reserves the right to build further copies from the retained scan on request. The Continuance Statement is filed with the carrier's survivorship office, and in the novel it determines three things: whether the arriving person inherits the original's contracts, parental rights and criminal liabilities; whether the carrier must register a branching as a death; and which fare a traveller is permitted to buy. I want the Statement to be a genuine instrument rather than decoration, and this is where I would like your help.

Please write me the questionnaire itself, together with the scoring machinery behind it, subject to the following.

It should run to ten items. Each item is a concrete case drawn from the world of the novel — the sort of incident a traveller might read about in the news or hear about in a departure lounge — with three answer options, plus wherever it is honest to do so an explicit fourth option saying that there is no fact of the matter and the question has no answer. The language must be that of an official form: no philosophical vocabulary, no labels, no mention of theories or of Parfit or of the literature on personal identity. A tired traveller should be able to tick an answer without knowing that a debate exists, and should be able to tick sincerely.

Among the cases I would like the following to appear, or close variants of them: the slow relay, in which the scan is performed over several weeks while the original progressively loses tissue, so that there is no single moment of destruction; the unconsented revision, in which the carrier's software normalises an extreme trait in transit, so that the recipient's dispositions are close but not identical to the original's — imagine a pathological risk-aversion softened by a few degrees; the alibi copy, in which a recipient is built from a sleeping traveller's scan and kept in storage, never to be woken, until the original dies of unrelated causes some hours later and the copy is woken in its place; the re-merge, in which two branches are later scanned and their psychological profiles spliced into a single recipient; the corpse scan, performed an hour after the original's death from a cause unrelated to travel; the unequal branch, in which one recipient receives the full scan and the second receives a truncated version missing the last few years of memory; the conversion case, in which a traveller arrives with every standing desire and evaluative commitment reversed; and at least one case in which the cerebrum is replaced by a synthetic device that preserves overt behaviour and nothing else.

The scoring key should sort respondents into the following positions, named for me but never named on the form: psychological continuity with the no-branching requirement; psychological continuity without it, so that a person can survive as two; the non-reductionist view that survival turns on a further fact beyond physical and psychological continuity; the view that we are essentially living organisms; the narrative or self-constitution view; the reductionist view that what matters is the holding of overlapping chains of continuity regardless of their cause; and the deflationary view that there is no self of the kind the question assumes. For each question, mark which distinction it is meant to separate, and tell me honestly where a question turns out not to be diagnostic and should be cut or rewritten.

Three further things. First, flag the pairs of positions the instrument cannot separate and say why — I expect trouble distinguishing the organism view from the psychological view on ordinary single-copy journeys, and trouble separating reductionism from the no-branching view for anyone who always buys the Guarantee. Second, note the combinations of answers that no actual view occupies, and say whether each is empty because it is incoherent, because it is merely unattractive, or because nobody has thought to stand there. Third, because the novel's legal plot turns on the gap between what a person is and what matters to them in survival, add a short apparatus note: which items sort people by their identity judgements, which by their survival judgements, where the two orderings come apart, and one or two answer patterns that are pragmatically inconsistent in a way an official could detect — a character who insists that fission preserves everything twice, yet insists equally on the destructive fare and a full death certificate — since a subplot involves a traveller filling in the form strategically to qualify for the cheaper fare, and I would like the form to be able to catch her.
