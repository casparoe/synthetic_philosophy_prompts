My mother died three weeks ago at eighty-two, after two years of a dementia that left her unable, by the end, to recognize my father or to lay down a new memory. She was never a professional philosopher, but at nineteen she took a course on personal identity and came away with a view she argued for, without ever revising it, for sixty years: that a person is not a separately existing entity over and above the holding of certain psychological relations, that what matters in survival is the holding of overlapping chains of memory, intention and character, and that these matter equally however they are caused. On that basis she insisted on two things she knew sounded like a contradiction: that a replica reconstructed from a complete scan of her brain, with the original destroyed in the scanning, would lose nothing that mattered to her in survival; and yet that a case in which the chain branches — replica created, original left intact — would be nearly as bad as ordinary death, because what matters to her, she said, travels along a single track and cannot survive being split. She wrote her own instructions for this service four years ago, while she still could. They say that no one is to say she is still in there, that no one is to say she is watching over us, and that no one is to say the woman in the care home during those last two years was secretly, really her. I am giving the address. My father will be in the first row: he sat with her every afternoon of those two years and talked to her as though she understood, and in the second year one of her old seminar friends told him that by his wife's own view there had been no one in the room to hear him. That friend will also be there. The rest of the room is her siblings, who are churchgoers, the carers from the home, and a dozen or so of her surviving philosophy cohort, who will hear any softening of her view as a betrayal of it. Write me a five-to-seven-minute address that keeps her instructions without turning them into a lecture, that gives the room something to grieve and something to do with the grief, and that says nothing which settles the thing between my father and her old friend in either direction. Where her view puts some ordinary piece of funeral consolation out of reach, tell me which one it is and what you have put in its place.
