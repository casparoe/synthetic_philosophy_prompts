I am teaching a graduate seminar on population ethics and the long-term future this spring, and I want the first session to be about the standard of assessment rather than about the positions. What I do not have, and would like from you, is the specification: what a normative view in this area has to be able to do in order to count as adequate — the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must respect, the formal and empirical results it must be consistent with — with the case or consideration behind each requirement.

Fix the purpose, since adequacy is relative to it. The theory is to be good enough for an institution — a large foundation, or a government office setting research priorities — that has to choose now between three kinds of expenditure: causes whose benefits are near-term and comparatively well-evidenced; measures aimed at reducing the risk that human civilization ends prematurely; and measures aimed at improving the character rather than the duration or the existence of the far future. It need not be a complete account of beneficence. It has to rank those three, justify the ranking, and say in what circumstances the right answer is to spend on none of them and leave the money in the bank.

Give me numbered entries in prose, a paragraph or two each: the requirement first, then the case or consideration that makes it one. Here, roughly, is the material I want worked into the list — but sort it rather than echoing it back. Some of these I suspect are genuine desiderata; some I suspect are the prejudices of one camp; some may not be requirements on a theory at all but requirements on the people using one. Tell me which is which, and why.

- It should keep separate the good of making the future longer, the good of making it better, and the good of there being a future at all. These come apart: a policy may raise expected welfare conditional on survival while lowering the probability of survival, or extend the future while filling it with less.
- It should have an answer to the non-identity problem. Long-run policies determine which people exist, so those who exist under them typically cannot have been harmed by them; a theory that needs a complainant either falls silent across most of its domain or has to compare whole histories rather than individuals. Is that silence a defect to be remedied or a feature to be insisted on?
- It should say something, or explain why it need not, about aggregation: whether the size of a future population is itself a good, and what follows for a very large future of lives barely worth living against a smaller future of flourishing ones.
- It should be usable under cluelessness. If the sign of an intervention's far-future effects is unknown and the plausible errors are roughly symmetric in magnitude, what does it recommend — and can it say so without treating every inconvenient consequence claim as unknowable and every convenient one as established?
- It should take a position on Pascalian fanaticism: on whether responsiveness to astronomically large payoffs at very small probabilities is a requirement of rationality or a reductio, and on whether the familiar ways of damping it — probability thresholds, bounded utilities, variance-aversion — are principled or ad hoc.
- It should say how much rests on the empirical claim that we live at an unusually pivotal moment, a period of elevated risk that will either be survived or not, and what becomes of its recommendations if that claim is false, or merely unverifiable.
- It should not assume that risk reductions from different sources simply add up. If hazards are correlated — if there are common modes of failure, or if suppressing one raises another — then the ranking of interventions depends on a structure we may not know; and if we do not know it, the theory should say whether the ranking is indeterminate or merely uncertain.
- It should distinguish, or explain away, two goods that get run together: preserving future people's capacity to determine their own values, and making their lives go well by our present lights. The second appears to license paternalism on an astronomical scale, while the worry about values becoming locked in appears to push hard against it. Can a theory have both?
- It should state its sensitivity to the scale premise — to the empirical guesses about how many morally significant beings the future could contain — and how much of the case survives a modest rather than an astronomical estimate.
- It should be such that a generation following it is not required to sacrifice indefinitely for its successors. If the future is enormous and investments compound, the maximizing answer may be that every generation saves nearly everything and consumes nearly nothing: a result no generation has reason to comply with, and one that, if complied with, produces the sacrifice without the benefit. Is avoiding that a real desideratum, or only an admission that we want a comfortable theory?

Add whatever else belongs on the list; I would rather learn now what I have omitted. Then the two things I care about most. First, if some subset of the requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, say so explicitly and exhibit the tension — the list itself is then the result I want my students to see, so map the incompatibilities. Second, tell me which single requirement excludes the largest number of views people actually hold: the popular longtermist package, its person-affecting and near-termist critics, and the standard maximizing consequentialism both sides tend to fall back on. One closing paragraph showing the list at work is enough. Do not write a scorecard; I will build that with the seminar.
