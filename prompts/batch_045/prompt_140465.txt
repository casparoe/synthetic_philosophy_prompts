i want to practice attacking Good's theorem, so i need someone on the other side who actually knows it: play a hard-nosed Bayesian decision theorist of the old school, the kind who thinks the value-of-information result is close to the last word on whether to go look something up. Ground rules: stay in character until i say "stop"; keep replies to two short paragraphs, conversational not lecture-y; concede the genuinely small points (yes, the value is gross and not net of the cost of looking; yes, a particular message can leave you worse off ex post; yes, if the info is about what some other agent will do it can hurt you) but never the main claim, that for an expected-utility maximizer free information about the world cannot have negative expected value. Correct me if i misstate the theorem, but don't rederive it at me — i've read all the standard blog posts and they all stop at the derivation. Argue it out, no papers or citations, and actually engage the family of cases i'm going to keep hammering on.

My opening: the theorem quietly assumes that learning something leaves your option set alone — that whatever act was available before is still available afterwards, at the same value. That hypothesis fails all over the place, and where it fails the value of free information is straightforwardly negative, which makes the theorem either false or way less cosy than people pretend.

Case in point: a trial judge. Juries get sequestered and told not to read the news, and judges step down from cases where they've picked up things they shouldn't have. For her, a free newspaper is strictly bad: it costs nothing, it's genuinely informative, and afterwards her option set is smaller — "hear this case" is gone, and she's left choosing between hearing it while knowing what she shouldn't, lying about what she read, or recusing. She'd pay good money to un-read it. Same shape, as far as i can tell: a clinician who orders a cheap scan and then has a duty to disclose and chase whatever the scan turns up; a negotiator who'd rather not learn her own side's bottom line so she can say "i don't know" and have it be true; anyone who wants to be able to testify honestly later that they never knew. And that's before we get to the nastier ones where the information changes what you care about rather than what you can do.

So: counterexample, or does the theorem survive by pointing at the fine print? And if it survives by pointing at the fine print, what is it still telling us — because "information is free and good provided it doesn't change what you're allowed to do" sounds like it's smuggling in the entire question i asked.
