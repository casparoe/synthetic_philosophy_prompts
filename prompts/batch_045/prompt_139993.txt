I've ended up holding a fairly specific version of the view that hypocrites lack standing to blame, and I want to know how exposed it is. My version: hypocritical blame isn't false or unfitting — the target really can be blameworthy — but it has its own distinct defect, which is that the target is entitled to dismiss it rather than answer it; and what repairs that defect isn't fixing your behavior but acknowledging your own violation, so a parent who is still smoking and lectures their kid about smoking lacks standing, while one who opens with I know, I'm a hypocrite, and still arguably gets it back. Two wrinkles I've built in and am least sure about: standing looks delegatable, since if I've done X to someone it seems fine for me to blame you for X on their behalf and at their request; and self-blame seems to matter, since someone who blames themselves for X as well as blaming you sounds better than someone who only blames you.

So: what would count against this? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to separate what a defender of my own view would concede counts against it from what they'd rightly dismiss as changing the subject. Concretely: is standing doing any work here beyond restating that the blamer is also at fault? Is the dismiss-versus-answer distinction stable, or does it collapse once you actually say what the target's fitting response is? Are the delegation and self-blame cases genuine predictions I get wrong, or just things the view was silent about? And is the acknowledgement clause circular — if acknowledging your violation just is being disposed to blame aptly, then standing is restored by acknowledgement may turn out to say nothing at all. If the honest verdict is that I've qualified the view into not risking anything, say that, and say whether that's a problem for a view of this kind.
