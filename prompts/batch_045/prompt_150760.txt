I am a PhD student in philosophy working on the epistemology of scalable oversight, and I need the strongest available version of a claim I think is true but have not seen stated cleanly. I would like you to construct the argument, not survey a literature: no citations, no named papers, no recapitulation of who said what. I know the sources; what I lack is the dialectic.

Here is the setup as I am using it. A debate protocol is meant to let a weak judge adjudicate a claim she could not check directly, by having two agents produce and attack arguments, with cross-examination doing the work the judge cannot. The usual reason for optimism is bisection: a long, false argument can be cut in half by the honest debater indicating the half containing the error, and recursion drives the dispute down to a single step small enough for the judge to verify herself.

Call the case I care about a flat error. The argument is long and cumulative — a ledger of a million additions whose reported total is off by one because an entry was silently dropped; a proof in which every inference is locally valid but one required case was quietly omitted. Every step is individually checkable, the conclusion is false, and no short certificate of the falsehood exists: the only way to establish that something went wrong is to redo the whole derivation. My worry is that on flat errors the bisection defense gives out, because bisection needs the honest debater to know where the error is, not merely that there is one, and so the amplification debate was supposed to buy collapses back into recomputation.

Please argue for the following position: the obfuscated-arguments objection is best reconstructed not as the claim that a judge can be talked into accepting a falsehood, but as the claim that the amplification factor of debate is one exactly on the class of arguments we most want it to handle — the long, cumulative, only globally checkable ones — and that this is not an artifact of one protocol but a consequence of any oversight scheme whose sole instrument is adversarial localization of error. I also want the argument to do two things on the way. First, show why the obvious repairs fail: letting the honest debater submit a succinct certificate of error (a recomputed total, a hash of the correct derivation) shifts verification back onto the judge in the way debate was designed to avoid, and random spot-checking of steps is powerless when every step is locally impeccable. Second, say what residual power the judge would have to retain for amplification above one to be recovered, and what retaining it costs the original motivation for the protocol.

Give me the argument as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion: the five points should be the argument itself, in order, and should read as one continuous case rather than as five separate remarks.
