I work as a systematic portfolio manager, about twelve years in, and the vocabulary I actually think in is the one I use every day: expected returns and variance, expected log growth versus expected value and the Kelly fraction (plus the fractional-Kelly haircut I apply for model risk), risk budgets and position limits, Bayesian model averaging across model classes with a minimax overlay when the model set feels wrong, heavy-tailed and truncated payoff distributions, tail indices, ruin and endogenous stopping times, the difference between a pricing measure and the real-world measure, and the gap between a time average and an ensemble average. Use all of that freely without defining it. I do not need to be taught finance. My philosophy is thin: some popular-level longtermist material, and a decade-old memory of the simulation argument. Please write in American English, and do not give me reading lists, citations, or names to look up — if a position is associated with someone, describe the position and move on.

Here is the debate I want translated. On one side: the argument that the expected number of lives in the far future is so enormous that even a minute probability of slightly improving the trajectory of the very long run dominates everything else in expected value, so that a rational allocation of marginal resources should be almost entirely aimed at long-horizon, low-probability, astronomical-payoff bets — reduce extinction risk, steer the trajectory, and tolerate the near-certainty that any given such bet fails. Call this the fanatical recommendation, and note that what makes it fanatical is not that it takes a risk but that it lets a vanishingly small probability of an astronomically large payoff override every near-term, high-probability, moderate-payoff use of the same resources. On the other side: the simulation argument, which offers a trilemma — civilizations at our stage almost always die out before they could run many detailed simulations of their ancestors, or almost none of those that could bother to, or else we are almost certainly inside such a simulation. Under the third horn plus a self-sampling assumption, our credence that we are simulated is high; and a simulated civilization's future plausibly has a much shorter expected length than a base-reality civilization's, because it runs on someone else's compute budget, is concentrated on a historical window of interest, and can be paused, rolled back, truncated, or shut down. If so, the astronomical payoff that drives the fanatical recommendation may be an artifact of assuming we are in base reality, and the recommendation deflates.

Now the part that is not in the standard presentation, which I want you to build into the translation rather than set aside. Stipulate, with me, three further claims. First, the nesting clause: simulations can contain simulations, with no level given as the bottom, so the measure over levels is not supplied and may not normalize. Second, the endogenous-horizon clause: the remaining runtime of a run is not independent of what the simulated civilization does — a run may be terminated, extended, or subsidized depending on its contents, including on whether it itself becomes capable of running simulations. Third, the reflexivity clause: the simulators' objective is unknown to us, may partly consist in observing how a civilization like ours deliberates, and may therefore be affected by our coming to believe we are simulated.

The deliverable, in four parts.

One, restate both sides in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can, and include a mapping table: what, here, is the bankroll, the bet size, the probability, the payoff, the model space, the model averaging, the ruin event, and the objective function? Be explicit about the formal kind of thing each move is. Is the simulation hypothesis an extra model in the average, a change of measure, a change of numéraire, a truncation of the payoff distribution, a haircut on the Kelly fraction, or an adversarial constraint applied after optimization? Is the fanatical recommendation an argument for maximum-Kelly betting at very long odds, and is the deflation a tail truncation, a change in the tail index, or a switch from an ensemble objective to a time-average objective? Where my vocabulary offers more than one candidate reading of a term — probability as betting odds versus measure versus limiting frequency, say — say which you are using and what the translation looks like under the others.

Two, tell me where the translation is doing genuine work and where it is only a metaphor. Which of my tools has a real formal analogue here: the gap between expected value and expected log growth under fat tails, a tail index that makes the mean undefined, an improper prior over a non-normalizable model space, an endogenous stopping time, a reflexive or performative feedback from the model to the thing modeled, a minimax move against an unknown objective? For each, say whether it is the same structure or merely picturesquely similar, and be willing to say that a tool has no purchase.

Three, the residue report, which is the part I care most about. Give three lists. What carries over without loss. What carries over only with distortion, naming the distortion in each case. And what cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all. For each item in the third list, take a view on whether it is a defect of my framework — something a decision-theoretic or risk-management vocabulary ought to be able to say and cannot — or a sign that the original claim was less well posed than it looks: an artifact of an unnormalizable measure, a probability with no sampling procedure behind it, or an objective that was never specified. In the second list, pay particular attention to places where the translation quietly imports an assumption of mine: if my framework pushes toward a fractional-Kelly answer for reasons that come from having a finite bankroll, repeated bets, and a survival constraint, say so, and say whether those reasons carry any weight in the original problem or are pure artifacts of the translation.

Four, the bottom line in my terms. Does the simulation-based deflation actually work, or does it relocate the fanaticism — if we are in a run, is there a new long-odds astronomical bet, on the simulators' objectives or on being at a level with a long remaining horizon, that is structurally the same bet in a different tail, and would that look to me like re-levering into a different position? And what would a risk manager in my chair have to see, in the model space and in the joint distribution of our actions and the truncation time, before cutting the long-horizon position rather than resizing it?

Aim for something around 1,100 to 1,400 words, in prose with the one table, and do not pad it with caveats about how hard the question is.
