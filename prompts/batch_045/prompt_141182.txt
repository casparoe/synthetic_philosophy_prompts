I'm an undergraduate in a philosophy-and-literature seminar this term on punishment, guilt, and the absurd, and my final paper is a reading of the second half of Camus's The Stranger — the trial, the months in the cell, the chaplain's visit, the last night — through the arguments of his 1957 essay "Reflections on the Guillotine." The instructor has asked us to treat the two texts as equal partners: not to use the novel as decoration for the essay, and not to stretch the essay until it fits the novel. I'd like help doing exactly that.

The essay's arguments, as I understand them: executions no longer deter, since they have been withdrawn into prison courtyards and the crimes they are supposed to prevent are mostly crimes of passion, impervious to any calculation; a penalty that deterred nothing would, if it were honest, be advertised on billboards rather than hidden; the state's killing is the most premeditated of murders, such that equivalence would require a criminal who warned his victim of the date of death and then held him confined for months, a monster not encountered in private life; in a secular order no one judging possesses absolute innocence, and the condemned is dispatched with no chance of atonement; and, because verdicts are fallible, an irreversible penalty should give way to lifelong labor, which is harsher but at least reversible.

Three things I want the reading to work out.

First: Meursault admits the killing. The fallibility argument — the one the essay leans on hardest — is therefore unavailable to him. So if the novel is to make his execution unjust, it must locate a wrong in capital punishment that survives full factual guilt. My candidate is what I'm calling the monopoly of final meaning: the trial does not merely sentence him, it authors him, producing a narrative in which a man is executed for failing to weep at his mother's funeral, and the chaplain afterwards offers him a redemptive frame he violently refuses. Is that a genuine wrong the essay misses, or only a metaphor wearing argumentative clothing? And is it still available to an abolitionist who wants to say something about the many cases where the state's story is true?

Second: the deterrent case needs the condemned man to be at once representative — someone the law-abiding can imagine becoming — and radically other, a monster. The prosecution's rhetoric can only secure the second. Does the novel stage this as a dilemma for exemplarity, or does it not care about deterrence at all?

Third, and most important: where does the novel resist the reading? Meursault's closing acceptance of the world's indifference, and his wish for a jeering crowd of spectators at his execution, both sit awkwardly with the moral indignation the essay runs on. Does the wished-for crowd supply the publicity the essay says the modern state withholds, and if so does that help or hurt the abolitionist? Does the novel's refusal ever to name the man on the beach indict it as much as the court?

I don't need citations or a bibliography. I'd like roughly a paper's worth of argument in prose form, anchored in specific scenes, ending with what the novel adds to the death-penalty debate that the essay's arguments alone do not.
