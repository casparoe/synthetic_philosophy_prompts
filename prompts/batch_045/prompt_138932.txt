i write a lot of incident reviews at work and i keep running into a thing i dont have a word for.

Example one: a writeup said the cause of an outage was "no timeout was set on the downstream call" — an absence, not an event. But the same absence showed up six months earlier in another review as "we should add a timeout", filed under recommendations, not causes. Nothing physical changed between the two, just who was on the hook for that service. Example two: everyone agrees "nobody reverted the bad deploy" was a cause, but "nobody was in the office that day" and "no asteroid hit the rack" are not, even though all three are things that didn't happen and all three would have made a difference. And in the meetings the line between cause and background condition slides depending on whether its an SRE review or a product one.

We hacked around it: our template now requires every cause line to be an event that actually happened, and the non-happenings go in a separate "gaps" column. It works fine in practice but it feels like its hiding something rather than fixing it.

A friend said philosophers talk about causation by omission, but as far as i can tell that literature is mostly arguing about whether a not-doing can be a cause at all. That's not my puzzle. My puzzle is the picking: why some absences get promoted to causes once a role or an expectation gets attached to them, and why which ones count can change while the world stays exactly the same.

Is there a name for that picking part? If there is, i want the carving, not just the label: what exactly the thing is, what nearby things it isn't, and where the edges are. If there isn't, say so honestly and give me a name and a definition i could actually put in a doc, plus what would and wouldnt count. No reading lists please, i just want to know what im looking at.
