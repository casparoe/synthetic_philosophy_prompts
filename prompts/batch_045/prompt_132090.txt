so I build codecs for a living — rate–distortion curves, context models, arithmetic coding, lag constraints, the whole business — and I've somehow let myself get dragged into a reading group that's going through Dennett on real patterns and the three stances (physical, design, intentional). Everyone else there is a philosopher and they keep losing me in a fog of words like 'aboutness', so I want to try forcing the whole thing into the only vocabulary I actually trust and see what falls out.

Roughly what I think the view is, in my words: a pattern in some data counts as real if there is a description of that data which beats just writing out the raw stream — compression is the test of pattern-hood — and the three stances are three different ways of predicting a system. Physical: model it from the bottom up, particle by particle. Design: you know what it was built to do, so you predict on the assumption it does that. Intentional: you treat it as a more or less rational agent with beliefs and desires, and you predict what it'll do by working out what it ought to do given what it wants and what it believes.

Here's my half-baked mapping, which I want you to repair rather than humour. Physical stance = bit-exact simulation, zero distortion, no model class beyond physics. Design stance = you hold the constructor's spec, so you predict by assuming near-optimality for the objective it was built against. Intentional stance = you have no blueprint and a generous distortion budget, so instead you model the system as *itself* running a lossy predictive code: its beliefs are its internal model of the source and channel, its desires are something like its rate–distortion allocation, and you predict by assuming it picks the coding that minimises expected loss given that model. Fix whatever that gets defintely wrong, and tell me what belief, desire and 'rationality' each come out as — I suspect my desires-as-rate-allocation bit is doing far too much work.

Then the residue report, which is the bit I actually care about. Three things I want it to cover.

First: in my field, two codecs with different block sizes, budgets or lag limits will carve the same stream into different patterns and neither is wrong, so we've all learnt to say pattern-hood is budget-relative. Dennett wants to be a realist about patterns, but not a full-blooded one — more than 'real for us', less than mind-independent. There's an asymptotic result in my world that feels like the one he'd reach for (swap the machine and it only costs you a constant), but for any stream of the length anyone actually encounters that constant eats the entire message. So I can't see the move from 'compressible under some coding scheme' to 'a pattern in the world' without smuggling in a privileged compressor or a privileged budget. Is there a move I'm not seeing, or is the mild realism just budget-relativity in a nicer jacket?

Second: is the rationality assumption in the intentional stance a statistical assumption or a normative one? My vocabulary has expected loss and nothing else — there is no word in it for what a system *should* do. If it's normative, I'd like to know what breaks if I pretend it isn't.

Third, and this is the one that nags at me: essentially every system I deploy now is trained, not designed. There's no readable blueprint, only weights that got optimised against some loss, and half the time even the loss isn't written down anywhere. The design stance as they seem to mean it doesn't obviously apply to the only interesting artefacts I deal with. Does the three-fold split just not have a slot for that, or does trained-but-not-designed collapse into one of the other two?

At the end, give me an honest verdict rather than a diplomatic one: where the translation leaves something over, is that a hole in my vocabulary or a sign the original distinction was never as clean as it's sold as?
