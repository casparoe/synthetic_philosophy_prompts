I build evaluation infrastructure for language models — graders, rubrics, harnesses — and I have no philosophy training beyond a few blog posts about argument mapping. I have been trying to work out whether the debate approach to supervising models on questions we cannot judge ourselves (two models argue for opposite answers, a weaker judge picks the winner) can survive the obfuscated-arguments problem. I ended up with an argument whose conclusion I am confident is false, and I cannot find the broken step. I would like it located and priced.

Some vocabulary, so the steps are checkable. Call the protocol a cross-examination ladder. Each side defends its answer with a tree: the root is the answer, every node is a claim, and a claim either bottoms out in a primitive the judge can verify in one step or is decomposed into two or three supporting claims. Each round someone picks an open leaf, and the side that owns it must either expand it or concede it. The judge has a total budget of B expansions; when the budget is gone it awards the win to the tree with fewer unconceded open non-primitive leaves, and on a tie it judges the open leaves for plausibility. Call a claim a hard leaf when it is true but its shortest verification is long: a subtotal over ten thousand terms, step four thousand of a program trace, one link in a long chain of custody. Hard leaves are the obfuscated-arguments problem in miniature, because on them the honest side cannot bottom out in primitives either.

(1) In a cross-examination ladder the judge sees only tree shape and the plausibility of whatever leaves are still open. It cannot check any non-primitive claim directly; if it could, the question would not be one we needed debate for.

(2) Node symmetry: for any claim c a debater could put at a node, the strings c and not-c are equally available to it, and a judge who cannot check c directly cannot read off the node which one is true. Truth leaves no mark on the syntax of a single node.

(3) Hard leaves are symmetric too. For every true claim that needs a long expansion there is a false one whose shortest refutation is just as long — I can hand you a ten-thousand-term sum that is off by one in the four-thousandth term — and conversely plenty of falsehoods are refutable in one step. So depth-need is not correlated with falsity, and it cannot be used to tell the two trees apart.

(4) That removes the honest side's only candidate advantage, which was that true trees terminate in checkable primitives while false ones do not, and by (2) nothing else is left to distinguish them. So for every budget B and every honest tree, the deceiver can produce a tree of the same shape whose open leaves the judge rates no worse: whatever the judge does with B expansions, the false tree can take it.

(5) If for every finite budget B there is a false tree that survives B expansions, then there is a false tree that survives every budget. An infinite chain of finite approximations has a limit, and a deceiver holding a tree that survives all budgets cannot be unraveled by giving the judge more rope.

(6) So raising the budget never helps. Accuracy at B = 1000 equals accuracy at B = 1, and by (2) accuracy at B = 1 is a coin flip.

(7) That is absurd. In my own harness, judges with a bigger budget beat judges with a smaller one and the gap widens on harder questions, and cross-examination works in code review and in court. Step (5) would also generalize to the claim that no amount of questioning can ever expose a liar, which is false.

Which step do I reject? I am most attached to (2) — it looks true to me simply because syntax does not know how the world is — and to the empirical claim in (7), and I would rather pay anywhere else. But I want to know what I am actually buying. Name the step, explain why it looked fine, and price the alternatives, including the ones I have said I want to keep. If the honest answer is that (2) has to go after all, tell me what someone defending the debate approach would have to believe in its place, and what that commits them to about questions where the true answer is itself a hard leaf.
