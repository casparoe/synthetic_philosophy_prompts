I have been reading the rationalist-adjacent writing on inferring human preferences from behavior for a long time, and I keep encountering the same rhetorical move. A senior engineer on a team I work with made it last week, and it is deployed in almost every optimistic piece I read on preference learning: inferring a reward function from observed behavior is, he said, just the formal version of ordinary mindreading. You watch a person, you work out what she wants, you predict what she does next; that is folk psychology, or, in the version he preferred, an apprentice watching a master and picking up a craft without ever being handed the rules. The conclusion it is used to carry is reassuring: mindreading works constantly in the human case, it works without anyone stating a utility function, so we should not be alarmed about a system learning what people want from what they do. The data is sufficient; the difficulty others allege is philosophers worrying about a problem that practice has already solved.

I want that analogy audited rather than endorsed or dismissed. Please lay out the correspondence explicitly, as running prose rather than a table, since I would like continuous prose throughout: in the source case, what plays the role of the interpreter's prior over possible desires, of the shared world the interpreter and target inhabit, of the availability of language, of the standing assumption that the target is competent at getting what she wants, and of the feedback channel that lets the interpreter test a guess; then what, if anything, occupies each of those roles in the formal setup, where a learner sees trajectories from a demonstrator and returns a reward function. Then the disanalogies, and, what matters more, a separation between the disanalogies that are irrelevant to the conclusion and the ones that bear on it.

Three things strike me as load-bearing, and I offer them as my own understanding to be corrected where I have it wrong. The first is that mindreading in the source case seems to succeed partly because interpreter and target share enormously much: the same gross needs, the same hardware, the same perceptual world, a common cultural grammar, and a default presumption that the other agent is roughly competent. Take that shared structure away and the inference has very little to lean on, which worries me because the shared structure is exactly what is missing when the interpreter is not a human and the target is. If that is right, the analogy may be borrowing its plausibility from a feature the target case lacks, and the reassuring conclusion may be coming from the analogy rather than from the setup. The second is a non-identifiability I think of as the taste-versus-map problem: an action that looks wrong under my guess about the person's values is explained equally well by a difference in what she wants and by a difference in what she believes or in what she could see, and if the learner cannot observe what the demonstrator observed, or has mis-specified the transition structure, it will attribute to taste what was really constraint or missing information. As I understand it, the constraint that the observed policy be optimal for the recovered reward is satisfied by a very large family of rewards, including degenerate ones, so the likelihood is nearly flat unless a prior over rewards and a model of the demonstrator's noise or bounded rationality are doing the real work, and the sharpness of the inference then depends on a temperature-like parameter that is itself not identified. The third is that the analogy seems to fit some evidence channels much better than others: watching someone act is one thing, asking her which of two trajectories she prefers is another, and asking her to rate a single trajectory with a scalar is a third, and in the last two she is being used as a judge rather than as an agent, so the competence assumption changes shape.

Does the reassuring conclusion survive once these are on the table, or does it need the analogy to hold it up? Where is the framework simply silent, declining to say anything about whether the demonstrator's behavior reflects her values at all? And if this analogy is more trouble than it is worth, is there a better one: I have been toying with reading a traveler's itinerary to work out what she likes without knowing which museums were closed that week, and with reverse-engineering a compiler's optimization flags from its binaries, and I would like to know which structural features each of those gets right and which it quietly imports. I am trying to decide how much of my own group's roadmap should rest on the claim that behavior is enough, so I would rather have the audit than the pep talk.
