I'm in the final year of a politics and philosophy degree, and this term I am taking a seminar on paternalism and behavioural public policy. I keep noticing the same shape in the readings and in seminar discussion, and I do not have a word for it. Or rather, I have several words that almost fit, and I cannot tell whether one of them is the right one or whether the thing has never been separated out.

The first case I noticed it in was automatic enrolment in workplace pensions. The defence of it always comes in two parts. First, nobody is compelled, because the enrolment can be reversed by a form or a phone call. Second, the policy is a success, because participation rises by tens of percentage points. But the second part is produced almost entirely by people who never returned the form or made the call. The freedom that is doing the work in the first part belongs to the employees whose behaviour the policy leaves untouched; the employees whose behaviour actually changed are the ones who never exercised it. The justification and the effect seem to be running on different sets of people.

The second case was a hospital's electronic prescribing system, where the generic version of a drug is pre-selected in the order form and the branded version has to be actively chosen. The same two-part defence: prescribers are not forced, they can always override the default; and the generic's share of prescriptions goes up. Once again, the increase comes from prescribers who were not attending to that field at all, and the reply about overriding is about prescribers who were. And there seems to be a trade-off between the two parts: the more successful the default is, the fewer the people there are who can be pointed to as having freely gone along with it, and so the less work the freedom can do.

I have three half-fitting labels, and none of them seems to be about this. The condition, central to what gets called libertarian paternalism, that an arrangement must be easy to avoid tells me that the opt-out exists; it does not tell me what to say when the opt-out is never exercised by the very people the intervention moved. The asymmetric-paternalism framing seems to assume that the people who are helped and the people who bear the cost are different groups, whereas here the person who is helped, if she is helped, is the same person whose behaviour was changed without her attending to it, viewed under two descriptions. And the manipulation objection looks like a complaint about the mechanism, about bypassing or subverting agency, rather than about the mismatch I am pointing at.

So my question is this. Is the mismatch a recognised phenomenon with a name of its own: the gap between the population whose freedom is cited in the justification and the population that generates the measured effect, together with the apparent see-saw between a default's effectiveness and how much justificatory work its opt-out can do? Or is it better described as two separate worries that happen to travel together, one about the mechanism by which the effect is produced and one about who has to be able to do what in order for the defence to succeed? If the nearest concepts are the three I listed, I would want to be told exactly where each of them stops and where mine begins, and which of them fits best. And if there is nothing established, I would like a name, a definition that fixes the boundaries, and a statement of what falls outside it: does the pattern apply to a default that has been loudly publicised in advance, to one that works merely by making the desired option more salient, to a tax rather than a default, to an intervention that moves people towards something they themselves report wanting? Finally, if the pattern is real, is it an objection to nudging at all, or only a constraint on how a nudge may be defended? I am not after a label to attach to my essay. I want to know whether I have found a distinction or whether I am rediscovering something the literature already handles under a different description.
