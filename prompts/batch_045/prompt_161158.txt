I co-organize a decision-theory reading group — mostly philosophers, plus a few people from economics and statistics — and we have now spent three sessions on the Pasadena game without converging, so I want to force the issue by asking for a document rather than another discussion. Set the game up as we did: a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads; if the first heads falls on the n-th toss, the payoff is (−1)^{n−1}·2^n/n dollars (so +2, −2, +8/3, −4, +32/5, …), each outcome having probability 2^{−n}. Both protagonists accept the algebra: the expectation series is the alternating harmonic series, which sums to ln 2 ≈ 0.69 in its natural order, but the expectation of the payoff's positive part and of its negative part each diverge, so the expectation is genuinely undefined, and by the Riemann rearrangement theorem the series can be reordered to yield any real number or no limit at all.

Call the two positions the Restrictionist and the Completionist. The Restrictionist holds that expected-utility maximization is the correct theory of instrumental rationality for options whose expected utilities are well defined; that the Pasadena game has no expected utility in the strongest sense; and that the correct thing for a decision theory to say about it is nothing, because the option lies outside the theory's domain — last session she compared it to dividing by zero, whereas the Completionist compared it to the liar paradox, and they have been circling that analogy ever since, so part of what I want is for the analogy to be made exact or abandoned. The Completionist holds that a theory which falls silent on a fully specified, physically implementable gamble is extensionally inadequate, that silence is not a verdict, and that we need a rule that ranks such options — by limits of finite truncations, by dominance relations that survive when expectations fail to exist, by comparing the game against a benchmark, or by some other device — together with an account of what the rule is tracking and why the tracking relation holds.

Write the joint statement they could both sign. Open with a numbered agreed-claims section: the algebra; what the Pasadena game does and does not have in common with the St. Petersburg game (nobody should be allowed to smuggle in a bounded-utility or anti-fanaticism moral here, and I want the difference stated precisely); what survives when expectations fail to exist, sure-dominance above all, since Pasadena-plus-a-dollar surely beats Pasadena and neither side should be able to wriggle out of that; and whether "no verdict" is distinguishable, for the agent and for the theory, from "the agent is indifferent" — including in sequential and planning contexts, where what is permitted later may matter to what may be chosen now. Then give a disagreements table, one row per crux, with columns for the crux itself, the Restrictionist's position in wording the Restrictionist would accept, the Completionist's position in wording the Completionist would accept, the case or argument both accept as bearing on the crux, and — written down in advance — what each side predicts that case will show.

Use the bearing cases we kept returning to rather than tidier ones of your own: (a) the truncation ladder — truncate the game at N, so that if no heads falls in the first N tosses nothing changes hands, and every truncated game has a well-defined, positive expectation oscillating around ln 2, which is to say the finite games all look worth playing while the untruncated one does not; (b) the vanishing-perturbation variant — multiply the payoff on the n-th outcome by (1 + ε_n) with ε_n → 0, so that on any ordinary standard no individual payoff is changed appreciably, and the expectation can be made to be anything you like or to diverge outright; (c) the planning variant — an agent who knows with certainty that she will be offered the game tomorrow, choosing today between a sure dollar today and nothing today. Tell me explicitly which parts of the quarrel you take to be verbal rather than substantive — about "undefined," about whether permissivism is itself a verdict, or elsewhere — and which are not, and where you think no argument both camps accept could settle a crux, say so rather than manufacturing a test. No citations and no reading list: this is a two-page handout for people who have read the literature, and I want claims and arguments, not references.
