I have recently been appointed to the board of the mutual insurance association that insures my own farm and most of my neighbours' holdings, as the member responsible for signing the statement on actuarial assumptions in our quarterly reserving filing. I am not a statistician by training — my formal background is an engineering degree with one graduate course in Bayesian methods — and the file comes back to me about four times a year, with roughly a week to work on it, an external reviewer who will not accept a prior whose justification is that it came with the software, and a set of assumptions that ends up minuted and binding on the premiums our members pay, including my own.

The item now on my desk is the choice of priors for the parameters we have no useful data on. My predecessor's filing described these priors as non-informative, by which he appears to have meant that he placed a uniform distribution on whatever quantity the software happened to parameterise. Our reviewer's note says this is a concealed assumption rather than an absence of one, and I have been reading about the principle of indifference and Bertrand's chord problem in order to understand the objection. My provisional reading, which I set out so that you can tell me whether I have it right, is this: that the three classical answers to the chord problem show the principle of indifference to be simply inconsistent, since one and the same state of ignorance yields a probability of one third, one half, or one quarter depending on which construction one uses; and that the only workable remedy is therefore to fix one convention — always impose indifference on the parameter as presented, or always impose it on the logarithm of a positive parameter — and then apply it uniformly across every filing, so that at least our numbers are reproducible and consistent from quarter to quarter.

What I need from you is a procedure I can actually run, not a discussion. Specifically:

A sequence of steps, to be applied to each unknown parameter in turn, with a statement at each step of what would count as a sufficient answer and what would count as a failure. It must be runnable by me, in a spreadsheet, in the time I have.

At each step, an explicit indication of where the procedure stops with a no — where the honest output is that no defensible ignorance prior exists for this parameter — and where it hands the decision back to a person because the remaining question is one of judgement rather than calculation. Please mark these hand-off points plainly rather than papering over them.

A worked application to three parameters from our file: the standard deviation of claim sizes, which is positive and whose order of magnitude we genuinely do not know; the proportion of claims that settle without any payment, bounded between zero and one and otherwise unknown to us; and the shape parameter governing how heavy the upper tail of the claim-size distribution is, which is the quantity our reinsurance pricing most depends on and the one we have the least data about. I expect at least one of these to come back with an answer I will not like, and I would rather have that stated directly than have it softened.

Some further requirements that I think matter, but which you should overrule if you think they are misguided:

A reparameterisation stress test. For each parameter, I want to be able to write the sentence "we know nothing about this quantity" in three or four equivalent-sounding forms — about the quantity itself, about its square, about its logarithm, about its reciprocal — and see whether the resulting priors agree. Where they disagree, I take it my claim of ignorance was incomplete, and I want the procedure to say what I must then supply.

An account of which distinctions have to survive the simplification and which can be dropped. I cannot carry the full theory into a board minute, but I also cannot afford to carry a version of it that is false. Tell me which is which.

A rule for when the choice stops mattering. Some of these parameters will be pinned down by next year's data and some will not. I need to know how to tell the difference, and what I owe the reviewer in each case — whether a sensitivity analysis is an acceptable substitute for a principled choice, and in what regime it is not.

Finally, a short closing section stating plainly what the procedure cannot do, and a note on the reading I described above: if my diagnosis of the chord problem, or my proposed remedy of fixing a single convention, is wrong, I would rather be corrected at the outset than have the procedure built on top of it.
