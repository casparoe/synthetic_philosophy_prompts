I've ben arguing with a friend for something like two years about whether "reduce suffering" is really a different axiology from "increase happiness," or whether it's just classical utilitarianism with a strange exchange rate and some person-affecting intuitions duct-taped to the side. We never manage to locate the disagreement, because every time we get close it turns out one of us was answering a different question. So I want to settle it with an instrument instead of with more arguments.

What I want from you: a decision tree plus a scorable quiz for the suffering-focused end of ethics. Audience is my blog's readers — old-LW / SSC-adjacent, most of them have heard of negative utilitarianism, have opinions about wild animal suffering, and have strong feelings about extinction risk, but nobody's actual intuitions are labeled, and the whole point is to sort them by what they say about cases rather than by which banner they think they're under. Start everyone from the shared minimal commitment that suffering is morally significant at all and that happiness isn't just its mirror image (the old Popper-flavoured thought, whatever the exact wording is — don't quote it, I don't need the phrasing, and don't bother with attribution).

How I want it built:

Construct the logical space first — the discriminating questions and the combinations of answers they permit — and only then place the named positions into it. Don't start from a list of -isms. Eight branch questions, nine to twelve leaves. Every branch question should be phrased as a concrete case with two or three answer options that someone could answer without knowing where it leads. Then give me a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto leaves.

Cases I definitely want in there:

1. The Last Curtain: a button that painlessly and permanently ends all sentient life on Earth — no one experiences dying, no grief, no disappointed preferences, no lost future. Is pressing it good, bad, neutral, or impermissible either way?
2. The False Recovery: a patient in agony you can help only by inducing a vivid delusion that they're fine and recovering, versus actually fixing the cause at a hundred times the cost. Does the delusional route count as removing the suffering?
3. The Rehearsal: suffering that the person chose, refuses to give up, and which partly constitutes an achievement they value (a genuinely brutal climb, say). Does it still go in the ledger as a cost, and does the fact that it was consented to change anything?
4. The Creation Asymmetry: declining to create a life that would be net-positive, versus creating one with a small guaranteed net-negative. Does the first count as a reduction of suffering, a forgone gain, or neither?
5. The Exchange Rate: one unit of torture-grade suffering versus a very large number of mild headaches — finite tradeoff, lexical priority above some intensity threshold, or no tradeoff at all? And the aggregation twin: a mild toothache in a billion people versus severe pain in one.
6. The Ambient/Induced split: suffering nobody caused versus suffering an agent caused, holding consequences fixed — does the doing/allowing distinction carry weight of its own, or only through what it predicts?
7. The Override: imposing a small amount of suffering on one person as the only way to prevent much larger suffering for others. Purely aggregative answer, or is there a side-constraint?
8. Something you add yourself, in the decision-theoretic or uncertainty-about-sentience neighborhood (expected value under uncertainty, whether risking vast suffering is treated differently from forgoing vast happiness, that sort of thing).

Leaves must include strict/classical negative utilitarianism, a negative-leaning consequentialism with a lexical threshold, classical symmetric utilitarianism as a contrast leaf, an asymmetric person-affecting view, and at least one hybrid that keeps suffering-focused axiology but adds a deontic side-constraint. Extinctionism should not be a leaf — it's a downstream verdict that several different leaves issue, and I want it marked as such, with a note on which leaves issue it and which don't. Each leaf gets one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Two more things I care about more than the leaves themselves:

Unoccupied cells. Give me at least three combinations of answers that the space allows but that nobody seems to occupy, with a one-line diagnosis each — is it empty because it's incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because nobody has bothered? And if a real reader's answers land in one of those cells, tell me how you'd distinguish the tree being wrong from the reader being confused.

Where the instrument fails. Flag any pair of leaves your tree genuinely can't separate (I suspect steep prioritarianism and lexical-threshold negative-leaning consequentialism are behaviorally identical on every case I can think of, and likewise experiential versus preference-based versions outside the False Recovery case, but you tell me), and say what further case would separate them or why nothing would. For each leaf, mark how confident you are that it's actually occupied by real defenders: widely defended, defended by a handful, or I'm not sure anyone holds exactly this. No citations, no bibliography, no as-argued-by-someone-in-2019 — I can't check them and neither can you.

Finally, the bit I actually most want: a closing note saying which of your own branch questions you're least confident really discriminates, which leaf you suspect you gerrymandered to make the tree come out tidy, and what pattern of answers from an actual reader would make you redraw the whole thing. If you think my starting commitment already smuggles in something only one family of views can accept, say so there too.
