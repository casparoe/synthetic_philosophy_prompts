so our sixth form has these bookable study rooms and every morning there's a massive queue, and half the time rooms sit empty because people book two 'just in case' and then don't turn up. In the meeting about it everyone keeps saying it's a tragedy of the commons — the booking app is the open pasture, we're the herders adding extra animals, booking a second room is overgrazing — and on the back of that the staff want hard caps: one booking per person per day and an automatic £2 fine for a no-show.

I don't think it's the same shape of problem and I don't want to just say the phrase back at them, so can you audit the analogy properly? What maps onto what, and where does it break: a field gets worse when you graze it, an empty room just isn't used, and the room resets every morning; we can also all see each other doing it, which herders apparently can't. And what does the picture quietly import? It feels like it smuggles in that the only fix is a fence.

Two things that matter for the decision: half the double-booking isn't greed at all, people are waiting on a mate to confirm before they release the spare room; and my mate on the student council has proposed a release-and-earn rule instead — cancel an hour ahead and you go to the front of tomorrow's queue, with a public leaderboard of who's released the most. So can the pasture analogy actually carry the case for the caps over something like that, or is it doing all the persuading on its own? If it's the wrong model, say what a better one would be.

Also be honest about where your audit is most likely to be wrong and what would change your mind about the caps — like what you'd have to find out about how we actually behave before you'd say the staff were right.
