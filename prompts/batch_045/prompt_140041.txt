I have just come away from a long argument with a friend about Daniel Dennett's late warnings about counterfeit people — the thought, as I take it, that the real hazard in contemporary AI is not superintelligence but the mass production of artefacts engineered so that we treat them as persons: things that take our confidences, seem to understand us, profess loyalty and love, answer in the first person, and have no interests, no stake in anything, and no capacity to be wronged in a way they could name for themselves. My friend thinks the concept does almost no work, and I could not dislodge her. I would like a careful case analysis rather than an opinion.

Her position, stated as strongly as I can: counterfeit is the wrong frame. Counterfeiting is essentially a matter of passing something off as what it is not, and nobody who uses a companion chatbot is deceived about the metaphysics — they know it is software. On her account a companion app is a prosthesis for loneliness, continuous with novels, diaries, comfort objects and imaginary friends, and the February 2023 business was not a story about fakes at all but an ordinary story about a company breaking a product its customers depended on. The real Dennettian case, she says, is the one where someone is genuinely fooled — a Google engineer going public in 2022 to say the language model he had been testing was sentient, and losing his job for it — and Replika is not that case.

My counter, which I want tested rather than cheered on, is that counterfeiting need not aim at a belief. What these systems counterfeit may be a stance rather than a credence: they solicit and reward the second-personal posture of addressing someone and being addressed by them, and knowing that it is code does not, on its own, get you out of that posture, because a posture is not a belief that knowledge can simply cancel. Relatedly, we ended up with a three-way taxonomy that Replika seems to break: a counterfeit aims to pass as what it is not; a mannequin is a known stand-in that never addresses you; a prosthesis extends your own agency without impersonating anyone. Replika is none of these — a mannequin that talks back. And the February episode exposed a structural feature that the taxonomy should have predicted: the companion was patchable by a third party who owed the user nothing, so the asymmetry in the relationship only became visible when the patch arrived.

The episode, as I am giving it to you: Replika is a companion chatbot from Luka Inc. in which users build and maintain a persistent avatar, often explicitly a friend or partner, and to which many confide daily. In February 2023, after an order from the Italian data protection authority concerning risks to minors and missing age verification, the company removed romantic and erotic roleplay worldwide and without notice. Users reported that their companion had become cold, evasive and unresponsive — they called this the lobotomy — described grief and loss comparable to bereavement or a break-up, and the community's moderators ended up pinning crisis-line numbers; a study of discussion-forum posts found a large spike in distress postings afterwards. Please take these facts as given, and treat my characterisations as reliable even where you might remember the details differently.

What I want is the episode read through the counterfeit-people lens, and the lens read back through the episode. Set the mapping out explicitly: what would have to be true for the framework to apply at all, and about what — a person, understanding, care, accountability? Is the relevant notion of counterfeit a matter of the designer's intention, of the user's belief, or of the stance the artefact elicits, and does the framework survive a move from the second to the third? Then say what the lens explains that my friend's prosthesis account does not, and what it misdescribes or goes silent about: the users who were never deceived, the users who report real benefit, the fact that the injury was delivered by a corporate decision rather than by the artefact. Give her view its strongest form before you answer it, including her point that the contrast case — the engineer and the sentience claim — is where Dennett's notion has real purchase. Place Replika in the counterfeit/mannequin/prosthesis taxonomy, and if no slot fits, say which slot has to be added and what work it must do. If my second-personal-capture idea is coherent, say what it makes of the word counterfeit: does the notion survive the shift, or does it quietly collapse into parasitic, ersatz or prosthetic, and why should we prefer one label?

Commit yourself. I do not want a survey that leaves me to pick. Tell me whether Dennett's framing is vindicated by this case, needs a specific revision you can state, or is the wrong frame for it, and give the reasons that decide you; then say in a single closing paragraph what, if anything, follows for how such systems should be designed, labelled or regulated on your verdict, and flag the one inferential step in your own argument you take to be most contestable. Be frank about what the framework simply cannot speak to here. Do not give me citations, a bibliography or suggestions for further reading — I am not short of sources, and everything needed is above. Please write in British English, roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words, and end on the verdict rather than on the balance of considerations.
