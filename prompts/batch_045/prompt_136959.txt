I write about science and ideas, and I've agreed — perhaps rashly — to run a practical session at a science-journalism conference rather than sit on another panel. The slot is ninety minutes. About twenty people: reporters, four or five graphics editors, two or three press officers, aged roughly twenty-five to sixty, nearly all of them impatient with anything that sounds like theory and several of them committed to the view that the journalist's job is to get out of the way and let the numbers speak for themselves. The room has long tables seating four, a projector I'd rather not use, wifi that will fail if I depend on it, and no budget, so: paper, index cards, sticky notes, pencils, and whatever fits in a bag I carry myself.

The subject I want them to walk into is Otto Neurath's picture-language work — the Vienna Method, the picture statistics of the interwar years — and specifically the part of it that is the opposite of what his reputation suggests. Three claims, as I understand them. First, that pictures do not speak for themselves: a drawing is not self-interpreting, and the reader of a pictogram has to be taught a convention exactly as the reader of a sentence does. Second, that the test of a picture language is not whether a picture looks right or strikes you as natural, but whether it can be *transformed* — whether the same content can be mechanically re-expressed, rescaled, re-sorted, or converted from one formulation into another, so that what the picture asserts can be checked against other pictures and against numbers rather than merely admired. Third, that words and pictures are not different in kind here: words are irreducibly ambiguous, pictures are ambiguous too, and a picture language buys clarity not by resembling the world but by standardization and training. This is, I take it, why Neurath was hostile to the Tractatus-flavoured idea that a proposition is a logical picture of reality and therefore carries its own sense in itself. What my participants will bring in the door is the opposite: that images are immediate, universal, cross-cultural, harder to lie with than prose, and that a good chart is self-explanatory.

So I need a runnable session, not a lecture. The constraint I care most about is that they should hit these points by doing something and failing at it, and only afterwards hear them named. I do not want to say the word Isotype, or transformation, or convention, until the debrief.

What I'd like from you is the whole apparatus. The task and its phases, with timings that fit ninety minutes including a late start and a slow cleanup. The rules and material constraints that do the philosophical work: I have in mind forbidding all words and all numerals in a first round so that quantity has to be shown by repetition of identical signs, banning colour and perspective and shading, and forcing a fixed grid — but tell me what each ban is *for*, which ones earn their keep, and which will just produce an art project. Most importantly, two mechanisms I've been turning over and can't quite engineer. The first is an absent reader: each team's panel must be read by someone who never saw the source table and was not in the room when it was drawn, and that reader must then perform a transformation on it — restate the panel's claim as rates rather than counts, or re-sort it by a second variable, or re-express it for a population twice the size — without being handed the data, only the panel and whatever key the team wrote. I want them to find that the picture silently omitted the denominators, and that the omission is not a drawing mistake but a fact about what a picture can hold. The second is a wager: teams stake points on how many of three stranger-readers will recover their claim, with a payoff scheme that costs nothing and doesn't need money, arranged so that overconfidence is expensive and so that the teams who win are the ones who spent their effort on an explicit rule rather than on a prettier drawing. Fix the scoring so it actually does that. Tell me what data table to give them — my own thought is something epidemiological or climatological, but I suspect the right choice is a table whose ordering disagrees with what participants' background knowledge leads them to expect, so that recognition gets punished and only reading pays.

Give me also the debrief, mapped: which moment in the room corresponds to which of the three claims above, in what order I should draw them out, what I should let a participant say rather than saying myself, and which line I must deliver because the activity can't produce it. Be blunt about what will go wrong on the first run — smuggled labels, someone drawing a map, the team with a confident graphics editor, readers who guess the content from real-world knowledge instead of the signs, a planted misreader who gets unmasked, the transformation task turning trivial because a team hoarded the table, the session ending with people concluding that pictures are just unreliable — and what I should do in each case. And be equally blunt about the limit: tell me which feature of Neurath's position this kind of exercise cannot stage, so that I know what I am obliged to say out loud at the end and what I should openly decline to demonstrate in ninety minutes with paper and pencils.

One last thing: I'm not asking for citations, a bibliography, or a reading list — I can't check them from a conference hall and they'd be useless to me there. If I've misstated Neurath in a way that would sink the design, flag it in a clause and correct the design, don't correct me at length.
