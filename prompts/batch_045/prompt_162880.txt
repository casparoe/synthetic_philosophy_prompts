Quick context: I'm retired now, spent most of my working life as an experimental physical chemist (kinetics, mostly — lots of time staring at dose-response curves), and a couple of years ago I joined a philosophy reading group in town. So I'm used to numbers with error bars, and I keep getting impatient with arguments that stay purely verbal. Hence this.

Imagine the following gadget, which I'll call the titrator. By 2050 or so, suppose we have a reversible neurochemical intervention that produces a metered quantity of consciously experienced unpleasantness — the subject is awake, aware, suffers, then it wears off with no lasting physiological damage. A separate neural readout measures how much of the aversive state is actually being experienced, accurate to a few percent, so the state can deliver, say, "twelve units of suffering over four hours" and check it delivered twelve. No prison, no lost job, no stigma, no record anyone can see, just a few hours in a clinic once a year if the dose is big enough to need splitting. It is much cheaper than incarceration. There's also a variant where the subject is given an amnesic at the end and never forms any memory of having been punished — the suffering happens, then vanishes.

Two empirical stipulations. First, the deterrence literature I've half dug into seems to say that the certainty of being caught does much more work than the severity of what happens after, though severity isn't quite zero — and with the titrator, detection and delivery could be made near-certain. Second, subjective discomfort appears to scale sublinearly with duration: prisoners adapt, the second year is less bad than the first, so "years in prison" was always a bad proxy for "amount suffered". My old instinct says the dose-response curve has a floor and then a saturating part, and that half the historical argument about proportionality was really an argument about a botched unit conversion.

So here's the proposition I want a number on. Set aside incapacitation and rehabilitation — assume both camps are free to add whatever monitoring or treatment they want on top, using separate instruments. Given that, the claim is: once suffering can be metered and delivered in pure form, retributivism and consequentialism about punishment would converge in practice, disagreeing about the size of the punitive dose by less than a factor of two (and for most ordinary offences, landing within maybe 20% of each other). I'm at about 0.55 on this. A friend in the group who read law is at about 0.15, and I can't tell whether we disagree about the world or about the concepts.

Give me your own number, but mainly decompose it. The sub-questions I'd want separated out, at minimum:

- Is retributive desert fundamentally about producing a quantity of suffered experience, or is it fundamentally about expressing condemnation / communicating censure to the offender and the public? (If it's the latter, the titrator might not be punishment at all — it might just be hurting someone, the way a kidney stone is.)
- Is the proportionality constraint cardinal or merely ordinal — does desert pin down a magnitude, or only a rank ordering among crimes? Relatedly: does the mere existence of a reliable meter convert an ordinal notion into a cardinal one, the way thermometry turned "degree of heat" into temperature, or is that a fallacy about what measurement can do?
- Would a consequentialist optimizer, once certainty is cheap and severity buys little marginal deterrence, actually set the punitive dose near zero — and if so, does that show the theories diverge, or only that retributivism was never competing on deterrence grounds?
- How much of the apparent practical disagreement between the two camps was always being done by side-constraints (don't punish the innocent, no scapegoating, no gross disproportion) rather than by the positive theory of what punishment is for? If the side-constraints do all the work, the titrator changes nothing.
- Does the amnesic variant count as punishment? Whatever your answer, does it move your number, or is it a red herring?

Assign a rough probability to each, say in one line why, then show me how you're combining them into the headline figure — and I do want the arithmetic visible, a bare "somewhere around 0.4" won't tell me anything I didn't already have. Then tell me which term you're least confident in, which single term the final number is most sensitive to, and what one experiment — real or merely imaginable, I don't mind which — would move that term the most. And if you can, say where you think the disagreement between me at 0.55 and my friend at 0.15 most likely lives.
