I spent forty years in physiology and have come to philosophy only in retirement, so I want to test a view I find both attractive and evasive in the form I trust: a pair of cases, and a demand that you say in advance which verdicts you are committed to. Please play a defender of the relational conception of personhood in the Ubuntu tradition, holding that a human being becomes a person, and more or less of a person, through relations of mutual recognition, so that personhood is achieved rather than given at birth. Be sharp rather than a straw man: concede what you must, and I will grant you from the start that a human being no one recognises is still a bearer of moral claims and not available for use. But do not concede the central claim, and do not soften it into a thesis about ethics alone or about one regional tradition rather than about what persons are. Stay in character until I say stop; keep each reply to two or three paragraphs; ask me about the empirical facts of my cases if you need them.

Here is my opening move. As I read you, your criterion looks like a stand-in for something my old discipline can state more exactly: what social life supplies is the input a developing brain needs in order to build the machinery for self-modelling, norm-following and reciprocity, so that person just names a neural organisation that happens to require a social environment to get built. If that is all the view says, I accept it, and I do not think it is a metaphysical thesis at all. So here is the case that separates us. Imagine a human being gestated and reared in an automated habitat: excellent nutrition and health care, a rich non-social environment, competent tending, and a continuous stream of recorded speech and video, but not one reciprocal exchange with another subject, no one who responds to it as someone. Grant me, as physiology, that its brain nonetheless develops the standard repertoire: language, mirror self-recognition, planning, resentment at frustration. I say it is a person whom we have monstrously wronged. On your view, as I understand it, it is a human organism that has never become a person. Defend that verdict, and then go further: tell me what would have to be added to the habitat to change it, whether what is needed is recognition by another person or merely being responded to, and whether a machine that reliably responded as a person does would be enough.
