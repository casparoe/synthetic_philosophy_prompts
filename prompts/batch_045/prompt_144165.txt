I'm writing a 5,000-word term paper for a seminar on desert and moral responsibility, due at the end of a twelve-week semester, and I need a proposal I can get signed off on. My half-formed idea is that most treatments of karma as a theory of moral desert run together three things that ought to be separated: whose suffering is at issue (the same agent, or merely the same causally and psychologically continuous stream of mental events); whether that suffering is apportioned by someone or something with standing to do so, or simply befalls the agent as an outgrowth of her own action; and whether the fittingness of the suffering is backward-looking at all, given that it is usually also supposed to instruct or steer her.

Two cases make the separation bite, and I want the paper built around them. First, fission: if a karmic stream were to divide so that two later persons were equally continuous with one earlier wrongdoer, would the desert split between them, double, or lapse? Second, transfer: some traditions hold that the good fruit of one being's action can be dedicated to or shared with another, which looks like desert that is alienable in a way retributivist theories standardly deny. My suspicion is that once those three threads are prised apart, karma starts to look less like desert and more like what I'd call positional fittingness: it is fitting that wrongdoing be followed by suffering within the same stream, but nobody thereby has a reason to bring that suffering about, and it need not be good for anyone. The paper's question would then be whether a notion of desert that generates no reasons for any agent to act, and that does not require the recipient to be the wrongdoer, is still desert at all, or only a metaphor wearing desert's clothes.

Please turn this into a proposal that states the question precisely enough to have a determinate answer, explains why both the retributivist reading and the no-self reading of karma leave it open, sets out a method — what I would actually do across the twelve weeks, stage by stage, and why that method rather than working through the traditional commentaries — lists the possible conclusions and what would decide between them, and gives an honest account of what would count as failure or as too ambitious for one paper. Describe positions and arguments rather than naming sources; I'll add the references myself.
