THE QUESTION (100 marks; answer all parts)

We have discussed two families of view about causation by omission. Difference-making accounts (broadly Lewis-style counterfactual ones) treat someone's not-doing-something as a cause when, roughly, things would have gone better had they acted. Process accounts resist: an omission isn't an event or a process but the absence of one, and absences can't bump into anything. We also introduced the following distinction: call an omission anchored when there is some actual event in the neighbourhood — a decision reached, a hand pulled back from a switch, a clock glanced at and ignored — that the omission is anchored to, so that it has an approximate time and place; call it free-floating when there is no such event, as when the agent simply never thinks about the matter at all across the whole relevant interval.

(a) [20] Imani is paid to water the plants in the department greenhouse over a hot weekend. She does not water them. They die of dehydration. Did Imani's failure to water them cause their death? Explain what each family of view says and what you think follows.

(b) [45] Same greenhouse, same weekend. The head of department, Orlando, had also promised Imani that he would water them, and he does not either; each assumes the other has done it. One watering by either of them would have been enough, and either could have done it alone. Priya, who lives next door to the greenhouse, knows all about the plants and could have watered them in two minutes, but never does, and she has no connection with the department whatsoever. Work through all three failures to water. What, if anything, does Priya's case show about the difference-making account — and can the account deal with her without bringing duties or norms into the causal test itself?

(c) [35] Ravi holds the only key to the pump room; switching on the pump at any point before noon on Saturday would have saved the plants. Ravi never goes near the pump room, never deliberates, never thinks about the greenhouse at all — a free-floating omission, in our terms. Suppose instead that he had walked to the room on Saturday morning, looked at the switch, thought "not my job", and walked away — an anchored omission. Does that difference make any difference to whether his failure caused the deaths? What does your answer show about what sort of item an omission would have to be in order to be a cause? Finally: unbeknownst to anyone, Ravi's key had been stolen on Friday evening and he could not have got into the room. Does that change your verdict?

THE ANSWER AS SUBMITTED

I'll take the causal question and the moral question separately. Whether an omission caused something is a factual matter; whether the person who omitted is to blame, or owes anyone anything, is a further and separate matter. Only the first is my business here, and I'll try to flag it if I slip.

(a) On the difference-making account, yes: had Imani watered the plants they would have survived, so the deaths depend on her not watering, and her omission counts as a cause. This is the right result, and it's what anyone would say. The process account can't say it, since there is nothing in Imani's not-watering for the plants to interact with; what actually did the work was the heat and the soil drying out, and that is a perfectly ordinary causal process. The counterfactual does have to be read carefully — we hold the weather and the state of the greenhouse fixed and ask whether her watering would have made a difference, and it would have. So I conclude that the dehydration is the cause of death in the full-blooded sense, and Imani's omission is a cause in the thinner difference-making sense. That is enough to make "the plants died because Imani didn't water them" true, and it is also enough for the department to stop paying her, though that last bit is the moral question and I said I'd leave it alone.

(b) Orlando's omission is not a cause of the deaths. Since Imani was never going to water them either, the plants were going to die whatever Orlando did, and his watering them would have made no difference, because the outcome was already fixed by her failure. The most that can be said is that the two failures taken together caused the deaths. This is structurally the same as the standard overdetermination case we did with the two arsonists, where neither fire is a cause on its own because each is enough to burn the building down; the only novelty here is that both contributions are negative, and that makes no difference to the logic.

Priya is the more interesting one. On the bare counterfactual test her omission is a cause as well: had she watered them the plants would have lived, and she could easily have done it. But that is absurd — you don't want to say that every neighbour, every passer-by, and the Queen is a cause of the death of the plants. So the difference-making account needs a normative filter, and the filter is this: an omission counts as a cause only if the agent was supposed to act, that is, only if she had some duty, role or commitment that made acting required of her. Priya had none, so she drops out; Orlando had promised, so he comes in — though, as I argued above, he then drops out again for the overdetermination reason. The filter has to be part of the causal test itself rather than an extra bit of moralising bolted on afterwards, because otherwise the theory doesn't merely give an unuseful answer about Priya, it gives a false one.

(c) Anchored or free-floating, the verdict is the same: Ravi's failure caused the deaths, because the counterfactual is the same either way. Whether he decided not to switch the pump on or never thought about it at all, had he switched it on the plants would have lived. The distinction between the two cases is a psychological one about what went on inside Ravi's head, and causation isn't the sort of thing that can turn on the contents of someone's head. The contrast does show something about what omissions would have to be to be causes, though: in the anchored case there is at least a real event there for a process theorist to point at, whereas in the free-floating case there is nothing at any time or place at all. So if free-floating omissions can be causes, as I think they can, then we are committed to causes with no location, which is odd but not fatal — plenty of things we want to call causes are pretty abstract.

The stolen key does change things. Causation by omission presupposes that the agent could have acted; Ravi could not have got into the pump room, so there is no omission of his to speak of, and his not switching on the pump is no more a cause than the failure of a passing cloud to water the plants.

To sum up: omissions are causes whenever the outcome counterfactually depends on them, and the process theorist's objection is really only an objection to calling them causes in a full-blooded sense; it doesn't touch the counterfactual claim itself.
