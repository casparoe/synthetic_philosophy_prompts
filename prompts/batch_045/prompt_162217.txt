I'm introducing a screening of *Amadeus* at the film society I help run next month, and I want to do more than tell the audience it is a film about envy. I've been reading Hume on the standard of taste — how we can say some works are better than others while conceding that tastes plainly vary — and the film looks to me like a dramatisation of the conditions Hume sets for a competent judge: Salieri has the delicacy of taste, the training and the practice, and lacks precisely the disinterestedness Hume asks for. But I also think the film presses a worry Hume never quite answers, which I've started calling the Salieri problem: whether fine discrimination and envy are separable at all. The person best placed to judge a rival's excellence may be the person least able to judge it impartially, precisely because they can see exactly how good it is and have the most to lose by it. If that is right, Hume's ideal critic is not merely hard to find but internally unstable, and the standard of taste is doing less work than it appears to.

I'd like a reading that takes both sides seriously. Which scenes would you point the audience to, and what does each show — Salieri's first encounter with Mozart's music, the Requiem dictation, the blessing of the mediocrities at the end? Where is the Salieri problem a genuine objection to Hume, and where does it shrink into a psychological fact about one bitter man that the theory can absorb? And where does the film resist the reading: Salieri is an unreliable narrator about his own motives, and possibly about Mozart's genius too, so I don't want to take his testimony at face value. Finally, does the ending vindicate objectivity at all, or is the verdict of posterity just dramatic irony wearing an argument's clothes?

Please say how confident you are in each of your main claims and flag the one you're least sure of. I'd rather not stand up in front of a room and assert something a Hume specialist would wince at.
