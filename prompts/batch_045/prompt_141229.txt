I am revising a paper on the identity of indiscernibles and have talked myself into a conclusion I am confident is false. I would like you to locate the step I should reject and to tell me what rejecting it costs me elsewhere.

Set-up. Take a world to be a domain of objects together with the qualitative properties and relations instantiated in it and its laws. Call two objects indiscernible in a world when they share every qualitative monadic property and stand in all the same relations to all the same third objects; call them weakly discerned when some irreflexive relation holds between them. Let the principle under discussion be: necessarily, any two objects that are indiscernible are identical. The test case is Black's two-sphere world: two exactly similar spheres some distance apart in an otherwise empty universe, each indiscernible from the other.

My argument:

(1) Indiscernibility is symmetry. Objects x and y are indiscernible in a world w if and only if some automorphism of w — a permutation of w's domain preserving every qualitative property, relation and law — maps x to y. This is just the model-theoretic rendering of sharing all qualitative features, and it is the only rendering I know that is precise.

(2) The mirroring lemma. Suppose w contains indiscernible x and y, with s an automorphism of w exchanging them. Now enrich w in any way you like — add a landmark off to one side, an asymmetric field, anything meant to break the symmetry. Add that enrichment together with its image under s, with the image's internal structure and its relations to the rest defined as the s-image of the original's. The result is another world in which s extends to an automorphism exchanging x and y. So for every enrichment somebody proposes in order to discern the spheres, there is a further enrichment in which the spheres are still indiscernible.

(3) The two-sphere case therefore cannot be dismissed as an artifact of a deliberately empty world. Indiscernible pairs are not confined to sparse worlds; they survive enrichment designed to eliminate them.

(4) And these worlds are possible. They are completely and non-redundantly describable, no contradiction follows from the description, and the conceivability involved is not the thin kind we get from merely failing to see an impossibility.

(5) So it is possible for x and y to be distinct while indiscernible, that is, to differ solo numero. The obvious escape is weak discernibility: the relation being ten miles from is irreflexive and each sphere bears it to the other. But weak discernibility yields no property that one sphere has and the other lacks, and if it sufficed, then bare numerical distinctness would suffice as well, since distinctness is irreflexive too, and the principle would collapse into a tautology that no one would have bothered to assert.

(6) What makes the spheres two in that world is not their distance. Distance is how the example happens to be told. Once distinctness is allowed to outrun quality at all, what is possible is a possibility about identity itself rather than about arrangements, and nothing in it requires separation, or requires the objects not to share a location, or not to share all their parts.

(7) Hence there could be a world qualitatively indiscernible from the actual one — same furniture, same arrangement, same history, same evidence available to everyone in it — in which each object is accompanied by any number of coincident duplicates sharing its location, its matter and every relation it bears. Every sphere in front of me is a stack of seventeen.

(8) But then how many objects are in front of me has no ground, no possible evidence and no answer, and every ordinary count-claim might be false without there being any difference at all. I do not accept that, so one of (1) through (7) is false.

If I must choose, I would rather keep (1), (2) and (4) — the symmetry criterion, the mirroring lemma and the possibility of the mirrored worlds — than accept (8). But I want to know what I am paying, and whether the step I most want to keep is the one that is actually broken.

Please tell me: which step fails; why that step looked obviously right when I wrote it; and, if it fails by an equivocation or a shift, exactly which expression changes meaning between which two steps. Then price at least the following alternatives, whether or not you choose them: denying the possibility of the mirrored worlds on Leibniz-style grounds, and what such a denial commits you to about other symmetric worlds; the weak-discernibility reply, and what it leaves of the principle's content; the view that distinctness is grounded in spatial position or in exclusive occupancy of a region, including whether that merely relocates the ungrounded difference into space and reawakens the shift argument; and biting the bullet at (8). One further candidate to evaluate: my supervisor says (1) is question-begging, because if there were non-qualitative thisness properties then no permutation would preserve them and nothing would ever count as indiscernible. Is that right, and if it is, does the principle survive it as a substantive thesis or only as a trivial one?

I want the structure of the argument rather than the state of the literature, so please argue from the steps themselves and do not rest anything on what a particular author has claimed.
