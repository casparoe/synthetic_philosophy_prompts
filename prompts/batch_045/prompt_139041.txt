I'm a fourth-year PhD student in political science working on crisis bargaining, and I'm leading a ninety-minute seminar section on coercion for advanced undergraduates: eighteen students, mostly international relations majors with a scattering of philosophy majors, nearly all of them with one game-theory course behind them and two or three who have read some of the classical mid-century deterrence literature. I need a game for that session, and I need it to do something specific.

The situation I want them to walk into is this: two states, a disputed territory, a limited crisis, and a shared catastrophe they both want to avoid. Two players per board, several rounds with rotating pairings, and a stake small enough to be symbolic — participation credit or a token prize, no money. I've tried versions of this before and they all fail in the same place. Students quickly work out that threats are cheap, conclude that nothing anyone says is credible, and settle into a dull standoff. The genuinely paradoxical part never appears: that an actor in a crisis may need to engineer a situation in which its own later behaviour is no longer fully under its control, and that this can be the *reasonable* thing to do. I want the mechanism of the game to produce that, not my introduction to it.

Two features I'd like built into the rules, and I'm offering them as hypotheses about the design rather than as instructions:

First, a separation between raising the *probability* of the catastrophic outcome and raising its *magnitude*. My sense is that what coercion requires is probability-raising, but that the only probability-raising instruments available to a state are also irreversible — they burn options, alert forces, commit prestige — so that real coercion works by degrading your own future agency rather than by cleanly manipulating risk. Could the game give players two distinct and separately purchasable kinds of escalation move, one that moves the chance and one that forecloses a retreat, and then arrange the payoffs so that the players discover the two are less separable than they looked? I'd like them to feel the pull toward irreversibility as a solution to a credibility problem, and only later to see what it costs.

Second, a last-clear-chance asymmetry: at some point in the crisis, one side rather than the other will have the final opportunity to step back before the risk machinery takes over. I want that assignment to be visible, to be resented by whoever holds it, and to be tradeable or manoeuvrable in some way, so that a part of the game becomes an attempt not to be the one holding it.

Then a second block. I want the same game run again with a misperception parameter introduced — each escalation move has some chance of being read by the adversary as more aggressive than it was, with neither side knowing the true reading at the time — so that the group can compare the risk players *intended* to run with the risk they actually generated. In my experience students conflate deliberate risk-taking with accidental escalation, and I'd like the debrief to use the contrast between the two blocks to pull them apart rather than to merge them.

Practical constraints: no lecture before play, and the words I care about shouldn't be uttered until the debrief. Risk must be lived rather than described, so the catastrophic outcome should be settled by an actual randomising device at the table — cards or dice, not a number on a handout. Materials need to be things I can carry into a classroom. I need a timeline in minutes that fits ninety minutes with room for the debrief, and I need to know what to do about the pairs that resolve their crisis in ninety seconds and the pairs that are still manoeuvring when time is up.

For the debrief I want specific moments in the game mapped onto specific features of the problem, including at least: why cheap talk is worthless here and what that has to do with the structure rather than with anyone's sincerity; why the player who most convincingly lost control of the situation tended to do best; and how the misperception block changes the verdict delivered by the clean block. I also want an honest forecast of what will go wrong — gaming the scoring, collusion between partners, students treating the catastrophic payoff as just a big negative number, the one pair that escalates for the fun of it and derails the session — with handling notes for each.

Finally, tell me plainly what the game cannot reproduce and what I will have to supply myself. My own suspicion is that the fatal simplification is the payoff structure: real crises involve adversaries who do not know each other's resolve, and a nuclear exchange is not representable by any finite negative number, so the game may reproduce the logic of brinkmanship while quietly deleting exactly what makes nuclear brinkmanship distinctive. If that's right, say so and tell me how to raise it honestly in the last ten minutes without pretending the game settled it. If I'm wrong, say which simplification is actually doing the damage.
