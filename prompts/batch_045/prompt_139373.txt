I build perception stacks for autonomous mobile robots, and my working vocabulary is this one: sensor models and noise, intrinsic versus extrinsic calibration, Bayesian state estimation, latent state and learned world models, occupancy grids, labels versus ground truth, simulation-to-real gap, distribution shift, loss functions and training distributions, error budgets, failure modes, and the gap between a specification and an implementation that satisfies or violates it. I have read no philosophy beyond a few blog-level run-ins with the Chinese room argument.

What I want restated in that vocabulary is the dispute over original (or intrinsic) intentionality versus derived intentionality, together with Dennett's two-bitser reply to the way Searle draws the line. The dispute as I have it: Searle holds that a person's thought is about a thing in a way that borrows nothing from any interpreter, whereas sentences, maps, and the symbol shuffling inside a computer are about things only in a way borrowed from the minds of whoever makes, uses, or interprets them; Dennett's two-bitser, a crude device with a couple of input lines hooked up to the world, is meant to show either that the line between the two sorts of aboutness collapses or that the un-borrowed sort is far cheaper than Searle allows. If I have that wrong, say so in a sentence and then carry on with the corrected version.

First, give me the translation: state each position as carefully as you can using only my vocabulary, telling me what maps onto what (what plays the role of a designer's intention, of content, of misrepresentation, of an interpreter) and flag explicitly wherever you find yourself importing an assumption from my framework instead of translating.

Then run the translation against three cases I actually worry about, and for each say what each side is committed to:

1. Calibration drift. A lidar's extrinsic calibration drifts slowly over months, so the robot's occupancy grid is systematically rotated by about two degrees. Nobody intended that transformation, nobody interprets the grid that way, and the system's own error model does not represent it. Is the drifted grid about a world that is not there, and if so, from what does that aboutness derive?

2. A latent with no label. A self-supervised model trained on an objective that never mentions chairs develops a direction that a linear probe recovers as tracking chair-ness, and a downstream planner later comes to rely on it after reinforcement fine-tuning. Does the content of that direction come from the designers, from the training distribution, from the downstream use, or from none of them?

3. An unspec'd protocol. Two robots trained with multi-agent reinforcement learning converge on a signal neither engineer wrote into the message specification; interpretability work later lets the team decode it. Is its aboutness derived from the post-hoc interpretation, from the training objective, or from something else?

Finally, the residue report. Which claims of the dispute translate cleanly into my vocabulary, which survive only with distortion, and which have no expression in it at all — and is that residue a defect of engineering vocabulary, or a sign that the philosophical claim was less clear than it looked? I especially want your verdict on whether the notion of misrepresentation and the notion of grounding in my sense form a genuine third option that is neither of Searle's two categories.

Lead with your bottom line on whether the distinction survives the translation, in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restatement of my question. Use American English, and keep the whole answer under about 1,100 words.
