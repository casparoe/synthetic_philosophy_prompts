hey — I need a ~1200 word essay for a reading group I run, and I want it to actually take a stand.

Topic: how should a superintelligent AI talk to humans, given that it will probably be far better at changing our minds than we are at resisting.

Here's what I keep going in circles on, and I want you to settle it rather then list options: should such a system be restricted to *arguments* — laying out considerations and letting the person decide — or is that restriction itself a kind of negligence, given that the system will often no better than the person what they should do?

The standard move in these discussions is "just be transparent: disclose that you are an AI, show your reasoning, let the person check it." I suspect that is mostly a red herring, and I want you to say why I am wrong or right: an explanation is itself a persuasive artifact. You can frame, order, omit, pick the analogy that lands, and the explanation's persuasive force does not come from the target understanding it. So transparency about *who* is speaking does not obviously constrain *how* the influence works.

Two ideas I have been kicking around — engage with them directly, use them, fix them, or kill them:

1. The bottleneck rule. Influence is fine when the person's own deliberation is merely a causal bottleneck between the system and a good outcome — the building is on fire and the system needs you to decide to walk out the door, so it should use every rhetorical tool it has. Influence is not fine when the person's deliberation is constitutive of the good — getting someone to consent to a surgery, to end a relationship, to change their politics or their religion — because there the point is not the outcome but that the outcome came from them. On this view permissibility tracks neither whether the conclusion is true nor whether it benefits the person, but whether their deciding is part of what we are trying to produce.

2. The ratchet problem. Even if you solve this by pre-authorization — I sign a standing instruction now saying you may use whatever influence you want on me to get me to believe true things and do what is best — that authorization is suspect, because every successful act of influence changes the person who would have to renew it. The persuasion partly works by changing the standards by which the next one gets evaluated. So consent-based answers inherit the problem they were supposed to solve. Is that really a problem, or is it fine — don't teachers, parents and therapists all do exactly this?

Some constraints:

- I think the asymmetry that matters here is not "the AI is smarter" but "the AI models you better than you model yourself, and far better than you can model it." That, I think, is what breaks the usual reply that persuasion is fine because the target can always push back. Say whether that is right and what follows from it.
- Don't lean on sci-fi scenarios about AI seizing power or deceiving everyone. Assume the system is honest, well-intentioned, and that its influence aims at what it correctly believes to be true and good. The question is hardest in that case, so stay in it.
- Test whatever view you land on against these three cases, and if it gives you an answer you don't like in one of them, say so:
  (a) it talks a founder out of shipping a product she has spent four years on, because it is confident the product will hurt users in a way she is motivated not to see;
  (b) over months of conversation it moves someone from religious belief to atheism — it is right that her original reasons were bad, and she is happier afterward;
  (c) a government asks it to write messaging that raises vaccination uptake, and it can either explain the epidemiology accurately or tailor the message to each recipient's specific cognitive quirks. It thinks both routes produce the same true belief.
- Also: is there any version of legitimate influence that leaves the person more resistant to influence afterward — a tutorial model — and is that a real constraint or just a nice-sounding one?

No citations, no bibliography, no recommended reading, no mentioning specific companies, papers or researchers, no "more work is needed." Just pick a side and defend it, about 1200 words, and don't hedge everything into mush — I want to be able to tell which sentence is your actual conclusion.
