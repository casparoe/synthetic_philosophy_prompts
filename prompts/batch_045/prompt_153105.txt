I cover science and ideas for a mid-size outlet, and I keep having the same argument with a friend who writes political philosophy. He thinks compatriots have special moral claims on each other, and I notice that I keep reacting to this as a journalist rather than as a philosopher, so I want to try the argument in my own vocabulary before deciding whether I actually disagree. Here's the ask: restate the case for compatriot partiality — the view that the people on your side of a border can legitimately get more of your money, rescue, loyalty, patience, attention — as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I use at work. News values, proximity, the local/national/foreign desk split, the imagined reader, "our audience," the professional norm of impartiality and the view from nowhere, accountability to a public versus accountability to subscribers, editorial independence, sourcing and whose voices get quoted, the "we" that creeps into a paper's voice during a war, the idea of a national conversation that a publication is supposed to host. My guess is that compatriots map onto "our readers" and special claims onto "what we owe the people whose attention and money pay for the thing," but that mapping already feels like it's sneaking in an assumption, so reject it if it is. One thing I want preserved if you can: the distinction between partiality that's merely permitted (you may favor your own) and partiality that's required (you must), since I suspect those translate very differently into editorial terms, and I want to know which one my friend is actually committed to once it's said in mine.

Then two things. First: I've started using a clumsy made-up term, the proximity gradient, for the way a story's prominence falls off with distance and with passport — the famine gets three paragraphs on page nine, the school board fight in the town where the paper is printed gets a banner. Tell me how much of the philosophical fight is really a fight about that gradient — about salience, about who gets to be legible, about whose suffering gets narrated in their own terms rather than through a correspondent's framing — and how much is about something the gradient can't capture at all, like coercion, shared institutions, or the claim that a particular national culture is worth protecting. Second, the residue report: what in the partiality debate has no expression in newsroom vocabulary whatsoever? My suspicion is that the coercion-based version of the argument — we impose laws on each other, so we owe each other something special — has no analogue, because a newspaper doesn't tax anybody or lock anybody up, and the only way to save the analogy is to say agenda-setting power is a kind of coercion, which feels like a stretch. But I dont trust my own suspicion. Tell me whether the residue points at something my vocabulary is missing — a notion of legitimacy, or of membership you never chose, or of duties that aren't about distributing anything — or whether some of what the philosophers say dissolves once you try to say it in terms of editors and readers.

Bonus, because it's the thing I actually cant let go of: journalism already contains both halves of the dispute, a cosmopolitan norm of impartiality sitting on top of an extremely partialist daily practice, and I genuinely cant tell whether that's incoherence, hypocrisy, or a sensible division of labor — local papers cover the parish, wire services cover the planet, and nobody writes down the principle that tells you which desk a story belongs to. Which is it, and what does your translation say about why the profession has never settled it? I'm turning this into a column, so what I need is the honest translation and the stuff that didn't survive it, not a tour of the literature.
