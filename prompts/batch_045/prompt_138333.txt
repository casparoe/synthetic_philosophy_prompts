I work on a four-person team building an always-on sensor chip — wake-word spotting plus a bit of on-board inference — that runs off an energy harvester, so our whole power budget is a few milliwats and every design review is an argument about where the last microjoule goes. Lately people have started invoking the Maxwell's demon story and the Landauer limit (kT ln 2 per bit erased) at each other in both directions: to shoot down a proposal as impossible, and to claim that some other proposal is thermodynamically "free". Two reviews have already dissolved into an hour of arguing about whether the cost is in the measuring or in the erasing, and I don't want to relitigate that every time. I have undergrad thermo and can follow a derivation slowly; nobody on the team has a physics PhD. Reviews are every two weeks, maybe thirty minutes per proposal, and a staff engineer has to sign off on anything that goes into the spec.

What I want is a decision procedure — a checklist in words, with the questions in order — that we can actually run on a proposal in a review. My guess at the steps, fix it if it's wrong: what physical thing is carrying the information; is the operation logically reversible or does it collapse several input states onto one; what actually gets erased, and into what bath at what temperature; how many bits we're counting and whether we're accidentally counting bits written instead of bits reset; whether an apparent saving is real or is just the waste being moved somewhere we're not looking (off chip, into a colder reservoir, into a later pipeline stage, into accuracy); and whether we're even comparing against the right floor at all, since I've seen wildly different numbers for how far above kT ln 2 a real gate actually sits. For each step, say what counts as a good enough answer in thirty minutes, and mark the steps where the answer is really a judgment call we're dressing up as procedure. Say where the procedure can stop with a flat no without going any further, and where it has to hand the question to a person because it's a product requirement rather than physics.

Then run it on three things we've genuinely had in front of us: (1) a vendor's "reversable" multiply-accumulate array that supposedly dissipates almost nothing because it never erases bits — I need tests I can apply to their spec sheet, not a verdict on whether reversible computing is possible in principle; (2) our own trick of not clearing the 8-bit feature buffer every frame but rotating through a bigger SRAM and only resetting it once a day, so the erasure gets amortized; (3) a proposal to let the classifier return a wrong answer one time in a thousand and treat the resulting slack as free energy. Include at least one case where the procedure gives me an answer I won't like — I suspect it's (3), or possibly the whole exercise — and say so plainly if that's what happens.

Also flag the cases the procedure can't settle and say who decides them. Tell me where you're confident versus where you're extrapolating past what the physics actually licenses — I'd much rather see "this step is my judgment" than a tidy checklist that hides it, and if a step needs a number about real hardware you can't actually vouch for, say so instead of inventing one. And if the whole premise is off — if the demon story doesn't say what we think it says, or if kT ln 2 is the wrong thing for a team like ours to be checking against — tell me that up front instead of politely building me a procedure on a bad foundation.

Hard limit: 800 words, I'm pasting it straight into our review template.
