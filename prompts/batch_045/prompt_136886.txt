I am writing a short position paper for a graduate workshop on cooperation across moral disagreement, and before I circulate it I would like a consistency audit of the commitments it commits me to. The paper's thesis is that moral trade — roughly, the practice of one party performing what another party morally values in exchange for that party performing what the first values, in cases where each ranks the other's performance above their own — is an underused instrument, and that it is attractive precisely for agents who disagree deeply about what matters. My commitments, stated as plainly as I can, are these.

(a) Trade is required and not merely permitted: wherever two parties each prefer the other's favoured performance to their own, there is a Pareto improvement available in moral-preference-satisfaction, and when the gains are large relative to the costs of arranging the trade I hold that failing to seek it is a moral failing rather than an exercise of discretion.

(b) Value pluralism with genuine incommensurability: there is no master currency of moral value, and the point of trade is to accommodate divergence between value-outlooks rather than to adjudicate it.

(c) Rejection of interpersonal comparisons: I do not think one person's gain can be weighed against another's in any action-guiding way, and I hold that only comparisons within a single person's ranking are epistemically respectable. I rely on this both in defending (b) and in arguing that moral trade is a modest instrument that avoids the comparisons other approaches need.

(d) Integrity: an agent's ground projects can supply reasons that are not reducible to impersonal value, and it is not a defect in an agent that she would not abandon such a project even when the impersonal calculus recommends abandoning it. I take this line of objection to consequentialism to have real force.

(e) The one-thought-too-many point generalises beyond the intimate cases it is usually illustrated with: there are contexts in which the correct deliberation is not of the form what would best promote the good, and in which the very availability of that formulation is a sign that something has gone wrong in the agent's relation to what she cares about.

(f) A case verdict: I would refuse a trade whose performance on my side requires me to do something my own ground project forbids — say, a week of campaigning for a political party I hold to be gravely unjust, in exchange for their £20,000 to a cause I rank far higher — even though the trade is net positive by my own ranking. Doing strikes me as different from allowing.

(g) A second case verdict: in negotiating the terms of a trade I take it to be permissible to let my counterpart believe that I care more about my side than I in fact do, in order to secure better terms, since what matters is the outcome produced and their beliefs about the intensity of my concern form no part of what makes that outcome good.

(h) An institutional proposal: with two colleagues I am designing a standing moral trade ledger — anonymously brokered, with escrowed performance, and including a clean-hands rider permitting either party to decline any performance that their own ground project forbids — and I claim that the rider is very nearly costless, because it removes only trades that the parties would not in fact have performed anyway.

What I want is an audit rather than a survey: I do not need citations or suggestions for further reading. Please rank the tensions you find by severity, and for each say which commitments are pulling against each other, what exactly would have to give, and what the cheapest repair would be. I suspect some of what looks unstable to me is stable and some of what looks fine is not, so please say explicitly which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection and why — in particular, I want to know whether (b) and (c) really do undercut (a), or whether trade needs only intrapersonal comparisons and so survives them. Two things I cannot see clearly: first, whether the doing-versus-allowing distinction I lean on in (f) survives being embedded in a trade, given that in a trade each party's performance is causally downstream of the other's; and second, whether the clean-hands rider in (h) really is costless once (g) is granted, since both bear on how credible a commitment to perform can be. Finally, tell me how much of the trouble turns on the strength of (a): if it were weakened from a duty to seek trades to a permission to enter them, which of the tensions above would disappear and what attractive consequence would I lose by that weakening? Then say which single revision to the set would clean up the most, and what it would cost me.
