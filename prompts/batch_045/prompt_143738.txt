I've been working through this pop philosophy-of-mind article on the private language argument and it summarizes it in a way that sounds super clean, which makes me suspicious. Roughly, here's what it claims:

- Wittgenstein imagines you keeping a diary where you write 'S' every time you have a certain sensation, and argues this cant work, because youd have no way of telling whether you're using 'S' the same way twice. Whatever seems right to me is right, and where there's no gap between seeming right and being right, there is no such thing as being right. So 'S' doesn't really name anything, and a language whose words refer only to what its speaker can privately access isn't a language at all.

- The moral, it says, is that meaning is use, and use is a shared public practice: a sign means something only if there's a practice of using it that other people could in principle check you against.

- So Wittgenstein is basically a behaviorist. Pain isnt some hidden inner thing behind the behavior — it just is the behavior plus the circumstances in which we're inclined to say 'pain'. The famous beetle in the box drops out as irrelevant, and that's the whole point.

- It also says first-person authority goes the same way: I dont have any special access to my own pains, I find out I'm in pain by observing my own behavior and the situation, more or less the way everyone else does.

- The payoff, near the end, is that this settles a bunch of modern debates at once: once you see that there are no inner facts over and above the public criteria, the mystery of qualia dissolves, the problem of other minds was never a real problem, and there's no fact of the matter about whether some non-human animal, or a totally paralyzed patient with no outward response, is in pain — beyond whatever we could in principle check.

The bit that really got me was an aside right after: it says this is why someone who grew up entirely alone, never encountered another speaker, and invented signs for their own sensations couldnt have a language at all, and arguably couldnt even think, since there'd be no community to check their usage against.

I vaguely remember hearing from somewhere that Wittgenstein wasnt a behaviorist and wasnt trying to deny that sensations exist, so I'm fairly sure at least one of these bullets is off — but I cant tell which, or by how much, and I dont know whether the rest is just the standard simplified version. Can you go through it and seperate out what a Wittgenstein scholar would happily accept, what they'd call a fair simplification, and what they'd reject or say is a straight misreading? And does the big payoff at the end — the qualia / other minds / animals-and-locked-in-patients stuff — actually follow from the parts that are right, or is it leaning on the distortions?
