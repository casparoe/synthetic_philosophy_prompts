In the last decade or so of his life Mill moved, in essays that appeared in print only after his death, towards a heavily qualified and gradualist socialism. I want to know what that late Mill — not the Mill of *On Liberty*, and not the Mill of the earlier editions of the *Principles* — would have made of developments he did not live to see, and I want an answer that commits to a verdict rather than one that surveys the options.

Take his late position to be roughly the following. If you think this capsule misdescribes him, say briefly where, and answer on the basis of your own reading instead; but I would rather you used my characterisation than hedged. (i) The relation of employer and employed is a stage in industrial development rather than its terminus, and it is to be outgrown through associated production in which the workers are collectively their own capitalist and take the profits of the concern. (ii) Private property and competition are to be reformed, circumscribed and outcompeted, not abolished; production is not to be absorbed into a single salaried officialdom, since a bureaucracy governing industry is, in his view, among the gravest of political dangers. (iii) The transition must be gradual, voluntary and experimental: cooperatives should have to prove themselves against ordinary capitalist firms in the market, and their failures are information, not tragedy. (iv) The point of the whole thing is moral rather than merely distributive — the education of the labourer in prudence, independence and self-government — and any arrangement that purchases material security at the cost of that education has made a bad bargain. (v) Communism, in the Owenite and Fourierist forms he discussed, is rejected as impracticable precisely because it severs the connexion between a person's exertion and their reward, and effort would slacken. (vi) Large fortunes should not pass intact between the generations; the right of bequest is to be cut down, not abolished. (vii) The indefinite accumulation of capital and growth of population are not clearly desirable: he wrote, unusually for a political economist of his century, that a stationary state might well be an improvement on the universal scrambling and elbowing of commercial society.

Now suppose he is shown three things. First, the actual record of twentieth-century socialism: central planning, its chronic difficulties with incentives and with the local knowledge that prices carry, the stagnation, and the collapse of the planned economies around 1989–91, with the Chinese variant thereafter combining party control with markets and private ownership. Second, the Nordic settlement: universal welfare provision, strong unions, high taxation, generous public services, and yet capitalist ownership and market discipline left essentially intact, with cooperative ownership a marginal phenomenon. Third, a package now being proposed in a rich democracy in which advanced automation has been driving the share of national income going to labour steadily downwards: a universal dividend paid out of the returns on a publicly held capital fund; a legal right of first refusal for employees whenever a firm of any size is sold, closed or relocates, backed by subsidised credit for buy-outs; board-level codetermination; and a default rule under which a firm whose owner dies or retires without a successor passes into an employee trust unless the state expressly licenses a sale.

Where does he land? I am interested less in a list of endorsements than in the reasoning that produces them, and in particular in these cruxes. Between the dividend and the cooperative, which does the late Mill regard as the genuine successor to the wage relation, and why? Given commitment (v), does he not have to treat an unconditional income as a new and more insidious dependency, or does he have resources to argue that it is precisely the security that makes cooperation, refusal of bad work, and theeducation of character possible? What does he do with the fact that the demographic transition has inverted his population anxiety, so that the scarce factor in rich countries is now labour rather than capital? Does the ecological ceiling — a stationary state imposed by climate and resources rather than chosen — vindicate (vii) or pervert it? And which of (i) to (vii) survives the posthumous record least well: is his gradualism refuted, or was it the one part of him that events vindicated?

Finally, tell me what single piece of evidence from after 1873 would most change his mind, and give me his answer to two objectors: a Marxist who says the history of the twentieth century showed that gradualism was always an illusion, and a social democrat who says the whole question was settled by the Nordics without any of his machinery at all.
