I'm in my third year, philosophy of physics, writing a chapter on background independence (my supervisor is an extremely committed relationist; im... less sure), and for a reading-group/public-engagement thing we're doing I want a short story that *is* the hole argument, rather than a story with a physics lecture stapled to it. I can't write fiction to save my life. So: write it for me?

Premise, which you should feel free to steal, rewrite or throw out entirely as long as the concept survives:

A few centuries on, physicists think they've found a way out of the hole argument. Call it the Weft — a fixed, non-dynamical web of "nodes" threaded through spacetime at Planck-ish scales, which is *not* dragged along when you slide the metric around. If the Weft is real, the slide leaves a trace: you can say "the curvature bump is at node 7 and not node 12" and that's a genuine physical difference, determinism is back, and the substantivalists were right all along. The story follows the people running the experiment meant to settle it (call it the Slide, or the Drift, whatever), and the thing I want dramatised is the trap: if the Weft is a real *dynamical* field, it gets dragged along with everything else and the hole argument just reappears one level up, now about the enlarged set of fields; if it's genuinely non-dynamical, then it's an absolute object of exactly the kind GR was supposed to have abolished, and determinism has been bought by smuggling absolute space back in through the Planck-scale door. Something is paid for either way.

Things I actually care about:

- Get the argument right, and right enough that nobody in my reading group sends me an angry email. The version I mean: take a matter-free region (the hole); take a diffeomorphism that's the identity outside it and non-trivial inside; apply it to a solution of the field equations. Because the stress-energy vanishes in the hole, the map is the identity outside it, and the equations don't care which point is which, what you get is a second solution that agrees with the first everywhere outside the hole and on any initial-data surface before it — but hands different curvature to the very same spacetime points inside. If points have their identity independently of the fields, that's two genuinely different futures from one past: indeterminism. And not just in some exotic region either — any old vacuum region will do, so it's everywhere, all the time. If points are individuated only by what goes on at them, the two solutions are one physical situation described twice and the indeterminism evaporates. Also worth getting in somewhere: no rod, clock or detector inside the hole can tell the two apart, because the rods, clocks and detectors get slid too. That's precisely why the Weft would change everything.
- At least one scene where the argument gets said out loud — a seminar, a corridor fight, a recorded briefing, a training sim, a drunk conversation at 3am — but doing something while it's said. An accusation, a threat, a seduction, a confession. Not a wise older professor explaining matters to a nodding student.
- A reason to care that isn't "physics is neat". My half-baked idea: the team has laid a claim on, or engraved a plaque for, one particular spacetime point inside the hole — the spot where somebody died — and if there's no primitive fact about which point that was, the plaque doesn't denote, and the grief was aimed at nothing in particular. Use it if it helps, find something better if it doesn't.
- The ending has to commit. No "both horns turned out to be true in a deeper sense", no vaguely quantum-gravitational wave of the hand, no waking up and discovering it was all a coordinate choice. Have someone choose, or have something happen that settles it, and let the reader feel what that cost.
- No Asimov-style dialogue where one character exists only to be explained at, and no explaining GR from first principles — but don't be so elliptical that the argument goes invisible either. Assume a smart reader who's never heard the word "manifold".

If it matters: I'd definately rather it came out a bit strange and a bit sad than tidy. And if you think my premise is metaphysical nonsense, tell me in two or three lines after the story — then write it anyway.
