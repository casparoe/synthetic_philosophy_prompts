The question I set was:

In no more than 400 words, explain the spandrels critique of adaptationism (Gould and Lewontin, 1979) to a general reader without caricaturing it. Give one biological example of a trait that might be a spandrel, and say what would have to be true for that example to work.

Here is one student's answer:

Adaptationism is the habit of taking each feature of an organism separately and asking what problem natural selection designed it to solve. Gould and Lewontin's 1979 paper, The Spandrels of San Marco and the Panglossian Paradigm, attacked this as an unreflective research programme, and compared adaptationists to Voltaire's Dr Pangloss, for whom everything is for the best in this best of all possible worlds.

Their central point is about by-products. The spandrels of the title are the tapering triangular patches of masonry you necessarily get when you set a dome on four rounded arches: nobody designed them, they just fall out of the geometry, and only afterwards did the mosaicists of St Mark's fill them with figures. In the same way, plenty of features of organisms are not there because selection built them for a job, but because they come along for free with something else — a change in overall size, a shared developmental programme, what the lineage happened to inherit. And since a spandrel is by definition not a product of selection, it is invisible to selection and can never afterwards be shaped or refined by it; a by-product is stuck with whatever form it happens to have. If it does turn out to be useful later on, it becomes an exaptation — Gould and Vrba's term — so spandrel and exaptation are really two names for the same thing, just viewed at different stages of the trait's history.

A good example for a general reader is the nipples of male mammals. The basic mammalian body plan is laid down before sexual differentiation kicks in, so males have nipples not because nipples ever did anything for males but as a consequence of a developmental programme shared with females. What has to be true for this to work is that nipples in males are not currently doing any work and did not arise because of anything they did for males.

None of this denies that selection is the main source of adaptive complexity. The paper is a plea for pluralism: check the alternatives and test the adaptive story instead of assuming it.
