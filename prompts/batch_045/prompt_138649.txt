hey — i'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training beyond blog posts, and i've gotten into a recurring lunch-walk argument with a friend who works in formal verification and is some kind of finitist. his position as near as i can state it: there is no such thing as a completed infinite collection; infinite just means you can always take one more step; and when mathematicians talk about all the natural numbers as an object that's somehow all there at once they've made a category mistake that happens to be harmless, because it never changes any answer about finite things.

i keep bouncing off everything i try to read about this, because it's all either a defense of one camp or a history-of-math story. so before i read any more positions i want the spec: what does a position on the actual infinite have to get right in order to count as adequate?

purpose, so adequacy isn't just floating: i want to come out able to say, for the math i actually lean on at work, whether my friend's view should make me stop trusting it. continuous probability in simulations, limits and series in numerical code, lazily-defined infinite data structures, the type theory baked into the tools i use. i want a position that returns a verdict on which of that is load-bearing, and i want to know which bits of the verdict follow from the position and which are just my friend's temperament.

here's my rough candidate list, with the case that made me think of each. tell me which are real desiderata, which are one camp's prejudices in a suit, and what i'm missing.

1. it has to say what's wrong with the hotel, or why nothing is. full hotel, infinitely many rooms, infinitely many new guests arrive, everyone shifts to the even-numbered room, done. and it has to take a stand on whether being putable in one-to-one correspondence with a proper part of yourself is the *definition* of being infinite or a *symptom* of it, because my friend uses it as the definition and then says the definition is bogus in the same breath.

2. here's my variant, which is the thing that actually bugs me: same hotel, but the manager can only carry out finitely many room reassignments per minute, and all guests have to be in rooms by a fixed deadline. now the trick looks like it needs infinitely many moves in finite time. so has the hotel story been quietly helping itself to a supertask all along? requirement: the position has to say whether it's assuming infinitely many operations can be completed, and if it denies that, whether it still owes me an explanation of why the hotel felt fine.

3. the halving loop. a program that sleeps 1/2^n seconds before its nth step and toggles a boolean each time; after two seconds it has run infinitely many steps. what is the boolean at t=2? the honest engineering answer is that the question has no answer, the clock model breaks, the machine would have crashed. is that a legitimate philosophical answer or a dodge? requirement: take a stand on whether a completed supertask has a determinate final state, and if you say the question is ill-posed, say what makes it ill-posed rather than just gesturing at it.

4. lazy structures. in a lazy language i can define all the primes and take the first ten; the runtime materializes finitely many cells and the rest is a recipe. my friend says: potential infinity, obviously. i say: the thing in the source has infinitely many elements and i can meaningfully ask whether some property holds of all of them. whichever of us is right, requirement: the position has to place never-fully-materialized objects somewhere on the potential/actual line, because this is the case that makes me suspect the line is blurrier than either camp wants.

5. the always-add-one trap. if finitism is just no completed infinite collection but you can always extend, then is there a number N at which N+1 fails to exist? if there's no such N, haven't you just quantified over all of them? requirement: don't collapse unbounded into infinite, or the other way, without arguing; and state whether the position is committed to strict finitism (a largest number, or numbers we can't in reach) or not, because my friend slides between the two whenever i push.

6. floating-point reals. on a machine there are only finitely many representable reals, and cantor's diagonal argument doesn't obviously have anything to say about them. requirement: the position has to tell me what the uncountability of the reals is a fact *about* — a discovered feature of a real object, a consequence of rules we adopted, or something with no cash value for a finite machine — and whether a position that can't preserve it is thereby in trouble.

7. the trust requirement. infinitary math is inside engineering that works. a finitist has to either say it's eliminable in principle (and say what that means for the parts nobody can actually eliminate in practice), or say it's a useful fiction (and say what makes a fiction reliable), or give up and call it true. separately: is the claim that infinite detours never let you prove a new finite fact something the position gets to assume, or does it owe me an argument? i have a fuzzy memory of some incompleteness-ish result from the 1930s that's supposed to make a finitary guarantee of that kind impossible; if that's right, say so and say which desideratum has to go.

8. scope. is the claim about the world or about mathematics? my friend doesn't seem to care whether space is infinitely divisible or whether there are infinitely many particles, but cares a lot whether the reals form a completed object. requirement: separate the in-the-world claim from the in-the-theory claim and say which one is being made. i suspect a lot of arguments run on sliding between them.

9. and one i can't tell is real: we all seem to agree about what's true of the natural numbers even though on his view there's no such object to agree about. does a position owe me an account of that, or is that only a problem for positions that already made a wrong turn?

then: sort the cleaned-up list into non-negotiable versus this-is-just-one-camp's-preference; flag any subset you think can't be satisfied together and say which one gives; tell me which single requirement does the most work in knocking candidates out; and tell me which of my items are secretly the same requirement written twice. if you think some part of the list has no tensions at all, say that explicitly, because i'll be suspicious of a list where nothing pulls.

a short scorecard of the main camps against the list is welcome at the end — finitism, the standard sets-are-fine view, strict finitism — but keep it brief, the list is the deliverable. and please no reading recommendations, i'll find those myself.
