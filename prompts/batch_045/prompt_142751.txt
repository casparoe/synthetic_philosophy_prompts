Exam question:

In On the Uses and Disadvantages of History for Life, Nietzsche famously distinguishes three modes of history—monumental, antiquarian, and critical—and insists that history should serve life rather than become a detached science. For each mode, (a) say what sort of need of life it answers, and (b) state the characteristic danger Nietzsche associates with an excess or misuse of that mode.

Then consider the following claim: Antiquarian history is really Nietzsche's version of the suprahistorical perspective. Because it cultivates a reverent attachment to origins and a sense of continuity, it lifts us out of becoming and lets us see existence as finished, stable, and justified—much as art and religion do.

Assess this claim: is it correct? Does it confuse anything? In your answer, explain where the three modes sit relative to Nietzsche's contrast between the historical, the unhistorical (the power of forgetting and present-mindedness), and the suprahistorical (the eternalizing standpoint associated with art and religion).

Student answer:

Nietzsche's target is the idea that history is a value-neutral science that should just accumulate facts for their own sake. For him the standard is life: history is healthy only when it serves the needs of living, acting, and creating. He divides historical inquiry into three modes. Monumental history looks to the great moments and great individuals of the past. It serves life by giving us models and examples, reminding us that greatness was once possible and so might be again, which can inspire action in the present. Its characteristic danger is that it can turn into myth-making: it rips deeds out of their context, exaggerates the exceptional, and can leave the present feeling dwarfed or pushed toward empty imitation, or even paralyzed by a sense that the age of greatness is over. Antiquarian history is the conserving, reverent attitude that wants to preserve the past of a community, its customs, language, and origins. It answers the need for belonging, identity, and rootedness, and it protects a culture from being swept away by mere novelty. Its danger is that it can degenerate into mummification or a blind collector's mania: it refuses to judge or prune, gets buried under trivia, and smothers new growth because everything inherited is treated as equally sacred. Critical history is the mode that drags the past before a tribunal. It serves life when an individual or an age needs to break free from an oppressive inheritance; by ruthlessly interrogating and condemning the past, it opens space for the future. Its danger is that it can become purely destructive: because we are the products of the very past we condemn, we cannot fully escape it, and if critical history dominates it can leave us with nothing to build on and no reverence for what came before.

Now, the claim about antiquarian history and the suprahistorical. I think the claim is basically right, and it is one of the more perceptive things one can say about the essay. Nietzsche distinguishes the historical from the unhistorical and the suprahistorical. The unhistorical is the animal-like power of forgetting and being absorbed in the present; the suprahistorical is the standpoint that turns away from becoming altogether and sees existence as eternal, finished, and justified, and Nietzsche associates this with art and religion. Antiquarian history, as I described it, is precisely the historical mode that tries to step out of the flux of becoming: it is not chiefly concerned with action or with changing the future, but with preserving and contemplating the past as something stable and complete. That is why it lifts us out of becoming and gives us a sense that existence is finished and meaningful. Among the three modes, it is the one closest to the suprahistorical perspective, and indeed the suprahistorical is the highest standpoint in the essay because it alone escapes the sickness of pure becoming. The deepest answer to the modern sickness of too much history, then, is not more critical history or a better balance of the three modes; it is a recovery of antiquarian piety, a reverent preservation of the past that quietly reattaches us to something eternal. The claim that antiquarian history is Nietzsche's version of the suprahistorical therefore captures an important truth.
