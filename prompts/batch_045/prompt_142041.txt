I teach an upper-level seminar on formal epistemology — 14 students, one 90-minute slot, and they've already had the standard possible-worlds treatment of belief (you believe P just in case P is true in every world compatible with what you believe). I want a game that makes them feel the problem of logical omniscience in their gut, not just be able to state it on an exam.

Set it up as a betting market. Everyone gets a pile of points, no real money, and buys or sells stakes in propositions about some concrete setup I can vary (a card deal, a seating plan, whatever works best). Arrange the propositions in disguised layers: a few are immediate consequences of what they were told, a few need three or four steps to get to, a couple are literally the same claim they already bet on with different words wrapped round it, and one or two are tautologies nobody will recognise in the time available. Score them with a strictly proper scoring rule and run an explicit Dutch-book check at the end, so they catch themselves having paid more for a repackaged A than for A, or gone high on A and low on A-or-B. I also want a wrinkle: they can spend points to buy extra reasoning resources — more time, a calculator, two minutes with someone else's notes. That should make the question of whether you were being irrational before you paid, or just poor, something they start arguing about on there own. No lecture up front, and nobody says the name until afterwards.

Then give me the debrief: which moments map onto closure under logical consequence, which map onto the difference between patching the models (impossible worlds, awareness, syntactic or structural belief) and giving up the norm itself, and which map onto the resource-bounded reply. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about what the game can't reproduce — my worry is that it only ends up showing people are slow, which is an empirical fact and not the philosophical problem.
