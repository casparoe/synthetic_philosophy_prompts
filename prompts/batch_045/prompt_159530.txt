I am putting together the normative methodology section of a report that will grade the long-horizon decision procedures of real deliberative bodies — endowment committees, research funders, central banks — and before I commit to any one framework I want a specification: what would a theory of diachronic rationality have to deliver in order to be adequate for that purpose? Please give me the requirements, not a survey of the candidate theories or of who has defended them; I know the families and I do not need citations.

The purpose matters, so let me fix it, since adequacy is relative to it. The theory is to be good enough to grade agents that are bounded, that are not fully unified over time, and whose precommitment technologies are imperfect — rules that successors can repeal, mandates that later majorities can reinterpret, costs that later stages can simply pay. So I want tractability, implementability, and robustness to only partial persistence treated as genuine desiderata with cases behind them, not set aside as engineering detail that a normative theory may ignore. If you think that collapses two separate lists — a theory of rational planning and a theory of what may be demanded of bounded agents — say so, and tell me which requirements belong on which.

Here are the cases I currently take to be load-bearing. For each, I want the requirement it generates, and a verdict on whether that requirement is non-negotiable or merely the preference of one camp. Please also correct my reading of the cases and add any you think I have missed.

The pump that no one profits from. A chooser's preferences over three holdings cycle — she prefers the first to the second, the second to the third, and the third to the first — and she will pay a pound at each step to trade. An exploiter offers the trades in sequence, but each trade costs him a fee equal to what he extracts, so he finishes no better off; she finishes holding what she started with and three pounds worse off; the money is dissipated to the exchange. Every step was one she preferred at the time, and no future stage of hers was treated against its own lights. Is there a defect here beyond the wasted three pounds? I take this case to separate a view on which the wrong-making feature in a money pump is exploitation or loss from one on which it is cyclic or intransitive change itself, and I want both readings stated as rival requirements rather than one quietly assumed.

The rotating committee. A standing investment committee of seven, three of whose seats rotate each year, so that consecutive years share four members and years five apart may share none. It faces a sequence of decisions structurally identical to a pump: each year's committee prefers the trade on offer that year, and the sequence returns the portfolio to where it began, minus costs. No member serves throughout; no member individually reverses a preference. Is this a case of diachronic irrationality at all, and can such a body be said to precommit when its only instrument is a rule a later majority can repeal? What must a theory say about partially persisting agents — is that a requirement on the theory of rationality, or a separate module it may decline to supply?

The reversal that answers to reasons. An agent ranks a demanding career above family life; after the birth of a child she reverses; fifteen years later, once the child has left home, she reverses again. Temporally this is as unstable as a case of weakness of will, but by her own account at each stage it is a response to a change in what she has reason to do. What must a theory be able to do here — distinguish instability from responsiveness — without falling back on the agent's own retrospective verdict, which is only one stage's judgement? My suspicion is that this generates a requirement for an independent account of which changes in preference are reason-tracking, and that it is the requirement most of the familiar families quietly fail. Test that.

The leaky Ulysses device. An agent foresees her own future reversal, and would benefit from binding herself, but the only device available to her raises the cost of reversal without preventing it, and is administered by a future self who has an interest in dismantling it. What is the verdict on an agent who fails to bind, or who binds badly? Is there a real ought-implies-can constraint here — a requirement that the theory's demands be satisfiable by an agent who can raise costs but not close options — or only a pragmatics about which devices happen to exist?

Finally, the part I care about most. Tell me whether some subset of the requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, and state the incompatibility as a result if it holds. My hypothesis is that the impossibility concentrates in a trilemma: never act against your own current all-things-considered judgement; never adopt or persist in a plan you know to be worse than another plan available to you when you adopt it; and never adopt a plan you know you will abandon. On my reading each of the familiar families — act-by-act, sophisticated planning, resoluteness, and the treatment of person-stages as separate claimants — is identified by which horn it drops, so that the choice among them is a choice about which of three things to stop demanding. Check whether that is right, say which horn is cheapest to give up and why, and tell me which single requirement on the final list excludes the largest number of candidates. A short scorecard of the families against the list is welcome, but the thinking should be in the criteria.
