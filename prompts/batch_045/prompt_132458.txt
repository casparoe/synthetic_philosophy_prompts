I'm not a philosopher or an economist — I work in logistics and read this kind of thing in the evenings. I belong to a small giving circle: about a dozen of us, pooling somewhere in the low tens of thousands of pounds a year, deciding together where it goes. Over the last two years we have split into two fairly entrenched camps over a single question, and we have now agreed to stop arguing in the abstract and produce one document that we could all put our names to. I've been asked to write the brief for it, and I've also smuggled in two ideas of my own, flagged below, which I want tested rather than indulged.

The dispute: one camp thinks a "safe Pareto improvement" screen is exactly the discipline that small, ignorant donors need. On their view we should look for the comparatively rare interventions where nobody is made worse off, because that lets us act without pretending to know how much good we are doing, without aggregating one person's gain against another's loss, and without imposing our own weighing of values on people who didn't ask for it; if we cannot show that nobody loses, we should pass and keep looking. The other camp thinks the screen is close to vacuous: any real intervention has losers once you trace things out, so the test either rubber-stamps whatever we liked anyway (because we quietly decline to count the losers) or vetoes everything (because we count them all), and its actual function is to let us avoid admitting that we are making trade-offs on other people's behalf and to leave existing arrangements unchallenged. They would rather rank grants by expected good done and handle distribution, consent and injustice as separate considerations.

What I want from you is the document itself, in three parts.

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — propositions both camps can sign. Please keep them out of the realm of the vacuous: I want claims that at least one outsider to the argument would contest, including about what a small donor can realistically know and about what our ignorance does and doesn't license.

2. A disagreements table, with a column for the issue, a column for the formulation the Pareto-first camp would accept as a fair statement of its own position, and a column for the formulation the consequence-first camp would accept as a fair statement of its own. No straw men on either side.

3. A list of cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp — and, for each, one concrete case, argument or piece of evidence that both camps would accept as genuinely bearing on it, together with each camp's prediction about it, written down in advance.

My own guesses at the cruxes, which you should correct or replace as you see fit: whether "made worse off" tracks what people would say about their own lives or some more objective list; how far knock-on effects must be traced — prices, wages, the people the money would otherwise have reached; whether compensation has to actually be paid or only be payable in principle; whether "safe" means we are confident that nobody loses or merely that nobody loses in expectation; what time horizon is in view; and whether people who exist only because of the intervention can be made worse off by it. If you think part of our dispute is merely verbal, say precisely which part and why.

The two ideas I want tested. First, what I've been calling the counterfactual-Pareto test: instead of comparing a grant to doing nothing, compare it to the next-best use of the same money, and count as losers the people who would have been helped by that alternative. As far as I can tell this makes the test unsatisfiable in nearly every case, and I can't work out whether that is a problem for my test or a problem for the whole Pareto approach; I'd like each camp to say which, and why. Second, what I've been calling the voiceless-losers clause: some of a project's losers can never register a complaint — animals, people who never learn they were affected, people who lose only in relative standing, people who do not yet exist. My proposal is that when all of a project's losers are of that kind, we should stop calling it a Pareto improvement and call it a silent transfer instead, to be decided on the ordinary balancing of benefits and harms. Can both camps sign that, and what does each think it commits us to about animals and about future people?

Please run three cases through all of this as shared tests, recording what each camp predicts before the tracing is done: unconditional cash transfers to the poorest third of a village, where local food prices rise slightly and non-recipients who are net buyers end up marginally worse off; a corporate welfare pledge on broiler chickens that raises retail prices a little, improving the birds' lives at a cost borne mostly by low-income shoppers; and a grant whose only losers are the people who would have been helped by the alternative grant we chose not to make.

A few constraints. Write in British English. Don't cite studies, papers or authors and don't give me figures — none of us will check them, and I'd rather have arguments from the structure of the cases than borrowed authority. If you can see a decision rule we could actually use at our next meeting while the deeper disagreement stays unresolved, give it to us; if you think no honest rule of that kind is available, say so plainly rather than manufacturing one.
