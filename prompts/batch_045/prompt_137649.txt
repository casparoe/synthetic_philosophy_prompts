Before retiring I spent my career in experimental physics, and I have since been reading philosophy seriously for the first time, mostly where it bears on probabilistic inference. I have become stuck on the doomsday argument, and I would like a number from you rather than an overview of the literature.

Let me fix the proposition precisely. Let N be the total number of human beings — Homo sapiens, born and unborn — who will ever exist, and let r be my birth rank, which on the usual demographic estimates is roughly 1.1 × 10¹¹. Let D be the claim that N < 10¹³: that the human population never expands by more than about two orders of magnitude beyond its present cumulative total, whether because of extinction, because of a civilizational collapse from which we never recover, or because we settle into a stable low-fertility equilibrium. My prior on D, formed from what I take to be ordinary empirical considerations — impact and supervolcano hazard rates, pandemic risk, nuclear and climate trajectories, and the very large uncertainty about machine intelligence — I would put at about 0.2, and I do not trust that figure to better than a factor of two. I am setting aside the variant of the argument that infers a total duration from a single randomly located observation time; I take that to be a different argument with its own difficulties.

My question is what your credence in D is after taking the birth-rank considerations into account. Give me one number, but the number is not the point — I want the decomposition that produces it, with a figure and a line of justification for each term, and then the combination. The terms I would like you to separate are at least these:

(a) the probability that the self-sampling assumption, applied with the reference class fixed to all human beings, is the correct way to handle the indexical fact that I find myself at rank r, as against the self-indication assumption or a view on which that indexical fact carries no evidential weight whatever;
(b) the probability that a prior of the shape the argument requires — roughly uniform in the logarithm of N across many decades — is a defensible representation of my actual prior, given that I also hold a hazard-rate picture on which the annual probability of civilizational termination is roughly constant and small;
(c) the probability that human being is the correct reference class, rather than something wider (all observers, all technologically capable observers, all observer-moments, including future machine or uploaded ones) or something narrower;
(d) the probability that the shift the argument generates is genuine new evidence, rather than an artifact of the fact that I have known my approximate rank all along and learned nothing new this morning;
(e) the probability that D, as I have defined it, tracks anything I actually care about — a future containing a trillion people at very low welfare is not obviously preferable to extinction, and a small but indefinitely long-lived population would make D true without any catastrophe at all.

I would also like you to take up a thought of my own, which may well be wrong. If I model the human population as a process that grows and then terminates with an approximately constant hazard rate, then conditioning on my rank appears to do little more than penalise hypotheses on which the population grows for a very long time before ending. On that reading the argument is not a prediction of imminent doom but a constraint on the ratio between growth rate and hazard rate, and its apparent force comes almost entirely from a prior that treats that ratio as unknown across many orders of magnitude. If that is right, then the doomsday argument is best understood as a sensitivity result about vague priors, and the long-standing dispute reduces to a disagreement about conventions for setting priors under near-ignorance. If it is wrong, I should like to be told where the error lies.

For context: a former colleague from my old department, who takes the argument seriously, arrives at a posterior near 0.35; a member of the reading group I attend arrives at 0.03, which is essentially her prior. Given your decomposition, which of these sub-questions do you think their disagreement actually lives in, and which single term is your own number most sensitive to? Finally, what would move your number furthest: an empirical finding, a formal result about self-locating evidence, or a decision about which question is being asked in the first place?
