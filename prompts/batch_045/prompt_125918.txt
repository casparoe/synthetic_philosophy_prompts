I am in my fourth year of a PhD in gravitational-wave source modeling, and I spend most of my working life doing the following: specify puncture initial data on an initial hypersurface, evolve a binary black hole spacetime in a 3+1 formulation, choose a lapse and a shift with something like 1+log slicing and a Gamma-driver, and then read off quantities I take to be physically meaningful — the strain extrapolated to null infinity, the apparent horizon masses and spins, the proper distance between the horizons along some chosen curve, the gravitational wave frequency. I have been sitting in on a philosophy of physics reading group where we recently worked through the hole argument, and I want to understand it in my own terms rather than in the metaphysicians'. The argument, as I came away with it: if the points of the manifold are individuals whose identity is fixed independently of the fields defined on them, then general relativity looks indeterministic, because I can take a solution, apply a smooth reshuffling that is the identity outside some bounded region and nontrivial inside it, and get a second model that agrees with the first everywhere outside that region and everywhere on the initial surface, satisfies the same field equations, and differs from the first only over which point inside the region carries which metric properties. Nothing in the theory selects between the two. My supervisor's reaction, delivered in about fifteen seconds in a corridor, was that this is just the statement that the diffeomorphism group is a gauge group and is therefore uninteresting. I do not think that can be the whole story, but I also cannot tell whether the residue is a real technical point I could press in my own vocabulary or just leftover metaphysics that my formalism was built to be unable to express.

So here is what I am asking for. I am fluent in manifolds, charts, initial-value formulations, constraints versus evolution equations, gauge fixing, well-posedness and numerically stable formulations, and above all the practice of comparing two spacetimes only after either fixing a common gauge or reducing everything to invariants; I have no training in analytic metaphysics beyond the reading group, so the translation should be substantive rather than merely terminological. Please restate, as faithfully as you can, the argument and the main responses to it in that vocabulary: the claim that manifold points are individuals with their identity independent of the fields; the rival claim that the two models represent one and the same physical situation, usually defended by something like a Leibniz equivalence principle; the sophisticated version that grants the equivalence of the models while denying that there are distinct possibilities differing only in which individual plays which role; and the counter-move that points can be dressed or individuated by matter or by curvature invariants, so that the bare points over which the argument runs were never available in the first place. Where helpful, make it concrete at the level of an actual run: I can, in about ten lines of Python, read in a checkpoint, apply a compactly supported smooth map to the interior of some coordinate sphere, and write out a dataset that is again a solution of the same equations and that would instead be obtained by an exotic choice of lapse and shift; I would never count that as a second physical possibility, and I want to know precisely what the substantivalist has to say about that operation and whether my dismissing it is itself a philosophical commitment or just good numerical hygiene.

Two further things, one specific and one general. The specific one is a wrinkle I have not seen in the readings and that I suspect is where my own intuitions are coming from: in my corner of the subject we distinguish diffeomorphisms that die off at the boundary from those that do not, where the former are gauge and the latter are physical symmetries with actual conserved charges attached, the asymptotic symmetry group at null infinity. The map in the hole argument is, as standardly set up, compactly supported and hence of the first, harmless kind. So I want you to tell me what happens to the argument under that distinction. Is the hole argument simply the observation that a pure gauge transformation can masquerade as a physical difference if you insist on labelling points, in which case my supervisor was closer to right than I wanted to admit? Would the argument gain any traction at all if the reshuffling were of the second, large kind, or in a bounded region with a boundary that the map moves, and if so would it still be the same argument? Does the gauge-versus-symmetry distinction settle anything between substantivalism and relationalism, or is it orthogonal to that debate? The general thing I want is the residue report: which parts of the dispute translate cleanly into the vocabulary of evolutions, gauge fixings and invariant outputs, which translate only with distortion, and which cannot be expressed there at all — notions like substance, the identity of a point across possible models, or the idea that a model represents reality rather than merely predicting my detector's output seem to me to be candidates for the last category. For each item in the residue I want your view on whether it reveals a genuineblind spot in the working relativist's framework, or reveals that the original disagreement is thinner than it looks. Please do not send me to the literature or give me things to read; I want your reconstruction and your assessment, and I would rather have an honest statement that a question is unsettled than a tidy verdict.
