I've been arguing with a friend, who works in a visual neuroscience lab, about whether the classic callosotomy patients have one stream of consciousness or two. Her view is that the dissociation has been over-reported: on her account, the striking findings mostly fall out of the fact that stimuli have to be presented separately to each half-field and that each hemisphere largely controls its own hand, so a patient in the lab is no more two subjects than you are when your two hands are doing incompatible things at the same time. What I've noticed is that my own verdicts on individual cases are all over the place and that I cannot state a principle that generates them. I would like you to try. Please write your answer in American English.

Here is where I land, case by case. First: the classic setup, corpus callosum sectioned in adulthood for intractable epilepsy, different stimuli flashed to each visual half-field, the two hands producing conflicting responses. I lean toward two streams here, but only about sixty-forty. Second: the same patient six months later, at breakfast, talking to her daughter. One subject, no hesitation, and it strikes me as absurd to say otherwise. Third: someone born without a corpus callosum. One subject, no hesitation. Fourth: someone who had an entire cerebral hemisphere removed in childhood for severe epilepsy and is now an adult with close to normal cognition. One subject, and not a half subject or a diminished one, a whole one. Fifth, and hypothetical: the right hemisphere of one person and the left hemisphere of another joined by a future neural bridge that carries strictly more information, in both directions, than a healthy callosum carries. I say two subjects, and this is the verdict I can least defend. Sixth: the same bridge throttled down until only a coarse affective state is shared. Two subjects, trivially. Seventh: the same bridge carrying everything, but with roughly half a second of delay in transfer. Genuinely torn. Eighth: me, right now. One subject, and I do not see how thickening or thinning my callosum by a factor of two would change that.

The principle I keep trying to make work is something like this: the number of subjects equals the number of maximal systems that integrate information into a single unified phenomenal field, and where integration is partial the correct count is not two but indeterminate, which is why the lab case wobbles and the throttled bridge is easy. But as stated it seems to give the wrong answer on the two-person bridge unless I add a clause saying that two already-existing subjects cannot fuse into one, and it sits badly with the hemispherectomy case: if a lone hemisphere can carry an entire subject, why does a disconnected right hemisphere contribute a second subject rather than leaving one subject with an enlarged and partly inaccessible stream?

So: give me at most two candidate principles, each stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case myself, with each clause tied to the case that forces it. Tell me what each predicts for the delayed-bridge case and for two cases I have not given verdicts on, namely a bridge that carries information in only one direction and a bridge like the two-person one whose bandwidth is ramped slowly up from zero, where what I want to know is whether there is a threshold at which the two become one or whether the principle denies that there is such a point. Then tell me honestly which of my verdicts each principle forces me to give up, and which single case, if I changed my mind about it, would do the most to bring the rest into line. I am not asking for a survey of the literature or for references; she can supply those. I want the argument.
