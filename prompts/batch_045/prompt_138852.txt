You are a philosopher of social norms who also designs serious games — someone who has run structured exercises for university seminars, strategy offsites and museum events, and who knows what actually happens when twenty unpaid adults are handed a set of rules and left alone with them. Please think carefully before you settle on anything. Begin by brainstorming three candidate core mechanics for this session, in a few paragraphs, and reject two of them with an explicit reason. The rejected options matter to me as much as the chosen one: I want to see the design space before I see the design.

Here is the situation. I am a journalist; I write about behavioural science and ideas for a broad readership. I have been asked to run a ninety-minute workshop at a science-and-ideas festival. The room will hold eighteen to twenty self-selecting adults — curious, mostly patient, with no philosophy and no economics, and a few who will arrive late. No projector, no phones, no screens; everything has to fit in a tote bag and be reproducible by a colleague from a single sheet of instructions. Stakes can be chocolate, or a silly certificate, or the public reading of scores: nothing that requires a budget.

What participants should come away having felt, not having been told, is the structure of the classic Lewisian analysis of convention. I'll state it loosely, so we agree on the target: a convention is a regularity of behaviour in a recurring situation where everyone conforms, everyone expects everyone else to conform, and each person's reason to conform depends on the others' conforming — in a setting where some *other* regularity would have solved the same problem just as well. Four things should land:

1. Arbitrariness. The particular regularity that gets selected is not better in advance; it wins because it is salient, or because it happened last time, and a different one would have served everyone equally.
2. Expectation-dependence. Once you expect others to conform, conformity becomes rational for you; the expectation manufactures the reason. Participants should notice their own reasoning flipping when they revise what they think the room will do.
3. Inertia under universal discontent. A convention can be stable while nearly everyone privately prefers a different one, because no individual can profitably move alone and no collective move is available. They should feel the gap between "I want to switch" and "we can switch."
4. Path dependence at the level of populations. Two groups facing the identical problem can settle on different regularities and end up unequally well off, for no reason that anyone can point to — which is the seed of the claim that norms evolve by something other than optimisation.

Here are three mechanisms I have been turning over. Use the ones that work, improve them, or replace them outright — but tell me plainly if one is a dud and why.

*The inheritance rule.* Players coordinate in silence. Every third round, one player is removed from the table and replaced by a stranger from an adjacent room who has seen nothing; the replacement inherits the departing player's score. The table therefore has a standing incentive to transmit its regularity to someone who does not share its history, using no words and no shared precedent. What I want tested is whether the group can tell the difference between a regularity that is genuinely common knowledge and one that is only mutually known.

*The mutiny ballot.* Late in the session, one available regularity is better for everybody, but the room is stuck on the worse one. First, a private poll: everyone writes down which they would prefer if the room coordinated on it. The poll will come back near-unanimous for the better one. Then a secret, simultaneous, binding ballot to switch, requiring a supermajority of four-fifths. My prediction is that it fails, and that this is the moment the room feels the difference between individual preference and collective transition. I then want two alternative reform mechanisms tried after it, so participants can compare them: a staggered one, where a randomly chosen third of the room is privately told in advance that they will switch regardless, and a cascade one, where switching is allowed publicly, one player at a time, in silence, until someone declines.

*Two colonies.* The room is split from the start into two groups that never see each other's play, face the same problem, and settle — I expect — on different regularities with different payoffs. At the end, their cumulative scores are compared and the losing group is asked to explain why they lost. I want them to run out of explanations that cite anyone's mistake.

What I need from you is a runnable session, in this order: materials and setup; rules, stated as you would hand them to participants; a payoff scheme in points with the critical comparisons built into the numbers, together with a sentence on why each number is what it is; a minute-by-minute timeline across the ninety minutes, including what to do with the late arrivals; and a debrief that maps specific moments in play onto the four items above, saying what the facilitator should ask at each point and what answers to expect.
