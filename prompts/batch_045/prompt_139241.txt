Hey — writing this between classes so sorry if it rambles a bit. I'm in a cross-listed philosophy/religion seminar on Buddhist ethics this term and the last three weeks have basically been one argument: is Buddhist ethics secretly consequentialist or not. We did the sea-captain case — you probably know it, the captain finds out that a man aboard is about to murder and rob everyone on the ship, the only way to stop him is to kill him first, and the captain does it knowing he'll take on the consequences himself — and everyone keeps grabbing for the same four or five labels while meaning totally different things by them. Our TA is no help, he just says it depends which text you ask. For the final project I want to build a "which reading are you actually committed to?" decision tree for the seminar blog: seven or eight branch questions, six to nine leaves, plus a scoring key. Audience is the first-years in the course plus a couple of grad students who will be smug about it, so every node has to be readable by someone who has never opened a Buddhist text.

The thing I care most about: it has to sort people by what they say about cases, not by which label they like. No node may contain an -ism, a Sanskrit term, or anything that tips the reader off to which leaf they're drifting toward; if you need a technical notion, gloss it in plain English in parentheses and let the gloss do the work. The first node has to be the sea captain, with roughly four options — he did something bad and shouldn't have / he did something bad but was still right to do it / he did nothing bad at all / he did something good and would have been at fault to refuse — and every branch has to stay live, no dead ends where you just get handed a leaf after one answer. Forks I want in there, because these are the ones my study group kept circling: whether what makes the act what it is is the state of mind it came out of (reluctant, compassionate versus cold or angry) or what it actually brought about; whether the good an act is supposed to produce needs some enduring someone it happens to, or whether its fine to just total up what happens to a causal stream with no owner behind it (I've started calling this the ownership gap, and I want it placed on the tree — or an honest explanation of why it isn't a real fork and what's actually doing the work there); whether it changes anything that the agent is an ordinary person rather than someone very far along the path, and I want each leaf to say what it does with that rather than just stipulating an agent type; whether the rules are exceptionless, have exceptions, or aren't really what's guiding the agent at all — a case where no rule could have anticipated the situation would be great here; whether the goodness someone builds up can be redirected toward someone else afterwards, the way dedication rituals seem to assume, or only accrues to whoever acted; and whether the point of the whole thing is the agent's own transformation or the state of the world.

Leaves: I need at least the character-and-development reading (my TA says this one goes back to Keown), the outcome-first reading, the no-fixed-principles reading, the everything-is-convention reading where there aren't really ultimate moral facts, and — this is the part I'm most interested in — at least one combination of answers that is coherent but that you think nobody actually occupies, with a one-line diagnosis of why the cell is empty: incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Every leaf gets one sentence on its most awkward commitment and one sentence on what would have to be true for that leaf to be the right one. And please flag any two leaves your tree can't actually separate instead of manufacturing a difference between them, then tell me at the end which node is doing most of the discriminating work and which node is really two questions squashed together. Don't just hand me the standard four-way chart from the survey literature — my group has read that and it hasn't settled anything for us.
