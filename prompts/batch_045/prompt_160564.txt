I'm in a small grad seminar on strategy and statecraft, and last week we ran a tabletop exercise that I can't stop thinking about. The scenario was fictional on purpose: a mid-sized republic ("Meridia") has been making incremental demands against its neighbour over a strait and a mostly-empty border province — naval patrols pushed a bit further each month, a suddenly-organized referendum in the province, then a demand for a transit corridor, each step small enough that conceding it looks cheaper than fighting. Half the room framed the whole thing as Munich 1938 and wanted to draw a line immediately. I kept pushing back: the revisionist side has no real alliance system behind it, the disputed territory has almost no population or industry, the domestic politics on our side are a mess, and nobody in the scenario has actually demonstrated that the demands are unlimited rather than opportunistic. Every time I said something like that, the TA leaned back and said "those who cannot remember the past are doomed to repeat it," as if that settled it, and once the professor added "history doesn't repeat itself, but it rhymes." I'm not trying to win the argument for its own sake, but I do think the line was doing a lot of unearned work, and I want to show up to the next session with something better than "that's just a slogan."

So: can you lay out, as a menu I can literally put on a slide, the distinct precise claims "those who cannot remember the past are doomed to repeat it" could be making, from the trivial to the wild? I've started a rough taxonomy of what a "lesson of history" might even be — (i) a statistical regularity about how often behaviour of type X leads to outcome Y; (ii) a mechanism, i.e. a causal story about why it does; (iii) a decision rule or heuristic for actors in a situation ("don't concede to salami tactics"); (iv) a rhetorical framing device that changes what the room notices; (v) a warning about a specific failure mode like appeasement dynamics, audience costs, or credibility spirals — but I'm not married to it and would happily use a better one. I'd also like the ambiguities in the sentence itself pulled apart rather than smoothed over: whose memory, and what kind of remembering counts (knowing the facts? drawing the right inference? having the right analogy loaded in working memory?); what "the past" is — the whole thing, or some curated set of episodes; what "doomed" means (literally unavoidable, or just elevated probability, and elevated relative to what baseline); and what exactly gets "repeated" — the event type, the mistake, the outcome, or the mechanism.

For each reading I want three verdicts, briefly: is it a platitude, is it substantive and defensible, or is it false/incoherent? Please include the ones that cut against the TA rather than for him — e.g. does remembering a past analogy too vividly ever cause the very outcome it was meant to avoid, by locking you into a credibility commitment or making a limited revisionist look unlimited? And then the part I actually care about: which of these readings would have to be true for the slogan to genuinely support importing the Munich frame into our Meridia exercise, and is that the reading people usually mean when they say it, or are they leaning on the platitudinous version while helping themselves to the strong one? If you can, rank the readings by how well they'd do that job, from "this actually gets him somewhere" to "this is pure vibes." No need for citations or a reading list — I just want the disambiguation and the verdicts.
