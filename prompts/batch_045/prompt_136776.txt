I'm writing a short paper on Avicenna's flying man and I keep losing track of why I believe what I believe about it, so what I want from you is a credence with its innards showing, not an overview of the debate. Assume I have the secondary literature covered: I don't want citations, a reading list, or a summary of what commentators have said.

The claim I want a number on is this: run in its strongest form, the flying man gives a non-question-begging reason to deny that a human person is identical with, or wholly constituted by, her living body. Call that claim F. I'm not asking whether F is currently fashionable or how specialists read the historical text; I'm asking whether the argument actually works.

Set-up, so we are arguing about the same argument: a subject is brought into existence as an adult, suspended in midair, with sight, hearing, touch, proprioception and interoception all removed, and with no memory of ever having had a body. The argument invites us to notice that this subject would still affirm his own existence, and concludes that whatever he is aware of in being aware of himself, it is not his body.

My credence in F sits around 0.45; my advisor's sits around 0.1. Give me yours as a single number, then decompose it. The sub-questions I can see F resting on are these, and you should feel free to relabel them, split them, or replace them with better ones:

1. Is the scenario possible in the sense the argument needs? In particular, can a subject be stripped of bodily awareness altogether and still be a subject of experience at all, or does removing interoception and proprioception remove the very thing that was supposed to be doing the affirming?
2. Granting the scenario, would the subject affirm anything? The concept-acquisition worry: does affirming that one exists already require concepts, of existence and of oneself, that a subject with no bodily history could never have acquired?
3. Even if he does affirm something, is what gets established merely epistemic or conceptual, namely that self-awareness does not need bodily awareness as its object or ground, rather than ontological?
4. Does the argument at most show that the flying man is aware of himself under a description that does not mention the body, leaving it open that what he is aware of is in fact his body?
5. Is whatever survives the subtraction a person at all? Does bare self-awareness secure personhood and identity over time, or does a successful argument here establish only a very thin self?

I also want you to weigh a variant I have not seen discussed and that I think does real work: the two-stage flying man. Instead of being created without a body, the subject first lives an ordinary embodied life, acquiring the concepts of existence, of himself, and of his body, and is only then reduced to the flying-man condition and asked what he can affirm. This looks to me like it blocks the concept-acquisition worry in item 2. Does it? Or does it merely relocate the question to whether concepts acquired through the body can be deployed while the bodily vehicles that carry them are absent, and if that is where it lands, does the relocation help the argument, hurt it, or leave it exactly where it was? Give me a second credence in F conditional on the two-stage variant being the version under discussion, and tell me whether the gap between that number and your headline number is where the real action is.
