Me and a friend got into about a three hour argument about the extended mind last night, over too much wine, and I think he built something I can't find the exit from. He calls it the Co-op case and I've been turning it over since.

Setup: six people run a big shared garden. On the kitchen wall there's a jointly maintained ledger — what's planted where, when things were last watered, who's supposed to do what, what failed last year. Everyone writes in it, everyone reads it, nobody fact-checks it before acting, it's been trusted for years, it's right there whenever anyone needs it, and it's corrected when someone spots an error. No single member could garden at anywhere near this level without it. Each person also knows who maintains which part of it, so when someone wants to know about the tomatoes they go to the ledger, and when the ledger is ambiguous they ask Dana. Nobody's individual memory is doing the work; the remembering is spread across the ledger, the divisions of who-wrote-what, and the other five people.

His argument, as I reconstructed it this morning:

(1) Parity. If a process outside the head plays the same role an internal memory process would — reliable, automatically endorsed, easy to access when needed, trusted, durable — then it is a memory, and the skull marks no interesting boundary.

(2) What matters is functional role, not what the thing is made of, where it sits, or whose it is. Paper, silicon, another person's neurons: all equally eligible vehicles.

(3) So the boundary of a mind falls around the functionally integrated system, wherever that happens to fall — not at the skin.

(4) The standard integration conditions (availability, reliability, automatic endorsement, durability over time) don't require that the resource be exclusively yours or permanently yours or housed in one agent. Parity as stated tests the resource against a counterfactual, not against a property deed.

(5) In the Co-op, the ledger plus the other members' contributions satisfy every one of those conditions for each member, and satisfy them better than the famous notebook case does: the resource is more reliable, checked by others, faster to consult, and self-correcting.

(6) So, by parity, each member's memory literally includes the ledger and the others' memories. The six of them, while gardening, share one distributed memory.

(7) And it doesn't matter if one of them moves out or is asleep, because the system keeps functioning fine. So the system, not the person, is where the cognition is. What exists is a shifting, task-relative cognitive system, and "whose mind is this" has no answer.

(8) Once you've granted that, run it on everything: me and my search bar, me and my department's shared drive, me and the group chat where someone always knows the answer. I don't have a mind; I'm a node in a pile of overlapping ones.

(9) Nobody believes that. So (1) is false, and if (1) goes then the notebook was never part of anyone in the first place and the whole thesis is dead.

I want to keep (1) and (2) — I like the extended mind, and I think something real is going on with Otto — but every difference I try to name between Otto's notebook and the Co-op gets eaten. If I say "it has to be mine," he says that's just (3) under another name and begs the question. If I say "it has to be in my head," he says (2) rules that out by construction. If I say "someone has to endorse it," he says all six do. If I say "someone else's neurons can't be part of my memory," he says the counterfactual in (1) can't even be stated for that case, so I'm the one breaking the test, not him. And then he adds that if you patch (4) by adding an ownership or single-user clause, you've admitted the functional story was never doing the work and you've got a thesis about possession dressed up as one about cognition.

So: which step do you reject, why did it look fine when it did, and what does rejecting it actually cost you? I'd especially like the difference between Otto and the Co-op stated precisely if there is one, because "trust and glue" just sounds like a label to me. And if you're going to use any of the usual moves — the coupling-versus-constitution complaint, the distinction between cognition and cognitive systems, scaffolding instead of constitution, the integrationist version — tell me what each of them has to pay, and whether the price is paid in (1), (2), (4), or somewhere between (6) and (7). I'd rather give up (9) than (1), but I want to know what I'm signing up for.
