I am writing up my position on empire, using Adam Smith's chapter on the colonies in Book IV of the Wealth of Nations as the peg, and I want the package audited for consistency before I commit to it in print. What I am inclined to hold is the following.

1. Smith's case against the mercantile system is the core of what I believe about political economy: privileged channels for capital — monopolies, chartered companies, protected colonial trade — draw stock away from the employment that supports the most domestic industry, and the wealth of a nation is the annual produce of its labour, not its stock of treasure or the balance of its trade. It is for this reason that I distrust industrial policy today.

2. I read the chapter on the colonies as an application of (1). The monopoly of the colonial trade raised profits for British merchants and manufacturers, while the cost of defending and administering the colonies fell on the British public; the nation as a whole would therefore have been richer with free trade and no empire.

3. I also hold that empire was unjust independently of that ledger — rule by a distant legislature without consent, conquest, dispossession — and that this, not the economic loss, is what grounds present-day duties of repair.

4. Those duties are not extinguished by lapse of time, by the fact that living people on both sides are better off than their ancestors were, or by the difficulty of tracing particular gainers and losers.

5. On distributive justice generally, though, I am a forward-looking institutionalist: justice is a property of a structure going forward, and neither the luck-sensitivity of market outcomes nor the bare fact that one party captured most of the gains of a bargained exchange is, by itself, an objection.

6. I also find Smith's own remedy attractive: colonial representation in Parliament proportionate to taxation, with free trade on both sides; and failing that, amicable separation.

7. Finally, I treat the convergence of the economic and the moral case as a point in favour of the whole package rather than as its ground. The verdicts in (3) and (4) should stand even if the ledger had come out the other way — and it well might have, since Smith himself allows that the utility resulting from the modern colonies has been very great.

Please audit the set rather than explain it. Which of these actually pull against each other, and which only look as though they do? Order the real tensions by severity; for each, say what would have to be given up and what that would cost me elsewhere in the set; and identify the single revision that would clean up the most. Set the order yourself — I do not trust my own sense of where the trouble is.
