Hey! I do a column on science and ideas (and a newsletter that goes out whenever I get around to it), and I want to run a short satirical document as this weeks piece. I've got the format in my head but not the comedy chops, so I need you to write it.

Setup: a contractor — call it something bland, Derivation Services — hired under a fixed-price contract to deliver a string called MU. The work is governed by a formal system I made up in the spirit of the old MU-puzzle. Specify the system correctly inside the document, because the joke depends on it:

- alphabet: M, J, U
- the only string you may write down to begin with is MJ
- Rule 1: if a derived string ends in J, you may write down that string with a U on the end
- Rule 2: if you have derived Mx, you may write down Mxxx (the bit after the M, three times over)
- Rule 3: you may replace any occurrence of JJJJ with a single U
- Rule 4: you may delete any occurrence of UU
- deliverable: MU

What I think, and I'm not totally sure, is that MU is simply unreachable — every derivable string has an odd number of J's, and MU has none. Check me on that. If I've got it wrong, say so and build the piece on whatever the truth turns out to be, but the math has to be right, because a reader who knows this material should laugh rather than wince.

The document itself: a leaked internal FAQ for new hires at the firm. 750-900 words, numbered Q&As, deadpan corporate voice, plus one annex at the end. What I was picturing for the annex is a Level-2 Request Form — the form you file to request permission to reason *about* the system rather than *inside* it — where every field accepts only strings over M, J and U. No winking, no nudging, nobody turns to the camera.

What the satire should land: the firm is not wrong that MU isn't derivable. It's wrong about what that means. The jokes should come out of the real features of working inside a formal system — that at the object level the rules really are all there is, that the counting argument really is invisible from in there, that the form for requesting a meta-level move really can't express the request. I'd like the invariant to surface somewhere in the doc — a deprecated annex, a footnote, a strikethrough — somewhere a careful reader catches it and the Bureau has tried to bury it, and I'd like the Bureau's response to whoever noticed to be the darkest thing in the piece.

Ending: I want the Bureau's own fix, a new rule that tries to absorb the meta-level move by fiat and only relocates the problem one floor up. My idea was a rule providing that any string demonstrated to be underivable is deemed derived as of fiscal year end. Use it or beat it. By the last line a reader should have felt the actual bite of the thing: whether there is any way to teach a rule-follower to step outside, or whether every rule you add just builds a higher storey.

Constraints: no real people, don't name the author or the book any of this comes from, and don't let the piece depend on the reader already knowing it. No citations, no further reading. It has to work for someone reading on a phone who has never heard of a formal system, and still be worth it for someone who has spent years with them.
