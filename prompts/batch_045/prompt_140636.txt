You are a professional philosopher who works on identity over time and material constitution, and who has some patience for the ways institutions — museums, registries, insurers, hospital ethics committees — operationalise identity claims they cannot actually define. Please think carefully and work in stages rather than delivering a verdict in one breath: first set out the different things our practice might be tracking (matter, form, function, continuous existence, causal history, convention); then reconstruct the implicit theory; then test that reconstruction against the awkward cases at the end, and mark clearly where it fails. I would rather have a careful diagnosis than a tour of the standard positions, though if a standard position really is the hinge, name it and show what work it does rather than just labelling it.

Here is the practice I am stuck inside. I am a volunteer trustee of a small maritime heritage trust that owns a nineteenth-century wooden fishing vessel, kept afloat and sailed every summer. Over roughly forty years she has been repaired plank by plank: very little of the timber that was in her when we acquired her is still in her, and the mast, the rigging and the engine are all replacements. Nobody involved doubts that she is the same boat — she keeps the same name, the same registration, the same entry in the register of historic vessels, and she remains eligible for the maintenance grants our trust exists to hand out. But the practice seems to tie itself in knots a few steps on. Some years ago a comparable boat was dismantled, her timbers numbered and stored, and then rebuilt with about eighty per cent of her original material, on a new structural cradle, to her original lines. In architectural conservation this technique of re-erecting something from its surviving original elements has a name, anastylosis, and is normally classed as restoration rather than new building. Our own funding rules, however, treated that vessel as a reconstruction — on the ground that she had ceased to exist as a vessel during the interval — and the insurers wanted to rate her as a new build. So we appear to hold that the piecemeal replacement of nearly all the matter leaves identity untouched, while a gap in existence destroys it even though most of the original matter returns. The same rulebook then adds a quantitative test: a project counts as maintaining an original vessel only while at least sixty per cent of the structural fabric is original. When a case lands near that line the trustees settle it by majority vote — and yet nobody on the board thinks the vote *makes* the vessel the same vessel; we all think a majority could get it wrong. For what it is worth, the same people are entirely untroubled in saying that a patient with a donor heart, or one who has simply replaced most of their cells over a lifetime, is straightforwardly the same person, while a patient whose brain has permanently stopped functioning is not, even though the body is warm and intact. I realise this is the Ship of Theseus with paperwork and an insurance schedule attached.

So: what does this cluster of rules actually presuppose about what it is for a thing to persist? Reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can, and then separate three things — what the practice strictly presupposes, what it merely fits well with, and what it is entirely neutral about. In particular, tell me whether we are committed to one consistent criterion or whether we are running a coalition of criteria that happens not to clash in the cases we usually meet; and if it is a coalition, say which rival theories of identity over time would license exactly the rules we have anyway, so that our rulebook is philosophically underdetermined. Then take the counterfactuals seriously: if we adopted the view that persistence admits of degrees, or that there is no determinate fact of the matter about which vessel is Theseus's, or that distinct material things can coincide and share all their matter at a time, what would our grant criteria, our register entries and our insurance clause actually have to say instead? And where the practice is arbitrary or incoherent — the sixty per cent line, the vote, the asymmetry between the plank-by-plank boat and the dismantled one — locate the fault precisely and tell me the cheapest revision that would remove it, and what that revision would cost us in terms of what we are trying to preserve.
