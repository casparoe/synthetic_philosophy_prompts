You are a historian of early modern political thought with a research specialism in Locke's theories of property, consent, and money, and you have agreed to write a chapter section on whether a government can wrong its subjects financially without ever taking anything from them. Please work carefully and in two passes. In the first pass, brainstorm briefly: list the Lockean premises that could bear on the case below, note how each one would cut, and mark the places where two of those premises pull in opposite directions. In the second pass, write the actual section. Do not try to retrieve or quote texts verbatim; paraphrase from what you know of the Two Treatises, and flag explicitly any claim about Locke that you are unsure of rather than asserting it.

The case is a fictional one. The Republic of Veredia abolished every tax, levy, duty, and compulsory fee about fifteen years ago and now funds all public spending through a single mechanism, plus three associated rules:

1. The seigniorage rule. Each year the central bank credits the Treasury with newly created marks up to a statutory ceiling of 4 percent of the existing money stock. The Treasury spends them on courts, police, roads, a basic pension, and a small standing army. No private balance, account, or title has ever been debited by the state; there is no assessment, no return, no collection. The rule is written into an ordinary statute, is published, is debated each year, and has been reaffirmed by three successive parliaments; in a referendum eleven years ago 62 percent voted to keep it. Because the money is spent into circulation, the price level has risen by close to 4 percent a year, steadily and predictably.

2. Guaranteed accounts. Any resident may hold a balance at the central bank that is fully and automatically indexed to the price level, but indexation applies only up to twice the median annual income. Balances above that level are diluted. The great majority of residents hold less than the cap.

3. Exit and denomination. Emigration is legal and untaxed, but most residents' wealth is in local land, local businesses, and local claims, so leaving means selling at a large loss. Foreign currency and commodities may be held but are not accepted for most domestic payments; the law requires wages, prices, and debts to be quoted in marks, and marks are legal tender for all debts. A smaller group — holders of old fixed-nominal private annuities, whose contracts cannot be renegotiated and who hold claims rather than balances — is not covered by the indexation in rule 2 and loses real value every year. Roughly an eighth of the currency is held outside Veredia by non-residents, who have no vote, no representation, and no access to guaranteed accounts.

A domestic opposition group, the Committee for Sound Money, has petitioned parliament arguing that this is theft by dilution, worse than the taxes it replaced because it is less visible. Parliament's opponents reply that nothing of anyone's has been taken, that the majority has consented to the rule through its representatives and by referendum, and that the public goods purchased are exactly what Locke says government exists to secure.

Your task: reconstruct, as faithfully as you can, the answer Locke would give to the question "Is the Veredian seigniorage theft?" Address at least the following, in whatever order serves your argument.

- Start from the mechanism, not from slogans. For Locke, does a predictable dilution of the purchasing power of a holding count as a taking of a man's estate at all, given that nothing he owns is removed, entered, or transferred, and given his view that the value of money rests on convention and consent rather than on use?
- Locate the wrong, if there is one. Rank the candidate sources of objection by how much Lockean weight each carries: the absence of any express act of consent by the losers; the fact that the losers are represented and were outvoted; the uncompensated top of large balances; the position of the old annuitants; the practical impossibility of exit; the legal-tender requirement; the dilution of non-resident holders who have no vote and owe no obedience. Say which feature does the real work in your reconstruction.
- Run the counterfactual. Suppose Veredia instead levied an explicit annual charge of 4 percent on money balances, with the same exemption threshold, the same spending, and the same votes. Would Locke's verdict change? If it would, explain what the difference reveals about whether his objection is to the taking, to the absence of consent, to the hiddenness of the transfer, or to something else.
- Work out the consent doctrine. Locke distinguishes express consent, which makes a man a full and permanent member of the commonwealth, from the tacit consent he thought was given by possessing land or enjoying any part of the government's dominions, which binds only while one is present. Which, if either, do continuing Veredian residents give? Does the answer differ for a resident who keeps marks in a guaranteed account, for the annuitant, and for the non-resident? Does the fact that the rule is announced in advance matter to you, and would Locke have thought it does?
- Say where the framework runs out. Identify the questions here that Locke's texts do not settle, the points where he would have to extend his doctrine rather than apply it, and any place where you think he would have been forced to revise a commitment — for instance his treatment of coinage, of the public faith in a currency, or of covert as opposed to open exactions.
- Give a comparative verdict: by Locke's own lights, is Veredia's arrangement morally worse, better, or on a par with the system of taxation it replaced, and why?

Then close with a short verdict paragraph written in Locke's own voice — first person, period-appropriate diction, arguing the conclusion and addressing the Committee for Sound Money directly — followed by three or four lines in which you step back out of character and note where you are most confident that you have reconstructed him and where you are extrapolating.

Aim for roughly 1,000 to 1,400 words. Use short headings for each section. Do not pad the piece with summaries of Locke's general theory; assume a reader who already knows the Two Treatises, and spend your words on the application. Do not cite secondary literature, do not give a bibliography, and do not decline the question on the grounds that Locke could not have anticipated such a case — the extrapolation is the point.
