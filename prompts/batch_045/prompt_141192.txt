I've gotten sucked into arguments about religion in politics lately (mostly local school board stuff) and I keep tripping over the same thing, and I dont have a word for it. Let me describe it badly and see if you recognise it.

So. A few weeks ago two people gave what I thought was the *same* argument against some policy. One was from a church group and it had scripture in it. One was from a secular parents' association and had basically no god-talk at all — it went: institutions make mistakes, some mistakes here can't be undone, a person's life isn't just a unit to be weighed, so don't build a system that can quietly kill somebody. The first one got told it was imposing a religion. The second one got called civic-minded. That felt wrong to me, because the load-bearing part of the first one wasn't the verse either — the verse was kind of bolted on, you could delete it and the argument would still stand.

Then there's the mirror image. Someone at work argued for a position by appealing to a very specific account of what persons are — something about rational self-conscious agency being the thing that makes a life matter — and I'm just supposed to accept that? I have no idea why I should. I felt exactly as shut out as I imagine a religious person feels when they're told their reasons aren't public. And it's not unusual: appeals to "the science", to a nation's founding promises, to the direction history is going, all seem to work the same way — fine if you already share the premise, useless if you don't.

And a third thing. I keep noticing that the same person's argument gets treated as admissible or inadmissible depending on whether they *mention* where it came from. Say the quiet part out loud and you're a theocrat; keep it to yourself and say the same sentences and you're a model citizen. Nothing about the argument's content changed. (I've read the wikipedia-level version of public reason, including the bit where you're allowed to bring religious reasons in as long as you later give a properly political one — that's not what I mean, so please don't just explain that at me.)

What I think I'm circling is this: the thing doing the work in these fights isn't whether a reason is religious. It's something like how much of the argument rests on premises the other person is expected to take from a source they don't share, versus premises they already accept or could go and check for themselves. Plus how much effort the audience is willing to put in — a lot of religious arguments are perfectly inspectable if you'll learn that tradition's internal moves, but nobody will, and somehow that gets counted against the argument. Or maybe I'm wrong that it's epistemic at all, and what people really object to is the certainty religious reasons come with, their motivational oomph, the suspicion that you can't compromise on them.

Is there an established concept for that dimension, as opposed to the religious/secular one? Or for the specific pattern where an argument flips status depending on whether you name its source? Every time I go looking I just get the religion debate again, and I'm fairly convinced that's the wrong axis. If nothing established fits, say so and give me a term and a definition I could actually apply to hard cases — including cases where a reason is unshared but perfectly checkable, or shared but only for tribal reasons.

Also, please commit to something. I don't want a menu of options to pick from. Tell me what you think is actually going on, whether this is one phenomenon or two that I'm conflating, which existing label (if any) has earned the right to be used here and why the near misses fall short, and where you're giving me your own judgment rather than something people in the field actually say. And say what would show I'm wrong — what kind of case would break whatever account you give me.
