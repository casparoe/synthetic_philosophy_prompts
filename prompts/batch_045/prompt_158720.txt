I am a third-year doctoral student in history, working on a dissertation chapter about the explanatory weight historians assign to narrowly individuated episodes — a delayed telegram, a minister who happened to be in the room, an order countermanded and then reinstated — in accounts of the outbreak of the First World War. My chapter turns on so-called minimal-rewrite counterfactuals: the claim that, holding the rest of the past as close to actual as the counterfactual allows, some small, well-specified change would have altered the outcome.

In my department's methods seminar, a senior colleague regularly closes discussion of this material with the old couplet — for want of a nail the shoe was lost, for want of a shoe the horse was lost, and for want of a horse the man was lost. He deploys it as a conversation-ender. On his view the chain from nail to kingdom has no principled stopping point: pull any thread and the whole tapestry unravels, so counterfactual history is speculation piled on speculation and, in the end, there is no fact of the matter about what would have happened. I suspect he is sliding between several claims, and I want to pin down which.

Could you set out, as a menu rather than a single favoured reading, the distinct precise theses that piece of language could be expressing — understood as claims about history and about counterfactuals, not as claims about proverbs? Here is a rough taxonomy I have been working with; sharpen it, correct it, or replace it:

1. A triviality about necessary conditions: every event stands at the end of a long chain of necessary conditions, so of course the nail "matters" — a claim about causal history, not about difference-making.
2. Fine-grained difference-making: for any outcome there exists some arbitrarily small, narrowly individuated difference in the past such that, had it obtained, the outcome would not have occurred.
3. Modal fragility: at some specified grain of description, most (or a large share of) nearby perturbations of the past lead to very different outcomes — a claim that seems to need a measure over perturbations.
4. Non-additivity: the causal field is not buffered, so small causes cannot safely be neglected, because feedbacks can amplify them.
5. Unrunnability: even granting determinism, we cannot compute or empirically check the counterfactual trajectory, so counterfactual claims fail some evidential standard.
6. Indeterminacy: there is no fact of the matter at all.

For each, I would like a verdict — trivial, substantive and defensible, or false — and I am especially interested in three things: whether the fragility reading can even be stated without a measure over "nearby pasts" and whether history supplies one; whether strict sensitivity in formal models transfers to history, given that history is not rerunnable; and whether the indeterminacy reading is entailed by the unrunnability reading or is a separate and much stronger claim. Finally, tell me which of these readings would actually license my colleague's dismissal of minimal-rewrite history, which would only license a modest methodological caution, and which reading people who reach for this proverb are usually reaching for.
