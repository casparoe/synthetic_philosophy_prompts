I am applying for a three-month, full-time early-career fellowship in philosophy, and I need a proposal of about two pages. The sole required output is a paper of roughly 9,000 words plus a short note to the funder. What I want from you is the proposal document itself: the question stated precisely enough to have a determinate answer, why the existing responses leave it open, the method I would actually follow and why that method rather than the obvious alternatives, what the possible results are and which considerations would decide between them, and an honest account of what would count as the project failing. I will supply the references myself, so please do not cite anything by name and do not give me a reading list — refer to positions and arguments by description.

The area is cluelessness about the long-run consequences of action and what it does to consequentialism. My half-formed idea is that the debate has been running three different complaints together, that the standard replies dispose of the first two, and that the third has not really been faced.

The first complaint is sign-cluelessness: we can rank acts by their near-term effects, but the difference in long-run value between them is small relative to our uncertainty and we cannot tell which way it goes. The standard answer is to be a subjective rather than an objective consequentialist — act on the evidence you have, maximise expected value relative to your credences. The second is what I have been calling tail-cluelessness: the spread of long-run effects is so enormous that it swamps everything we can calculate, so that no credible near-term advantage is safe. The usual comfort here is that the unknown effects are roughly symmetric and wash out in expectation. My worry is that this comfort assumes the relevant expectations are well defined, and that symmetry in the spread of effects is not the same thing as a determinate expected difference: if the probability of an effect of a given size shrinks only as a power of that size, there may be no mean to take at all.

The third complaint is the one I want the project built around, and it is not in the literature as far as I can tell. An expected value is a sum of probability-times-value across a partition of the space of possible consequences. If that sum is only conditionally convergent, then its total depends on how the space is carved up and on the order in which contributions are taken, and by re-partitioning you can make it come out however you like — favouring either act in the pair. In that case the consequentialist maximand is not merely hard to estimate, it is ill-posed. And, importantly, this would arise from perfectly ordinary causal sprawl: the fact that anything I do ramifies indefinitely into an open-ended future. It would not depend on infinite populations, on a wager about a deity, or on a contrived gamble with a tiny probability of an astronomical payoff. That is what makes it a problem about cluelessness specifically rather than a rerun of the fanaticism debate, and the proposal should say plainly if it thinks that line cannot be held.

There is a further twist I want tested. When act-consequentialism looks clueless, the familiar retreat is to move up a level and evaluate rules, policies, habits or institutions instead of individual acts. But if the partition problem is real, that retreat should make things worse rather than better: assessing a rule means aggregating over every occasion on which it would be followed, including occasions arbitrarily far in the future, so the sum runs over a strictly larger and more heterogeneous space than the sum for a single act. If that is right, the two-level move is not the safe harbour it is usually treated as.

Please write the proposal in prose with short section headings, in a register suitable for a fellowship application — confident, precise, not grandiose, and with no marketing language. Include: a statement of the question; why the existing responses leave it open, taking in turn the subjective-consequentialist reply to sign-cluelessness, the 'the long-run effects wash out' reply to tail-cluelessness, the retreat to imprecise credences with a decision rule for acting under them, and the two-level retreat; a month-by-month method for three months, with an explicit say on why this approach rather than doing empirical forecasting about actual long-run effects, or doing formal work on aggregation over infinite domains, or abandoning consequentialism for a framework in which the question does not arise; the range of possible conclusions — including the possibility that a bounded or saturating value function dissolves the problem by making the sums converge, and the possibility that some privileged partition supplied by the causal structure does the work — together with what would decide between them; at least three distinct failure conditions, one of which should be the risk that the whole thing collapses into the fanaticism literature and another the risk that it is technically sound but makes no difference to what anyone should do; and an honest statement of scope, including what to cut if the third complaint cannot be established in the time available. Close with a paragraph on why a funder should support this rather than something applied: what the deliverable would be for people who have to choose between interventions under deep uncertainty, and what form a usable product — a test of whether a consequentialist ranking is robust to re-carving the space of outcomes, say — would actually take.
