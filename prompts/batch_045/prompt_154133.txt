I'm part of a small team designing a cross-national survey module on moral intuitions, meant to update the standard moral-foundations battery (care/harm, fairness/cheating, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, sanctity/degradation, liberty/oppression) for use in countries where the six-foundation structure fits worse than it does in the US. A colleague on the team wants to add two further candidates, and I need to decide whether to spend item budget on them. The first is what he calls the legibility foundation: the intuition that the rules, algorithms, and institutional procedures that govern you ought to be inspectable and answerable to you, such that opacity, unexplainability, or hidden discretionary power is itself experienced as a moral offense — with an emotional signature closer to the outrage at cheating than to the discomfort of subversion, but directed at unreadability rather than at unfairness, and with a characteristic motivation to demand explanation, audit, or disclosure. The second is the stewardship foundation: the intuition that goods you did not create but received — institutions, practices, craft knowledge, languages, built and natural landscapes — carry an obligation of maintenance and transmission, with a distinctive resentment at those who liquidate or squander an inherited good for present advantage; he argues this is not reducible to loyalty (bond to a living coalition), authority (deference to rank), or sanctity (purity and contamination), because the object is a continuing practice rather than a group, a hierarchy, or a taboo. Suppose, for the sake of argument, that a small pilot in two countries found legibility items loading on a factor separable from both fairness and liberty, and that stewardship items split off from loyalty and authority but predicted a pattern of political attitudes that neither of those did.

Please do three things. First, sort all eight — the six established foundations plus these two candidates — into tiers according to how much you would trust each to do independent explanatory work in accounting for left-right moral disagreement, using tiers along the lines of workhorse, real but secondary, probably reduplicative of something already on the list, and probably not a foundation at all, with one sentence of justification per item. Second, score each of the two candidates from 0 to 5 on each of the six admission criteria we have been using: signs of innate preparedness or early emergence, a recognizable eliciting pattern of events, a characteristic emotion, a characteristic motivation, culturally variable elaboration on a shared core, and predictive power for political attitudes that is not already captured by the existing foundations. Third, give me a number for how to split the item budget we set aside for new material between the two candidates (for instance 0.7 to legibility and 0.3 to stewardship), and say why. In each case I would rather have the reasoning that drives the number than the number alone, since we can always renormalize.

Finally, because the module is going into a deliverable that also has to say something about whether this psychology tells us anything about which side is right: tell me whether admitting either candidate would strengthen, weaken, or leave intact the symmetric conclusion the six-foundation picture is usually taken to support — namely that liberals and conservatives each draw on a genuine but partial subset of the moral domain, so that neither side can be dismissed as morally deficient — and in particular what it would mean for that conclusion if one of the new foundations turned out to be politically asymmetric in the way the established six are not.
