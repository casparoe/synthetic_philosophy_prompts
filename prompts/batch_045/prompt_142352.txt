I teach a 400-level undergraduate seminar on Minds and Machines, and I am writing the in-class final. The exam is 90 minutes long; students answer one essay question in about 700–900 words, and the question is worth 25 points. I would like you to draft that question, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme. The topic is Alan Turing’s treatment of extrasensory perception in his 1950 Mind paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence, especially the closing worry about whether a contestant might win the imitation game by telepathy, clairvoyance, or precognition rather than by the capacities the game is meant to measure. I am not interested in a question that can be answered by reciting his reply: that the usual precautions of separate rooms and teleprinter communication exclude ordinary sensory leakage, and that any leftover psi ability could be tested independently by some experiment rather than simply excluded, and that if the ability turned out to be real it would be a striking empirical discovery rather than an automatic disqualification. Any student can summarize that; merely doing so should earn a C at best.

To push them into the weeds, I have invented a variant I call the Duplication Game. Here the interrogator does not try to identify which contestant is the machine. Instead, the machine is given a fixed list of open-ended questions one at a time and is asked to produce the same answer that a particular human, the Source, would produce; the machine and the Source are in causally isolated rooms and all traffic is text-only. The machine is scored on how closely its answers match the Source’s answers. Now suppose the machine does far better than any known statistical model of human verbal behavior, but the explanation turns out to be what I will call the Reverse-Channel Worry: the machine has no serious linguistic model at all, and instead uses a narrow psi capacity—say, precognition of the exact future string the Source will write, or clairvoyance tuned only to that sealed answer—to read off the target output itself, rather than deriving it from the question plus a model of the Source. This is not ordinary sensory leakage, and it need not show up in independent tests for broad psi, because the psi capacity is narrowly tuned to the game’s target answers. I want the exam question to ask whether this success counts as passing the test, whether the Reverse-Channel Worry should be excluded as cheating, as irrelevant, or as a legitimate marvel, and, most importantly, what the right way to exclude it (if it should be excluded) reveals about the point of the imitation game. Students should be forced to distinguish two things that are easy to conflate: excluding a channel because it is not the capacity under test, which is ordinary test hygiene, like forbidding a student to look at the answer key; and excluding a channel because it would make the test question-begging, or would change the target from can machines think to the narrower can machines think using only non-psi mechanisms. I also want them to evaluate a test-design principle I have been toying with, the Post-Hoc Source Reassignment Control: after the machine has produced and stored its answers, the identity of the human whose answers count as the target is reassigned by a procedure the machine could not have foreseen at the time of output; the idea is that a genuine modeler should still beat chance, whereas a precognitive reader of one fixed future string should drop to chance. Students need not endorse this principle, but the question should require them to say whether Turing’s own discussion gives us the resources to handle the narrow Reverse-Channel Worry, and whether the Post-Hoc Source Reassignment Control (or a better principle they propose) is a legitimate elaboration of Turing’s method or an ad hoc rescue.

So please write, in polished American English suitable for a course syllabus, first the exam question exactly as the students would see it. Then give a model answer of about 700 words: it should take a clear stand on whether output-reading by narrow psi defeats the test, explain the distinction between capacity-based success and output-reading success, show what goes wrong if we collapse test hygiene into a substantive assumption about mechanism, and use the Duplication Game and the Post-Hoc Source Reassignment Control to make the argument concrete; it should not merely summarize Turing. Then give a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, with marks allocated so the total is 25. For each point, state the marks it is worth and add a one-line note on the mistake candidates typically make instead of hitting it. Finally, add a short paragraph after the scheme saying exactly what separates an A answer from a B answer: an A answer must do more than report Turing’s precautions and assert that psi should be excluded; it must say what the exclusion is for, apply the capacity-versus-output-reading distinction to the narrow-psi case, and either defend or criticize the Post-Hoc Source Reassignment Control as a principled extension of Turing’s method, whereas a B answer will recite the standard reply, grant that independent tests might catch broad psi, and then assert without argument that any psi channel is cheating. Please do not include any quotations from Turing, any page citations, or any bibliography; paraphrase his discussion in your own words, and rely only on the apparatus I have described above.
