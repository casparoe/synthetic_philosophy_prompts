I'm in a small reading group working through the philosophy of probability — we've covered the familiar standoff between frequentists and subjectivists and have arrived at objective chance, and one member of the group, who defends a propensity-style view, keeps leaning on a single comparison: that a chance stands to an experimental setup exactly as fragility stands to a wine glass, or solubility to a lump of sugar. He uses the comparison to do four jobs at once, and I'd like to know how much of that it can really carry. First, to secure objective probabilities for single cases: his favourite example is the chance that a particular unstable nucleus decays within the hour, or that a one-off engineered structure fails in its single planned use, where there is no long run of similar trials for a frequency to be read off. Second, to demote long-run frequencies to symptoms of the chance rather than to what chance means, so that the stability of relative frequencies becomes something to be explained rather than the explanation. Third, to underwrite the requirement that a rational agent, told the chance of an outcome and given no other relevant information, set her confidence in that outcome to the same number — the idea being that the chance is a feature of the world she is tracking, not a feature of her attitudes. Fourth, to reconcile objective chance with a deterministic underlying physics, on the grounds that dispositions like fragility and solubility are perfectly at home in a deterministic world, so there is no tension in saying that the chance of heads was one half even if the coin's trajectory was fixed by prior conditions.

I am not yet convinced the comparison does any of that work rather than relabelling it, so before we meet again I'd like an audit. Set out the mapping explicitly: what plays the role of the bearer, of the stimulus or standing conditions, of the manifestation, and of the degree to which the disposition is had; and say which elements of the disposition side have no counterpart on the chance side. Then give the disanalogies, and separate the ones that matter for the four jobs above from the ones that are merely cosmetic. The ones I keep circling are these. A disposition such as fragility points one way, from stimulus to manifestation, whereas probabilities get inverted: from the probability of an effect given its cause one can derive the probability of the cause given the effect, and it is unclear what the analogue of that inversion is for fragility. A fragile thing's manifestation is what normally happens when it is struck, whereas an outcome with chance 0.7 that fails to occur is not thereby abnormal or unaccounted for. Dispositions are sensitive to what philosophers used to call masking and finking — the glass that never breaks because it is packed in foam, the glass that would have been vapourised before the hammer arrived, the pill that cures only because something else in the bloodstream does the work — and I want to know what the counterpart of those cases is on the chance side, and whether their existence is a cost or a resource. Fragility and solubility are supposed to have categorical bases in microstructure and thermodynamics; I want to know what the categorical basis of a chance is meant to be, and whether the answer that it is the setup together with the laws collapses the view either into a frequency view or into the claim that the real chance is always zero or one. And I want to know whether a subjectivist can simply accept the entire comparison, pointing out that a person's confidence is itself a disposition — to accept certain bets, to act in certain ways — so that the analogy alone cannot be what makes chances objective.

Finish by telling me which of the four jobs the comparison can genuinely perform, which it merely restates in a new vocabulary, and where a frequentist or a subjectivist would say it is doing covert work; in particular, say whether the determinism point is a strength of the view or an embarrassment for it. If there is a closer analogy available — something that behaves the way chance behaves without importing a single-track causal direction or a hidden categorical basis — say what it is and what it would cost the propensity theorist to switch to it. Please write all of this in continuous prose, the way you would in a long email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. And don't give me papers to read or names to look up; I want the argument worked out, not a reading list.
