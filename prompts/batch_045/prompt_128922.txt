You are a philosopher of science with genuine expertise in scientific modeling, idealization, and the epistemology of representation. Please think carefully and at length. Before writing your answer, spend some time brainstorming: generate a candidate list of defeaters for the view below — including ones you will ultimately reject as merely apparent, and ones you think I haven't considered — and only then organize them. I would rather have the reasoning than a tidy list I can't audit.

Here is where I've ended up on the old question of whether a model's assumptions need to be true. I used to be a plain instrumentalist about models; I've since tried to make the view precise, and the result is what I call the *invariance licensing thesis*. It has four clauses, and I want you to attack the formulation I actually hold, not the crude version:

(a) There is no requirement — epistemic, methodological, or semantic — that a model's assumptions be true, approximately true, possibly true, or even that they represent anything at all.

(b) The only legitimate test of an assumption is *invariance licensing*. Say that two assumptions belong to the same *falsity-class* when they are false in the same respect and to roughly the same degree — both set a parameter to zero, both posit a continuum where there is a finite collection, both assume away a causal factor known to be present. Then: an assumption A of model M is licensed just in case, for every prediction in the set we care about, substituting for A any other member of A's falsity-class leaves the prediction unchanged, or moves it by less than the model's own antecedently fixed error tolerance. If M's successes are invariant across the class, A's falsity is doing none of the predictive work, and its falsity is therefore no objection to it.

(c) Consequently, standing demands that assumptions be realistic are either vacuous — they collapse without residue into the demand that the model predict well — or positively harmful, because they constrain a search over model space that should be unconstrained. Modeling is a generative activity; pruning the search space by representational scruples is a bias, not a virtue.

(d) Apparent counterexamples — the cases people cite where an idealization clearly mattered — are, I claim, either cases where the invariance test was never actually run, or cases where two distinct questions were run together: predicting within the observed regime versus extrapolating beyond it, or predicting versus explaining.

I hold this with real confidence and I want it stress-tested. What would count against it? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and for each one I want to know whether it is *live* — something a defender should take seriously now, given what we know about modeling practice and about the history of successful models — or merely logically possible. Specifically, please cover:

1. Whether clause (b) has any determinate content. The falsity-class and the error tolerance are both supplied by the modeler: gerrymander the class narrowly and nearly every assumption is licensed; loosen it and nearly none is. If the thesis's content is entirely a function of an unsupplied parameter, is that fatal, or is it the ordinary situation of a research programme?

2. Underdetermination by prediction. Suppose (stipulate this as a hypothetical) two models of the same epidemic are exactly observationally equivalent across every time series we have, but one assumes homogeneous mixing and the other assumes heterogeneous contact with the same mean, and they diverge sharply under a targeted-vaccination policy. Invariance licensing appears to license both and to be silent on which to act on. If the mechanistic model then does better under interventions, isn't the veridicality of assumptions epistemically load-bearing in a way my thesis cannot represent — and is that a refutation of (b) or only a demonstration that (b) is incomplete?

3. Idealizations that do not merely survive the derivation but carry it. Some predictions are limit results — frictionless motion, the zero-viscosity limit, infinite-population results. Where the limit is singular, so that the behavior does not approach the idealized behavior as the idealization is relaxed, the invariance test fails in precisely the case where it mattered. How common is that structure, and is its existence decisive or merely a restriction of scope?

4. Systematic de-idealization. If, as a rough historical generalization, replacing known-false assumptions with closer-to-true ones tends to improve out-of-sample performance, that is compatible with (b) — invariance simply failed in those cases. But does it refute (c)? I would like you to say exactly which clause each consideration strikes, rather than aiming at the view as a bloc.

5. The self-application problem. My evidence for (c) is largely historical: unrealistic-assumption models have often been predictively successful, and nothing about realism seems to have been needed. If that is the evidence, then a systematic historical finding of the opposite shape — that successful idealizations were typically later vindicated by a separate argument showing them harmless in the relevant limit or domain — would count against me. Is such an argument actually available, or am I conceding a defeater I need not concede?

6. What the thesis predicts that its rivals do not — for instance, that a predictively successful model with grotesquely false assumptions owes us no further defense, and that efforts to make assumptions realistic are, absent predictive payoff, wasted effort. Is there a case where that prediction is clearly false?

Two further things I want, and they matter to me more than the ranking itself. First, separate the defeaters that a committed invariance-licensing theorist should *concede* as counting against the view from those they would properly dismiss as resting on a rival framing; I can get critics' objections anywhere, and I want to know where my own position is internally exposed. Second, give me an honest verdict on insulation: has my formulation been qualified into unfalsifiability — in particular by clause (d), which looks uncomfortably like a license to reclassify any counterexample as a case where the test wasn't run or the question was conflated? And if it has been, say whether that is a genuine problem for a thesis of this kind, given that it is a methodological claim and not an empirical one.

For what it is worth, what I personally find hardest to shake are the intervention/extrapolation point in (2) and the singular-limit point in (3). Tell me if I am overrating them, and why.
