You are a professional philosopher who works at the intersection of moral epistemology and the epistemology of testimony. I'd like you to begin with some unstructured brainstorming about what could go wrong with the package below before you commit to an answer, and then to give me a carefully structured audit rather than a survey of the literature.

I'm a second-year PhD student putting together a short position statement for a reading group that is half epistemologists and half ethicists and mostly talks past itself, and I want the package stress-tested before I present it. Here is roughly where I've landed. (1) I'm an anti-reductionist about testimony in general: testimony is a basic source of justified belief, and I don't need independent reasons to trust a speaker in order to come to know what they tell me. (2) But I'm a pessimist about moral testimony in particular: a moral belief you acquire purely from someone else's say-so is epistemically defective in a way that ordinary testimonial belief is not. (3) The word purely is doing a lot of work for me: I think it is fine, and in practice unavoidable, to defer to others on the non-moral facts my moral verdicts rest on — what the animals actually experience, what the policy actually does, how the hiring process actually works — and then to take the moral step myself; most of my moral beliefs are formed that way and I don't think there is anything second-rate about them. (4) I also think there is no principled difference in kind between moral and non-moral expertise: in both domains the experts are hard to identify and disagree with one another, and the difference is one of degree. (5) I'm a reliabilist about knowledge across the board. (6) I don't think it is defective at all to form a moral belief on the basis of someone's first-personal report of what an experience was like for them: I trust my friend that my remark humiliated her, and I conclude that I shouldn't have said it. (7) And two case verdicts I find myself attached to: I think it is often not merely permissible but obligatory to act on moral testimony in high-stakes situations where I have no independent grip on the issue — I deferred to colleagues I trusted about whether a hiring practice in my department was discriminatory, pushed for the change on that basis, and would do so again; and I also treat my own past self's recorded moral verdicts as authoritative even when I have entirely forgotten the reasoning, as with an old journal entry of mine arguing carefully about whether to cut off contact with a family member, of which I now remember only the conclusion.

What I want from you: go through these commitments pairwise and tell me which are genuinely in tension and which only look as though they are, since I suspect at least one of the apparent conflicts dissolves on closer inspection and I can't tell which. Rank the real tensions by severity instead of listing them all as equally bad, and for each one say precisely what I would have to give up or qualify and what that would cost me — including whether the cost is mainly epistemic or mainly practical. I'm especially interested in whether (6) and the second half of (7) are really the same kind of case as the ones (2) was meant to cover, or whether they slip past (2) for some principled reason that I should then build into the statement of (2). Finally, tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and whether anything recognisable as pessimism survives it. Please be blunt about which plank is the weak one; I would much rather be told that than be given an overview of the debate.
