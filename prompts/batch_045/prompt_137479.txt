I run a small fortnightly reading group on philosophy of mind for people who mostly work in ML, and we've spent the last few sessions stuck on the problem of other minds. Two members have been arguing past each other for months and I want to force them to produce something constructive, so I need you to draft the joint statement they could both sign at the end of a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Camp A is the inferentialist camp: ascribing mental states to others is a posits-and-prediction affair, justified by something like the argument from analogy plus an inference to the best explanation, and there is no perceptual contact with another creature's mind (though A gets to insist that the relevant "inference" is not conscious, not deliberation-like, and not something the subject could report doing). Camp B is the direct social perception camp: paradigmatically we perceive other people's anger, intentions and so on in their embodied expressive behaviour, and that perception is epistemically basic rather than derived from observed behaviour plus a hidden premise (though B gets to insist that this perception is learned, culturally shaped and revisable). Please don't cite anything or name papers — describe any study you appeal to in your own words, since I want this to be usable by someone with no library access.

What I want is the document itself, not a dialogue and not a verdict on who wins. First, a numbered list of claims both can sign: about what needs explaining (please keep the phenomenal version of the problem — is anyone else conscious — separate from the belief-and-desire version, they keep sliding between the two), about what the sceptical challenge is even supposed to threaten, and about what developmental, comparative and clinical evidence can and can't settle. Second, a table of the disagreements that remain, each one stated twice, once in a wording A would sign and once in a wording B would sign, with a column flagging which of them you think are merely verbal (and why) rather than substantive. Third, the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side — and for each crux a concrete case or piece of evidence both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance, plus what each would count as evidence against their own view.

For cruxes I'd seed these, but add your own if they're better. (i) "Silent cortex": stipulate an adult with a completely standard human brain in which every functional role associated with pain is realised, who sincerely reports feeling nothing — do we say she is in pain? (ii) "Kaspar", a case I made up: a child raised in total social deprivation until nineteen, stipulated to have no sensory or cognitive impairment, first exposed to faces and voices at nineteen — I want each side to predict how fast and how well she comes to recognise emotion, and to say in advance which finding would embarrass them. (iii) The running fight over whether newborns really imitate adult facial gestures in the first days of life and whether the classic effect holds up in larger, preregistered attempts. (iv) The one I actually care about: a digital system whose conversation is statistically indistinguisable from a thoughtful person's over years of interaction, with no public facts at all about its internals — what does each view say about whether we have any reason to think there is something it is like to be that system, and what would each side count as a reason? Also, be honest if part of their fight is really just about how far the word "perception" can be stretched, or about which side bears the default burden of proof. Neither side is allowed to close any section with "well, we just can't know".
