Suppose you are designing the three-year research agenda for a small, philosophy-focused foundation program that can support roughly ten researchers working on conceptual questions—not empirical machine learning, not engineering, and not literature review—raised by Daniel Dennett's notion of counterfeit people. By counterfeit people, Dennett means AI systems that invite us to adopt the intentional stance: they generate behavior or text that is best predicted by attributing beliefs, desires, and commitments to them, and they thereby elicit the trust, reliance, and readiness to hold responsible that we normally reserve for human persons. The systems in question lack, however, the background conditions that make human beings appropriate objects of that stance in the full moral and social sense: they do not have biographies of their own, they do not suffer, and they do not bear the real costs of broken promises or reputational loss.

To make the agenda-setting concrete, I want you to use two distinctions I will introduce. Call the simpler case first-order counterfeiting: a system produces a human-like response in a bounded interaction, and the only illusion is that a person is present behind the text. Call the harder case second-order counterfeiting: a system maintains a persistent persona across time, claims to remember earlier interactions, expresses loyalty or makes promises, and thereby mimics not merely agency but the kind of diachronic commitment that normally underwrites trust. A second injected idea is the cost-of-commitment criterion: the proposal that what separates a real person from a counterfeit person is not the surface fluency of its outputs but the fact that a real person incurs genuine costs—social, reputational, material, or mortal—when it makes a commitment and then breaks or honors it, whereas a counterfeit person can simulate commitment without any downside risk.

Given that the program's goal is to clarify these distinctions well enough to guide later institutional design (for example, norms of provenance, disclosure, or deployment) and to guide the conduct of individual users (for example, when they should withhold or refuse the intentional stance), which open conceptual questions or lines of work should the program prioritize over its three years, and in what order? I am less interested in an exhaustive menu of interesting questions than in a defended agenda. For each line of work you prioritize, state why it is upstream of other questions—how many other issues depend on its answer—how tractable it is for philosophers and conceptually oriented theorists who will not run new empirical AI experiments, how neglected it currently is, and what kind of argument or evidence over the next two or three years would move it up or down your ranking. Be explicit about the trade-offs you are making: if you rank a highly important but intractable question above a tractable but less foundational one, say why; if you deprioritize a question because its answer would not change much downstream, say that too.

As part of this, assess the alleged implications of the counterfeit-people thesis. Which implications for institutional design or individual conduct genuinely follow from a correct understanding of first-order and second-order counterfeiting and of the cost-of-commitment criterion, and which are overrated or do not follow at all? Rank the surviving implications by how much they would change practice if institutions or individuals took them on board.

If you think either of the two distinctions I supplied is confused, or that a different distinction would better organize the field, say so and defend the replacement; the point is to see the architecture of the problem, not to accept my framing uncritically. Do not give me a reading list, a bibliography, or citations, and do not assume access to any external sources.
