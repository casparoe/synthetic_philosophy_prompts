I run a reading group on perception — half philosophers, half people who actually run psychophysics experiments — and we've spent something like a year on cognitive penetration and we are going in circles. Same three arguments every month, nobody moves an inch. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is, not a tour of the literature or a summary of the standard move and countermove.

Here's the shape of it. The pro-penetration philosophers want a claim about experience itself, not just about behavior: learn that a certain shape kind is dangerous, or that the face you're looking at belongs to someone who's been cruel, and things can genuinely look different to you afterward — not "you pay more attention," not "you draw a bigger coin," but a change in what it's like. They lean on the old classic paradigms (the coin-size studies, the hill that looks steeper when you're tired or hauling a heavy pack) and on armchair cases about expertise: once you can hear a style of music or pick out a bird call, it sounds different, and the claim is that there's something present in the experience that wasn't before.

The lab people say: fine, but every one of those paradigms has a boring explanation — response bias, demand characteristics, where your eyes happened to be, a shift in judgment rather than in seeing. There's a big skeptical review of the whole literature that basically concludes no study so far has ruled the boring stories out. And the other side replies that this is unfalsifiable: any effect you produce can always be redescribed as downstream of perception, so "rule out the boring explanation" turns into "produce an effect I can't redescribe."

Here's the bit I want you to actually chew on. A postdoc in our group ran something we thought would settle it locally. Participants learned arbitrary associations — some meaningless shapes paired with a small electric shock, others not. Then, in a continuous-flash-suppression task, the shock-paired shapes broke into awareness faster. Eyetracking with a gaze-contingent display kept retinal stimulation matched, a signal-detection control showed no shift in response criterion, and on an explicit judgment task people didn't report the trained shapes as more visible or more anything. Beforehand everyone agreed this would be informative. Afterward the group split exactly along the lines it already had: half said that's penetration, a learned value state changed what you consciously see at a level below judgment; half said that's a change in what gets access to awareness, which isn't a change in perception in the sense that anybody ever denied. Nobody could say what result would have settled it, which makes me think the disagreement isn't downstream of the data at all.

My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: "penetration" is doing three separable jobs in our discussions and nobody declares which one they mean. One is architectural — whether information from cognitive states gets in before the perceptual representation is fixed, i.e. whether the system is informationally encapsulated at all. One is about content and phenomenology — whether what the experience represents, or how it looks, changes. One is epistemic — whether an experience shaped by your beliefs could then justify those very beliefs, the circularity worry that made people care about encapsulation in the first place. I suspect these can come apart: a state might change what reaches awareness without changing what's represented, and might change how something looks without making it any worse as a justification. If so, "does cognition penetrate perception?" is three questions with different answers and different evidence standards, each side's favorite datum is evidence only for the question the other side isn't asking, and the impasse is a sign the question is ill-posed.

Is that right, or is there really one shared question with a live empirical dispute hiding under it? A related thing I can't tell: is part of this that one camp treats first-person reports and armchair cases as data and the other only treats third-person psychophysical ones, so they disagree about which cases even count as data?

And be honest about progress, because this matters practically: two of my grad students are picking dissertation topics next month and I'm the one telling them whether to spend three years on this. Given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a distinction we should be enforcing in the group, an experiment that would be decisive (sketch one, roughly), a change of question — or should we dissolve the debate instead of trying to win it? I'd especially like you to push on one thing: is the "it could just be attention / response bias / judgment" objection ever falsifiable, or is it a research-stopper wearing methodological rigor as a costume?
