For eleven years I was a senior moderator of a persistent sandbox world called Longshore — a world with its own currency, its own property registry, and its own appellate panel for disputes between players — and I am now writing the internal policy that says what counts as a thing in such a world and what counts as a harm to a player. I cannot write it case by case, because my own verdicts do not line up, and I would rather find out now which of them has to go. Here are the cases and what I think about them; please tell me what principle, if any, I actually hold.

One. A player spent roughly two hundred hours earning a rare mount through a publicized quest chain. Another player took it by exploiting a mismatch between the trade window's confirmation dialog and what the server actually committed. The mount was a real thing, and the second player did not merely break a rule; he took something from the first. I would call it theft without flinching.

Two. On Ashfall, a full-loot wilderness server whose advertised premise is that anything you build can be found and burned, a raiding party leveled a settlement that had taken four months to raise. Something real was destroyed and the builders genuinely suffered, but no one was wronged. They had been told exactly what they were walking into, and the loss is one they had taken on themselves in advance.

Three. Longshore's engine uses what we call witness-locked generation: terrain and objects in an unexplored region are produced on demand from a seed, and are written into the world-state only once a player has actually perceived them. In resetting the old continent we deleted, among much else, a crashed airship in a valley no player ever entered, which had nonetheless sat in the world-state for eleven months after being generated by an automated survey pass. My verdict: it existed, but nothing of value was lost when it went.

Four. Same engine, opposite shape. An item rendered in front of a player for perhaps forty milliseconds, in a chunk that unloaded before the item was ever written to storage; it was never persisted anywhere and will never be generated again. My verdict: that one was more of a thing than the airship, and its vanishing was more of a loss, despite its lasting a fraction of a second and leaving no trace at all. I know three and four pull against each other. Tell me which of them a defensible principle sacrifices.

Five. Two cases I cannot tell apart structurally and nonetheless judge differently. First: a rollback restores a backup, and for about six hours a unique artifact exists twice; we delete one copy and keep the other. No harm — there was never a second artifact. Second: the same duplication occurs, but instead of being repaired it becomes the basis of a permanent fork, two shards each with its own community, each treating its copy as the original, and a year passes; we then delete the copy on the smaller shard. By then there were two artifacts, and deleting one was a real loss to people who had built a year of plans around it. If what separates these is duplication plus elapsed time plus social uptake, say so, and say how much of each is required, because I need a rule I can apply to a live dispute.

Six. The game text describes a certain sword as unique; the engine can in fact produce as many as we like, and we produce a second for a promotional event. The holder of the first loses nothing: same statistics, same abilities, same everything he could do with it before. Still, I think something he had was taken from him and that we owe him something. Scarcity itself was part of what he owned.

Seven. On a social platform with haptic gloves, a stranger's avatar pressed against a player's chest and she felt it. She was harmed and she was wronged, and this is the case where 'it's only virtual' sounds most obviously false. Note that nothing of hers was damaged, taken, or diminished. So whatever my principle turns out to be, it cannot be merely a principle about losing things.

Eight. We are closing a server for a game nobody has played in years. One player still logs in every day to sit in a room he built with a friend who has since died. Closing it would be a serious harm to him; had he stopped logging in five years ago it would be no harm to anyone, even though the room would still have been sitting there in exactly the same state.

My own attempt, which I do not trust: a thing is real in a world if the rules of that world make it available to be encountered, and the loss of it matters to the extent that someone actually encounters it or would have. I think that handles three and four, badly mishandles seven, and has nothing whatever to say about five or six.

What I want from you: one principle, or at most two or three candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a dispute next month, with each clause tied to the case that forces it; then three cases I have not listed and would like your verdicts on — an object in a world that no one has logged into for ten years, in-world currency sold for real money in violation of the terms of service and then confiscated by us, and a companion creature raised over eighty hours that responded only to its owner and was then killed by another player; and finally an honest account of which of my eight verdicts your principle cannot accommodate and which you think I ought to abandon. Please write in continuous prose, the way one would write a long letter: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. American English throughout.
