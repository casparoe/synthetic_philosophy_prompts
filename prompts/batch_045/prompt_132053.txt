I hold a package of views about rigid designation and the necessary a posteriori, and I want a map of how exposed that package is — not a survey of the literature.

The package, stated as carefully as I can:

(1) A designator is rigid when it picks out the same thing with respect to every possible world in which that thing exists; ordinary proper names and natural-kind terms are rigid de jure, and their reference is secured not by any description that gives their meaning but by an initial act of anchoring plus a causal-historical chain of subsequent use.

(2) Hence identity statements flanked by rigid designators are, if true at all, necessarily true, and are knowable only a posteriori — the Hesperus/Phosphorus case, the case of the man wrongly believed to have proved a certain theorem, and the identification of a phenomenal or macroscopic kind with a microstructural one.

(3) I add a thesis of my own, call it the derivation thesis: every necessary a posteriori identity is derivable from an a priori knowable conditional — roughly, if the actual anchoring conditions obtain, then necessarily a = b — together with an empirical premise to the effect that those conditions do obtain. On this reading Kripke's cases do not show that modality outruns the a priori; they show that the antecedent is empirical, while the necessity itself is a priori once properly conditionalized.

(4) I extend (2) to theoretical identifications in science: that water is H2O, that a gene is (roughly) a segment of DNA, and their like, are necessary a posteriori if true at all.

What I want:

(a) For each of (1) through (4), what would count against it? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and separate what a defender of the package would themselves concede as counting against it from what only a critic would claim counts against it.

(b) Take the two-dimensionalist reply to (3) seriously. If the apparatus of epistemic or primary intensions recovers a priori graspable deep necessities, does the necessary a posteriori collapse into a species of the contingent a priori, or into a merely presentational surprise about which world is actual? Does the derivation thesis survive that result, and if it survives, does it survive as something still worth holding?

(c) Here is the wrinkle I am least comfortable with, and I want it addressed head-on. Our best theoretical identifications are only approximately true: the mechanical account of heat breaks down outside its regime, the actual stuff we call water is never pure H2O, and the mid-century molecular gene has fragmented into a family of related items. So the right-hand side of a theoretical identity is either the strict theoretical term, in which case the identity looks false rather than necessary, or a hedged role-specified term — something like whatever property in fact plays causal role R here — in which case the identity looks either a priori trivial, because the role-player is the role-player, or else contingent, because something else might have played that role. Call this the approximation fork. Which horn, if either, is the defender obliged to take, and does taking it put pressure on (4) alone or on the whole package?

(d) Assess a case of my own construction. Call a designator over-anchored when a community's usage traces back to two independent anchoring acts, each authoritative by that community's own lights at the time, which pick out distinct objects; the community never notices, because the two objects are indistinguishable by every test available to them, and only much later evidence separates them. Suppose such a term is then flanked in an identity statement the community accepts on empirical grounds. Is there a principled answer available from within the package as to whether that identity is necessary, and as to what the term designated all along? If the honest answer is that there is no fact of the matter, say so and tell me how much that costs the view.

(e) Finally, has the package been insulated by qualification into unfalsifiability? Restricting rigidity to worlds in which the object exists, and the retreat from plain rigidity to the de jure / de facto distinction, strike me as points where the view may have been made safe rather than supported. Tell me which qualifications you take to be load-bearing and which you take to be protective, and whether protective qualifications of that kind are legitimate for a view like this one.

For what it is worth about my own psychology: I would be most moved by an argument that the necessity in (2) is doing no work beyond the essentialist and causal claims that generate it — that the modal word is redundant once those are in place — and least moved by reminders of how surprising these identities were when first discovered.

Be explicit about your own confidence: attach a confidence marker to each ranking and each verdict, and single out the one claim in your answer you are least sure of, with a sentence on why. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
