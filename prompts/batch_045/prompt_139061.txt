I write about cognitive science and machine learning for a national newspaper, and I'm finishing a piece about the software tools that now generate typefaces on demand: you give them three or four letters drawn in the manner you want, and they produce the rest of the alphabet, including letters the user never specified. Reporting that piece has left me stuck on something I don't have a word for, and because I may need to name it in print, I would rather find out whether a term already exists than invent one and use it wrongly.

The thing I keep coming back to is the lowercase 'a'. In a humanist serif it is a two-storey shape with a bowl and a hook; in a geometric sans it is a circle with a stem on the right. The two share no strokes and, so far as I can tell, no feature you could isolate and point to — and each of them is arguably as close in shape to some other letter in its own alphabet as it is to the other. Yet both are unmistakably the same letter, and neither is a degraded or approximate version of anything. Three further observations seem to belong with it:

First, the identity appears to be fixed by neighbours rather than by shape. 'a' has to be distinguishable from 'o', 'd', 'q', 'g' and 'u' *as those letters are drawn in that particular alphabet*, so the same drawn shape can be a perfectly good 'a' in one typeface and unusable in another; and lifting a glyph stroke-for-stroke from one font into another reliably produces something wrong, even though nothing about the glyph itself has changed.

Second, the constraint seems to be on the whole set at once, not on the parts one at a time. Given a few letters of a font, a competent designer will produce the rest, and we judge the results as correct or incorrect without being able to state a rule that was violated. But it is also possible to draw every letter of a font acceptably and still produce a font that does not hang together; and it is possible to draw a single glyph that is fine in isolation and wrong for this set. Knowing how a font does its 'a' lets you predict something about how it will do its 'g', which is not a fact about resemblance between 'a' and 'g'.

Third, I don't think the usual frames fit. Someone told me this is just family resemblance, but resemblance seems too weak: it explains why the two 'a's are both 'a's, not why the difference between two fonts' 'a's predicts the difference between their 'g's. Prototype and exemplar accounts of categories don't fit either, since there is no single best example and, in the two-storey/single-storey case, no single example that both instances resemble. The word designers use, style, is doing far too much work to be a definition.

So my question is whether there is an established concept for this, in typography, in the psychology of categorization, in linguistics, or anywhere else — and, more importantly, how the thing should be carved. I am interested in whether the right account is resemblance-based with no essential features, or relational (the letter as a slot defined by its contrasts with the other slots in a specified alphabet), or something about systems whose parts are mutually constraining and must be solved together, or something else I haven't thought of; which of these the nearest established concepts actually support; and how each falls short. I also need the edges: a revival or parody that deliberately quotes another font's letters, a glyph that is individually fine but wrong for the set, a set where each glyph is fine but the family fails, and a reductive 'a' reduced to a bare circle with a tick. What I can't use is a bare label; I need a term I can define in a clause and defend to readers who know typography and to readers who don't.
