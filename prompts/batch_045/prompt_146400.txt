I sit on the committee revising our jurisdiction's model statute on advance health-care directives, and the remit has quietly grown. We are being asked to say whether the statute should recognize two instruments that no existing framework was drafted for, and we have discovered that we cannot even say what we would need from an account of the matter in order to decide. So before we look at theories, I want the specification.

What I need is the list of requirements an adequate account of the authority of an earlier self over a later self must meet. Adequate for us means: usable by a hospital ethics committee, within a few hours, on the evidence actually available at the bedside, and capable of being turned into statutory language that says who bears the burden and what happens when a directive, a named surrogate, and the treating team disagree. I want requirements that cannot be operationalized listed too, marked as such rather than dropped — I would rather know that a requirement is real and unlegislatable than have it quietly subtracted.

Here are the cases I think any account must get right, with the reason each earns its place on the list.

1. The contented patient with advanced dementia. She signed at sixty a directive refusing antibiotics and assisted feeding in that condition; at eighty she has advanced dementia, appears content, greets visitors warmly, and would resist withdrawal. The account must say whether present contentment is a reason to set the directive aside, and whether continuing to keep her alive can itself be a harm to her — or whether that thought is one the theory is required to avoid.

2. The convert. At twenty-five she refuses blood transfusion on religious grounds; at forty-five she has lost the faith, is competent and fully informed, and wants the transfusion, saying her earlier self was simply wrong about what matters. Distinguish from the variant in which she still avows both the faith and the directive but asks for the transfusion out of fear. A theory that handles these two the same way has collapsed something.

3. The psychiatric Ulysses. A directive, written in remission, authorizing involuntary admission and medication during manic episodes — that is, a request to be overridden precisely while competent and precisely while refusing. If a theory cannot override contemporaneous competent refusal, say whether it can give this instrument any force at all.

4. The addiction case. A directive written in remission refusing opioids except in defined circumstances; the same person now in genuine severe pain and asking for them; and no reliable way for the team to tell craving from pain. The account must say who decides that distinction and on what standard, not merely that it matters.

5. Unanticipated circumstances and indeterminate terms. A directive written before the treatment existed, or one whose language (no extraordinary measures, no prolonging of life) does not determine an answer for the intervention now proposed. The theory has to say whether the gap is filled by the signer's values, by her likely words, or by a default rule — and why.

6. Factual error. The directive rested on a belief that is false: that advanced dementia is continuous terror, that a stroke at eighty means institutionalization with no visitors, that a given prognosis is hopeless when a newer treatment has changed it. Distinguish the case where she was wrong about the facts from the case where she was wrong about her own future values, if the distinction can bear weight.

7. Beyond medicine. Instructions about identity-constituting projects rather than treatment: a novelist's direction that her unfinished manuscript be destroyed if she loses the capacities needed to complete it, with her present self content and wanting it published. I want to know whether a theory of precedent autonomy is a theory about bodies and treatment specifically, or whether it must generalize, and what it costs to confine it to the clinical case.

8. The two new instruments. First, the transformation clause: the signer specifies in advance what would count, in her own terms, as her having become a different person — if I come to affirm belief R, if I no longer recognize my children, if I stop caring about P — and stipulates that the directive lapses when the condition is met. Give me the hard variants, including the case where she meets her own stated condition and insists the directive should still apply, and the case where the trigger is the very state the directive was written to preempt. Second, the continuing-self model: a system trained on a person's own past writing, purchases, messages and records, which predicts her present values better than she can now articulate them. Should a written directive be read as deferring to that prediction? Is the prediction evidence of present values, a new kind of authority, or neither? Who has standing to invoke it against her present word?

Alongside the cases, I want the distinctions the account must not collapse: authority versus epistemics (the earlier self's standing to decide versus her supposed better knowledge of her own values, and what happens when the epistemic advantage reverses); who decides versus what should be decided; a directive being void, being overridden, and being reinterpreted; identity questions versus evaluative-change questions; the interests of the then-self, of the now-self, and of the person across a whole life; legal competence thresholds versus whatever capacities actually do the work.

Two further things. Tell me which pairs of requirements you think cannot be jointly satisfied — for my money the interesting candidates are the demand never to override contemporaneous competent refusal against the demand to ground authority in something other than present incompetence, and the demand to be sensitive to the kind and magnitude of personal change against the demand to be administrable in an afternoon; but find the real collisions rather than confirming mine. And tell me which single requirement does the most exclusionary work, the one that eliminates the most candidate positions.

I also want my own list audited: flag anything on it that is really a prejudice of one camp or an artifact of the fact that we are drafting a statute rather than doing moral philosophy, and add what I have missed. Where I have relied on an empirical claim — that many people with advanced dementia are not well described as suffering, that directives are frequently unavailable or unheeded at the point of care, that stated treatment preferences drift and directives are rarely revisited — tell me whether it is established or itself disputed.

Finally, a compressed scorecard against four position types, as a small table and not the centre of the answer: precedent autonomy as genuine authority rather than as evidence; directives as merely evidentiary input to a present best-interests judgment; the identity view, on which the question is whether signer and present patient are the same person and where they are not the instruction is a stranger's; and the supported-decision-making view, on which the whole framing is a mistake and the task is to support present capacity rather than to allocate authority between temporal selves. Name the thinkers associated with each only if you are confident of the attribution; I care about the positions.

Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, mark each requirement as near-universal, contested, or a commitment of one camp, and do not present a contested view as where the field has landed.
