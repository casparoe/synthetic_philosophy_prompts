I am a retired geochemist; most of my working life was spent on radionuclide transport in deep geological repositories, which means I spent it thinking about hundred-thousand-year timescales. Since retiring I have been sitting in, by permission, on a graduate seminar in practical ethics, and I find that my instincts there are formed by that habit of mind.

At our last meeting, on the discounting of future benefits, I raised the worry that standard cost–benefit practice seems to shrink harms and benefits falling on people centuries from now to almost nothing, and that the justification for this often looks like a moral claim smuggled in as a technical parameter. A member of the seminar with economics training replied, with the air of closing the subject, that a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush.

As far as I could tell, that remark was doing two jobs at once: first, presenting the discounting of future benefits as plain common sense rather than as a contested moral premise; and second, suggesting that anyone who insists on a zero pure rate of time preference is committed to something absurd, such as diverting a large share of present resources to the very distant future. I am not persuaded by either move, but I cannot yet say exactly where the proverb stops being a platitude and starts being the substantive claim his argument needed.

I would therefore like a precisification. Please set out the distinct precise theses that sentence could be expressing, in the context of weighing benefits that accrue long after everyone now living is dead. In particular, I want the following separated out:

Readings on which the difference between the bird in the hand and the birds in the bush is explained by something other than lateness as such — the risk that a deferred benefit never materialises; the chance that the beneficiary, or I myself, will not exist when it falls due; the opportunity cost of capital, given that resources invested now yield more later; or the diminishing marginal value of goods to generations expected to be wealthier than ours.

Readings on which lateness as such is doing the work, and the question of whose discounting is in play: a single individual ordering goods across her own lifetime; a planner choosing for overlapping generations whose membership turns over; or one generation weighing benefits that accrue entirely after its own death.

What is being discounted in each case, since a rate that is unproblematic applied to money may be quite another matter applied to consumption, and another again applied to welfare.

Whether the reading is time-consistent, in the sense that the same relative weighting would be applied from every date, or whether it amounts to a present-biased weighting that the same agent, choosing earlier, would have rejected.

For each reading on the menu, please give a one-line verdict: trivial, substantive and defensible, or false. Then tell me which reading, if any, would actually support the two moves the proverb was being used for, and which reading people who repeat it in arguments like this are most likely to have in mind. I suspect — but I would like this checked rather than indulged — that the readings which are true are mostly about risk, growth or mortality, that each of those is already represented elsewhere in any competent calculation, and that none of them yields the conclusion that the far future may be discounted merely because it is far. If the eliminative reading is the right one, say so plainly.

I would find it most useful as a table or numbered menu, ordered from the least to the most philosophically loaded, that I can copy and take to the next meeting. I am not asking for a survey of the literature and I have no need of citations; what I want is the distinctions and the verdicts on them.
