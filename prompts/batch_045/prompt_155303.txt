hey — I want a piece of in-universe satire, and here's the setup so you don't have to dredge up the literature (also: please don't name Kripke, Wittgenstein or any actual philosopher anywhere in the piece, and no authors note at the end).

The background is rule-following scepticism. The idea: there is no fact about a speaker that constitutes their meaning addition rather than quaddition, where quus is the function that gives x + y when both numbers are under 57 and gives 5 otherwise. Someone who has only ever added smallish numbers is perfectly consistent with having meant quus all along, and nothing in their past usage, their dispositions, their introspection, or any supposed semantic or platonic fact settles it; adding an interpretation of the rule doesn't help, because the interpretation itself needs interpreting. The sceptical solution concedes all of that and keeps our talk of meaning by swapping truth conditions for assertibility conditions: you count as meaning addition if the community is prepared, in the circumstances, to accept your answers, and an answer is correct when it would pass. Which leaves some awkward bits — a speaker with no community can't be wrong, a unanimous community can't be wrong either, and the felt obligatoriness of the rule (the sense that you ought to say 125) has nowhere to live.

What I want: an internal guidance circular, 800-900 words, from a fictional Bureau of Rule Adjudication to newly appointed Case Officers, issued some years after a court ruling (name it whatever) that accepted the sceptical conclusion and turned meaning into an administrative matter. Numbered sections, deadpan civil-service voice, full header block (To / From / Date / Subject), standard-issue sign-off. No winking: the Bureau should be entirely comfortable with itself.

The jokes have to come from getting the position right. Please include:

1. A standing instruction never to cite what the claimant really meant. Determinations proceed from community assertibility plus an invented metric — call it the Community Overlap Ratio, the fraction of probe cases on which the claimant matches the Bureau's registered panel, threshold 0.9. Explicitly rejected alternatives should get a line each: Dispositional Assessment (the finitude problem), Semantic Fact Petitions from the straight-solution crowd (unfounded), and the temptation to add a Rule for Interpreting the Rule, which the Bureau handles with a Schedule of sub-clauses (4.2.1, 4.2.1.1...) that just recurses and is treated as ordinary paperwork.
2. A worked example: a citizen who answers 5 to 68 + 57. Their protest that they meant addition gets logged as a subjective impression of obligation and given no evidential weight. And then the nasty bit — if the neighbours also say 5 on large-number probes, there is no error to correct, so the only available remedy is redrawing the panel, and the circular should say that straight-faced, without any hint that it is cheating.
3. Guidance on the solitary claimant (no community, so no determination is possible — adjourn, hold in abeyance) and on unprecedented probes beyond anything in the file: the Officer is told this is a decision and not a discovery, and is forbidden from writing was in fact correct in the record.
4. One quoted outside critic in a complaints-received box, a holdout from a straight-solution pressure group, whose objection is one they could actually make — e.g. that the Ratio just relocates the problem since the panel's own answers need interpreting — and the Bureau's reply should be pure procedure.

Two hard constraints: nobody in the piece may slip back into there being a fact of the matter, and the reader should still be able to tell what's being ironized without the narrator winking. Ideally someone who actually likes the sceptical solution finds it fair rather than a strawman — the Bureau should be applying the view consistently, just with clipboards. And it should also be funny to someone who's never heard of any of this. recieved typos are fine, keep it casual-ish in the voice of the memo but competent.
