I'm a PhD student in economic history, and the chapter I'm grinding on right now is basically an attempt to get past the two-column ledger version of the Raj debate — you know the version: Ferguson's column of credits, Tharoor's column of debits, and whoever talks loudest about the railways wins. I've got stuck on this bit from Marx's 1853 piece on British rule in India, and I keep feeling like it's doing something that neither column has room for:

"There cannot, however, remain any doubt but that the misery inflicted by the British on Hindostan is of an essentially different and infinitely more intensive kind than all Hindostan had to suffer before. [...] All the civil wars, invasions, revolutions, conquests, famines, strangely complex, rapid, and destructive as the successive action in Hindostan may appear, did not go deeper than its surface. England has broken down the entire framework of Indian society, without any symptoms of reconstitution yet appearing. This loss of his old world, with no gain of a new one, imparts a particular kind of melancholy to the present misery of the Hindoo, and separates Hindostan, ruled by Britain, from all its ancient traditions, and from the whole of its past history."

What I can't settle is what "deeper than its surface" is actually a claim about. I keep going between two readings:

(a) a claim about depth of target — earlier conquerors changed who ruled and how much was extracted, but left standing the institutions that did the actual day-to-day reproducing of social life (irrigation and tank maintenance, village-level risk pooling, the local credit and grain relations, the caste-anchored division of labour), whereas the British dissolved those; or

(b) a claim about time and irreversibility — the harm isn't that the old framework broke, since frameworks break all the time, but that the breaking wasn't followed within any relevant window by anything discharging the same functions, so the loss is a pure loss for the people living inside the gap.

The reason I care is that I've been toying with something I'm calling "replacement-rate accounting": you put on the debit side not an amount of welfare but a lag — the interval between the moment a given institution stops discharging a function and the moment anything else starts discharging it again — and then you force the credit column to be dated the same way. A railway opened in 1870 can't offset a system of tank irrigation that decayed from 1830, because the people who lived through 1830–1870 weren't compensated by a future most of them didn't reach. Marx actually says something close to this himself earlier in the same article, about artificial fertilization being dependent on a central government and immediately decaying with the neglect of irrigation and drainage.

So, two things I'd really like help with:

1. Does "without any symptoms of reconstitution yet appearing" support reading (b), and on either reading, what would Marx have accepted as a symptom? Would he have counted the railways as one — or is a railway, on his view, a conveyance for a new framework rather than the framework itself? I genuinely can't tell whether he'd have seen 1850s India as "not yet reconstituted" or as "constitution is impossible under foreign extraction."

2. Is replacement-rate accounting unfair to the credit column? I go back and forth. On one hand it looks like straight goalpost-moving: it shrinks any debit that eventually gets followed by something functionally equivalent, which seems to let empire off the hook precisely where Tharoor most wants to press. On the other hand, if the credits are structurally unable to cancel the debits under this framing — because they arrive late and denominated in a different unit — then the result is a much more radical claim than either side in the debate makes, and I'm not sure I'm entitled to it just by redefining the unit of account.

And one smaller thing: am I wrong to read the "melancholy" line as Marx registering a harm that isn't welfarist at all — something about historical continuity or meaning — that neither ledger can even represent? My supervisor says I'm definately reading my own chapter into him. Sorry if this is rambly, I've been reading to much 19th-century journalism this week and it's showing.
