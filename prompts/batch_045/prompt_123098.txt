My brother went down a conspiracist rabbit hole about three years ago and barely talks to anyone in the family now, so I've ended up reading way more about echo chambers than I ever meant to. Next week I'm giving a 20-minute talk to our county library system's adult-programming staff — they run the information-literacy sessions and they're committed to the line that anyone can learn to find reliable sources — plus the two high-school civics teachers who partner with them (they want something they can actually do with 120 students in a semester and somehow tell whether it worked), plus one county commissioner who thinks media literacy is a euphemism for telling people what to think.

My thesis: the failure isn't a gap in what people are exposed to. In an epistemic bubble you're missing the other side's evidence; in an echo chamber you've heard it and you've been taught to distrust whoever says it. Those are different failure modes and they point to opposite interventions, so exposure-based fixes — feeds with more cross-cutting content, source-checking drills — can actively make things worse. The variable I want to put on the table instead is what I'm calling testimonial surface area: the range of social roles a person would actually accept a correction from — the coworker, the brother-in-law who votes the other way, the nurse, the guy at the dive shop. More content arriving from a discredited source isn't a correction, it's one more data point that the outside is lying to you.
