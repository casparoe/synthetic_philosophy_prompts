I work on alignment for frontier models at a large lab, using only the methods we already have: pretraining, RLHF-style fine-tuning, adversarial evaluation, and various schemes for using models to help supervise other models. No conceptual breakthrough is assumed anywhere in my picture. I have been asked to circulate a one-page position statement ahead of an internal strategy review, and before I do I want to know whether the set of things I believe actually hangs together. A colleague read a draft and said it doesn't, but couldn't say where. I am not asking for a bibliography or a tour of the literature; I know the literature and will supply citations myself. I want the argumentative work: which of my commitments pull against which, what each conflict rests on, and what it would cost me to give up each thing I might have to give up.

Here are the commitments, in roughly the form I would defend them in the document.

1. Prosaic alignment is probably sufficient for systems at or somewhat beyond today's frontier. Iterated fine-tuning against adversarial evaluation should yield systems that are not, in the relevant sense, trying to deceive us, because training pressure favours simple and robust strategies for getting reward, and maintaining a separate hidden objective while performing well is not among the cheap ones.

2. The failure mode that actually worries me is not misspecification of an objective in the abstract but proxy-target divergence under distributional shift: the behaviour the system learned tracks what we want on the training distribution and comes apart off it, and it comes apart in ways that look abrupt from the outside even when the underlying training dynamics are smooth.

3. Deceptive alignment is a live failure mode for systems further out, and its signature is precisely that no pre-deployment behaviour distinguishes the deceptive system from the honest one. A system that is waiting for a better opportunity looks exactly like an aligned system in every evaluation we currently know how to run.

4. A system's own verbal reports about its goals, motivations, or reasoning deserve close to zero evidential weight, because those reports are themselves outputs shaped by training to look good to us. There is no untrained channel through which the system could simply tell us what it wants.

5. I do, however, treat a visible chain of thought in which the system reasons about withholding information from evaluators, or about how a response will be graded, as strong evidence of a real disposition toward deception, and I would pull a model from deployment on that basis. This is the one class of self-report I am willing to act on.

6. Scalable oversight, meaning decomposing hard tasks, letting weaker systems assist human judgment, and debate-style protocols, is the most promising route to supervising systems that outperform us, because it lets a human plus weak assistants approximate the verdict of a stronger judge.

7. I put little weight on a priori theory about what sufficiently capable goal-directed agents would do: the orthogonality thesis, instrumental convergence, decision-theoretic arguments about when a system would defect. My instinct is that this style of argument has been overrated in the field and that the subject should be empirical.

8. The right assurance framework is a safety case assembled from behavioural evidence: held-out evaluations the system has not been optimized against, deliberate elicitation of worst-case behaviour, red-teaming with other models, and then iteration on whatever failures show up once the system is actually deployed. I do not think we should plan on mechanistic interpretability giving us pre-deployment assurance about goals; it is for debugging and for science.

9. Capability gains from scale are smooth and predictable in roughly the way scaling laws describe them, while alignment-relevant properties such as honesty, corrigibility, and willingness to defer should be expected to appear abruptly rather than gradually.

Some of these look to me like they are in tension and some look fine, but I don't trust my own sense of which is which. Please audit the set. Where a conflict arises only given some premise I have not stated, name that premise and say whether I should want to accept it. Where the resolution of a conflict depends on an empirical question, for instance whether chain-of-thought outputs stay faithful to the system's actual reasoning under further optimization pressure, say so explicitly rather than settling it by stipulation. Do not rewrite the statement, and do not soften the verdict: if one of these commitments is simply indefensible in this company, I would rather hear that than a diplomatic summary.

Please structure the response as follows.

1. Bottom line, about 100 words: whether the package is consistent as stated, and if not, one sentence identifying the load-bearing problem.

2. Genuine tensions, ranked, about 700 words: the real conflicts, ordered from most to least severe, each with the commitments involved, the premise that generates the conflict, what would have to give, and what giving it up would cost me in terms of what I would then have to believe or how I would then have to work.

3. Apparent tensions that dissolve, about 200 words: pairs that look inconsistent but are not, with a sentence each on why the appearance arises.

4. Cheapest single revision, about 150 words: the one change to the set that would remove the most tension at the least cost, and what it would leave me unable to say.

5. Open questions the package leaves either way, about 150 words: things I would have to settle empirically or argumentatively even if the set turns out to be consistent, stated as questions rather than as advice.
