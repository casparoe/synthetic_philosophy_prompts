I sit on the small technical secretariat of a constitutional-reform commission, and I need something I can turn into a briefing note, not a literature summary.

The commission has spent two sessions fighting over one clause. The proposed mechanism — call it the *confirmatory suspension rule* — works like this: a statute passed by the legislature can be suspended for twelve months if a chamber of 150 randomly selected citizens, sitting for six weeks with expert witnesses and party-nominated advocates on both sides, resolves by a two-thirds vote that the statute is one that the people would not have willed had they had the opportunity to examine it. Suspension automatically triggers a referendum within the twelve months. If the referendum affirms the statute, it is reinstated and shielded from suspension for five years; if it rejects it, the statute falls.

Two commissioners have each shut down discussion with the same phrase. The first, a former minister, said that the will of the people is not a riddle to be solved by 150 strangers: the last general election produced a majority government on a platform that contained this statute, and that is what the will of the people is, full stop. The second, a deliberative-democracy scholar, said that an election result is a snapshot of poorly informed preferences under conditions nobody would defend, and that the assembly exists precisely to find out what the will of the people actually is, as opposed to what a transient majority happened to say. One phrase, two opposite institutional conclusions: the first to block the clause, the second to make it the centrepiece of the reform.

I want a disambiguation that would let the commission see what it is arguing about. Please lay out, as a menu rather than as a single favoured analysis, the precise theses that 'the will of the people' could be expressing — the aggregative or majoritarian one, the counterfactual-deliberative one (what the people would will under specified better conditions), the general-will style contrast with the will of all, the corporate-group-agent one, the constitutive one on which no will exists prior to a legitimate procedure and the procedure does not track but makes it, the purely legitimating one on which the phrase has no descriptive content and functions as an honorific claim to authority, and the exclusionary or moralised one on which 'the people' denotes the genuine or virtuous part against an alien elite. For each, say what would have to be true for it to be correct, whether it is close to trivial, whether it is substantive and defensible, whether it is false or incoherent, and what institutional consequence follows from accepting it. Then tell me which reading each of my two commissioners is committed to, and — this matters most — whether that reading actually delivers their conclusion, or whether they are each borrowing a strong-sounding reading and then relying on a weaker one.

My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the real disagreement is not about the word 'will' at all but about two prior questions: whether the will is something a procedure *tracks* or something a procedure *constitutes*, and whether the relevant 'people' is the current electorate or the set of everyone whose interests the statute affects. If I am right, the interesting consequence is that the clause is not neutral between readings: the two-thirds threshold already presupposes an epistemic, cautionary conception of what the assembly is detecting — a majority rule would encode an aggregative one, and something like unanimity would encode a general-will one — so the mechanism smuggles in a theory of the will before anyone argues about it. Tell me whether that diagnosis holds up, and whether there is a further axis I am missing.

Three wrinkles I would like addressed rather than smoothed over, because they are where our drafting actually stalls. First, specification sensitivity: the counterfactual reading seems to yield different answers depending on how the hypothetical conditions are filled in — how much information, what deliberative format, what time horizon, whether the costs falling on people who cannot yet vote are counted — and I cannot see what selects among these specifications. Second, the bundle problem: even the hard-nosed aggregative reading has trouble getting from a vote for a party and a platform to a will about one particular statute, since voters choose packages and any statute-level reading requires interpretation; so I am not sure the first commissioner's apparently modest reading is as modest as it sounds. Third, identity over time: the electorate that votes in the confirmatory referendum is not numerically the same as the one that returned the legislature, and some of those affected by the statute are minors, resident non-citizens, or not yet born.

Finally, a sentence on usage. Outside the commission, the phrase is used constantly by movements claiming to speak for the real people against elites, and by commentators dismissing those claims as fictitious. On your menu, which reading is actually in play in that ordinary use, and is it the same one either of my commissioners is relying on?

I do not want citations or further reading; I want the distinctions, the verdicts on each, and an honest statement of which parts of this the commission could settle and which it cannot.
