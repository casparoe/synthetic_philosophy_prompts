I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I maintain a system that stores anticipatory instructions people write about their own future incapacity and, when a trigger condition fires, hands a recommendation to the clinician or proxy who has to act on it. The clinical cases are the familiar ones: a written refusal of invasive life-prolonging treatment in the event of advanced irreversible dementia, and the psychiatric analogue in which someone authorizes compulsory admission or forced medication during manic episodes. I also maintain a consumer product in which people pre-commit to future actions, and the same question comes up there, which suggests to me that the issue is not really medical.

I do not want a survey of how any particular jurisdiction treats these documents. I am told the statutory rules vary and I have no way to check them, and I do not want a specification that rests on them. What I need is the normative core: when, and on what grounds, does an instruction issued by an earlier person have authority over a later person who is no longer in a position to have issued it?

Please give me the answer as a requirements document rather than an essay. I want the desiderata that any adequate account of the authority of the earlier self has to meet, where adequate means good enough to be implemented as policy at the bedside by a relative stranger, with incomplete information, in a few minutes, and defensible afterward to a review board and to the patient's family. For each requirement: state it, give the case or consideration that motivates it, say what it excludes, and mark it as non-negotiable or as the preference of one camp. Then tell me which of my requirements you think are not genuine desiderata at all but prejudices that happen to be widespread; whether some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, since if it is, that fact is itself the most useful thing you can tell me; and which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate theories.

Here are the cases I already think are load-bearing. Please add the ones I am missing, and tell me if any of mine is confused or is doing no work.

1. *The baseline.* A competent adult refuses, in writing, invasive life-prolonging treatment in the event of permanent unconsciousness or advanced irreversible dementia; years later the condition obtains. I take it that any theory that cannot deliver this verdict is disqualified.
2. *The contented patient.* As above, except the later person appears to take real pleasure in music and visitors and shows no sign of wanting to die, and the earlier document's stated reason was horror at being a burden on others. I want to know whether an instruction can be separated from the reasons that produced it, and whether the answer differs depending on whether the later person has preferences at all or merely affects.
3. *The Ulysses case.* A person with bipolar disorder authorizes forced medication during manic episodes and, while manic, revokes it fluently, at length, and with apparent coherence.
4. *The depressed author.* A refusal of aggressive treatment written during a severe depressive episode, never revisited after recovery, acted on years later.
5. *Drift.* A refusal of being kept alive by machines, written when that phrase meant an intensive-care ventilator, in a situation where the only intervention on offer is a portable ventilator at home or a daily tablet. Or a directive written before a therapy existed that turns the triggered condition from terminal into chronic and manageable.
6. *The corpus.* Five documents over fifteen years with no clean revocation, the most recent being the most restrictive but signed during a bad month, and an earlier one being visibly better informed. Last-writer-wins versus best evidence of an enduring will.
7. *The self-sealing trigger.* A directive that applies only if I no longer recognize my family, or only if I am no longer myself: a condition whose satisfaction is best evidenced by exactly the later report that the condition discounts. Does a theory have to permit triggers that entrench themselves against the only available evidence, and if not, what is the principled reason?
8. *The stack.* A clause saying that any revocation I issue while manic is to be ignored; a later clause saying that any clause stripping my future power to revoke is to be ignored; possibly a third clause about the second. Does the theory have to supply a fixed point here, or may it let the stack collapse to a default, and on what basis?
9. *The gate.* A hypothetical policy under which directives lapse unless reaffirmed on a schedule, as a lease lapses unless renewed. Is reaffirmation merely evidence that the person still endorses the instruction, or does it do independent normative work as a condition on authority? Does authority decay continuously or lapse all at once?
10. *Granularity.* A later person who is competent about some matters, such as recognizing family or refusing one particular drug, but not about others, such as weighing a long-horizon tradeoff. Is authority scoped decision by decision, or is the person one unit for this purpose?
11. *Third-party effects.* A directive demanding everything, which will consume years of a family's life and a large share of its resources; or a directive requiring clinicians to provide what they regard as physiologically non-beneficial. Is the earlier self's authority bounded by effects on others, and is such a bound a limit on authority or a distinct side-constraint that the theory of authority can stay silent about?

Beyond the cases, I think a theory has to respect some distinctions that I have seen collapsed in practice, and I would like you to correct the list: having authority versus how much authority; the authority of an instruction versus the authority of the values behind it; the earlier self's word being binding versus being merely good evidence of what the later self wants or of what is good for the later self; revocation versus supersession versus never having applied; and a trigger condition being satisfied versus a trigger condition being verifiable by someone other than the party who benefits from its being satisfied.

Two further constraints from my side, which you should treat as candidates for desiderata rather than as given. The first is observability: a criterion that turns on facts nobody at the bedside can establish in minutes is not one I can implement, however true it is, so for each of your requirements I want the observable proxy you would accept and your view on whether demanding observability is a genuine desideratum or a corruption of the theory for administrative convenience. The second is error asymmetry: when the record is silent or genuinely ambiguous, should a system treat the earlier instruction as in force or as not in force, and is the asymmetry between those two failures part of the theory of authority or a separate moral input that the theory should be required to declare rather than absorb silently?

Finally, and briefly, because I care much more about the criteria than about the scoring: I have been working with four rough positions, which I will describe rather than name in case I have them wrong. One says that competent choices about one's own future bind, full stop. One says that what matters is the interests and experiences of the person now in the bed, with the document at most evidence of those. One says that what binds is the earlier person's enduring values or character rather than the literal list of interventions, so the document is a fallible guide that can be overridden in its own terms. One says that what binds is something like authorship of a life, so the earlier self can have authority over how that life goes that outruns both the text and the later self's present preferences. Give me a short paragraph on where each fails your list, and tell me whether I have drawn the four wrong or have missed a position that the list makes available. A table at the end is fine.
