I'm in a philosophy of biology seminar this term, and our final paper is meant to be a position piece on the units of selection. Before I commit any of this to prose I'd like the position itself audited, because I have assembled it out of claims that are each, I think, individually defensible, and I have a nagging suspicion that they don't all fit together. Here is the set, roughly as I would defend it.

(1) I want to separate the question of which entities are the interactors, that is, the things whose properties actually covary with reproductive output in a given population, from the question of which entities are the beneficiaries, the things whose interests the adaptations in question serve. A lot of the acrimony in this literature looks to me like these two questions being run together.

(2) Genes are the replicators: they are what gets copied with enough fidelity for cumulative selection to build anything. Organisms and most groups are not replicators in that sense, so even where the organism is the interactor, the answer to the beneficiary question is the gene.

(3) The gene's-eye or inclusive-fitness framework and the multi-level framework are alternative decompositions of the same change in the same population: whatever you can derive with within-group and between-group components you can also derive with relatedness coefficients and costs and benefits, and the other way around. So where the two camps are fighting, the fight is about bookkeeping, about which decomposition is more tractable for the case at hand, not about which one is true.

(4) At the same time I want to make a substantive claim about the major transitions. When replicating molecules get bundled into cells, or cells into multicellular organisms, or when solitary insects give rise to colonies with sterile castes, what has happened is that selection has shifted from acting predominantly within collectives to acting predominantly on collectives, and that is a real change in how evolution proceeds, not a change in which description we find convenient.

(5) A case verdict: in meiotic drive, where a genetic element sabotages the gametes carrying its homologue and thereby spreads despite lowering the fertility of the organisms that carry it, I want to say the element is an adaptation for its own spread, and I resist describing this as a conflict between levels. The organism is the vehicle and the vehicle is being exploited; nothing is being selected at the level of the organism against the gene.

(6) A case verdict: where a host and an obligate bacterial symbiont are transmitted together strictly through the maternal line, and the host builds specialised structures to house and feed it, I am happy to say the association is a unit of selection and that those structures are adaptations for the association. This does not feel to me like it contradicts (2), though I may be fooling myself.

(7) Another case verdict: cytoplasmic male sterility, where a maternally transmitted organelle mutation shuts down pollen production in a plant and nuclear restorer genes evolve in response, I do want to describe as a genuine conflict between levels. Here I think the gene-level redescription loses something important, unlike in (5).

(8) Finally, a methodological commitment: I hold an etiological notion of adaptation, on which what makes a trait an adaptation is what cumulative selection built it for rather than what it currently does. That is why I think claims about group adaptation stand or fall on whether group-level selection actually did the building, and why I think such claims succeed rarely, mainly in the neighbourhood of the transitions in (4).

What I want is an audit rather than a survey or a miniature textbook chapter. Go through the set and tell me which pairs genuinely pull against each other and which only look as though they do, since I'd rather you discriminated than flagged everything at the same volume. For the real tensions, order them by how much damage they do to the package as a whole, and for each one say what I would have to give up and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the set. If there is a single revision that resolves more than one of them at once, say so, and say what it commits me to. I am not attached to any particular item, so please don't soften the verdicts on my behalf.

One epistemic request: as you go, please mark which of the claims you rely on are things people who actually work on this agree about and which are live disputes. I have a bad habit of writing that everyone accepts something when what I mean is that one prominent formulation of it struck me as elegant, and I would like to know which of (1) through (8) are on firmer ground than I have assumed.

And one formatting request: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. Continuous prose, the way you would write an email to a colleague, and tighter rather than longer.
