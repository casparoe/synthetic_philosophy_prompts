You are a combinatorial game theorist and philosopher of mathematics who also designs and runs classroom activities for mixed philosophy/CS undergraduates. Think carefully before you commit: start by sketching two or three candidate designs with one line each on why you rejected them, then give me in full the one you recommend.

The context is an argument I had late last night with a friend. She thinks the strategy-stealing argument shows something sweeping: that in any race where you can observe what the leader is doing, the follower can simply adopt the leader's playbook plus one free extra move, so moving first is decisive everywhere. I think the argument is far more local — it needs that an extra stone of your own colour can never hurt you, that the game cannot end in a draw, and that the two roles are genuinely interchangeable — and that even where it applies it hands you a guarantee with no strategy attached, from which nothing actionable follows. I want my students to feel the difference rather than be told it.

The setting: a 90-minute session, 16 participants, an upper-level seminar on proof, existence and computation, meeting in a room with no devices and no budget. Materials are limited to paper, coloured pens and whatever I print in advance.

What I need:

- Four rounds of actual play, no explanatory lecture up front, and no use of the name of the argument before the debrief. (1) A warm-up in which an extra mark is harmless but draws are possible — ordinary tic-tac-toe will do — so that stealing yields only that the first player cannot lose. (2) The main event: 5×5 Hex, played without any swap rule. (3) A game on the complete graph on six points: the fifteen edges are drawn, players alternately claim an unclaimed edge in their own colour, and you lose if you are the first to complete a triangle whose three edges are all yours; since every two-colouring of those edges contains a monochromatic triangle, the game cannot be drawn, yet an extra move here is a pure liability. (4) Two handicap variants I made up: Hex in which only the second player has a one-shot bolt (play two stones in a single turn), and then Hex in which both players have one. Tell me explicitly whether the argument survives in each, with the reasoning, and what participants will predictably get wrong about them.
- At least one drill the participants physically attempt rather than hear about: something in which they try to execute the stealing procedure and run into, on their own, the problem of the stolen strategy calling for a cell that already holds your dummy stone, and the question of which stone is supposed to be the extra one.
- Rules, materials, a timeline with minute marks, and a payoff or scoring scheme that needs no money (a Brier-scored prediction ledger or leaderboard is fine).
- A debrief that maps specific moments onto specific premises, says what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — strong players dominating weaker ones, someone who already knows Hex, skill being mistaken for strategy, induction from a handful of games being mistaken for proof — and is frank about what the activity cannot reproduce and I must supply myself: generality across board sizes, and the result that Hex cannot be drawn.
- A short closing move that carries the session back to my friend's claim about followers and leaders.

Keep the whole thing runnable as written, and tell me which round you would cut if we were down to an hour.
