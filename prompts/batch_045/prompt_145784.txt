so me and a friend keep going in circles about Douglass and the Constitution and I figure we're both missing something.

Quick setup so we're on the same page: you know how he breaks with Garrison. The Garrison side said the Constitution was basically a pro-slavery compact and no abolitionist should swear to uphold it; Douglass flips and starts arguing that read the way it ought to be read it's a liberty document. And the moves he uses are specific: there's no word for slave anywhere in the original text; the clauses that touch slavery use euphemisms about persons held to service; the three-fifths rule, he says, is a *penalty* on the slave states rather than a recognition that people are property; the clauses about the 1808 cutoff and about fugitives are narrow exceptions that hand out no affirmative power to anyone; and courts shouldn't go rummaging around in what the framers privately wanted. Then Dred Scott lands and he argues that "we the people" names a national people, and that Taney only got where he got by pretending to read the framers' intentions off of history.

Here's where we're stuck. My friend says Douglass is basically a living constitutionalist before the letter. I say no — he's a textualist all the way down, and it only looks like living constitutionalism because he thinks the words are better than the men who wrote them. And that distinction is actually on the menu now in a way it wasn't for him: originalism these days mostly claims to be after original public meaning, not framers' intent. So:

1. Would Douglass have signed on to originalism, and which version? My worry is that the original-public-meaning version cuts against him, since the ordinary meaning of the 1787 text *in 1787* probably tolerated slavery where it already existed. Relatedly, originalists say: don't like the original reading, amend the thing. Douglass did want amendments. So maybe he'd just say fine, amend it, and not feel the loss at all.

2. The Court these days sometimes asks whether a claimed right or practice is deeply rooted in the nation's history and tradition. My friend says he'd have to hate that, since at basically any point you pick the deeply rooted tradition includes slavery. I say no, that's exactly backward: his whole move is to say the tradition that binds us is the one the preamble announces, not the stuff people actually did. Which of us is closer, and does the answer change if you ask about a tradition dating to 1868 instead of 1787?

3. The Thirteenth's exception for punishment after a conviction. Say a modern case: people in prison made to work for private companies at sub-minimum wages, or lifelong civil disabilities after any felony conviction. Does the same move he used on the pro-slavery-looking clauses — read it narrowly, read it as a disability rather than a license — generalize here, or does it collapse, because this time the exception really was meant to permit what it permits? Because if it generalizes, it generalizes into something he'd probably find revolting, and if it doesn't, then his whole method starts to look like it was just tuned to get the anti-slavery result.

My friend has a name for what he thinks is going on, which he calls the Douglass inversion: vague aspirational language (secure the blessings of liberty, equal protection, cruel and unusual) does more anti-oppression work than any precise 1787 rule could have, because it lets a later and better generation read its own hard-won clarity into the same words — and the price you pay is that a worse generation can read its own bigotry in too, which is just Taney. Do you think Douglass would accept that framing, or would he think it concedes way too much? Does his own "we the people" argument actually depend on it?

What I'd like out of you: state whatever you think his real method was as a single rule somebody could pick up and apply, tell me where that rule lands on all three of the above, give me one modern case — mine are all 19th-century cosplay, give me something after 1895 — where the rule gives an answer that would surprise both me and my friend, and be honest about which of his most famous arguments the rule *can't* rescue. No need for citations or anything, I want the argument, not the footnotes.
