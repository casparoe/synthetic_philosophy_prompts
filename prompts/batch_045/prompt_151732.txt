I'm putting together a short handout for a decision-theory reading group. Its thesis is that the money-pump argument can condemn an agent whose preferences are, at every single moment and on every single menu she faces, perfectly coherent: complete and transitive, with no cyclic ranking of the options in front of her. If the argument has bite in such a case, the irrationality has to be located somewhere other than in an intransitive preference relation over outcomes. I need one worked case that carries that thesis honestly, so I want you to build it to specification.

The case:

An agent is choosing among consumer goods of an ordinary kind — holiday rentals, bottles of wine, phone tariffs, whatever you prefer — no science fiction, no demons, no predictors. A seller controls two things only: which options are displayed to her at each moment, and a small, fully transparent fee charged each time she switches from what she currently holds to another displayed option. The seller may not lie, may not hide or misdescribe the fee, may not threaten or withhold anything, and may not offer anything he cannot deliver. The agent reads menus perfectly, knows exactly what she is getting and what it costs, takes each offer as it comes, and has no commitment devices and no binding plans — she decides on the menu in front of her.

What the case must do:

1. She must start out holding one option, end up holding that same option again, and be poorer by a strictly positive amount — with the seller able to run the cycle again and again, so that the total extracted grows without bound over repetitions. Each individual switch must be small, voluntary, and strictly preferred by her at the moment she makes it.
2. Within every single menu she is shown, her ranking of the options must be complete and transitive — a genuine ordering, with no cycle in it. No option she takes may be dominated by another option in the menu she was shown.
3. Then the check that makes it a pump rather than a purchase: when the bundle she started with and the bundle she ends with are placed side by side — same good, less money — she must herself prefer the starting bundle. Show this.
4. The flip in her rankings must come from a real, familiar kind of context-dependence: an option becoming more attractive because it sits between the others on a trade-off dimension, or because a third option is clearly worse than it on both dimensions, or because a displayed but unavailable option shifts where the middle lies. Describe the mechanism you use and build the numbers so the flips go in the direction your cycle needs. The shifts must not come from confusion, ignorance, taste change, or a preference for novelty.

Deliverables:

- The scenario in under 200 words.
- A step-by-step walkthrough: for each step, the menu displayed, her ranking over it, the switch she makes, the fee, and a one-line verification that the ranking on that menu is a linear order and the option she takes is not dominated there. State the fee and the net loss per completed lap, and what the seller has extracted after ten laps.
- Name the one structural feature doing the work, and show what happens to the case if it is removed — specifically, why a seller facing menu-independent preferences would need genuine intransitivity to run the same pump.
- Then the hard part, and I want a verdict rather than a survey. Here are the three lines of attack I expect; tell me which is the strongest and why, and give me your reasons rather than a list of considerations.
  (a) Nothing new: index the goods by the menu or the reference point they are evaluated against, and the ranking over those richer objects is intransitive after all, so the money-pump argument was never about a bare relation over goods.
  (b) The culprit is not context-sensitivity but myopia: a sophisticated chooser who foresees the whole sequence refuses the first switch and is never pumped, so the case is really a failure of planning across time.
  (c) Context-sensitive rankings are not preferences but judgment errors, so the pump is only diagnosing a susceptibility to a framing effect, not any incoherence in what she wants.
- Finally: is the failure here best described as synchronic or as diachronic, and which single observation about your case would most change your mind? State how confident you are, as a number, that a case meeting all four constraints above really is a money pump in the sense that matters, and say what that sense is.

Keep the whole answer under about 900 words, take a definite position at every point where you are asked to, and do not stipulate any verdict that your construction is supposed to generate.
