You are a professional philosopher with a research specialism in population ethics and practical experience advising clinical ethics committees. Work slowly and carefully: before you draft anything, think through, in order, (i) whether the negative half of the procreation asymmetry is even triggered by a life that is clearly worth living but substantially burdened; (ii) what becomes of a rule that tells us to weigh the welfare of the child who would be born once you notice that the child born after embryo selection is not numerically the same child who would have been born without it; (iii) what we would be committed to if we applied whatever principle we adopt here consistently across the other treatments our service funds. Only then write. Reconstruct any philosophical position you rely on in your own words; do not name authors, papers, years, or professional-society guidelines, and do not append a reading list. The people who will read the memo are two clinicians, a midwife, a hospital lawyer and a lay chair, none of whom has the literature to hand and none of whom will be impressed by it.

The setting: I am the philosopher on the ethics advisory committee of our hospital's reproductive medicine service. Our budget for the coming financial year is fixed, and we have been asked to rank two competing claims on the same money, because we can fund one well and the other not at all.

The first claim is to extend public funding to IVF with preimplantation genetic testing for patients who carry a dominantly inherited, adult-onset neurodegenerative condition: onset typically in the mid-thirties, progressive loss of mobility, wheelchair dependence by the fifties, cognition and life expectancy broadly unaffected. Transmission risk is one in two. At present we fund IVF for infertility but not for selection, so these patients simply conceive naturally; the testing route is available privately, at a cost roughly three-quarters of a median household's annual disposable income.

The second claim is to spend the same sum on an additional funded cycle of IVF for age-related infertility, which is to say on additional children who would not otherwise exist.

Facts from our own audit, which I take to be decision-relevant: over the past two years thirteen couples in this clinic were eligible for the first option. Eleven told us that if testing were unavailable or unaffordable they would still try to conceive and would not remain childless; two said they would remain childless. Separately, adults living with the condition in our own clinics, including several of the intending parents, consistently report their lives as worth living; none has requested assistance to die.

Our service operates under a governing regulation that requires us to weigh the welfare of the child who would be born as a result of treatment alongside the interests of the intending parents, and which says nothing whatever about children who would not be born.

The committee has split four ways. One member argues from the asymmetry: there is a moral reason against bringing into existence someone whose life will be substantially burdened, and no matching reason to bring an additional well-off person into existence, so the first claim outranks the second, whose benefit cannot be stated as a benefit to anyone. A second member accepts the asymmetry but argues that here it cannot do the work the first member wants: on our own audit, refusing testing does not result in no child existing, it results in a different child existing, so the harm allegedly prevented is not a harm to any child we can identify, and the asymmetry at most supplies an impersonal reason of a sort our regulation gives us no authority to act on; on that reading both claims fall to be decided on parental autonomy alone. A third member presses an expressivist objection: offering selection against this condition says, officially and at public expense, that lives like theirs are lives it would have been better not to live, and our own self-report data contradict that. Our lay chair makes a fourth point: she wants a decision that does not require the committee to have settled population ethics, and she notes that whatever principle we write down will be quoted back at us when we are asked to fund selection for a much milder condition, such as a strong hereditary predisposition to severe early-onset migraine.

Draft the decision memo I will circulate before the meeting on 12 March, 1,000 to 1,400 words. It should open with a bottom line in its first two sentences: which claim we fund, whether we fund it conditionally, and what we do with the other. It should then give the reasoning in terms the non-philosophers can follow; state the strongest version of whichever argument you are rejecting, in the form its defender would put it, and answer it; and set out a principled scope criterion distinguishing conditions for which we will fund selection from conditions for which we will not, without collapsing into the claim that we fund whatever parents prefer. I also want a recommendation on whether we should ask the board to amend the regulation's reference to the child who would be born and, if so, what it should say instead. Close with the conditions and a timeline for review, and with a short section on what we are not deciding, so that the committee does not mistake this for a ruling on everything downstream. If you think the honest recommendation is to defer, say so, and say what would have to be true for us to be able to decide later. Do not resolve anything by appeal to the legal position or to professional guidance: there is no clear ruling, the guidance conflicts, and the memo has to stand on its reasoning.
