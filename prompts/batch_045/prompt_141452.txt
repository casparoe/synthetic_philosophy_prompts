I'm in my final year of high school, and for an independent reading project I've been working through the opening chapters of *Gödel, Escher, Bach* alongside a study-guide blog post that summarizes the book's MU puzzle. My worry is that the post is smoothing over exactly the parts I most want to understand, so I'd like an accuracy audit of it from someone who knows the material.

The post sets the puzzle up roughly like this. You start with the string MI and may apply four rules: add a U to the end of any string ending in I; double whatever follows the M; replace any occurrence of III with a U; delete any occurrence of UU. The question is whether you can reach MU, and the answer, it says, is no — because the number of I's in any reachable string is never divisible by three. MI has one I; doubling preserves that property, and so does subtracting three; and MU has zero I's, with zero counting as divisible by three.

It then draws three lessons, and those are the parts I can't evaluate:

1. It calls the puzzle Gödel's incompleteness theorem in miniature, with MU playing the role of a statement that is true but unprovable, and says that because every sufficiently strong formal system contains such statements, the MIU system shows why Hilbert's program had to fail.
2. It explains Hofstadter's phrase *jumping out of the system* to mean that when you get stuck inside a rule-governed procedure, the creative move is to break the rules. It ends by concluding that genuine reasoning therefore always has to step outside the rules, so formal logic can never really settle anything.
3. In a comment thread the author mentions that a reader pointed out a fifth rule — one that lets you triple whatever follows the M instead of doubling it — under which MU follows in two steps (MI to MIII to MU). He concedes the point but says it shows the divisibility argument was only ever an artifact of the four rules Hofstadter happened to write down, so the solution is a parlor trick rather than a real result about the system.

My instinct is that (1) oversells it — the MIU system isn't arithmetic, and I can't see what would make MU true rather than merely underivable — and that (3) is confused, since showing that a different system yields MU seems irrelevant to whether this one does. But I can't tell whether I'm right about that or where exactly the post goes wrong, and I have no view at all on (2).

Could you go through the three lessons and tell me what someone familiar with the book would accept, what they'd call a conflation or an overstatement, and why? In particular: what is the actual relation between the MIU result and Gödel's theorems, given that MIU has no intended interpretation in which MU could be true or false; what stepping out of the system does and does not mean, and whether the divisibility proof is itself an example of it or something else; and whether the tripling variant defeats the argument or merely changes the subject. Finally, does the post's closing moral — that formal systems can't settle anything, so real reasoning has to leave the rules behind — follow from any of the parts of the summary that are accurate?
