I am applying for a twelve-month dissertation-completion fellowship in normative ethics and need a two-page proposal that a committee of generalist philosophers could follow without prior work in the area. The topic is indirect normativity and the structure of idealized deliberation, and what I need is help turning a half-formed idea into a question with a determinate answer.

The half-formed idea. The debate as I have found it tends to assume there is a single question: given which standard determines what an agent objectively ought to do, which standard should she use when she decides what to do? I want to split that question along two axes and then add a third, nested one:

1. The synchronic question: which considerations may, or must, figure in deliberation at a time, given the standard that settles what the agent ought to do.
2. The architectural question: what the agent ought to do, at earlier times and as a standing policy, about the shape of her own later deliberation — whether to cultivate dispositions, avoid certain information, adopt rules, bind herself, or pre-commit.
3. The meta question: what the answer to (2) is for an agent who knows that her future selves will answer (1) imperfectly, and who can act now to alter how they will answer it.

The first thing I think is new is a test I want to call the erasure test. Say that a candidate account of ideal deliberation is self-erasing just in case an agent who accepts it, and knows that she accepts it, cannot execute it: acceptance destroys some condition the procedure needs. Perhaps the procedure requires the agent not to treat a certain fact as a reason, yet accepting the account makes that fact newly salient as a reason; or it requires a spontaneous responsiveness that reflection dissolves. This is meant to be distinct from the familiar issue of self-effacement, which concerns whether a theory should be believed, asserted, or taught; erasure concerns whether it can be executed by the very person who has it. I want the proposal to say plainly whether that distinction survives pressure.

The second thing I think is new is charging for deliberation. Standard treatments idealize deliberation as free and arbitrarily deep, so that the agent can always ask what to ask, and what to ask about that. Suppose instead that each round of meta-deliberation has a cost, and that the stopping rule is endogenous: the agent must in some sense decide how deep to go. My conjecture is that when deliberation is free and depth unbounded, indirectness either vanishes or becomes unanswerable, so that indirect normativity is only well defined once deliberation has a price. I do not know whether I want to spend the year defending or killing that conjecture, and the proposal should say which is the better bet.

What I need in the proposal:

- One precisely stated question, of a form that admits a determinate answer (a yes/no, or a small menu of positions), with working definitions of the load-bearing terms — deliberation, idealization, execution, cost, depth — rather than assumptions about them.
- A short section on why the positions on the shelf leave it open: the advice-model or ideal-advisor style of account, the sophisticated versus resolute pair in sequential choice, rule-based indirectness, and the self-effacement literature. The point should be that they are pitched at a different level, not that they are wrong, and the proposal should say which level each is pitched at.
- A month-by-month method for twelve months: what I would actually construct — a formal toy model, a worked case, a taxonomy of procedures scored against the erasure test, or some combination — and why that method rather than the obvious alternatives, in particular a survey of existing positions or an appeal to empirical work on how people in fact deliberate, both of which I want ruled out on scope grounds rather than left quietly available.
- The deliverables. I have in mind one article of about 9,000 words plus a two-page appendix of negative results. Say what a useful result would look like to someone who does not work on this, and why the appendix is part of the deliverable rather than an admission of defeat.
- What would count as the project failing: at least two distinct failure conditions, including the one I am most worried about, which is that the erasure test turns out to be the self-effacement problem redescribed, and that the conjecture about costly deliberation collapses into a familiar point about bounded rationality.
- An honest assessment of scope: what to cut to make this one year rather than three, and what I should say to a committee member who suspects the whole project is rule-consequentialism with new vocabulary.

Do not include a bibliography or any citations; I will fill those in myself. Where the framing needs a term I have not supplied, coin one and define it, and flag it as new.
