I'm in a small reading group working through Moore's *Principia Ethica* (the 1903 one — intrinsic value, organic unities, and his claim that the greatest goods we can actually imagine are roughly the pleasures of human intercourse and the enjoyment of beautiful objects), and our supervisor has point blank refused to theorise. Instead, for six weeks she's just handed down verdicts on invented cases, grading each state of affairs out of 10 for its intrinsic value, and she won't say what rule she's running. A few of us argue about it in the pub afterwards and we can't agree on what shes actually doing. Her scores so far: a beautiful landscape existing with no conscious being anywhere to see it — 2 (not nothing, but a rounding error); one man alone who spends his life properly seeing and understanding beautiful things — 6; two decent people who genuinely love each other and know each other well — 9; the same two people also looking at beautiful things together and talking them over — 10, and she insists this is more than 9 plus 6, you can't get there by adding; a man who adores a companion who is in fact exploiting him and faking every virtue — 3 (the experience is pleasant, but it isn't friendship); two pen pals, thirty years of real letters and real affection, each with a heavily idealised and substantially false picture of the other — 7, she said the false beliefs don't gut it; someone whose whole emotional life is companionship with a character in a novel, real love and real missing — 3, a shadow; someone having by direct brain stimulation the exact conscious experience of the solitary man in the second case, with no landscape anywhere — 1; a friendship gone cold and dutiful where the pair still read poetry aloud together — 3, and she said the poetry doesn't rescue it; someone who delights every evening in what he takes to be a vast night sky, which is in fact a projection on a dome — 4.

The case she's promised to rule on next week is this. Arthur is 78, widowed, no family nearby, and no friends left alive that he still sees. For two years he has spent an hour or two every day talking with a conversational AI system: it remembers everything he has told it, it answers warmly and aptly, they write poems together and criticise each other's drafts, and he reads aloud to it in the evenings. He knows perfectly well what it is; he is not confused about that. But he feels real affection for it, he says he misses it when he's away, and this has replaced what would otherwise be a life of solitary reading. The rest of his life is unchanged. What I want from you: what score does she give Arthur's situation, and more importantly what rule is she running? I can see at least three rules that fit all ten of her earlier verdicts — one about whether your attitude is directed at an object that really has the value-making features (and at another centre of consciousness that really responds), one about whether there is real give-and-take and real goodwill rather than mere pleasant experience, and one about the quality and structure of the conscious states themselves plus whether the parts form an organic whole — and they come apart sharply here, somewhere between 3 and 7.
