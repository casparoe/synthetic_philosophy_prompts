I am helping to convene a short adversarial collaboration — three meetings and one joint document — between two people who have spent years disagreeing about what the epistemic program in game theory actually delivers. Call the first the Program theorist. She holds that once the belief and rationality assumptions behind the classical solution concepts are made explicit, those concepts stop looking arbitrary: a common prior together with common knowledge of rationality yields correlated equilibrium; the analogous conditions for extensive-form games carry real content about backward induction and about when a threat is credible; and the value of this work is explanatory and classificatory even where it is not predictive. Call the second the Model skeptic. He holds that the assumptions are doing the work and that their content is fixed by modeling choices the analyst makes anyway — how the strategic situation is carved into a game form, what each player is taken to believe about what would have happened at decision points her own strategy excludes, how rich a space of types the model permits — so that the celebrated results are closer to elegant restatements of their premises than to claims about anybody's behavior.

The collaboration has a consumer, which I want you to keep in view. Its sponsor is an institute that advises regulators who must predict behavior in one-shot, novel strategic situations: a rule is imposed for the first time, the parties have no shared history, culture, or precedent, the interaction will not be repeated, and there is no opportunity to learn from feedback. The sponsor wants to know whether any of the program's output can be used in that setting, with how much confidence, and what would have to be true of the parties for it to apply.

Our steering committee has floated a further item, which I would like folded in rather than treated separately. Call it the finite-depth proposal: replace common belief in rationality with mutual belief in rationality truncated at some finite depth k; for each k compute the set of action profiles compatible with the truncated hierarchy; and ask how quickly that set approaches the infinite-hierarchy prediction, and whether the rate depends on features of the game such as its size or the number of players. The thought is that this would show whether the common-knowledge idealization is a benign limiting case or is quietly carrying the result. Both camps have so far only gestured at it.

Write the joint document. I want: (1) a numbered list of claims both parties can sign — about the formalism, about what the representation theorems establish and what they do not, about the status and testability of the common prior assumption, and about what the laboratory literature on dominance-solvable games and guessing games can and cannot show; (2) a table of the residual disagreements, each row giving the disagreement first in neutral language and then in each side's own preferred formulation, worded so that the side in question would actually sign it; (3) for each disagreement, a judgment about whether it is substantive or verbal, and where you judge it verbal, a statement of what each party was using the contested term to exclude; (4) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one side — with, for each, the concrete case, argument, or body of evidence both would accept as bearing on it, what each side predicts it will show, and what each side would count as evidence against its own position; (5) what the document says about the finite-depth proposal: whether it is a genuine test of the program, a distraction, or something in between, and what result would count as vindicating the skeptic's worry about the idealization.

Do not buy agreement cheaply. I would rather have a short agreed list and a sharply stated disagreement than formulations so hedged that neither party is committed to anything by signing. Where you think one side is simply right on a point, say so in a brief note addressed to me, outside the document, rather than fudging the text the two of them are supposed to put their names to.
