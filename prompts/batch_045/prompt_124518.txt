Our monthly dinner club — mostly people from the local rationalist / ACX-comment-section crowd — does blind tastings, and we've now had the same argument about four times running: does reading the pro reviews before the reveal actually give you a reason to think the wine is good, or only a reason to expect that you'll like it? The hardline position is that genuine aesthetic reasons are acquaintance-based, that you have to taste / see / hear the thing yourself, and that a critic's verdict can at most tell you where to spend your attention; the deflationary position is that a well-calibrated critic is just an instrument, deference to one is efficient, and there's nothing special about aesthetics — food and wine especially, since unlike a one-off performance the very same substance can literally be put in your mouth afterward and checked. We even coined a term for the case that broke us, "ghost-tasting": the trustworthy verdict is issued by someone who didn't have the experience, or the experience belongs to someone who gets no credit for it. Anyway I rewatched Ratatouille and I'm fairly sure Pixar got there first, and I want to write it up for the group blog.

So: read Ratatouille through the aesthetic-testimony question. The machinery is all there — Anton Ego, the critic whose printed verdicts apparently help destroy restaurants and who wields economic power over aesthetic value without anyone supposing his reviews transmit knowledge; Remy, who has the first-hand acquaintance and no standing to speak, and Linguini, who has the standing and none of the acquaintance, which is literally a ghost-tasting arrangement and is the film's comic engine. Then the ending, which is the part I care about: Ego has the full first-hand experience of the dish and writes a generous review on the basis of it, but his beliefs about who made it and what kind of creature made it are wildly false. Does the film think his verdict is nonetheless correct, i.e. that the value of the meal is provenance-independent in a way that vindicates the acquaintance view? Or does the ending quietly lean on Ego's authority — on his readers deferring to testimony — in a way that undercuts that? And what do you do with the "anyone can cook" motto: if the capacity for aesthetic appreciation (or creation) is universal, doesn't that pull against Ego ever having been the arbiter in the first place?

What I want is a committed reading, not a survey. Take a side on which view the film as a whole vindicates, name the single scene that's your strongest evidence, and then tell me where the film resists your reading — where it's just being a sentimental kids' movie, or where it trades on a confusion between "you can't outsource the tasting" and "you can't outsource the judging," or between aesthetic reasons and aesthetic understanding. I'd also like you to say which bits of the hardline view you think are actually true on the merits, since I'll be defending the writeup in the comments. Please don't hedge into "both things are true in a sense" at the end, and no quotes — I remember the scenes fine.
