I'm a retired solid-state chemist — 30-odd years of solving crystal structures from diffraction data, and about four years of reading philosophy of science since I packed in teaching. So I know the phase problem from the inside: the intensities plainly don't fix the structure, you get families of models that fit them equally well, and you only get an answer by imposing constraints — plausible bond lengths, non-negative electron density, whatever — plus a lot of checking against data you didn't use in the fit. Nobody in my old field concluded that the structure wasn't real, and nobody treated those constraints as merely pragmatic either; they were the sort of thing that keeps getting vindicated when the next data set arrives.

So when I read the realism/anti-realism argument about underdetermination — the Duhem-Quine end of it — I'm struck by how stuck it looks. The anti-realist says: any body of evidence has empirically equivalent rivals, so belief in unobservables is unwarranted. The realist says: actual cases are all transient, they dissolve once new evidence or new auxiliaries come in, and nobody has ever produced a genuine permanent case. Both things have been said many times and neither side moves.

My hypothesis is that they aren't really disagreeing about evidence but about what gets to count as evidence. The realist is quietly counting simplicity, consilience and constraints-that-have-paid-off as evidential; the anti-realist counts only the measured intensities. Then the dispute is verbal until it's settled whether those extra constraints track the truth — which starts to look like an empirical question about the track record of constraining strategies rather than a philosophical one. Is that right, or is there a better diagnosis: different questions under one heading (rational choice versus rational belief versus what the theory is claiming), a hidden formal issue about what empirical equivalence requires, or bedrock intuitions about observability that neither side can argue for? And given whichever diagnosis you prefer, what would count as progress — a real permanent case, a theorem, a change of question, or nothing at all? I'm taking this to my lifelong-learning philosphy group on Tuesday and I'd like something sharper than a survey of who said what.
