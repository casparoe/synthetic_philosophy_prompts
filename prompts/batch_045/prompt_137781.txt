I've been reading about decision theory on my own — no philosophy background, just blogs and old forum threads — and I've ended up defending a package that seems to annoy everyone: in counterfactual mugging you should hand over the $100, and in XOR blackmail you should refuse to pay. I get there with one rule rather than two: what matters is what your answer buys in the branch where the prediction is actually doing work. In the mugging, the prediction only does anything in the heads branch, and there "pay" is worth $10,000. In the blackmail case, your output only does anything in the no-termites branch, and there "pay" just costs you $1,000 for nothing, so refuse. The two styles of reasoning I keep running into (causal and evidential, I think they're called) seem to either refuse both or pay both, which is why I think the split is doing real work.

Three variants I made up won't resolve, though:

1. The predictor simulates a stranger who is only somewhat like me — my decision habits, totally different memories — and pays or charges me based on what the stranger did. The rule seems to say "pay if the stranger is similar enough," but I have no idea what enough means, or whether the answer fades out gradually or flips at some point.
2. Same setup, except on heads the predictor hands the $10,000 to the simulated copy that said yes and then switches it off; I get nothing either way. If I still pay, my reason was never about my own money. If I stop paying, the whole rule looks like ordinary self-interest with extra steps.
3. The prediction was about a slightly different question than the one in front of me: it asked whether I'd pay $1,000, the letter demands $2,000; or it simulated the me from before I spent a week reading about this and changed my mind. Now I can't tell whether I'm supposed to answer as the person who was predicted or the person I am now.

There's also a fourth thing I can't shake: the rule tells me to be the kind of person who pays, but by the time I'm actually asked, being that kind of person has already paid off and the money is long gone, so on the day it just feels like burning $100.

So what would count against the split rule? I want defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, including ones a defender of the rule would concede themselves rather than only what a critic would say. And I want to know whether it's been insulated into unfalsifiability: "the branch where the prediction does work" plus "the thing deciding is close enough to me" feel tunable enough to produce whatever answer you already liked — if that's true, is it a fatal problem or just what a decision rule has to look like? What would move me most personally is either a case where the rule gives an answer I can't bring myself to accept, or a case where it gives no answer at all; I'd like you to say which of those is the real threat.

Give me exactly seven numbered items, each one or two sentences, no introduction and no conclusion.
