hey — I run a small reading group (6–8 people, mostly philosophy grad students plus two econ people who are very into welfarist stuff) and we're doing one 90-minute session on population axiology. I want the handout to be a decision tree that sorts people into a position, rather than a list of the usual five views and their names. Everyone in the room already knows the standard Parfit-style setup — A versus Z, the mere-addition staircase, why the sum-maximising view is supposed to churn out the bad result — so please don't recap that. Start from "there is a puzzle" and get people to a leaf.

What I want:

1. Build the tree from the logical space first: give me the 5–6 branch questions, each one phrased as a claim or a case somebody can answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and only then locate the actual positions as leaves. Where a combination of answers is unoccupied, keep the leaf anyway and say in one line whether nobody is there because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

2. Branch questions I think have to be on there — add or swap as you see fit, but say why if you drop one:
   - does someone's coming into existence at a positive-welfare life make things better *for that person*, or only better impersonally?
   - is the sum of welfare all that matters, or does the *number* of lives matter in its own right, or does the average?
   - is there a level of welfare a life has to clear before adding it is an improvement — and is that level fixed, or does it move?
   - do you reject the chain rather than the sum: if you're walked from A to Z in a hundred tiny steps, each one looking like a mere addition plus a small redistribution, do you refuse the first step, refuse the transitivity of the chain, or refuse only the endpoint?
   - can a gain to *new* people ever compensate a loss to people who already exist?

3. Two cases I want built into the tree instead of left as prose, because I think they pull apart views that the A/Z case lumps together:
   - the "drip lives" case: one person living a life containing a very large amount of welfare, versus an enormous number of people each living a life containing a negligibly small dribble of positive welfare, with the totals equal. I want this to separate views that treat *persons* as the unit of value from views that treat *welfare quantity* as the unit. My guess is it does more work than the repugnant conclusion itself for this group, whose intuitions about the original case are already fried.
   - the "one jump versus the staircase" case: the same endpoint population reached in a single step versus reached by a long chain of tiny additions-and-redistributions. I suspect some people will answer differently in the two presentations without noticing, so tell me where in the tree that difference actually shows up.

4. One leaf has to be a view I've been kicking around and can't find anyone holding, because I want to know whether it's empty for a reason: a *sliding* critical level, where the minimum welfare a life must clear before adding it is an improvement is itself a decreasing function of how many people already exist — the first few lives need to be pretty good, but the ten-billionth life can be barely above neutral. Put it on the tree as a leaf even if it's unnamed, and tell me whether it collapses into something else, escapes the well-known impossibility results for these views, or just re-derives the repugnant conclusion in slow motion.

5. Leaves to include regardless: the bullet-biting sum-maximising view, critical-level / threshold views, average-ish views, the negative-leaning and asymmetry views where adding good lives does nothing and adding bad ones is bad, and at least one anti-aggregation or lexical view. Also make room for "the conclusion isn't actually repugnant" if the tree can hold it — one of the econ people is hovering there and I want him to see where that puts him.

6. Flag the pairs of leaves your tree genuinely can't separate. I'd guess anything that agrees on fixed-population cases will be hard to pull apart, and if so say which extra case would do it, or admit that none does.

Each leaf: the name (or "unnamed"), one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and one line on which branch question was load-bearing for it. Format it as something I can print — nested bullets or ascii, whichever is clearer. Aim for 10–12 leaves; if you end up with more, tell me which to cut. And I definately don't want footnotes or a reading list — this is a handout, not a bibliography.
