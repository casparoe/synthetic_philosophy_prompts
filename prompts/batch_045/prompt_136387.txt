Hey — I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so sorry in advance if this comes out muddled.

I'm writing a book set in a made-up country about fifteen years after a dirty-war-ish dictatorship ends. The new government sets up one of those truth commissions, modeled on the real South African–style one: if you committed a political crime you can apply, and if you publicly confess the whole of what you did, in detail, in front of the people you hurt, you walk — no prison time, no civil damages, no criminal record. Lie or leave something out and you're prosecuted normally, which in practice usually means nothing happens at all, because the bodies are gone and the files were burned.

What I can't get straight is what that trade actually assumes. It looks like the state is buying something (an officially certified account of the past) with something (immunity). But a state that can sell immunity is talking as if the punishment were a debt it holds and can waive — and I dont see why the state is the creditor when the people who were tortured are the ones owed, and they get no say and no compensation out of it. And the same legal order, in an ordinary courtroom, will only give a co-defendant who turns state's evidence a reduced sentence, never a full wipe. So cooperation buys something completely different depending on which door you walk through.

Here's my own wrinkle, which is where I really want help. In my draft the commission runs out of applicants after a few years and quietly changes the rule: amnesty now goes to whoever can supply the complete account of a crime, whether or not they were the one who did it. An intelligence analyst who only ever read the files trades the whole story for a walk; the man who pulled the trigger stays silent out of loyalty and gets life. My beta readers say this is obviously monstrous. I think it might just be the original logic with the sentimentality taken out, and I can't tell which of us is right.

So: what does the actual amnesty-for-truth trade presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view — of punishment, of what a public accounting of the past is worth and to whom, and of who has standing to forgive a wrong they didn't suffer — and be sharp about which commitments the practice strictly requires and which merely sit well with it, because I suspect several quite different theories would license it. Tell me where it's underdetermined, whether the two things I flagged (state as creditor; cooperation as a partial vs. total discount) are really in tension or can be reconciled, and whether my analyst is a corruption of the real thing or what it was already committed to. Please skip the history and the citations — I've read the background, and I want the argument, not a source list.

Also I'll be skimming this on my phone, so end with a single-sentence bottom line as the last line of your answer, in this exact form: \answer{...} — saying what the trade strictly presupposes, or, if nothing is strictly presupposed, what it's most plausibly committed to.
