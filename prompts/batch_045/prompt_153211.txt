Twelve months ago the river behind our house came over its bank for the third time in five years. In the aftermath I was asked to join the grants committee of a regional flood-recovery fund: a small independent charity that makes discretionary payments to households and small businesses whose losses fall outside insurance and outside the statutory scheme. We meet monthly, we dispose of roughly forty applications a quarter, our decisions are effectively final, and each of us has about twenty minutes and a four-page file per case. None of us is a philosopher or an economist. Our decisions have to be consistent between committee members, and defensible both to an applicant who appeals and to the regulator who reads our minutes.

Our governing instrument directs us, in its own words, to compensate "losses arising from circumstances the claimant could not reasonably have foreseen or avoided" and to weigh "the extent to which the claimant's own decisions contributed to the loss". Those two clauses pull against one another, and we argue about them every month. Reading a little of the political-philosophy literature on luck egalitarianism — the thought, roughly, that it is unfair for someone to fare worse than others through no fault or choice of their own, while inequalities that genuinely reflect choice are not, on that ground, an injustice — clarified the shape of our problem without giving me any way to decide a case. So what I would like is a procedure.

Please give me a decision rule my committee could actually run on a file: the questions in order, what counts as an answer to each that is good enough given the evidence we hold, where the sequence stops with a no, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than of fact. I care at least as much about the translation as about the outcome: which distinctions from that literature have to survive the move into something a lay committee can apply in twenty minutes, which can be dropped without losing anything, and where a simplified rule will systematically misfire.

Four knots in particular, which I would like untied rather than merely noted.

First, the instrument conflates foreseeability with avoidability. A loss can be clearly foreseeable and still not avoidable at any cost the claimant could bear; or avoidable in principle and yet not something a reasonable person would have anticipated. I want the two prised apart, and I want to know which of them is doing the real work.

Second, our deputy chairman argues that we compensate the wrong thing: that what is unchosen is not the flood but the exposure to it, and that a household which has spent a decade in a property nobody will insure at an affordable premium has already suffered the relevant misfortune whether or not the water ever comes in. That strikes me as right and also unworkable at our budget. Please take a position on whether a rule should be built around realisation or around exposure, and say what follows for a fund that can only pay out after the event.

Third, we cannot pay everyone, so in practice we compare applicants with one another. That turns an award into a judgement that this claimant is more deserving than that one, rather than a judgement that this claimant's misfortune was unchosen. Is that comparative element corrupting the rule, or unavoidable once there is a budget?

Fourth, a no from us is not a catastrophe: we signpost to other funds, and nobody sleeps in a flooded house because of our decision. Does that let us keep the harshness worry — the objection that a principle which allocates according to choice will visit ruin on people whose choices were foolish or poorly informed — outside the rule, as a separate safety net, or must it be built in?

Please then run the procedure as a worked application on three shapes of case from our last quarter. The first: a couple who bought the cheapest house in the village eleven years ago, the only one within their mortgage offer, on a street that the flood maps already placed in the highest risk band — they were told at purchase, they stayed, and their insurance lapsed three years ago when the premium exceeded a month's take-home pay. The second: a widower in his seventies in a cottage his family has occupied since the 1890s, flooded four times, who has twice refused a buy-out above market value because he will not leave. The third: a bakery on a low-lying industrial estate that was outside the flood maps when the lease was signed in 2014 and is now inside them, whose owners have put their savings into fitting it out and cannot relocate without losing the business. At least one of these should come out as a no, and where it does I want that stated plainly rather than softened.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot settle — we also get repeat claims from the same property, about which we are currently hopeless — and what an applicant is entitled to be told about why they were refused.

Please write in British English.
