Some background, so you can see what I want the number for. I spent a working life as a field biologist and ecologist, and in retirement I sit in on a small reading group that has reached the second book of Plato's Republic, where Glaucon sharpens the "why be moral?" question with the story of the shepherd who finds a ring that makes him invisible and promptly uses it to seduce the queen, kill the king and take the throne, on the suggestion that people act justly only under compulsion and that anyone who could get away with injustice would. The group has stalled: a retired mathematician in it treats the challenge as essentially self-evident, while I keep saying it is badly underspecified, and neither of us can locate where we actually disagree. So I want a credence with its derivation, not a discussion. Here is the proposition, stipulating as precisely as I can. Call it G: take an ordinary, well-socialised adult in a contemporary society who is currently law-abiding at modest personal cost, and suppose she is given — and knows with certainty that she has been given — a ring that guarantees, for every future action of hers, complete immunity from detection, retaliation, legal sanction and reputational damage, with no after-effects on her relationships. G is the conjunction of two claims: (i) she would proceed to act unjustly whenever doing so paid; (ii) in doing so she would be better off than a comparably placed person who remained just, where "better off" is read prudentially, as a matter of her actual welfare rather than merely of her wealth or power. My own credence in the conjunction is about 0.3; my mathematician friend's is about 0.7.

In decomposing, I'd like you to weigh — and to add any terms you think I've missed — the following, each with a rough probability and one line of justification, then a combination: that a substantial share of ordinary moral restraint is genuinely reputational in the way Glaucon's story needs, rather than an internalised disposition that fires even in the absence of an audience; what I think of as the detectability dial, since in my own field one almost never sees a step function and I'd expect moral behaviour to show a dose–response curve with a non-zero intercept as the probability of being found out goes to zero, so ask yourself how much of the challenge survives if the predicted discontinuity at full impunity is not there; the evolutionary mismatch worry that a disposition shaped in a world without rings tells us about its normal function, not about what it reveals when handed a novel stimulus, and whether that defuses or strengthens the "revelation" reading; the second-order problems that even a certain ring may not buy what the argument assumes, because the bearer cannot fully switch off guilt, anticipatory anxiety or self-deception about whether she is really unseen, and because she must still live with whoever she becomes; whether Plato's own reply — that the unjust person is factionalised and at war with himself — identifies a welfare-relevant state at all, and whether such internal disorder is the sort of thing that could be measured in principle; and the collective version, since if rings were widely distributed rather than possessed by one free-rider in an otherwise law-abiding population the cooperative surplus might collapse and the ring-bearers be worse off, which may make the argument self-undermining when generalised. Please do not let any of this rest on the precise effect sizes of particular experiments in prosocial behaviour under anonymity — I have read enough of that literature to distrust individual results, and I would rather your answer did not hinge on a study neither of us can check. Then, finally: give me your number for the conjunction; tell me which single term your number is most sensitive to and which you are least confident about; tell me what kind of evidence or argument would move it most, and how you think one should reason about a credence in a counterfactual that seems permanently beyond empirical test; say whether your number shifts if "better off" is read as preference-satisfaction or as wealth rather than as welfare; and say which of the two of us at 0.3 and 0.7 is most likely wrong about which term.
