I am writing a seminar essay on whether interventionist accounts of causation can handle actual, token-level causal claims, and I keep getting stuck on a case drawn from my own background: before I started this degree I spent a year as a test engineer on redundant shutdown systems, where the engineers around me had no difficulty saying things like "channel A's overvoltage caused the trip" even on runs in which channel B was overvolted at the very same instant. We have been working through Lewis-style counterfactual theories and Woodward-style interventionism, and I have come to suspect that the interventionist test simply has no verdict to give in such cases. I would like to practise attacking that view, and I want you to be my opponent.

Please play a committed, well-informed interventionist. You hold that causal claims are claims about what would happen under interventions on variables, that this is what gives causation its explanatory and practical grip, and that a bare counterfactual dependence test without the intervention constraint is either empty or secretly borrows one. Do not play the strawman: concede small points freely, including, if you must, that the crude test on which every other variable is held fixed at its actual value mishandles symmetric overdetermination; press me with distinctions and counter-examples of your own; and tell me plainly when you think one of my stipulations is illegitimate. What you will never concede is the main claim, that interventionism gives the right account of what causation is, and you will never retreat to saying that causation is just whatever it is useful for us to say. Write in British English, keep each turn to a single paragraph of roughly 150 to 200 words, stay in character until I type "stop", and if some detail of my case is underspecified, state the assumption you are making and carry on rather than stopping to ask me.

My opening move is this. Take a trip logic with two independent sensing channels, A and B, feeding an OR node whose output sets a latch; setting the latch is the trip, and the trip is all-or-nothing. I stipulate two things. First, once the latch is set, no variable anywhere in the system differs between the history in which only A went high, the history in which only B went high, and the history in which both did: the design goal of redundant hardware is precisely that the downstream state be indistinguishable, and I am asking you to take that idealisation seriously rather than hunting for a residual trace variable. Second, the graph I have described is the complete and true causal structure of the device: A and B are exogenous and independent, both run into the node, the node runs into the latch, and there is nothing else. In the actual run both channels go high at t and the trip occurs a millisecond later. Now the interventionist test seems to say that neither channel's going high caused the trip, since setting either one low while holding the other at its actual value leaves the trip exactly as it was; yet every engineer I worked with, and I think every untutored judgement, says both caused it, and that each would still have caused it had the other stayed low. So you owe me one of three things, and I have a reply ready for each: a finer-grained effect variable, which my stipulations are meant to block and which real hardware usually lacks; a re-description of the variables, which I will accuse of being gerrymandered to save the theory; or biting the bullet and denying causation throughout symmetric overdetermination, in which case tell me what the incident report should say instead and how you would handle two arsonists who each set a fire sufficient to destroy the house. If you think there is a fourth option I have not listed, take it, but say explicitly that it is a fourth option and not one of these three in disguise.
