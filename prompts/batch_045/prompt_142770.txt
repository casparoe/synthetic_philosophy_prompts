**The question, as set (25 marks)**

**3. Constructive logic.**

**(a)** (5 marks) Give the BHK clauses for ¬, ∧, ∨, → and ∃. Using them, explain why P → ¬¬P is constructively acceptable while ¬¬P → P is not. Do not appeal to truth tables, to classical validity, or to the observation that the latter is classically valid.

**(b)** (7 marks) (i, 3 marks) Show that ¬(A ∨ B) ↔ (¬A ∧ ¬B) is intuitionistically valid. Then say which direction of the second De Morgan equivalence holds and which fails: is it (¬A ∨ ¬B) → ¬(A ∧ B), or ¬(A ∧ B) → (¬A ∨ ¬B)? (ii, 4 marks) Give a Kripke countermodel for the direction that fails, specifying the frame, which atoms are forced where, and which semantic clause does the work.

**(c)** (6 marks) A classmate says: "By Glivenko's theorem, whenever a formula is classically provable, its double negation is intuitionistically provable — so intuitionistic predicate logic is just classical predicate logic with a ¬¬ in front. In particular ¬¬∀x(Px ∨ ¬Px) is intuitionistically provable: for each x we have ¬¬(Px ∨ ¬Px), so we get ∀x¬¬(Px ∨ ¬Px), and the double negation can be moved out across the quantifier." Assess this claim. Distinguish propositional from first-order logic, and say exactly what is true here, what is false, and, for anything false, what the correct statement is.

**(d)** (7 marks) Call a formula A *stable* when ⊢ ¬¬A → A, and let C = {A : A is stable}. The same classmate calls C "the classical core" and says that classical reasoning is safe inside it. (i, 2 marks) Show that ¬A is stable for every A. (ii, 2 marks) Show that if B is stable, then A → B is stable, for every A. (iii, 3 marks) The classmate adds that C is closed under ∨, so that a disjunction of two formulas in C is again in C. Is that right? Prove or refute it, and say what your answer shows about the picture of a "classical core".

**The submission (candidate 07)**

**(a)** In the BHK reading a proof is a construction, and the clauses say what a construction of a compound statement has to be. A proof of ¬A is a method that, given a proof of A, produces a proof of ⊥ — that is, a proof that A is false. A proof of A ∧ B is a pair of a proof of A and a proof of B. A proof of A ∨ B is a pair ⟨i, p⟩ where i is a tag telling us which disjunct is being proved and p is a proof of that disjunct. A proof of A → B is a method turning any proof of A into a proof of B. A proof of ∃x A(x) is a pair ⟨d, p⟩ of a witness d from the domain and a proof of A(d).

Given this, P → ¬¬P is unproblematic. ¬¬P is (P → ⊥) → ⊥, so if we are given a proof p of P, the method that takes any alleged refutation r of P — a method from proofs of P to ⊥ — and applies it to p, obtaining ⊥, is exactly a proof of ¬¬P. From a proof of P we can therefore always construct a proof of ¬¬P.

The converse is a different matter. A proof of ¬¬P is a method that converts proofs of ¬P, i.e. methods P → ⊥, into proofs of ⊥; nothing in that description tells us how to build a proof of P, and the clauses supply no operation for doing so. Take P to be "the 10^100-th digit of the decimal expansion of π is 7". We presently have neither a proof nor a refutation of it, so we have no grounds for asserting P, while ¬¬P is the weaker claim that P cannot be refuted. Accepting ¬¬P → P would require a uniform way of turning "P cannot be refuted" into "P is proved", which is precisely what the constructive reading of the connectives withholds.

**(b)** (i) Both directions of ¬(A ∨ B) ↔ (¬A ∧ ¬B) hold. From a refutation of A ∨ B we get a refutation of A, by composing it with the map A → A ∨ B, and similarly for B; so ¬(A ∨ B) → (¬A ∧ ¬B). Conversely, given refutations of both A and B, any proof of A ∨ B carries a tag saying which disjunct it proves, and whichever it is, the matching refutation yields ⊥; so (¬A ∧ ¬B) → ¬(A ∨ B).

Of the other pair, the direction that holds is (¬A ∨ ¬B) → ¬(A ∧ B): a proof of A ∧ B gives proofs of both conjuncts, and either a refutation of A or one of B turns one of them into ⊥. The direction that fails is ¬(A ∧ B) → (¬A ∨ ¬B): knowing that A and B cannot both be proved gives us no way of telling which of them to refute, and a disjunction requires us to say which.

(ii) Countermodel. Frame: two worlds, w₀ ≤ w₁. Force A at w₁ only; force B nowhere. Then A ∧ B is not forced at w₀ — nor at w₁, since B is nowhere forced — so w₀ ⊩ ¬(A ∧ B). But w₀ ⊮ ¬A and w₀ ⊮ ¬B: the first because A is forced at the later world w₁, and a formula can fail at a world without its negation being forced there; the second because B has not been decided at w₀ yet. Hence w₀ ⊮ ¬A ∨ ¬B, and the implication fails at w₀. The work is done by the clause for negation together with persistence: because forcing is monotone, once a possible future in which A holds is in view, ¬A is not yet available.

**(c)** The classmate is right, and I think the objection usually raised here is pedantic. Glivenko's theorem says that a formula is classically provable if and only if its double negation is intuitionistically provable. The notes state this for propositional logic, but that restriction is an artefact of how the proof is presented: double negation commutes with the quantifiers, so the same result holds for first-order logic, i.e. ⊢_C A iff ⊢_I ¬¬A. In particular ¬¬∀x(Px ∨ ¬Px) is intuitionistically provable. For each x, ¬¬(Px ∨ ¬Px) is provable, being the double negation of an instance of excluded middle; universal generalisation gives ∀x¬¬(Px ∨ ¬Px); and by the commutation just noted this is equivalent to ¬¬∀x(Px ∨ ¬Px).

What the classmate should concede is that the embedding is usually stated more carefully: the Gödel–Gentzen negative translation inserts ¬¬ in front of atomic formulas, disjunctions and existential quantifiers, rather than merely prefixing one to the whole formula. But for a formula that already consists of a disjunction under a universal quantifier, the extra double negations are already in place, so the prefix form is enough — the two formulations agree on this case.

**(d)** (i) ¬A is always stable, i.e. ⊢ ¬¬¬A → ¬A. One direction is the instance of A → ¬¬A with ¬A substituted for A. For the other, assume ¬¬¬A and A. From A we obtain ¬¬A, by part (a); and ¬¬¬A is ¬(¬¬A), so we have ⊥. Discharging A gives ¬A.

(ii) Suppose B is stable and assume ¬¬(A → B). To show A → B, assume A. From A and ¬¬(A → B) we get ¬¬B: for if ¬B, then A together with any proof of A → B would yield B and hence ⊥, giving ¬(A → B), which contradicts ¬¬(A → B). By stability of B, B. So A → B, discharging A; hence ¬¬(A → B) → (A → B).

(iii) No, C is not closed under ∨. Take a propositional letter P. Both ¬P and ¬¬P are stable: ¬P by (i), and ¬¬P because it is the negation of ¬P. But ¬P ∨ ¬¬P is not stable — it is not even provable. Consider the frame with w₀ below two incomparable worlds w₁ and w₂, with P forced at w₁ and nowhere else. At w₀, ¬P is not forced, since w₁ forces P; and ¬¬P is not forced either, since w₂ forces ¬P, no world above w₂ forcing P. So w₀ ⊮ ¬P ∨ ¬¬P. But ¬¬(¬P ∨ ¬¬P) is provable, being the double negation of an instance of excluded middle with ¬P in place of the atom, so it is forced at w₀. Hence ¬¬(¬P ∨ ¬¬P) → (¬P ∨ ¬¬P) fails at w₀, and the disjunction is not stable.

So the "classical core" picture is wrong: one cannot carve out a region of the language in which excluded middle holds and expect to stay inside it under the ordinary logical operations. What does hold is closure under ¬, ∧, and → into a stable consequent; disjunction — and correspondingly existential quantification — is where it breaks. Note too that if every formula were stable, intuitionistic logic would collapse into classical logic: ¬¬(A ∨ ¬A) is always provable, so stability of A ∨ ¬A would give A ∨ ¬A outright.
