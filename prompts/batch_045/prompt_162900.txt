I write a column on science and ideas, and for two years now I have been working intermittently on a long piece about the science of well-being: the national surveys that now ask people how their lives are going, the affect researchers who plot moods as points in a two-dimensional space of valence and arousal, and the reward neuroscientists who report that the machinery behind wanting something and the machinery behind liking it can come apart. Every time I try to bring the philosophical dispute about pleasure into that piece, I hit the same wall, and I would like to know what kind of wall it is.

The wall is this. Hedonism about welfare is asked what all the things we call pleasures have in common: the taste of cold water when you are thirsty, the relief of a joke well timed, the absorption of a hard proof, the satisfaction of finishing something difficult, physical ease after pain, the specific warmth of being taken seriously by someone whose judgment you respect. The objection, pressed by the nineteenth- and early twentieth-century critics of hedonism and repeated ever since, is that there is no single felt quality running through them. Run through the candidate unifiers — a distinctive sensation, a phenomenological tone, taking pleasure in something's being the case, wanting the experience to continue, a sensed fit between desire and object — and for each one there appear to be pleasures that lack it and unpleasant states that have it. The hedonist replies seem equally frozen in place: retreat to a thin property such as feeling good or positive valence; go disjunctive and say the class is held together by family resemblance or by a functional role rather than by any shared qualitative core; or insist that the unity is something only careful introspection can certify, and that the objector is looking in the wrong place.

My own hypothesis, which I would like tested rather than flattered, is that this is not really a dispute about pleasure at all, but three disputes run under one heading: a metaphysical question about whether there is a natural kind anywhere near the folk category; a question about what a theory of welfare is for, and whether it owes us a unifier at all rather than a correct list of the things that make a life go better; and a buried empirical question about whether what the affective scientists call valence is genuinely one dimension or several processes we have learned to place on a single axis. On that last point I think the scientists have quietly voted: they measure positive and negative affect as if there were a common currency, and the instruments work well enough that policy analysts trade off one kind of suffering against another as though the trade-off were well defined. I cannot tell whether that is progress on the philosophers' question or a change of subject.

So I want a diagnosis, not a tour of the positions. Using what each side actually argues, tell me what sort of disagreement this is: verbal, about what is to count as a pleasure and therefore about which cases are data; a difference in what a theory of well-being is required to do; bedrock intuitions about the unity or disunity of feeling that neither side can argue for; or an empirical question about the structure of affect that has been miscategorised as a conceptual one. I would also like two things taken seriously that I rarely see addressed. First, the negative case: is suffering any less heterogeneous than pleasure, and if not, does the symmetry help or hurt the hedonist? Second, the question of cross-modal exchange: if there really were a single currency, we should expect stable trade-off rates between very different pleasures, of the sort that would let one say how much of one kind of enjoyment equals how much of another. Does the apparent absence of such rates tell us anything, or is it merely a measurement problem?

Finally, and most importantly: given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here? A disjunctive formulation that escapes circularity, an empirical result about valence, a formal result about the possibility of a common scale, a reframing of the question, or nothing at all — and if nothing, say why, and say whether that is because the question is hard or because it is ill-posed.
