I have been trying to apply Lakatos's distinction between progressive and degenerating problem shifts to episodes in my own field, and I find that I have confident verdicts on individual cases but no principle that yields them. I would like the principle, or an argument that there isn't one.

My verdicts, in no particular order. (i) A programme answers an anomaly by modifying its protective belt, the modification is shaped entirely around that anomaly, and thirty years pass without its predicting anything new: degenerating. (ii) The same kind of modification, but a decade later it throws up a fact nobody had gone looking for, and the fact is then found: progressive — and I want to say progressive from the moment of the modification, though at the time it was indistinguishable from the one in (i), and I cannot say why the difference in outcome should reach backwards. (iii) A modification made and published before anyone had the relevant data, and then confirmed: progressive, and I feel it is more progressive than (ii), but I cannot say what the extra credit is for. (iv) A programme explains a fact that had been on the books for years and had played no part in building the theory: this counts in its favour. (v) A programme explains, very exactly, a fact it was constructed to fit: this counts for nothing at all — and here I am in trouble, because in both cases the fact was known first. (vi) A programme's central posit is eventually shown not to exist, while predictions derived from it keep succeeding: I still score it as progressive, uneasily. (vii) A programme keeps its name, its equations and its notation across a generation while quietly abandoning the commitment that made it distinctive — say, no longer claiming that the entities it is named after are real: I stop counting it as the same programme and I do not credit its later successes to the earlier one. (viii) Two groups work from the same hard core with different protective belts and publish against each other: that is one programme for purposes of appraisal, not two — which sits badly with (vii). (ix) A programme has stopped producing novel predictions and only absorbs anomalies, but there is no rival programme addressing the same phenomena: I will not say abandon it and I will not say it is healthy either; I want a verdict of not yet appraisable, even though degenerating looks like it should be a property of the programme alone. (x) Inside one nominally single programme, one small branch keeps turning up novel facts while everything around it only absorbs anomalies: I score the programme as degenerating, and I would still move a PhD student into that branch tomorrow — which makes me unsure what the unit of appraisal is supposed to be.

The best I can do by way of a principle: a programme is progressive over a period just in case, over that period, its belt modifications yield more corroborated facts they were not introduced to explain than facts they were, and degenerating in the opposite case; and every such verdict is provisional, since a degenerating stretch can be redeemed later.

Please test that against the ten cases and repair it. I want: the principle stated precisely, clause by clause, with the case that motivates each clause; its verdicts on cases I have not listed — a programme whose novel predictions are picked, after the fact, out of a large computer-generated space of its own variant models, each prediction being announced before the test; a programme sustained mainly by one well-funded group; two programmes that have merged their belts; and an honest statement of which of my verdicts the principle cannot keep, and at what cost in complexity it could keep them. One last thing matters to me practically: every formulation I have tried seems to need to know what happens over the next decade, and I have to decide this term whether to take a student off a line of work. If the appraisal can only be made in hindsight, tell me what the honest real-time surrogate is.
