I write the framing notes for a debating society at my college, and this term we are working through the Ferguson–Tharoor exchange on the British Raj. Every session collapses into the same shape: one side produces a list of credits — railways, canals, a unified legal code, English-language education, the suppression of certain practices, integration into a world market — and the other produces a list of debits — famines under revenue-maximising policy, the collapse of Indian textile manufacturing under tariffs set in London, the remittance of revenues to London under the Home Charges, indentured labour shipped to the colonies, the salt tax — and then the two sides argue about which column is longer. The metaphor everyone is quietly leaning on is that the empire is a balance sheet: put everything on one ledger, net it out, and the sign of the result answers the question of whether the Raj was, on the whole, a good or a bad thing. My grandfather kept ledgers as a district accounts clerk in Bihar and I grew up around double-entry bookkeeping, so I keep having the uncomfortable feeling that the metaphor is doing far more work than anyone has checked.

Two things make me suspicious, and I would like them treated as the hard cases rather than as rhetoric. First, an auditor friend pointed out that in consolidated accounts intra-group transactions cancel: what is a cost to the subsidiary is a gain to the parent, so at the level of the group the entry nets to zero. That seems to mean the choice of accounting entity — India taken alone, the Empire taken as a consolidated group, the British exchequer, or the East India Company before the transfer of rule to the Crown — settles the answer before any item is priced, because most of Tharoor's debits are transfers that disappear on consolidation while most of Ferguson's credits are transfers that disappear if India is the entity. Second, I tried to repair this with three admissibility rules for the credit column: an item counts only if it would not have happened anyway under an independent Indian state, only if it was not paid for out of Indian revenues and the guaranteed returns promised to British investors, and only to the extent that it exceeded what the counterfactual baseline would have delivered anyway. A friend says my rules are rigged, since no historical item can survive a but-for test applied that strictly, so I have stipulated the answer instead of arguing for it — and I genuinely cannot tell whether that is a defect in my rules or whether it is the whole point.

So I want an audit of the metaphor, not another verdict on the empire. Please set out the correspondence table explicitly: which features of a balance sheet are supposed to map onto which features of two centuries of rule — the accounting entity, the reporting period and the terminal date, the numeraire, the distinction between a gift and a purchase and between a transfer and a real gain, between capital expenditure and maintenance, the salvage value of whatever was handed over at independence, off-book items such as the wars fought with Indian revenues or the opium trade, and above all the counterfactual baseline, which nobody in the room states but which does at least as much work as the choice of entity. Then tell me where the mapping breaks rather than merely strains, and which of those breaks matter for the specific work the metaphor is being asked to do here — licensing a single net verdict, licensing the summing of items that look incommensurable, and licensing the assumption that there is one determinate answer rather than a family of questions indexed to a population and a baseline. Say what you think of my three admissibility rules: whether they are repairs to the ledger or a way of abandoning it, whether the but-for objection really is fatal or whether comparable tests are applied elsewhere without collapsing, and what happens if I keep the rules but drop the single numeraire and report separate columns for different populations instead of one total. And finish by saying whether any version of the balance-sheet framing can carry a verdict at all — if not, what a better framing would be and what it would be better at; if so, exactly which of the ledger's usual features have to be given up to keep it honest.
