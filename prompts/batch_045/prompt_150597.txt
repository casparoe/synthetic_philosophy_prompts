Background. The chapter argues that causation by omission is not one phenomenon but several, and I need to sort the standard stock of examples by how strongly each one supports the claim that omissions are genuinely causes rather than merely counterfactual dependencies, explanations, or grounds for blame. Two distinctions I introduce myself will do some of the work.

Call an omission anchored if, in the actual sequence of events, the agent had already initiated or committed herself to a course of action whose continuation would have prevented the outcome: there was a plan in place, the means were at hand, and the opportunity was present, and what happened is that this sequence was not carried through. Call an omission unanchored if no such sequence was ever in the offing: the agent never began anything relevant, and we are contrasting her inactivity with a course of action she could have initiated but never did. I also want to separate the question whether an omission is a cause from the question whether the omission is the sort of thing that makes blame appropriate; I suspect the two come apart, and part of what I want from you is a sense of where they come apart.

The cases. In every case, stipulate that the agent is competent, knows the relevant facts, is physically able to act, and that the outcome would in fact not have occurred if she had acted as described. For calibration, take the corresponding positive case in which the gardener actively pours weedkiller on the seedlings to be a clear case of causation, and grade the omissions against it.

1. The gardener under contract to water a greenhouse during a heatwave does not turn up on Tuesday; the seedlings die.
2. A nurse who has drawn up a dose that is part of the standard protocol, and who is holding the syringe, withholds it out of a grudge against the patient; the patient dies.
3. A driver, engaged in the activity of driving down a familiar street, never moves her foot to the brake; the pedestrian she would have avoided is killed.
4. An off-duty lifeguard, sunbathing on the same beach in civilian clothes and an excellent swimmer, watches a stranger drown a few metres away and does nothing.
5. An engineer who designed a chemical plant considered installing a backup pressure valve and decided against it, as she was entitled to; pressure builds during a heatwave and the plant explodes.
6. A wealthy stranger who has never heard of a particular village never funds a bednet programme there; children die of malaria.
7. A supervisor never publishes the duty roster; because of that no guard is posted at the gate; because of that the gate is never locked; the theft occurs.
8. A hospital's automated triage system does not flag a patient as high risk, so no alert is ever generated, and no individual was ever in a position to notice anything; the patient deteriorates and dies.
9. Two nurses are each independently responsible for checking a monitor; neither checks it; the patient dies, and the monitor was in any case broken, so nothing either of them could have done would have saved the patient.
10. The department chair never circulates the agenda; the meeting therefore does not take place; the vote therefore does not take place.

Task one. Sort these ten cases into four tiers by how compellingly each one supports treating the omission as a genuine cause of the outcome: tier A for cases that are indefensible to deny, such that someone who rejects causation by omission outright should be embarrassed by them; tier B for strong cases that a sophisticated denier can still resist with some cost; tier C for cases that are genuinely up for grabs; tier D for cases where the causal idiom is most plausibly shorthand for something else, such as mere counterfactual dependence, sheer explanatory relevance, or grounds for moral or legal responsibility. Within each tier, order the cases from strongest to weakest. Then assign every case a score from 0 to 100 for causal credibility, with the scores respecting your ordering, including across tier boundaries. Give me one or two sentences per case stating what puts it where it is, and name explicitly the case or cases where you think the causal question and the blameworthiness question pull in different directions.

Task two. I have been trying out three tests for whether an omission causes an outcome, and I want to know which of them is doing the real work.

Test one, the anchoring test: an omission causes an outcome only if it is anchored in my sense above.

Test two, the salient contrast test: there must be a course of action that is salient in the context, given what the agent was doing and what was expected of her, and the outcome must counterfactually depend on her taking it, with everything she actually did held fixed.

Test three, the available mechanism test: it must be possible to tell a positive causal story about how her acting would have brought about the absence of the outcome, and that story must not itself bottom out in further omissions, so that explaining the prevention requires appeal to nothing more than things happening rather than things not happening.

Rate each test from 0 to 100 on how well its verdicts track the tiering you produced in task one, and tell me which single test does the most explanatory work. Then identify any case where your own verdict comes apart from what your best test predicts, and say which of the two you would give up.
