im running a 40 minute seminar next week for incoming masters students in health policy — not philosophy people at all, most of them did policy or some life-science undergrad, and right after my session they start a unit on medication adherence and health behaviour change. one whiteboard, no assigned reading. what I want them to leave with is the *problem*: why "she judged that staying on the meds was best, and then didn't take them" is supposed to be puzzling rather than just unfortunate, and what the present-biased / hyperbolic-ish discounting story does and doesn't buy us on that. don't explain any of it to me, I know it well enough — plan how I should teach it.

so: what has to be in place before the puzzle is even visible to this crowd (I'm guessing something about the gap between wanting to do X and judging X best, and something about "at the moment of action"), in what order, and what specifically collapses if I reorder it. Should I actually draw a discount curve on the board for people who mostly haven't done calculus since school, or does the maths cost me the room — and if I skip the curve, what do I lose?

which one example should carry the whole session? I was going to use a woman with type 2 diabetes who every evening sincerely plans tomorrow morning's walk and every morning doesn't go. is that the right carrying case, and what does it need to have and lack? (my worry: it smuggles in habit or addiction, and they'll file the whole thing under "thats a disease thing, not a me thing".) if not that one, tell me why not the two obvious replacements — the snooze button and the last slice of cake at a department party.

then the misconceptions, in the order they'll genuinely arrive rather than a tidy list. my guesses: (a) akrasia is just a fancy word for laziness or low willpower — I was thinking of banning the word "willpower" for the first twenty minutes, good idea or does it block the only route they have in? (b) so it's just that people are impatient and discount the future too much; (c) if her preferences at 7am really differ from her preferences at 9pm, then there's no fact of the matter about what's best for her, so nothing irrational happened; (d) fine, so the fix is precommitment and nudges, can we move on. which of those are the same mistake in different clothes, and at exactly what point in the sequence should I head each one off — I'd much rather defuse them before they form than mop up afterwards.

one more thing I want stress-tested. I've been toying with a framing of my own: distinguish "cold reversal", where the evening plan survives completely intact and only the pull changes, from "hot reversal", where at the moment of action she also quietly revises what she believes is best — today genuinely is a bad day for a walk, one day off won't matter — and then claim that only cold reversal is real weakness of will, while hot reversal is just ordinary acting on a revised judgment. Does that split hold up? where does it beg the question, and does it belong near the start of the session as a way in, or near the end as a way of sharpening what they've just got?

finally: what should I openly refuse to get into at this level, and say out loud that I'm refusing — and give me one question I can put to the room in the last three minutes that would actually show me they'd understood the puzzle instead of having just picked up a new vocabulary word.
