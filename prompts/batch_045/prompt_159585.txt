I sit on the clinical ethics committee at my trust, and we're currently trying to draft a policy on what we've started calling 'Ulysses clauses' in advance care planning for people with relapsing conditions — severe anorexia, bipolar disorder, opioid use disorder: instructions written while well that are meant to bind during a relapse, including when the patient is currently objecting. We write maybe a dozen of these a year, they get signed off by the medical director and the trust's legal team, and the policy will be read by ward staff who've had no ethics training, so it needs to be defensible in about a page. At the same time a friend who's on a constitutional review commission in a country I won't name keeps sending me her chapter on entrenchment to poke holes in, and doing both at once has made me realise I'm not at all sure my own package hangs together. Different people tell me different bits of it are inconsistent and I genuinely can't tell who's right, so before I put anything in writing I'd like an audit. Here's the set, in no particular order and with no idea which item is the load-bearing one:

(a) Ulysses clauses should be enforced during relapse, over the patient's present protest. The reason I give isn't really 'she consented', it's that the earlier decision was made in better conditions and the current objection has exactly the shape the clause was written to defeat. I've been using a rough test: enforce only where the reasons the person gives now 'match the signature' of the predicted shift — the bipolar patient refusing admission because she feels fantastic and has a brilliant new business plan — and release where the reasons are genuinely new, e.g. she's become religious in the interim, or the intervention she refused has since been shown not to work.
(b) Same in the addiction case: I'm happy with commitment contracts that impose a cost the person is now begging to be released from, and 'that wasn't really me' doesn't move me much.
(c) But in advanced dementia I think the directive should give way to the person's present comfort and apparent well-being, because by then there's no one left who has a stake in the earlier project.
(d) I'm a hard anti-paternalist at the bedside: a competent patient's current refusal is sovereign as against doctors, family and the state, however stupid I privately think it is.
(e) On the constitution side, substantive entrenchment — locking in rights or structures behind a supermajority or a referendum — strikes me as illegitimate, on the dead-hand/equal-voice line: the living get to govern themselves, and it doesn't rescue it that the founders were allegedly calmer and wiser, or that the thing was re-ratified once twenty years ago. I am fine with purely procedural hurdles, though: a mandatory delay, a second vote after an intervening election, a citizens' assembly stage.
(f) And I've got a half-formed thought I can't shake: the dead-hand worry feels proportional to how few of the bound people ever had a say. Entrenchment that reaches a generation mostly born later appals me; entrenchment that binds largely the same voters grown older barely bothers me at all.

So: which of these actually collide, which only look like they do, and where's the cheapest place to give ground? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than flagging everything as equally bad, and for each one say what exactly would have to go, what holding the line instead would cost me (a case verdict? a rationale? a whole application?), and whether one single revision would clean up most of it. Be specific about the mechanism — I want to know what I'm committed to by what, not a tour of the topic. I've got no library access for a fortnight, so please don't cite anything or point me at reading; I want your reasoning. And British English throughout, if you wouldn't mind — the paper's going to a UK audience.
