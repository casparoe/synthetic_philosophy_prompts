I help write internal process documentation for a mid-sized logistics company, and I'm assembling a training packet for a body we've just stood up, the Universalization Review Board — an internal compliance desk that any team has to clear before rolling out a new policy, sales script, pricing rule, or warehouse workflow. The premise, which I want treated as bureaucratic fact rather than as a joke in itself, is that the desk has spent several years trying to run the Formula of Universal Law as an actual intake test: submissions arrive as maxims, reviewers check them for contradiction, and rejections come back with codes. There is a second lane for the Formula of Humanity, and an appeals tier above both. What I need is a mock internal-process manual for this desk — roughly 700 to 900 words, laid out as numbered sections with a short sample ticket log at the end, in the flat, acronym-heavy voice of a compliance document written by someone who has watched the same failures recur for years and has stopped being surprised by them. Give it the usual furniture: form numbers, a revision history, a standing roster of four reviewers who meet Thursdays, a "Maxim Description Standard" that is on revision 11.

The comedy has to come from the machinery actually being right about the test, not from the test being invented. So: submitters should be shown gaming it in the two familiar directions — writing maxims so hedged with irrelevant detail (a credit offer available only to customers named Douglas, on the second Tuesday of months containing an R) that no universalization can generate a contradiction, and writing them so vaporous ("act responsibly toward counterparties") that nothing can be derived either — with the desk's response being an ever-revised schedule of which circumstances count as relevant and an argument about who gets to decide. The two rejection codes should be distinct: a hard failure where the maxim's universalization destroys the thing the maxim depends on, and a softer one where the universalized world is conceivable but nothing a rational agent could will, and the manual should register, without ever saying so directly, that submitters quickly learn to aim for the softer code because it reads as regret rather than as contradiction. There should be a class of tickets the desk approves and nobody is comfortable with — competitive undercutting, for instance, where the practice survives its own universalization — and another class where reviewers cannot tell whether the second lane is doing any work at all beyond restating the first. The appeals tier should have three grounds, and the manual should note, deadpan, that most of the real caseload sits in the third: maxims the desk is obliged to certify as permissible while every reviewer privately regards them as unlovely, since permissibility is the only verdict the test issues and nothing in the intake form records anything else.

Two constraints, please. Nothing may be quoted or footnoted from Kant or from any commentator — no borrowed wording anywhere, no section that breaks character to explain the philosophy — and the desk may not be made to endorse a reading of the test that a Kant scholar would dismiss as a strawman; every exaggerated feature should exaggerate something that is genuinely contested or genuinely awkward in the procedure itself. The document should stay legible to someone who has never read a word of ethics and should still be fair enough that a Kantian specialist, reading it, recognizes that the author understands where the test is actually fragile: the dependence of any verdict on how an action is described, the gap between what can be willed and what merely can be conceived, the thinness of permissibility as a moral verdict, and the suspicion that the two formulas are not really two tests.
