I sit on the committee that sets the annual ethics comprehensive examination for our PhD students, and I have been asked to supply this year's question on the nonidentity problem. I want a question that cannot be answered by recapitulating the dialectic every candidate has already internalised: the same-number case, the claim that no one is made worse off than they would otherwise have been, the person-affecting restriction, the no-difference view, the appeal to impersonal principles and their familiar costs. A candidate should only do well if they can locate the load-bearing premise of the argument and say what happens when it is removed.

The angle I want is this. The standard argument gets its grip because the identity-affecting act is treated as *deterministic*: the person who exists under the risky policy would not have existed under the safe one, so there is no one of whom it can be said that the policy made them worse off. I want that structural feature isolated and then withdrawn. So please build the question around a pair of cases.

The first is the familiar deterministic pair: a state energy ministry chooses between Cheap (commission the reactor now, defer the cost of waste storage; containment fails in about sixty years; everyone then alive in the region contracts a chronic, non-fatal illness and has lifetime welfare 70 rather than 90; every life remains well worth living) and Safe (build the storage properly; no illness; welfare 90; the same number of people exist, but different ones, because the construction boom shifts where, when, and with whom people meet and conceive). Stipulate that no one already alive at the time of the decision is affected either way, and that the number of people is the same under both policies.

The second is the case I want candidates to work on, which you should stipulate as tightly as the first. Call it the Drift case. Here the policy's effect on *which* people come to exist is chancy rather than deterministic. There is a large pool of candidate gamete-pairs, indexed independently of what the ministry decides. Under Cheap, each candidate pair has probability 0.4 of producing a person who actually exists, and any person who does exist has welfare 70. Under Safe, each candidate pair has the same probability 0.4, and any person who does exist has welfare 90. Expected numbers are therefore equal, total expected welfare is lower under Cheap, and for no candidate is it true that she would exist under one policy and not the other. Make the stipulations airtight so that the case isolates exactly one thing — the absence of a determinate, de re counterfactual linking a person's existence to the choice — and does not smuggle in a same-person harm, a difference in the number of people, or a life not worth living.

Against that pair, I want the following from you:

1. The exam question itself, as it would appear on the paper: 90 minutes, 20 marks, with sub-parts that force the candidate to (a) state precisely why the no-harm step succeeds in the deterministic pair and what exactly becomes unavailable — rather than merely false — in the Drift case; (b) formulate the strongest complaint still available against Cheap in the Drift case (the natural candidates being each candidate individual's expected welfare, or her welfare conditional on existing), and then argue for a verdict on whether that complaint is genuinely person-affecting or only person-affecting in form; (c) identify the premise about the identity and individuation of merely possible people that the complaint in (b) requires, show that the standard argument does not itself supply that premise, and say what follows if it is granted and if it is denied; (d) trace what the move costs downstream, and (e) draw the practical upshot for duties to future generations, given that real policies plausibly affect identity in the chancy way and that we cannot in practice tell, for a given policy, whether the identity-affecting is deterministic or chancy at the level of individuals.

2. A model answer of roughly eight hundred and fifty words that takes a definite stand on (b) and (c) rather than listing considerations, and that reaches a verdict on whether the Drift case dissolves the nonidentity problem or merely relocates it. It should also state, in a sentence or two, why the candidate-indexed comparison is threatened by partition sensitivity: how the pool is carved up can matter once probabilities differ between candidates, and there is no obviously privileged carving.

3. A marking scheme built around five must-hit points worth four marks each, with a one-line note on how candidates typically get each of them wrong, and a closing paragraph stating what separates an A answer from a B one. The B answer should be characterised as one that deploys the familiar menu of responses correctly and applies it loosely to the new case without identifying the hidden premise; the A answer as one that says which single assumption is doing the work and what the argument looks like without it.

Two constraints. The exam is closed-book, so the question and the model answer must not depend on any literature: names may appear in passing, but no citations, quotations, or further-reading apparatus anywhere, and nothing presented as a quotation from a published work. And the invented case must stay a piece of philosophy rather than a puzzle about probabilities: keep the formalism minimal, and make sure every sub-part is answerable by someone reasoning from the case as stipulated.
