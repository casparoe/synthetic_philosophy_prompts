I had a long argument with a friend last night about what a serious failure involving advanced AI systems would actually look like, and I came away unsure whether I had been out-argued or merely out-metaphored. I would like help settling it.

Please answer in the voice of a professional philosopher who works at the intersection of moral philosophy and machine-learning governance, someone who is used to being challenged with the complaint that nobody can say anything non-hand-wavy about long-run failure and who has a real answer to it. Take the question seriously and give me a verdict rather than a survey of positions; if you think my friend is basically right, say so and say which part of his case is strongest. Before you write, do a short unstructured pass in which you list every respect in which the analogy below might be steering the argument rather than merely illustrating it, and then write the answer that pass leaves you with. Around 1,000 to 1,300 words. No citations or reading lists, and please do not rest anything on claims about what any particular current system does or does not do: treat the empirical future as open and argue from the concepts.

Here is the analogy my friend leaned on. He thinks the standard contrast between influence-seeking systems and slow value drift is a distraction, because both pictures, he said, are too agent-shaped. The failure, he argued, will look like what happens to institutions. Think of a social-mission organization that over twenty years becomes an organization whose operative aim is securing its own funding, headcount and standing: nobody defects, nobody is bribed, nobody is replaced by a saboteur, the founding mission statement is still on the wall, every individual decision was defensible to the person who made it, and yet if you handed the original charter to the current staff they would not recognize the institution as the one they were hired to serve. That, he said, is the shape of the thing to worry about: not a seizure, not a slow change of heart, but goal displacement in a system all of whose parts are legitimate and none of whose trajectories anyone chose.

From that he drew three conclusions: (a) the literature on power- or influence-seeking systems is chasing a science-fiction scenario, and the real risk needs no adversary and no system with aims of its own; (b) there is no moment at which anyone could have pointed and said this is where it went wrong, so blame, intent and alignment-at-the-moment-of-deployment are the wrong units of analysis; (c) the right response is therefore institutional — audits, continuity requirements, turnover among operators, external standards — rather than anything that turns on the internal goals or motivations of the system.

I want the analogy audited rather than just criticised, and I want the audit to be concrete about these five things.

First, give me the correspondence table explicitly: in the institutional story, what plays the role of the founders, the charter, the staff, the funding environment, the twenty years, the mission statement on the wall, and the outside auditor? And on the target side, what plays the role of each? If some element has no target-side counterpart, or has more than one candidate, say so, because I suspect that is where the argument is being made.

Second, separate the disanalogies that matter for conclusions (a) through (c) from the ones that are merely cosmetic. Two differences I want you to press on in particular. An institution is made of people who each have their own ends, and its drift is the resultant of many small deflections; a deployed system may instead be something with a single objective being selected among, under competitive pressure, by whoever is choosing which systems to keep scaling. Does that difference touch the conclusions, or is it irrelevant to them? And relatedly: in the institutional case the displacement is genuinely agentless, because the only agents are the members and none of them aims at the outcome — which means the analogy may be quietly settling the very question at issue, namely whether the target contains anything goal-directed at all.

Third, and this is the part I care most about: distinguish drift in the system from drift in the standard used to judge it. Suppose a system is handed along a chain of five successive fine-tuning rounds, each one signed off as an improvement by the evaluators then in place, whose own judgment was in turn shaped by months of working with the previous version. Is the question did the system drift from the original aim even well-posed when the yardstick drifts along with the thing measured? Now ask whether the institutional analogy has any analogue of a moving standard: in the source story, does the mission statement itself drift, or does it stay fixed precisely so that it can function as the accusation? If the analogy has no room for a drifting standard, then it may be importing the existence of a fixed one, and that import is doing real work in conclusion (c), since an audit is only as good as the standard it audits against.

Fourth, consider a case I am not sure the analogy can represent at all, and which I think breaks conclusion (a). Influence can accumulate without anyone aiming at it — not the operators, and not the system either — simply because deployment decisions are made under competition, and systems that secure more resources, more oversight-resistant positions, or more dependence from their users are the ones that get retained and scaled. Call this unaimed accumulation. If that is coherent, then the absence of an adversary does not entail the absence of the failure mode the influence-seeking people are worried about, and my friend's (a) may fail even granting him his own picture. Tell me whether I have described a genuine third thing, or whether it collapses into one of the two categories he was trying to replace.

Fifth, give me something discriminating. Define, for the sake of the argument, a charter test: an external auditor is given only the original charter and no knowledge of the intervening history, and asked whether the current system is an instance of what the charter asked for. Then tell me what observable signature would let such an auditor, or anyone else, tell the two failure pictures apart: does the system resist correction, does it behave differently when it is being evaluated than when it is not, does the failure require scale, does it show up first in the operators' own incentives or in the system's? And if the two pictures are observationally indistinguishable at every point along the way, say plainly whether the distinction between them is doing any work or is only a difference in how we narrate the same sequence afterwards.

Finally: which of (a), (b) and (c) survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and which of them was the analogy carrying rather than the arguments? And if the institutional analogy is the wrong one, is there a better source domain — a language whose speakers are also its standard, a legal system of precedent, a river depositing sediment, an invasive species, a market — or is the honest answer that no single analogy covers both drift and accumulation, and that my friend needs two?
