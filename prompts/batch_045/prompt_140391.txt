I'm putting together the final handout for the last session of a seminar I teach on coordination and collective action, and I want to end it with something that isn't a summary slide or a bullet list of takeaways — a poem, to be read out in the last five minutes and then left there. The term has gone by way of prisoner's dilemmas and their iterated versions, arms races, the tragedy of the commons, the dollar auction, adverse selection, and Gresham's law, and then the informal 'Moloch' writing that tries to tie that family together; and my students reliably come out the other side with one of two opposite errors, both of which I want the poem to refuse. Half of them have turned Moloch into an agent: a demon who wants the sacrifice, a 'they' who did this to us, someone to blame. The other half have concluded that if no one is to blame then nothing bad has really happened, since every single step was locally sensible. What I'd like is a villanelle — nineteen lines, the full repeating form, the two refrains taking turns and then meeting in the closing couplet — in which the form itself does the argument: the returns of each refrain should feel like the same piece of reasoning licensing itself one more time, not like a chorus or a hook, and the last two lines should place the two voices next to each other without letting either one win.

Here is what I need the two refrains to be, though the wording is entirely yours. The first is a line spoken from inside the trap by one participant about their own move — the move that was for them the only one available, and that would still be the only one available tomorrow even now that everyone can see where it leads. Something in the register of 'I'd have lost the season if I'd stopped' — yours will be better. The second is a line that no one inside the trap would ever have occasion to say: a flat description of what the arrangement has become, in which no individual choice figures as a cause, along the lines of 'the water is worse and no one poisoned it'. The poem should get across that this is a pattern rather than an agent — no line should attribute a want, a plan, a preference or an intention to the process — and no line should be readable as saying that the people in it were foolish, weak or wicked. It should also be right about the mechanism in a way that someone who knows this material wouldn't wince at, and in particular it should cover the variant we spent the final hour on: the trap that runs not on anyone defecting but on who opts in and who quietly withdraws, so that nobody's behaviour changes at all and the pool degrades anyway — the good coin hoarded, the careful seller leaving the market, the people who would never have played drifting out of the profession. If you can make that version and the ordinary defection version look like the same shape in the poem rather than two examples stapled together, so much the better.

Two negative constraints, and I care about these more than about the rest. First, no resolution: no turn towards 'so we must coordinate', no regulation, no heroism, no closing line that lets anyone off, because the point of the closing couplet is precisely the discomfort of seeing that the excuse and the outcome are both perfectly true and don't add up to anything. Second, no abstractness: I'd rather have one concrete setting held from first line to last — a fishery, a newsroom, a hiring round, an advertising budget, a housing market, whatever you choose — than a page of capitalised nouns, and if you can make both refrains do their work inside that setting without ever naming Moloch or the commons, that is exactly what I want. If you think a sestina is actually the truer formal picture here, on the grounds that in a race to the bottom the poet never gets to choose a new ending, say so in one sentence before the poem and write that instead; otherwise, the villanelle.
