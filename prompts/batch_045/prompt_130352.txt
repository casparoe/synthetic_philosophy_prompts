hey — I'm a novelist (four books, middling sales, one optioned and never made) and I've stupidly agreed to give a twenty-minute talk at a writers festival in the spring on what AI can't do. I dont want to get up there and say six things that can't all be true, so here's where I've actually landed, roughly in the order I believe each bit, and I want someone to tell me where it breaks.

1. LLMs don't understand anything. Form without grounding, outputs shaped by everything they've ingested with nobody behind them. I'm broadly with the stochastic parrots people, and I even still find the Chinese Room persuasive, which I know is embarrassing.
2. But meaning isn't in the author's head. I've said this in about forty interviews: readers find things in my books I never put there and they're often right, and my novels mean more, and other, than I meant. I do not get to be the referee.
3. What I do isn't so different anyway. I write by ear, I can't state the rules I follow, my characters surprise me, and whatever I know about structure came from reading a few hundred novels I wasn't consciously mining. When I draft I'm sampling from something I can't inspect.
4. Concretely: last year I fed a model forty pages of a messy draft and it told me that the narrator's irony about her dead brother is really self-protection. That's right. I couldn't have said it and now I can't unsee it. I still dont want to say it understood anything — I want the credit to go to me, or to the book, or to the prompt.
5. Methodologically: the only evidence any of us can ever have about what's going on inside a person or a thing is what it does and says. I'm not settling this by opening up the model, and I'm not settling it by defining words either.
6. Still: understanding needs someone home. A subject, something at stake, some inner life. I have no argument for this, I just believe it, and I'm not even sure how confident I am.
7. And politically: scraping my books was theft, because my sentences are me — an extension of a person, not a product. Which is also why, by the way, I run my drafts past a model to argue with and find it genuinely useful.

I've been trying to make 5 precise with a word of my own: I want to say X understands S only if X is answerable for S — there's such a thing as X getting it wrong for a reason X could come to own, and X being the kind of thing that could be held to it. Does that actually help, or does it just relocate the whole problem one step?

What I want is an audit, not a lecture. Tell me which of these pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like it — I suspect a couple just look bad — rank the real ones by how bad they are, and for each say what would have to give and what giving it costs me. Especially since 2 and 7 are load-bearing for the rest of the talk and I'd rather not drop those. Please don't just tell me everything conflicts with everything: I can do that myself. Under 900 words if you can. And write it in American English — I keep getting programme and sympathise back and it makes me want to throw the laptop out the window.
