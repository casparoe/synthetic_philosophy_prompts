Our reading group has been stuck for over a year on reasons without reasoners — whether there can be free-floating rationales, things there's reason to bring about or prevent that aren't anybody's reason. One side insists a reason just is a consideration that could guide or rationalise some agent, so an orphaned rationale is a category mistake, or at best shorthand for 'someone, somewhere, has non-derivative reason'. The other side says that's a prejudice about guidance: take a lone agent on a dying world who'll never learn about a distant child's suffering and couldn't reach her anyway — there's still reason for that suffering not to happen, and it isn't his, or hers, or the world's. Same story with the multipolar-trap cases: every firm has reason to keep defecting, nobody has reason to end the race, and yet 'there's reason for the race to stop' sounds true. Neither camp has budged an inch, and what bugs me is that they agree on every case and on the fact that sentences like 'there is reason to' are perfectly good English; they just read the data differently.

My hypothesis, which I'd rather have stress-tested than confirmed, is that this isn't a dispute about reasons at all but about which of two jobs a theory of reasons is supposed to do: modelling deliberation, i.e. what an agent can be responsive to, versus modelling betterness or fittingness, i.e. what there's most reason for, whoever or whatever happens to be around. If so they're answering seperate questions under one word, and each side's favourite examples are picked to flatter its own job description. But I can also see it being something else: a real argument about whether normative notions are irreducibly indexical or person-relative, a verbal dispute that a stipulation could dissolve, or a proxy fight over whether collectives — a committee, a species, a firm — can be reasoners in the first place. So what sort of stalemate is this, and given your diagnosis what would actually count as progress — a distinction, a new case (a reason that outlives everyone it was ever for, say), a formal result — or should we expect none, and why?
