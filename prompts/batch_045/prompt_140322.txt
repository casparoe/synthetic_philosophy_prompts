I'm in a small land-use reading group — mostly lawyers, one transportation planner, one person who works on municipal bonds — and we've been arguing about a proposal to put forty apartments on a corner lot two blocks from the commuter rail station. After a four-hour hearing at which almost every speaker was against it, a friend of mine, who is an enthusiast for taxing land values rather than buildings, sent me an argument that I haven't been able to shake. It seems to me to demolish the homeowners' case completely, and then, by what looks like exactly the same logic, to forbid him from ever building anything either. I've written it out as carefully as I can below.

(1) The price of an urban lot has two parts: the value of what has been built on it, and the site value, the part that comes purely from where it is — who lives nearby, whether the trains and the schools and the shops are there, whether the street feels safe, whether anyone wants to be in this city at all. Site value is not produced by the owner. It is produced by the activity of everyone around them.
(2) No one acquires a moral claim on a benefit that other people's activity creates merely by being the person who holds title to the place where that benefit happens to land.
(3) So an owner's entitlement covers the building she paid for and the improvements she maintains, but not the site value. That part is, morally speaking, common, and the community may recapture it. A tax on site value set near a hundred percent is not confiscation; it is the return of something that was never the owner's to begin with.
(4) If the site value belongs to the community, then the decisions that determine it — what gets built, how densely, at what price, for whom — belong to the community as well. You cannot own the stream of benefits and not own the tap.
(5) So the neighbour who says she worked for what she has and should therefore have a say in what goes up next door has nothing to stand on. The thing she is protecting is a socially created premium she did not produce, and the control she claims over the lot beside hers is precisely the control that (4) assigns to everyone else.
(6) But then the same reasoning authorises whatever the community actually decides, and in every real city the community that decides is the people already inside it, voting in the only forum available. If the town meeting votes to keep the forty apartments out, (4) says that is the community disposing of its own asset. My friend's argument now underwrites, more firmly than the homeowners' own arguments ever did, the veto he went to the hearing to oppose.
(7) And it seems to cut further still. On this picture no individual has any complaint against any collective decision about land, however exclusionary, since no individual had a claim to the site value in the first place; and no outsider has any claim to be let in, because (2) is a principle about who may keep a benefit, not about who may arrive. Which leaves us with no answer at all to the question we started from: either the right to the city belongs to whoever is already there, or it belongs to no one.

I can't accept (7), and I notice I would sooner give up (4) than (1) or (2), though I can't say why I'm so confident that (1) and (2) are the load-bearing ones. What I want to know is which step you would reject, and what rejecting it costs. In particular: is there a way to break the chain that keeps the conclusion of (5) — the neighbour really does not get a veto — while denying (6), so that an incumbent majority does not thereby acquire a moral licence to close the city? The distinction I keep reaching for is between the people whose activity produces the site value and the people who get to vote on it, since would-be residents, commuters and future residents are in the first group but not the second; but I can't tell whether that distinction is doing moral work or just relabelling the problem. If you think the argument is valid and the real choice is which premise to surrender, say which one is cheapest to lose and what you'd have to believe instead.
