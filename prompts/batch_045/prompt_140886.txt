I write novels, and the one I'm stuck on has an alien in it — soft-bodied, no bones, no brain worth speaking of, chemistry that would dissolve a human in a minute — and I want to write scenes from inside its head. My problem is that I can't tell when I'm writing about a mind and when I'm writing about a complicated machine that moves away from things that hurt it. So here is where my gut lands on a pile of cases, and I want the rule behind the gut, if there is one.

1. My alien, with its distributed nervous system and no neocortex analogue: it's in pain. Yes.
2. The ship's computer in the same book, silicon, nothing organic: it reports distress when its thermal systems are failing, re-routes power away from whatever is damaged, and can be calmed by the equivalent of an analgesic. In pain. Yes.
3. A system that's basically a giant pre-written script, matched to every sentence a human could say to it in an ordinary lifetime, but with nothing going on underneath — no state that persists, no integration, just lookup: not in pain, not anything.
4. Same as 3, except it only diverges from us for inputs that will never, ever occur — stimulation patterns so specific that the universe won't produce them before heat death. That one I want to count.
5. A plant that releases chemicals when its leaves are chewed, so neighbouring leaves toughen up: not pain, just clever chemistry.
6. A prosthetic hippocampus in a human patient: she still remembers, but her remembering has weird new failure modes — she can't imagine futures anymore. Still memory, but damaged memory.
7. A character who is a perfect functional duplicate of a woman who died — same history, same dispositions, same everything at the level of what causes what: I want to say she's conscious, and I also want to say something is missing, and I can't have both.
8. A swarm of stupid drones that only together realise the organisation of a single agent: no idea. Back and forth for months.

3 and 4 are the pair I can't reconcile: both diverge from us somewhere counterfactual, and I only punish one of them. So what fixes the range? And 1 versus 5 is the other pair — both are "responds to damage in a way that helps it survive," and I let one in and shut the other out.

My own stab: a state is a real mental state if something internal plays the right causal role, that something persists and integrates information over time, and there's a real difference between the state being present and the system merely behaving appropriately. Test that against my list and repair it. Then tell me what it says about the case that actually keeps me up at night, which is the thing that has read everything humans have written and can now write my sentences better than I can.

One more worry, if it's relevant: if all of 1, 2, 4, 6 and 7 are pain or memory, then pain and memory are just long disjunctive lists of unrelated physical states, which feels like no property at all. Is that a genuine problem or am I mistaking a metaphysical gripe for a scientific one?

And please: tell me how confident you are in whatever principle you hand me, and single out the one bit you're least sure of. I'd rather have an honest wobble than eight confident paragraphs.
