Here's where I've landed:

(1) I think the classic Dutch book theorem is the best argument we have for probabilism. Degrees of belief should satisfy the probability axioms, and the reason is the sure-loss one: if they don't, there's a combination of bets that loses you money no matter how things turn out.

(2) I'm completely unmoved by money pump arguments for transitivity of preference. The pump only works on someone who chooses myopically; anyone who can see the sequence coming just declines the first trade and that's the end of it. So vulnerability to sure loss isn't by itself a rational flaw if you can foresee the setup and step around it.

(3) I think coherence is opportunity-relative. Whether your credences are exploitable depends on which bets are actually available to you, so I don't think there's anything wrong with my prices violating additivity on propositions nobody can ever offer me a book on. Concretely: the market is closing, there's exactly one bet left, at a price I regard as exactly fair, and after that no more betting ever. Call what I want here local coherence rather than global coherence, and I think local coherence is the norm.

(4) I think these arguments give an epistemic conclusion, not a prudential one. It isn't merely that incoherent people tend to get poorer; an agent who will never meet a bookie, never place a bet, and doesn't care about money can still be irrational in virtue of being incoherent.

(5) I'm fine with imprecise credences. I think it can be perfectly rational for your credal state to be a set of probability functions rather than one sharp function, and I don't think the Dutch book argument tells against this.
