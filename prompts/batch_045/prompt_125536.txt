I'm a senior at my high school, and I work as a peer tutor in the learning center. I get one student at a time for about twenty-five minutes, mostly in maths and chemistry, and at the end of each session I write a short note for that student's teacher saying whether the student has the material down or needs more work. The notes are informal, but teachers use them to decide who gets pulled out of study hall, so they do something.

I'd like a procedure for deciding whether a student actually knows something as opposed to merely recognising it, feeling confident, or having memorised a routine without understanding why it works. I've read a little about the KK principle — roughly, the claim that if you know something then you are in a position to know that you know it — and about arguments that knowledge isn't luminous, so that you can know something while being unable to tell, from the inside, that you do. If those arguments are right, then no checklist I can run in a session is a perfect detector of knowledge, and I'd rather the procedure say so plainly than pretend to be one.

So: give me the questions to ask, in order, in a twenty-five minute session with no testing software and no homework set in between; what counts as a passing answer to each; where the procedure stops with a clear not yet; and which questions are really about values — how much risk of being wrong is acceptable for this particular student, given what rides on the exam — and so have to go to the teacher rather than to me. Then run it on three students: one who gets every practice problem right and says it was easy, one who works slowly and gets things wrong on a first pass but can say why the wrong answers are wrong, and one who can explain the material perfectly but falls apart on anything timed. I suspect the procedure will give me an answer I don't like about at least one of them, and I'd like to see where.

Two last things. Say explicitly what the procedure cannot do. And separate what is actually settled among philosophers who work on this from what is one side of a live dispute about self-knowledge — I don't want to hand a teacher a note that presents a contested claim as established.
