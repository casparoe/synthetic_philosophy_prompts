I'm in a final-year seminar on philosophical logic — we spent the last two weeks on Fitch's paradox, and since section on Tuesday I've been stuck on a half-formed idea that I want to turn into a proposal. My thought has two parts. First: the proof seems to use only a handful of structural features of the knowledge operator — that it is factive, that it distributes over conjunction, and that the knowability principle says every truth is possibly known — so I suspect it is really one instance of a general collapse lemma: any factive, conjunction-distributing operator O such that every truth is possibly-O collapses into every truth being O. If that's right, the same shape of argument should go through for verification, for correct assertibility, and for something like checkability by an idealized machine, and the dialectic would be reframed as a choice about which structural feature to surrender rather than as a dispute about truth itself. Second, and more speculatively: as I reconstruct it, the last step of the argument — from the unknowability of the conjunction of p with not-known-p, to the claim that if p then p is known — looks like it needs double-negation elimination. In a constructive or information-state setting I think one only reaches the weaker result that if p then p is not-unknown, and I want to know whether that weaker result is worth anything: whether an anti-realist could restate their manifestation requirement in terms of this weakened, possibly iterated operator, whether the sequence obtained by iterating the double-negation twist stabilizes at some finite stage or collapses immediately back into the classical result, and whether either outcome leaves the anti-realist with a notion strong enough to do the work knowability was supposed to do.
