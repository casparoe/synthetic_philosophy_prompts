I am a high school senior who runs my school's philosophy club, and last week I set Kavka's toxin puzzle in front of about twenty people who had never met it before — the case where an eccentric billionaire hands you a million dollars at midnight if by then you have formed the genuine intention to drink, the following afternoon, a vial that will make you miserably ill for a day and do no lasting harm, with the money yours whether or not you ever actually drink it. What surprised me was not that the room split roughly evenly between "yes, I could form that intention" and "no, nobody could" — it was what happened when my faculty advisor proposed a variant in which the billionaire announces that a randomly selected 5% of participants will be audited, meaning locked in a room with the vial until they drink or the day ends. Nearly everyone flipped to yes at 5%. But when I then asked them to walk the audit probability down — 1%, then one in a thousand, then one in a million, then zero, holding the payout fixed — the group came apart rather than converging: some held that the capacity fades smoothly and survives all the way down to an arbitrarily small positive chance, some held that it disappears below a threshold, and two people insisted that even at 5% nobody is really forming an intention at all, but only hoping to get unlucky or planning to acquire the intention once the audit is announced. I now have a bet with a friend about which of those shapes is correct, and I have come to think the real disagreement is about something further: whether the confident "yes" answers in the original zero-audit case are reports of an actual capacity or a flattering misunderstanding of what one is doing when one resolves to resolve.

Here is what I would like from you. Give me a single number: your credence that in the original puzzle — no audit, no commitment device arranged in advance, one cold attempt — a normal adult can genuinely form the intention to drink the toxin, where "genuinely" means the resulting state would qualify as an intention on the best account of what intentions are, and not merely as a vivid resolution, a hopeful plan, or an intention to acquire an intention later. I do not want "it depends on your theory of intention" as an answer; I want the number decomposed. At minimum I would like separate probabilities for: whether intending to do something is compatible with believing, at the very moment of intending, that one will not do it, or whether intention is transparent to such belief in a way that rules the state out; whether the forming of an intention is under any direct voluntary control at all, or only reachable indirectly through attention, rehearsal, and self-manipulation; whether the indirect routes — arranging a commitment device beforehand, dulling one's own aversion first, or resolving to revisit the matter in the morning — yield a real intention or only something that plays its role in deliberation; what shape the audit-probability curve actually has, graded or thresholded, and what a graded curve is even supposed to imply about the zero case as a degenerate limit; and how much weight first-person "I could do it" reports deserve, given that people will also tell you they could hold their breath until they faint. Say how you are combining these — which multiply, which screen off the others, which are not really independent of one another — and then tell me which single term your final number is most sensitive to and what one argument, observation, or experiment would move it furthest. For calibration, my friend sits at about 0.8 and I sit at about 0.35, so please also say where you suspect that gap really lives, since I am no longer confident the two of us are disagreeing about the same thing. If you think I have carved the question up wrongly, recarve it and give me the numbers on your own carving instead. And please keep this to reasoning rather than reading: no citations, no bibliography, and nothing that turns on who has published what about the puzzle.
