My mom is retiring from the state legislature after 32 years and there's a dinner for her next Saturday — maybe 150 people — and I'm giving the toast. Half the room is her old staff, lobbyists, committee chairs, possibly the governor; the other half is cousins and her bridge club. Five minutes, tops.

Complication: I've spent the last couple of years deep in sortition stuff — citizen assemblies, legislatures picked by lot — and I wrote a long, annoying essay about it that she actually read and that we then argued about for a month. She thinks its nonsense. Her view is that legislating is a craft, that her constituents kept re-electing her because they wanted somebody who knew where the bodies were buried, and that a room full of random people would get eaten alive by staff and lobbyists inside a week. I don't want to recant at her own retirement dinner, and I also don't want to give a speech that's secretly a lecture with her as the target.

The version of the argument I actually believe, fwiw: sortition doesn't matter because random people are wiser or more descriptively representative — it matters because of the no-second-term part. A legislator who can never run again has nothing to trade a vote today for, and my claim is that most of what's ugly about legislatures comes off that gradient rather than from who happens to be sitting there. I call it the lame-duck-by-design argument when I'm being pretentious, but please don't put that phrase, or anything that sounds like it, in the speech.

Here's the hook I'd like you to try to build around, if you can: the vote her whole family brings up as her finest hour was the time she broke with her own leadership on the Medicaid expansion — and she did it about three weeks after announcing she wasn't running again. Her side of our argument basically predicts that. I want her best moment honored in a way that sees that clearly, but without it landing as "my own mother is Exhibit A for my theory," and without implying the other 31 years were just capture.

So: write the toast. Warm, with enough concrete detail that it doesn't sound like it could be given to anyone, able to survive her knowing exactly what I think, and followable by people who have never heard the word sortition — you can name it once if you have to. No jokes at her expense, nothing she'd call false, and nothing that requires looking anything up. Then, as the very last line, give me the toast itself — the one short sentence the room raises a glass to — inside \answer{...}.
