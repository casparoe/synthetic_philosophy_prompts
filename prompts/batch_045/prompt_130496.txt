I have no formal training in philosophy, and I have been turning a problem over on my own for a while. Below are my notes toward a short essay on why taking a moral belief on someone else's word feels different from taking any other kind of belief on someone else's word. I would appreciate your comments on them, especially on whether the central move actually works.

The notes:

The puzzle, as I see it. If a mechanic tells me my brakes are worn, it seems entirely fine to believe him and act on it while understanding nothing about brakes. But if a trusted friend tells me that a certain promise is permissible to break, or that some practice is unjust, it seems somehow off — almost a failure of character — simply to take her word for it rather than seeing the matter for myself. The usual explanations I have encountered say that moral belief needs to be backed by understanding, or that moral deference fails to give you the right kind of access to reasons, or that it displays a bad sort of reliance on other people.

My suggestion is that these locate the problem in the wrong place. Start from what deference normally buys you. When I defer to the mechanic I get two things at once: a true belief, and protection. If he is wrong despite being competent and honest, I am not to blame — I did what a careful person would do. Deference is attractive partly because it shifts the risk of being wrong onto someone better placed to carry it.

Now consider moral deference. Suppose I ask a friend whether breaking the promise is permissible, she says yes, and she is wrong. I now have a false moral belief — but I do not seem to have acquired any protection at all. I was told it was fine does not function as an excuse in the way the mechanic checked it does. The blame seems to stick to me regardless. So moral deference delivers one of the two goods that deference normally delivers and withholds the other: belief without safe conduct.

If that is right, the felt wrongness of moral deference is not really about testimony, or understanding, or autonomy. It comes from half-noticing that I am reaching for a transaction that cannot go through: adopting someone else's moral view while hoping that any guilt will land on them. Call it the non-transferability claim — moral responsibility cannot be delegated, so the ordinary economics of deference break down.

Two consequences I would want to draw out. First, the discomfort should fade when deference is not aimed at protection but merely at better odds of getting things right: if I am angry, exhausted, or personally invested, and I defer to someone whose judgment on this matter I have independent reason to rate above my own, deferring should feel fine. Second, the discomfort should fade where the blame really would be shared, as when a whole group defers to a committee.

What I would like from you:

1. Is the non-transferability claim true? Are there cases where being reliably told that something was permissible does genuinely excuse?
2. Does the account over-generate? If blame failing to transfer were the whole story, we should feel the same discomfort whenever we defer to someone about a matter for which we will still be held responsible — a manager deferring to a subordinate's safety judgement, say. Do we?
3. Does it under-generate? Can you describe a case where deference cannot transfer blame and yet feels perfectly acceptable?
4. Is treating deference as a transaction that purchases two separable goods coherent, or does it quietly assume what the essay is trying to explain?
5. Does the machinery do real work, or is it only a redescription of the discomfort it is meant to explain?
