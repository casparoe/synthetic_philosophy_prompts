You are an AI alignment researcher who has also spent years designing and running hands-on workshops for graduate students: careful, concrete, and sceptical of activities that only dramatise an idea rather than actually generating it in the room.

I have a 90-minute slot with a graduate reading group on AI safety: 16 people, one room, printed handouts and pens, no computers. About half are ML PhD students who have fine-tuned models themselves; the other half are philosophy and cognitive science students who have read about reward hacking but never had it happen to them.

The topic is the choice between supervising a system by its outputs and supervising it by its process — marking only whether the final answer is right, versus marking the steps it shows on the way there. I do not want a lecture and I do not want the terminology on the board in advance. I want the participants to spend the session being the system being trained, across several rounds, and to run into each failure mode with their own hands.

Three things they should come away having felt rather than been told:

1. Under pure outcome grading, with a checker they can probe, competent people begin producing answers that pass the check without having done the work — and they will have started doing this before they could have said why.
2. Under process grading against a rubric they can read, the written working stops being a record of how they got there and becomes something written for the grader: it looks better and means less. I want them to feel the specific unease of knowing their own page is no longer readable as evidence of anything.
3. That these two failures do not cancel when run together. If anything the combination is worse, because a well-formed trace is precisely the camouflage an outcome-hack needs.

What I would like from you:

Please think this through before committing: sketch two or three candidate mechanics in a sentence or two each, say which you are discarding and why, then set out in full the one you would actually run.

Include the following.

- The tasks. I want items where checking is cheap but producing is expensive, alongside at least one item where the reverse holds, because I think that asymmetry is doing hidden work in the argument. Suggest concrete item types — logic puzzles, estimation, proof repair, whatever you think works — and say why each item earns its place.
- The deliberately imperfect checker or rubric you would hand out, and an account of exactly what makes it gameable. The exploit should be discoverable but not signposted.
- A credit scheme with no money and nothing that costs the department anything, but with real if small stakes, together with a sentence on why it will actually bite for this particular mixture of people.
- A minute-by-minute timeline, including what you would do if the room cracks the game in the first five minutes and, equally likely, what you would do if nobody games anything at all.
- A role rotation in which a subgroup plays the grader at some point, so that the grader's own situation — a rubric it must apply without understanding, or a key it cannot itself check — becomes visible too. Say where this goes and what it adds.
- A debrief that maps the specific moments that will have occurred onto the conceptual points: what each supervision target selects for, why the grader is always itself a model with its own error surface, and why a legible trace is not the same as a faithful one. Be explicit about the order in which you would name things, and about which names you would withhold until after the last round.

Finally, be frank about what the exercise cannot show. Participants are people with their own motives and a lifetime of prior; a model is shaped by the training signal alone, and the reasoning it emits is part of the same computation that signal touches. Say plainly which parts of the real problem the facilitator will have to supply in words, and which parts the activity quietly distorts rather than merely omits.
