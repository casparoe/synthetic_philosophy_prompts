I'm retired — I spent about thirty years as a research chemist, and a friend dragged me into the philosophy department's reading group last year, which is somehow how I ended up as the "community member" on our hospital's Innovative Practice Review Committee. So I'm the one who has to write this up.

The proposal in front of us is from the neuropsychiatry group. They want to offer permanently implanted electrodes (deep brain stimulation) to patients with severe, long-standing treatment-resistant depression, but with a two-stage consent process. Their worry, which seems honest to me, is this: patients can't really know in advance what it will be like to live with the thing switched on, and afterwards a fair number of them report not recognising their own moods, and some of them, once the depression lifts, stop caring much about the very things they said they wanted the treatment for. So the group proposes giving each candidate a temporary sample first — a few days of an externally applied, fully reversible stimulation, or of a drug meant to mimic the post-implant state — so that the person consents to the permanent surgery having had something closer to actual acquaintance with what they're choosing.

Half the committee thinks this obviously, definately makes the consent better, and I see the appeal. But I've been reading about transformative experience and I can't shake the feeling that the trial run doesn't do the work they think it does — that a few sampled days, judged by the person you are right now, isn't the same question as what you'd want after you'd been that way for two years, and that maybe this just relocates the problem instead of solving it. On the other hand I really don't want to be the person who blocked a treatment for people who are suffering that badly.

Could you draft the short memo I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting? It should recommend a concrete decision — approve, approve with conditions, or send them back to redesign it — say what would count as evidence the sampling step is or isn't doing any real work, and give the strongest version of the argument for whichever side it comes down against, not a strawman. Keep the philosophy in it, but write it as something a committee can act on rather than a seminar paper. And please write it in American English.
