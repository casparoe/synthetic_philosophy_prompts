I'm teaching a graduate seminar in formal epistemology this term, and I have a single seventy-five-minute slot to introduce imprecise probabilities to a room that has spent the previous six weeks doing everything with one sharp probability function and expected-utility maximization. The students are comfortable with notation and with elementary Bayesian conditioning, but they have no prior exposure to the literature on imprecision — and, this being the part I botch every time I teach it, an economist friend is going to sit in who will, at some predictable point, object that what I am calling imprecision is just ordinary uncertainty about a model parameter and should be handled with a sharp higher-order distribution and one more round of conditioning. I don't want the explanation itself; I want the plan for it. So give me the order in which the components must appear, and say what depends on what. In particular: whether the choice-behavior data from Ellsberg-style urns has to come first as motivation, or whether it is better held until the machinery that explains it is on the board; at which point the distinction between an assignment of intervals to individual propositions and a set of probability functions has to be drawn, given that two students every year leave thinking these are the same object, and given that I want the distinction made in a way that survives their noticing that intervals on A and on B do not settle an interval on A or B; and where in the sequence the updating question belongs, since conditioning every member of the set and then applying a choice rule can come apart from applying a rule to the unconditional set, and I want that non-commutation to land as a structural feature rather than as a surprise.

Then the carrying example. I want one case that does the work of the whole session rather than a fresh toy example for each point, and I want you to say what it must contain and what it must not. My own thought is that it needs a situation where the imprecision is not merely ignorance that further evidence would erode, and where averaging over the set gives a different answer from the rules I actually want to defend; what I can't decide is whether it should also be a case that exhibits dilation — the setup in which you care about one question and receive a message bearing on it through a channel whose reliability you yourself cannot assess, so that learning the message widens your interval rather than narrowing it — or whether dilation should be held back and raised later as a separate worry. Tell me which, and where it goes.

Finally, the misconceptions, in the order they will actually arise rather than in the order a survey article would list them, each with the point in the sequence at which it should be headed off. Here is what I expect, though tell me if I have the order or the inventory wrong: imprecision as a disguised sharp distribution over first-order probabilities (my friend's objection, which I suspect should be answered rather than conceded, and which I want placed where it does the least damage to the motivation for the view); the slide from "no single number" to "the midpoint"; the assumption that more evidence always shrinks the interval, and the panic that sets in when dilation appears; the thought that an imprecise credence is a permissivist permission to pick any member of the set and carry on as before; and the reflex that the choice rules — maximizing the worst case, ruling out dominated options and stopping there, and narrowing to the admissible options and then maximizing among them — are cosmetic variants of one another, when they disagree in cases I can construct. For that last cluster I want a judgment call from you: which rule to present as the default, which to present only as a contrast, and which to name and then openly decline to discuss. What should I explicitly refuse to cover at this level — I am assuming the full behavioral foundations and anything about conglomerability, but say what else — and what single question can I put to them in the last ten minutes that would show me they have understood why the set is not a distribution over the set, rather than merely learned to manipulate the notation?
