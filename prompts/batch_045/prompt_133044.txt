For the past six years I have been caught in a pattern that I can describe exactly but not evaluate, and I would like your help with both halves of that.

I am 43, an architect, with a practice in Aldermere and my partner, our two children, and my 84-year-old mother in Vantol. One of the two cities is always my formal "base," which determines which regional scheme my mandatory professional pension is paid into, which office I am attached to, and where I am resident for tax purposes. I change my base roughly every eighteen months, and every change is one I want wholeheartedly at the time. Vantol winters are dark and hard, and I spend much of them caring for my mother; by March I am certain I should be based in Aldermere. Aldermere summers are hot and airless, and I am away from my family; by October I am equally certain I should be based in Vantol. I have now lived complete years in both cities, in both arrangements, so I do not think this is simply the grass being greener. My considered verdict on the two lives swings, and it swings with the season I have just come through.

Each move costs me around €14,000 in duplicate rent, removals, agency fees and unbilled days. It also resets to zero the continuity clock on the pension, whose annual payout is calculated from the longest unbroken period contributed to a single scheme, with a large step up at five years. My longest unbroken period is three years. My landlord noticed the pattern long ago and re-lets on a standing fee; he is not hostile about it, he has simply priced it.

A friend thinks I am being hard on myself, and her argument has two parts. First: every move was genuinely preferred by me, with full information, when I made it, so I have made no mistake; the fees are just what I pay for the option of living where I prefer, and options are worth paying for. Second: even if the fees exceed what that option is worth, that is a fact about a world containing landlords and continuity rules, not a defect in my preferences — nothing in rationality, she says, requires me to arrange my life so as not to be exploitable.

I am unpersuaded, and I cannot say why. So, two questions.

First, is she right? If someone's preferences reliably cycle them through a set of options and back again at a sure and predictable cost — a cost that no individual move feels like it incurs, since the pension loss only appears at the level of the pattern — is there anything irrational there, or only something expensive? And does it matter, to whether I am being irrational, that someone profits from my changes of mind, and that most of the loss would vanish if the fees and the continuity rule did?

Second, what should I do? I can see four possibilities: (a) pick one city and bind myself, signing a long lease or a lock-in that makes a mid-period transfer financially ruinous, in which case I need to know which city and on what grounds; (b) keep the flexibility but restrict when I am permitted to reconsider — only in a fixed month every five years, say, or only after I have lived a full year in the city I am currently based in; (c) fix a sequence now and adhere to it — base in Vantol while my mother is alive and the children are at home, then move once to Aldermere for good — which is what I suspect a planner would advise, but which asks me to act against what I will want in eighteen months; or (d) accept my friend's framing and treat the €14,000 and the reset as the running cost of an option I value.

Please tell me which of these rationality requires, if any, which it merely permits, and what the difference between them turns on. I am less interested in being told to commit, or to weigh the costs against the benefits, than in what the fact that I am being cycled tells me — if it tells me anything — about what I should have willed at the outset.
