I am writing a novel — literary fiction, third-person limited, set among a small fishing people on an archipelago — and I have built them a language, Kænnavan, with three features I want to do real psychological work in the story rather than serve as local colour:

(i) Kænnavan has no relative frame of spatial reference. There are no words for left, right, in front of, or behind. Every locative expression and every verb of motion requires one of eight winds, fixed to the sky, so a speaker must place a cup on the north side of the table and must say that she walked west along the reef even when she is indoors, below decks, at night.

(ii) The language carries obligatory evidential marking on every finite verb. A speaker must encode, grammatically, whether her claim rests on having seen it, on having heard it from a source she can name, on having inferred it from traces, or on having been told by someone who did not see it. There is no unmarked, evidentially neutral form.

(iii) Kænnavan colour vocabulary partitions the spectrum into a single term covering what English divides into blue and green, and a second term covering the blue-green range together with grey. There is no term that maps onto blue alone.

What I need from you is a taxonomy, in the form of a decision tree, of the positions one can hold about the relation between the grammar and lexicon of a language and the cognition of its speakers — the family of views that travels under the name of the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis and its successors. I am not asking for a survey of the evidence and I do not want citations: assume as background that the strong deterministic version is generally regarded as discredited, that weaker effects are live but contested, and that nothing in what follows turns on which studies came out which way. I want the structure of the space.

Constraints on the instrument:

1. Build it from the logical space, not from a list of names. Start from the questions on which the positions come apart and the combinations of answers they permit, and only then locate the actual positions in that space.

2. Every branch node must be a question I can answer about Kænnavan, or about a concrete scene in the novel, without already knowing which position it leads to. Phrase each as a case: a speaker who has never left the islands is shown two colour swatches and asked which ones go together; a woman describes a death she did not witness, first in Kænnavan and then in a trade language with no evidentials; a child is raised bilingual and tested in silence. No node may ask whether language determines thought, or anything else that merely restates a label.

3. The distinctions I care about, and which the tree should therefore be able to separate, are these: obligatoriness (what the grammar compels a speaker to encode) versus what the lexicon merely makes easy to say; effects that appear while a speaker is planning or producing speech versus effects that persist in silence and in non-linguistic tasks; effects on attention, salience, and memory versus effects on what is conceptually available to a person at all; and whether the effect is supposed to survive a switch of languages within a single bilingual speaker.

4. Six to eight leaves, eight to twelve branch nodes. Name each leaf where a name is standard in the literature; where a label is not stable, or where you are not confident it is, give the leaf a descriptive name and say that you have done so. For each leaf: one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and one sentence on what I am licensed to put inside a character's head if I write from that position — including the cases where the honest answer is that I am licensed to write nothing beyond what any speaker of any language would feel. I would rather be told that a premise of mine is empty than have it flattered. Two of my characters argue about this, and I need their disagreement to be a real one rather than a quarrel over wording.

5. Identify at least three combinations of answers that no position occupies, and say of each whether it is empty because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because nobody has bothered to defend it.

6. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, and say what further question would separate them, or why no available evidence would.

Then the part I actually need: run each of my three features through the tree. Tell me which leaf it lands on, tell me at which node the answer stops being a question about my fiction and becomes a question about real languages, and — this is the crux — tell me whether the position I land on for feature (ii) licenses a scene in which a character, alone, silent, and with no one to report to, feels the difference between what she saw and what she inferred. I want the mapping tight enough that a reader who knows the literature could not object to the psychology, and I want to be told plainly where no such guarantee is available.
