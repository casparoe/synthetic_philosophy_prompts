The thing I can't sort out: is Machiavelli's virtù a property of the person, or just a name for the fact that things went well for him? Every example he gives of a man with virtù seems to be a man who won, and when a prince gets overthrown the explanation offered is that fortune turned. That feels circular and I can't tell wether it actually is or whether I'm missing a distinction.

Specific things I'd like you to get into:

1. Trait or policy? That sounds like he's saying what matters is the fit between a strategy and an environment, not a fixed character trait. But he also says nobody can really adapt, because nature inclines them one way and they can't deviate from it. Does that kill the policy reading, or is the inability to adapt itself part of the point he's making?

2. Is fortune just a residual category — whatever my model of the agent fails to predict? 3. The part I most want your take on: fortune doesn't look neutral to me. Boldness seems to do better with her than caution does, and the flood-control metaphor (you can't stop the torrent, but in the calm season you can build weirs and channels) plus the closing comparison of fortune to a woman who yields to the impetuous makes me think he's making a structural claim — that outcomes for a prince are heavy-tailed, so a policy that usually fails but occassionally wins hugely beats one that reliably does okay, and glory or reputation is exactly the kind of quantity where that's true. Does that fit, or does it turn him into a decision theorist with a utility curve that he isn't?

4. If you buy that, the weirs-and-moles advice starts to look like ruin avoidance rather than maximizing expected value. Is that in tension with the audacity advice, or do the two sit together fine?
