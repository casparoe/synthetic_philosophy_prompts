write me a deadpan internal memo, ~600 words and numbered clauses, from the communications desk of a national flood-warning service announcing that staff may no longer issue indicative conditionals. No more "if the river tops the embankment, the lower ward floods." Every advisory has to be replaced by a flat statement of conditional credence — P(flooding | river above 4 m) = 0.9 — with the antecedent drawn from the official Antecedent Register. The ruling comes from an invented body, something like the Standing Committee on Non-Propositional Statements, whose members are never named and never say the words "triviality result" out loud.

The comedy has to come from getting the actual material right. The Ramsey-test idea that your credence in a conditional just is your conditional credence; the fact that this blows up once you let conditionals take probabilities of their own or sit inside other operators; and above all that a bare conditional probability is not something anyone can act on — it tells a householder nothing until they learn the antecedent and update for themselves, it can never be contradicted by anything that happens when the antecedent doesnt obtain (which is precisely why the legal office loves it), and its headline number can be made to look terrrific simply by choosing an antecedent that is narrow, unobservable, or nearly impossible. Let the Committee exploit all of that without ever admitting it: guidance on picking antecedents that flatter the number, a ban on "would" and "in the event that" that keeps failing because every paraphrase sneaks an if back in, and a rule for flattening nested conditionals ("if the pumps fail, then if the gate sticks, the ward floods") that quietly changes what was meant.

Close with a clause forbidding conditional constructions in the memo itself, written as one. No character explains the philosophy, and there is no punchline that winks at the reader.
