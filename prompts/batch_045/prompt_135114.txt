I'm writing a chapter for an edited volume on philosophy of language in popular film, and my case study is the paternal reveal in *The Empire Strikes Back*, together with the two scenes that frame it: Obi-Wan's earlier account of Luke's father's death and his later defense of that account as true from a certain point of view, and the confirmation scene in *Return of the Jedi* where Luke is told to settle the question for himself. I want a reading of these scenes through the Kripkean apparatus of rigid designation and the necessary a posteriori — strict enough that a philosopher of language could accept it, and honest about where the film does not cooperate.

What I need worked out is this. First, the mapping: is the reveal the same logical shape as the canonical Hesperus/Phosphorus case — two rigid designators for one object, an identity that is necessary if true and known only empirically — or does it differ? In particular, is the black-armored figure's name a name, or is it partly a title and role (Darth being a Sith honorific, the figure being introduced under a mask, by reputation, as the killer of Luke's father)? If it is closer to a description or to a rigidified description in Donnellan's sense, what happens to the claim that the identity is necessary?

Second, the drama. If the identity is necessary, the shock has to be a matter of cognitive significance rather than of modality, and the film should be dramatizing the gap between epistemic and metaphysical possibility. But the plot seems to need the modal question to be genuinely open: Luke's struggle is not only over whether he has been lied to but over whether the man in the armor could still be reached, and over whether he could have turned out to be someone else entirely. Is that just the familiar confusion Kripke diagnoses, or does the saga supply resources for a real contingency — a contingent-identity reading, a sortal-relative reading in which Anakin has ceased to be Anakin and become someone else, the claim that the man is largely machine now?

Third, two refinements I want used rather than name-dropped: the distinction between designators that are merely persistently rigid and ones that are obstinately rigid, applied to the counterfactual the film invites us to entertain — the one in which Anakin never falls and no such Sith Lord exists. Does the name fail to designate there, or does it still designate him? Which answer does Luke's deliberation require? And a two-dimensional framing: what would it be to say that Luke's epistemic situation changes by his learning which world is actual, so that the primary and secondary intensions of the name come apart, and does that framing do better justice to the scene than the bare necessity claim?

Fourth, a complication specific to fiction: the name entered circulation in 1980, before the character it now designates had any history, and the prequels retrofitted both a bearer and a causal chain. Does that affect whether it designates rigidly at all, and is that a defect in the reading or an interesting case of reference for fictional names?

Finally, where the reading misfires: is Obi-Wan's self-defense a claim about reference at all, or about implicature and deliberate misleading? Does the film know the difference between epistemic and metaphysical modality, or does it trade on running them together? And what, if anything, does the film add to the debate that the astronomical examples do not — for instance that a necessary truth can arrive as catastrophic news, and that discovering an identity can reorganize what one is permitted to do to the person one has thereby found. Tell me which scenes you would build the chapter around, and where you would concede that the film simply is not playing by the Kripkean rules. I do not need citations or suggestions for further reading.
