Some background. I spent a career in stratigraphy, reconstructing ancient shorelines out of a rock record that preserves some small fraction of the time that actually elapsed and preserves some kinds of environments with grotesque partiality. Since retiring I have been attending a philosophy reading group at the local university, and last week we discussed the old maxim about the winners writing the history. One member — a graduate student in history, and considerably sharper than I am — produced an argument that I have been unable to dislodge and unable to accept. I have reconstructed it as faithfully as I can.

(1) Every account of a distant conflict that we now possess has come down to us through a chain of custody: someone wrote it, someone kept it, someone thought it worth copying, someone thought it worth preserving. At each link, the parties able to perform that operation were disproportionately the winners, since winning is largely what gives you the archives, the scribes, the stone, and the leisure to commission the monument.

(2) Therefore, for any controversy in which the winners had a stake in the answer — who moved first, how many died, on what terms the settlement was made — the surviving evidence is not a neutral sample of what happened. It has passed through a filter that systematically favours the winners' version.

(3) A body of evidence selected by a process that systematically favours one answer cannot, by itself, make that answer more probable than the answers it excluded. A filter that reliably passes some candidates and blocks others carries no information about which candidate is true; it reports on the filter, not on the world.

(4) So, for exactly the controversies in (2), the surviving record cannot make the victors' version more probable than the versions it displaced — and those displaced versions are, by (1), largely unavailable to us. Historical inquiry has no route to a verdict in precisely the cases where a verdict matters.

(5) Nothing in (1)–(4) turns on the subject matter being human. The argument uses only one feature of the situation: that the evidence base is the survivor of a selective filter. Wherever that holds, the conclusion follows. The palaeontological record is such a survivor, since only some organisms, in some settings, in some windows of deposition, followed by the right exposure and the right collector, ever become evidence at all. So is the published experimental literature, and so is the set of buildings still standing after an earthquake.

(6) Therefore no discipline whose evidence base is a filtered survivor can use that evidence to raise the probability of any hypothesis about the past above the probability of the hypotheses the filter removed.

(7) Now turn the argument on itself. Steps (1) and (2) are themselves empirical claims about who controlled the archives, and our only evidence for them is the same filtered record; what tells us that the victors kept the chronicles is, overwhelmingly, the victors' chronicles.

(8) So if (1)–(4) are correct, the argument cancels its own warrant: it cannot be more probable than the victor-friendly accounts it discredits, and it gives us no reason to prefer it to them.

(9) Hence we face a choice. Either we accept (6) — a skepticism that covers history and every historical science, my own former field included — or we accept that the argument destroys itself at (7)–(8), which merely relocates the question to where in (1)–(6) it actually went wrong.

I cannot accept (6). In my old field we spent our working lives raising and lowering confidence with filtered data, and we had a name for the artifact whereby a genuinely abrupt extinction gets smeared backward into an apparent gradual decline purely by the incompleteness of the record; the right response to that artifact was not to stop believing in extinction events but to model the incompleteness and subtract it. Yet I also do not want to give up (1) or (2), which read to me as unglamorous empirical facts about who gets to keep records. Between those constraints, I am happy to pay almost anything except (6).

Two exits I tried, both of which were closed:

First, I proposed that our filters are indifferent and theirs are interested — sedimentation does not care which hypothesis is true, whereas power does. The reply was that a filter with interests behind it is not peculiar to history, since the file-drawer problem in experimental science is exactly that, and that (3) as stated contains nothing that would distinguish the two cases.

Second, I proposed that we simply invert the filter: once you know how it selects, you can correct for it, which is what we did with preservation bias. The reply was that in the historical case the correction would itself have to be extracted from the filtered record, so we are back where we began, whereas in my own field we had independent hold on the filter.

When I objected that (7)–(8) looked like a cheap tu quoque, since a thesis can be true even if it cannot be shown to be true from within its own evidence, I was told that this concedes everything: a thesis that is no more probable than its rivals on the available evidence has no claim on my assent in the first place.
