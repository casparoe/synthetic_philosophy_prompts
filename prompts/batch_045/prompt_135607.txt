so i need a dialogue for something i'm writing, and i want the two people in it to actually be arguing rather than taking turns at a podium.

setup: a town of about 3,000 people in a mountain valley, no theater, is deciding whether to build one. the proposal on the table is not a normal theater. no tickets, no company, no director. the plays are written collectively by the townspeople about their own town, the cast is drawn by lot at the start of each season, rehearsals happen in the open, and the performance is on a platform in the middle of the market square with no stage front — no architectural separation between performers and everyone else, and the crowd is meant to join in at certain points. basically, one of the councillors read rousseau's long letter against putting a theater in geneva (the one addressed to d'Alembert) and tried to design a theater that the objection couldn't touch.

write the dialogue between two other people on the council:

Mara — thinks the design doesn't dodge the objection at all and that the money should go into a yearly civic festival instead: dancing on the square, games, a shared meal, everyone present, no fiction, nothing to watch.

Dov — thinks this proposal basically already is a civic festival, and that Mara's clean line between spectacle and festival dissolves the moment you look at it closely.

how i want it to go:

both of them are sharp and both actually know the material; neither one is a strawman. Mara is not a killjoy who thinks fun is corrupting, and Dov is not just somebody who wants to be entertained. if one of them starts to sound like a parody, the dialogue has gone wrong.

they have to respond to each other's actual points. when Mara draws a distinction, Dov should try to break it with a concrete case; Mara then either repairs the distinction or gives up that bit of ground and relocates her objection somewhere else. i want at least two moments where one of them basically says "ok, you're right about that, but" and then moves the argument to new ground rather than repeating themselves. Mara wins some of it, Dov wins some of it, and it should not end with one of them turning out to have been right all along.

stuff for them to fight over — pick the three or four that generate the most real disagreement, don't try to do all of it:

- whether the lottery-plus-amateur setup actually removes rousseau's problem with actors, or just distributes it. you still memorize words someone else wrote and pretend to be someone you're not, and if you're bad at it the whole town watches you fail in public.
- whether a play the town writes about itself is really the community representing itself to itself, or whether the moment you script it you've introduced selection and fiction — somebody decided which version of the town gets shown — which is exactly what the festival is supposed to avoid.
- whether free admission and no ticket market kill the luxury objection, given that the town is still sinking a season of labor and materials into a platform, and people might start coming over from the next valley, at which point it's a regional attraction and not a town thing.
- whether "everyone joins in" is actually voluntary. if the plays are the town's big collective event of the year and you sit it out, are you opting out of civic life? and if it really is optional, does it still do the unifying work it's supposed to do? this is the one i care most about, so make it the crux rather than a side point.
- whether what's really doing the work in the objection is theater at all, or any specialization of roles at all — in which case the critique has a blast radius Mara might not want.
- second-order stuff: in year two somebody starts coaching people, or writes more of the script than everyone else, and you quietly get specialists back. Dov should argue the design has to be able to survive that; Mara should argue that it can't, and that this is the whole point.

a few format things: keep the turns short, let them interrupt each other a bit, let them agree about something in passing. and please don't have them recite or quote rousseau at length — i know the text, i want them arguing from it, not lecturing about it. 800 to 1200 words is fine.

(also if it's useful, bring in his positive picture — the open air festival, the dancing, the genevan social clubs, people seeing each other rather than watching a show — but make it do argumentative work in the exchange, don't just describe it.)
