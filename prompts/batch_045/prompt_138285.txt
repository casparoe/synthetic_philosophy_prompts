Hi — I TA for a final-year seminar that's cross-listed between philosophy and politics (something like Judgment and Forecasting in International History, which is why the enrolment is so lopsided), and I'm drafting the final exam. I need one 90-minute essay question, 20 marks, on the outside view and reference-class forecasting applied to the durability of empires. The problem I keep having is that my students can all recite the move — pick a reference class, take the base rate, ignore the details of your case, brace for the planning fallacy — and then they stop. I want a question where that paragraph gets you maybe half credit, because the interesting stuff starts after it.

Here's the scenario I want to hang it on, and please feel free to rework it: it's 1938, an analyst is asked how likely it is that Britain still rules India in fifty years' time, and she decides to use the outside view. Give her three candidate reference classes to choose between, arranged as rungs on what I've been calling the *matching ladder*: rung one, every polity in history that has ruled a territory more than 5,000 km from its capital (lots of cases, wildly heterogeneous, arguably worthless); rung two, European overseas empires founded after 1500 (a dozen-ish cases, better fit, several of which are still alive in 1938 so they're right-censored, and two of which are Britain); rung three, maritime empires that had just come through a global war while facing a mass nationalist movement in their largest possession (two cases, chosen using exactly the causal story the outside view was supposed to bracket). The crux I want students to chew on is that every rung is defensible and every rung is compromised, and moving up the ladder buys relevance by spending data and by smuggling in the inside view.

Two other things I'd like built into the question, and I've given them names so I can refer to them in the rubric. First, what I call the *coroner's reference class*: a class whose membership is settled partly by how things ended, so that the frequency computed over it is inflated in a way that isn't visible from the class's own description. (Empires are the obvious worry — we mostly count the ones that are over, and things that never ended get relabelled as nations or unions or long-lived states — but the same worry shows up in参考 classes like "failed states" or "housing bubbles," so let them generalise if they want.) Second, the reflexivity problem: if a base rate like this becomes known to the people being forecast, they act on it, and the frequency the outside view was leaning on stops being the frequency that governs the future — so does the outside view have any reply here that doesn't just concede that its authority was borrowed from frequencies it can no longer claim? I'd also like the question to make them say what epistemic modesty actually amounts to in this case: deferring to a shaky base rate, splitting the difference between the inside and outside estimates, or declining to put a number on it at all. And please don't let the question reward knowing what actually happened after 1938 — either tell the students to set it aside or set the case up so hindsight can't do the work.

Deliverables, if you can: the question exactly as it should appear on the paper (single question, maybe with one short clarifying sentence of setup, no sub-parts that turn it into a checklist); a model answer of about 700 words; and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points at 4 marks each, where each point names the specific move a first-class answer makes. For each point I want a one-line note on how students typically muff it — e.g. asserting that the reference class problem refutes the outside view, treating the right-censored cases as if they'd resolved, or noticing the selection effect but not saying what it does to the number. Finally, tell me what a clean B answer looks like (say 13 or 14 out of 20): I want to be confident that someone who has the vocabulary but hasn't really thought it through lands there and not higher.
