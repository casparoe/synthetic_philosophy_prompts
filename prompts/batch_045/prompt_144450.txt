Here's my opening move. I've been trying to separate two stories someone like you might be telling, and I want to know which one you're actually committed to. Call the first aggregation: the price is like the mean of many noisy independent instruments — errors partly cancel, the average beats any single reading, and more independent readings is strictly better. That's the picture I understand from my own work, and it's the one that would make a market an epistemic engine. Call the second ventriloquism: the price mostly ends up tracking whoever is most confident, most capitalized, or most willing to bet, while the people who quietly know better simply don't trade — so the market doesn't know more than its members do, it just amplifies the loudest one. My worry is that the no-trade theorem pushes me toward the second story. If trade requires disagreement, and disagreement requires either different priors, different Preferences, or somebody being wrong, then the folks who actually move prices are the ones willing to put money down, not the ones closest to the truth — and the rest of the volume looks like plumbing (liquidity, hedging, rebalancing), not like evidence about the world. So before we go further: aggregation or ventriloquism, and why isn't the theorem a reason to prefer the second? And feel free to tell me where my instrument analogy breaks down — my suspicion is it's the independence assumption, since my spectrometers weren't trying to outguess each other and weren't paid for being right.
