For eleven years I have belonged to a reading group of nine people, and over that time we have fallen into a habit. Whenever we disagree about what a book does or does not say, we put the question to one member — I will call her M. — and take her answer as settled. She has no academic training in anything of this kind; she is simply the most patient reader among us, and her rulings have been steady enough that the rest of us now defer without arguing. I have come to suspect that she is applying some rule she has never stated, and I want to know what it is, because there is a dispute coming up in the spring that I think will split us. I would like to know in advance how she is likely to rule, what she would be committing herself to either way, and what further case would settle the question if her past rulings do not. I have thought about this on my own but I do not know the vocabulary for it, so please say plainly which view is which.

Her rulings that I can remember, reported as accurately as I can manage.

1. A novel of 1911 in which a young woman is introduced with a single adjective that in the English of that year meant accomplished in the arts and somewhat affected, and that every present-day reader takes to mean insincere. M. ruled that the novel does not say the woman is insincere. She added that it would be different if the author had meant the modern sense and merely written the sentence badly, and she said she did not care that most readers now miss the point.

2. A novel of the 1990s. Twenty years after publication the author said in an interview that the first-person narrator had been lying throughout about a central event and that he had left signs. No reviewer had noticed at the time. When critics went back, the discrepancies turned out to be in the text and to be systematic. M. ruled that the novel does say the narrator is lying; she said the interview did not settle it on its own, but the discrepancies did, and that they had been available all along.

3. A sequence of poems. In a memoir published long afterwards the poet wrote that the heron appearing in four of the poems stood for her father's silence. Nothing in the poems connects the bird to anything, and no one read them that way before the memoir. M. ruled that the poems do not say this. She allowed that it may be the truest account of how they came to be written, and said that this is a different matter.

4. A short story. The writer's notebooks, published after her death, show that a detail repeated three times — a door painted green — referred to the door of the house she grew up in, and one entry says she hoped no reader would ever pick it up. M. ruled that this is not part of what the story says. Her reason, as I remember it, was that a meaning has to be the sort of thing a reader could be credited with getting, and an association the author wanted to keep to herself is not that.

5. A novel written in English by a novelist whose first language was Norwegian. A passage in which one character presses another to stay the night reads, to any competent reader of the English of that year, as an invitation to sleep with him. His letters from the time show that he meant ordinary hospitality and would have been appalled. M. ruled that the novel does say it, on the ground that the author does not get to decide what his sentences do.

6. Two brothers wrote a screenplay together and later disagreed in public about what the final scene meant. The stage directions support one brother's reading, but weakly. M. ruled for the reading the directions support, and said that neither brother's saying-so counted as such, although each brother's account was evidence about which readings the script could bear.

The new case. The book we are reading in the spring is a novel of 1962 whose narrator is a man who has come back to his home town after two years away. The text is full of small, unexplained details: he cannot sit with his back to a doorway, he sleeps on the floor beside his bed, he loses stretches of time, he cannot bear to be touched without warning, and he reports his memories in a flat present tense as though they were happening again. He is called cold by the other characters, and calls himself cold.

In 2019 a scholarly edition of the novelist's letters appeared; she had left instructions that they not be published for fifty years. The letters from the years of writing show that she had been reading the then-new clinical literature on a condition that had no name in ordinary English in 1962 and that only entered general use decades later; that she intended the narrator to be understood as having it; and that she chose each of those details deliberately. Nothing public at the time said any of this — no interview, no preface, no review, no blurb. The details are, however, all in the text, and a reader who has both the later vocabulary and the letters finds them unmistakable, while a reader with only the vocabulary of 1962 finds them merely strange.

Our question, in the form we always put to her, is whether the novel says that the narrator has this condition, and we have to answer yes or no in the notes we keep and hand on to whoever joins the group next.

So: what will she say? What rules fit all six of her earlier rulings equally well, and what does each of them predict here? Which one is she most likely running, and what is the reason for preferring it? If her track record genuinely underdetermines the new case, tell me what further case would separate the rules that remain — I would rather be handed a case than a longer argument — and say what she would be signing up for under each of the readings you cannot rule out. One last thing I would like your view on: I have a suspicion that our question is really two questions, about what the book said then and what it says now, and that some of our past disagreements have been the two questions in disguise. If that is right, say so, and say which of the two she has in fact been answering all along.
