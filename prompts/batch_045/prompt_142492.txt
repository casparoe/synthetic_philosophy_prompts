I am a professor of moral philosophy, and — I suspect for that reason alone — I was asked last year to join the board of a small endowed trust. Its deed directs the income to be applied for the relief of suffering among persons living or yet to be born, and says nothing about any time limit. We have about £400,000 to disburse this year and two proposals shortlisted, and I have the casting vote on Thursday. I am embarrassed to find that my training gives me very little grip on the argument that has emerged, and I would be grateful for your help with it.

The first proposal is a perinatal mental health service attached to a district hospital: twenty years of funding, roughly twelve hundred women a year, good evidence behind it, outcomes we can actually audit, and nothing left once the funding stops. Call it the hospital scheme.

The second is a proposal to build and endow a physically durable, deliberately low-technology repository of practical knowledge — agriculture, midwifery, basic surgery, water and sanitation, mechanical repair — designed to stay legible and useful to whatever human communities come after us, with no planned terminus. Its advocates are candid: the chance that it survives and is used at all is maybe one in fifty, and if our species is not here in two centuries it will have achieved nothing. But if it works, they say, it will slightly improve some lives in every generation for as long as there are generations.

Our chair, an economist by training, says the comparison is not close. Multiply a bounded benefit by any chance and it stays bounded; multiply an unbounded one by any positive chance at all and it stays unbounded. However small the probability, and however thin the benefit per person, an improvement spread over an endless future outweighs one confined to twenty years and twelve hundred people a year, and it is not a matter of degree. He adds that the deed's reference to persons yet to be born was written precisely with this sort of benefaction in mind.

I have three worries, and I cannot tell which of them is doing the real work. First, his reasoning seems to rank every long-shot scheme aimed at the open-ended future equally: two such schemes both come out unbounded, so it cannot choose between them, and it appears to be indifferent between one chance in fifty and one chance in fifty million, which I take to be a defect rather than a feature. Second, if instead of totals we ask what share of the people alive in the first N generations are better off, and then let N grow, the repository's contribution shrinks towards nothing and the hospital scheme's does not — and I do not know what should settle which of these two ways of adding up is the right one. Third, and perhaps most to the point: a future of ten trillion people is unimaginably large but still bounded, and there his argument simply becomes a very big number calculation of the ordinary kind, one that might well go the other way. It troubles me that whether the future has an end should swing the decision entirely, when nothing else about the two proposals changes.

A fourth option has also been put to us: rather than improving lives within the future, spend on reducing the chance that there is much of a future at all. My own sense is that this is a different sort of thing from the other two in a way that matters, but I cannot say what the difference consists in.

One of our number, a retired mathematician, has proposed a rule she calls the gardener's rule, which I find I half want to accept. Forget totals, she says: for each generation ask how far short of the best that generation could have been it actually falls, require that this shortfall stay under a fixed ceiling in every generation and shrink as time goes on, and exclude any scheme that breaches the ceiling even if it leaves a larger total of good behind. She likes it because it gives the same verdict whether the future is bounded or not and never asks us to compare unbounded quantities. What I cannot see is whether it is right to be indifferent to sheer numbers in that way — whether the rule would have us ignore an enormous amount of suffering in one generation, provided that generation's shortfall stays under the ceiling, and whether that is a feature of it or a fatal objection.

There is one more thing I noticed and cannot formulate properly. If the future really is endless, then in some sense the people the repository helps and the people the hospital helps are equally many — the two collections can be paired off against one another — so that more people cannot be what distinguishes the proposals. Yet it is plainly not true that the proposals are equally good. I assume the answer has something to do with the fact that the people come in an order and the order is not up to us, but I would like that made precise rather than gestured at.

So: how should I vote, and what is the argument I put to the board? Please give me the strongest version of the chair's case and tell me where, if anywhere, it is simply right; tell me which of my misgivings is load-bearing and which I should abandon; say what you make of the gardener's rule and of the fourth option; and if the honest conclusion is that no comparison between these proposals is available, tell me what someone in my position ought to do in light of that. Assume the other trustees are intelligent, unsentimental, and unmoved by appeals to how things are normally done. I do not need references or suggested reading — I need an argument in a form I can defend under questioning on Thursday afternoon. Please write in British English.
