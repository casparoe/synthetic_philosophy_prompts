Hey — I teach a final-year undergrad aesthetics seminar (small group, about fourteen people, mostly philosophy majors plus a few art-history people who are much better than me on the material details), and I'm writing the take-home final right now. The last block of the course was on fakes, forgeries, and why we care about originals, and I really don't want another pile of essays that recite the autographic/allographic distinction and then announce that the original matters more because of provenance. Literally everyone wrote that last year and it told me nothing about who'd actually thought about it. So I want one essay question, answerable in about 45 minutes or 900-1000 words, built around some cases I've been sketching, that forces them to locate the real crux and defend a position against a case designed to break their favourite reply.

Here's the raw material, which you should feel free to reshape or re-cut. (1) The *Certainty Stipulation*: a painting is scanned at molecular resolution and rebuilt atom-placement by atom-placement by a machine, and an exhaustive verification procedure certifies that the facsimile is intrinsically identical to the original down to craquelure, pentimenti, varnish layering, everything; the exam should stipulate that there is no epistemic gap left, and I want the question written so that students can't just quietly reintroduce doubt by asking how we could ever really know it's perfect. (2) The *Swap*: the facsimile is hung in the museum and the original goes into a climate-controlled vault; no visitor, curator or conservator can tell; the museum puts a small printed note saying which is on the wall, and later, in a variant, runs the same swap with no note at all. Is anything lost, and if so, lost *by whom*? (3) The *Restoration Case*: one of my students works as a conservator's assistant and got me thinking about this — stipulate for the exam that something like two thirds of the paint layer you're actually looking at on some seventeenth-century canvas was put there by restorers in the 1800s, so the object we call the original is itself substantially a later production. (4) The *No-Complaint Collector*: someone knowingly buys a facsimile, at facsimile price, gets exactly the experience she wanted, and never deceives anyone. If forgery is wrong, is the whole of the wrong the lie, or is there a residue — e.g. something about the corruption of the historical record, or objects as a kind of testimony? And then flip it: if it's the lie that does all the work, why does it feel like a loss when the original is destroyed after the facsimile is made and nobody ever finds out.

What I want back: the question text as I'd hand it to them (with however you'd stage the cases — including whether you'd cut any of mine or add a better one), a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 built on five must-hit points, each with the marks it's worth and one line on how students typically get it wrong or wriggle out of it. I especially want the scheme to reward answers that separate the epistemic worry from the non-epistemic remainder, that distinguish at least three candidate kinds of value rather than using the word value loosely, and that say something explicit about whether the loss in the swap and destruction cases is a loss to any person. Please also add a short note on what separates an A from a B answer — the specific move or concession that has to appear for the top grade — and flag any place where you think my cases are rigged so that only one answer is available. No reading list or citations needed, I know the literature; I just want the question and the standard I'll mark it against.
