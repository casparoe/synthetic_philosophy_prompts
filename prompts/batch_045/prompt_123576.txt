Hey — quick favor, and I promise it isn't a request for a reading list. I'm drafting a short position paper (20-odd pages) on what an official state apology is even supposed to *do*, and I want the requirements list written by somebody who isn't me, because I keep smuggling my own answer in and then forgetting I did it.

So: write me the desiderata that any adequate account of political apology has to satisfy. Purpose fixed up front, since adequacy is relative to it: the account has to be good enough to hand to an actual government office that is deciding whether to apologize for something that happened before anyone alive was born, to whom, in what words, and what (if anything) has to accompany the words. Not interpersonal apology with a collective agent bolted on at the end — the collective-agent weirdness is the topic.

Each requirement should come with the case that motivates it, and I want at least these cases covered, plus any you think I've missed:

- The founder's problem (my coinage, sorry): a successor state apologizes for the very act that constitutes it — the atrocity, expropriation, or rupture that its own founding narrative celebrates. Any account that can't say something determinate here is, I think, useless to the office I described.
- The twins: two states apologize for the same event on the same day in near-identical language; one is received as an apology and the other as a press release. What does an account need in order to distinguish them?
- Over the heads: the state apologizes to a group's current representatives, and the descendants of the direct victims publicly reject it.
- Regret without repudiation: the state "deeply regrets the loss of life" in an episode it continues to regard as justified. Is that an apology, a near-miss, or a distinct and legitimate thing?
- The fifth apology: does the fifth official apology for the same wrong do anything at all, and is there such a thing as an apology that is *wrong to make*?
- Nobody left: a wrong done to a people with no surviving claimants.

Here's the half-formed bit I want you to either use or argue against: I suspect "apology" bundles at least four separable things — factual acknowledgement of what happened, taking ownership rather than merely noting it, repudiating the authority the earlier act was done under, and committing to repair. I want to know whether a good account should insist these come as a package, or should let a state do some without the others, and what each choice costs in the cases above.

Also: mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of one camp; tell me which pair you think probably can't be satisfied jointly (that's the part I care most about); and, very briefly, with no citations, say which single requirement knocks out the most popular accounts. I'll add the footnotes myself — please don't spend words on literature. And what would make an account a failure — e.g. one that implies every actual apology of the last few decades was either invalid or trivially easy to discharge?

Prose or a table, whatever's clearer. American English, please — it's for a US press. Thanks!
