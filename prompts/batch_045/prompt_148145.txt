I staff the policy office that supports the Northgate Housing and Labor Standards Board, a nine-member body created by a 2018 ordinance with authority over both minimum wages and residential rents within the city. The ordinance directs the Board, whenever it regulates a price, to weigh four things: affordability for current residents, displacement risk, housing production, and fiscal sustainability. It does not rank them, and the Board has repeatedly declined to rank them when asked.

The Board has now been asked to rule on a proposal, and I need to predict how it will come down given its own record rather than argue for what the best policy would be. Its record on price interventions is as follows.

1. In 2019 it approved, 6–3, an annual cap on rent increases for residential buildings first occupied before 2008, set initially at CPI plus three percentage points, while exempting all new construction and any unit first rented after 2018. Its stated ground was that sitting tenants face relocation costs and search frictions that landlords can price against, but that new supply must not be discouraged.

2. In 2019 it rejected, 5–4, full vacancy decontrol, which would have released a unit from regulation at each turnover, on the stated ground that the regulated stock erodes one turnover at a time. It approved instead a one-time turnover increment of five percentage points above the cap, applicable once per change of tenancy and then folded into the base rent.

3. In 2021 it approved, 7–2, a phase-in of the city minimum wage to a level equal to roughly 62 percent of the local median hourly wage over three years, with no tip credit and no youth wage, but with a twelve-month delay for employers with fewer than twenty employees. Its stated grounds were search frictions and concentrated local employment, and it cited evidence that reduced turnover partly offsets the employment effect at that level.

4. In 2022 it approved a higher sectoral floor for home-care and building-services workers whose wages are paid out of contracts reimbursed by public and institutional payers, and in the same session rejected extending that floor to platform delivery work, on the ground that earnings there are not hourly and that a floor would be defeated by deactivation.

5. In 2021 it rejected a proposal to index the rent cap strictly to CPI for five years and replaced its formula with a cost-based standard: documented increases in taxes, insurance, utilities, and capital work amortized over fifteen years, plus a two percent allowance, subject to a six percent annual ceiling and a floor of zero. Its stated ground was that a CPI cap with no cost pass-through can drive rents below operating cost and produce abandonment and deferred maintenance.

6. In 2020 it rejected a cap on ground-floor retail rents in the Central and Waterfront districts despite evidence of above-normal landlord returns there, on the ground that commercial tenants are repeat players with bargaining capacity and that the cap would chill new entry and leave storefronts dark.

7. In 2020 it rejected a universal twelve-month rent freeze during the public health emergency, and instead approved a means-tested emergency rental assistance program for households below 50 percent of area median income. Its stated ground was that a freeze benefits higher-income tenants in below-market units while imposing concentrated losses on small owners, and that the emergency was better handled with money than with price controls.

8. In 2022 it rejected a requirement of twenty-four months of relocation assistance for no-fault evictions and owner move-ins, approving four months plus a filing requirement instead, citing the risk of pushing small owners into insolvency and of over-regulating the decision to withdraw a unit from the market.

9. In 2023 it approved a per-unit fee on residential units kept vacant and off-market for more than ninety days, with revenue dedicated to the assistance program, and in the same year rejected 6–3 a proposal to cap rents only in buildings owned by landlords holding more than one hundred units. On the latter, the majority's stated ground was that bargaining power in renting is exercised at the level of a unit and a renewal notice, not at the level of a portfolio.

Proposal 24-07, which the Board takes up next month, is called Tenant-Anchored Stabilization, and it would do the following. Rent caps would attach to households rather than units: any household that has rented in the city for at least five of the last eight years and currently pays at or below the cap for its unit size could move to any other covered unit in the city and pay no more than its prior capped rent, with the receiving landlord receiving a quarterly credit from a new Stabilization Fund equal to the difference between that rent and the unit's registered market rent. Any unit vacated by a participating household would be immediately decontrolled, so that the landlord could charge market rent to the next tenant. The Fund would be financed by a four percent surcharge on short-term rental registration fees plus an annual per-unit assessment of roughly one hundred eighty dollars on buildings of fifty or more units. The cap itself would be frozen in nominal terms for five years and then reset to the thirty-fifth percentile of then-current market rents by unit size, and the Fund would sunset after ten years unless reauthorized. Owners of four or fewer units would be exempt as receiving landlords unless they opt in. The city actuary projects annual Fund outlays between forty-eight and one hundred forty million dollars depending on take-up, against projected revenues of seventy to ninety-five million; the chair remarked at the June hearing that the unit is the wrong thing to anchor a cap to, but said nothing about the rest of the design.

What I need from you is the following. Set out the two or three decision rules that fit every item in the record above, stated sharply enough that I could apply them to a new proposal myself, and say what each predicts about 24-07 both as a whole and clause by clause, including the small-landlord opt-in, the immediate decontrol of vacated units, the nominal freeze with a percentile reset, and the funding structure. Give me your best guess at the vote with your reasons, identify the closest analog among the precedents, and tell me honestly if the precedents underdetermine the answer: if they do, say what the Board would be committing itself to by voting either way, and which single further proposal or amendment it could rule on that would best separate the leading candidate rules, with each rule's prediction about it. Finally, tell me whether the Board's treatment of employer size in 2021 and of tipped and platform workers in 2022 transfers to the small-landlord exemption here, or whether that analogy is misleading and why. Keep the analysis at the level of rules, cases, and consistency; I need something I can put in front of the Board, not a survey of research or recommendations for reading.
