(2) In a long sequence of favorable bets whose winnings get reinvested, the policy that maximizes expected log wealth (the Kelly fraction) has exactly that property: with probability going to 1 the Kelly bettor is ahead of any other fixed-fraction bettor, and ahead by a factor going to infinity. This is just because the difference in growth rates compounds.

(3) Your financial life is one long sequence of reinvested bets. Every paycheck, every purchase you skip, every thing you put money into -- the money left at the end of one decision is the stake for the next one. There are no genuinely one-shot financial decisions.

(4) So: in every financial decision, maximize expected log wealth. Log utility isn't a taste you're allowed to have, it's forced on you. (He also says this is why the same math shows up in biology, with expected log number of offspring and bet-hedging -- whatever quantity is compounding, maximize the expected log of it.)

(5) Consequence: since log goes to minus infinity at zero, you must refuse any bet with any nonzero chance of losing everything, no matter how tiny that chance is and no matter how big the upside. A one-time offer that doubles your net worth with probability 1 - 10^-9 and wipes you out otherwise has to be turned down.

(6) Consequence: nothing in (1)-(4) mentions a horizon or a target, so you should be maximizing the growth rate of the pile forever, which means never spending on anything that doesn't itself compound. Consumption just permanently shrinks the base that everything else multiplies on.

(7) Consequence: by the parenthetical in (4), run it on anything that multiplies. Your chance of being alive at the end of the year multiplies across everything you do, and expected log of a product is the sum of the expected logs, and log(0) is minus infinity -- so any action with any nonzero chance of killing you is infinitely bad and you should never leave the house again.
