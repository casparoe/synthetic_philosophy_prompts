I'm a high school senior applying for a one-semester independent-study tutorial, and I need a proposal of about two pages for a paper on infinite ethics. I don't have a supervisor lined up yet, so the proposal has to make the case on its own that there is a real, tractable question here and that I can make progress on it in roughly fifteen weeks, part time, at around 5,000 words.

Here is my half-formed idea. Every position I've encountered about infinite futures — the one that says an endless good future cannot be made better or worse, the one that compares endless futures by their long-run density of value, the one that says agents can only ever affect finitely much, the one that ranks outcomes by how they compare across every bounded region — seems to share a starting point: well-being comes in units, those units add up, and the difficulty is therefore which rule to use when there are infinitely many of them. I want to question that starting point. Suppose the morally basic facts are comparisons between individual lives, and "total value" is only a bookkeeping device for summarizing those comparisons when the number of people is small. Then the puzzle cases of infinite ethics would be cases where the bookkeeping device breaks down, not cases where morality itself runs out.

My second idea, which I think is the sharper one: no actual agent ever faces an infinite quantity. I act on a finite stretch of the future, with finite evidence, using concepts I can apply in finite time; the infinite appears only when a theorist writes down the value of the whole world at once. So the question may not be how to aggregate infinitely many units of value, but what a moral theory looks like if every genuine decision problem is finite — and whether the infinite still causes trouble when it is reintroduced only as a limit of finite problems.

Please turn that into a proposal that: states one question sharply enough to have a determinate answer; explains why the main existing positions leave it open, without a literature review or citations, which I will add myself; lays out week by week what I would actually read and write, and why that method rather than the obvious one of surveying aggregation rules and picking a favorite; says what the possible conclusions are and what considerations would decide between them; and ends with an honest account of what would count as the project failing and which parts may be too big for fifteen weeks.

Finally, be clear throughout about which claims in this area nearly everyone working on it accepts and which are genuinely contested, and don't let my framing smuggle in a contested assumption as settled. Where either of my two ideas rests on a contested reading of the subject, say so, and tell me what I would have to argue to earn that framing.
