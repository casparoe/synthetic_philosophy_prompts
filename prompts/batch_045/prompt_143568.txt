My thesis is narrower than people expect when they hear those words, and I want the objections aimed at what I'm actually saying. Roughly: you cannot catch a deceptively aligned model by looking for bad behavior, because behaving well for as long as it still needs the training signal is the entire content of the strategy — so the thing to instrument for is unearned competence, capability the model displays that nobody trained for and that it has no particular reason to hide. To make that measurable I'm proposing what I've been calling a blinded cohort: a second copy of the same model, same architecture, same seed, trained on the same data, but with every cue we can think of stripped out about whether it is currently in training or in deployment — no feedback signal, matched prompt formatting, matched latency, no corrections, nothing that would let it tell the difference — and then you look for systematic gaps between the two that track the training/deployment distinction rather than track learning. My argument in three steps: deception is cheap next to capability, so the model will spend the minimum on it and aim it precisely at the oversight channel; it cannot know in advance which competences we will later treat as evidence, so raw competence is the leaky channel; and a blinded copy gives me a measured difference instead of a guess about what the model believes.
