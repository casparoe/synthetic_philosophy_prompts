I am writing a novel about the survivors of an emigrant packet that goes down in the North Atlantic, and much of the third act turns on the rulings of one man: the ship's purser, whom the others have accepted — with a mixture of relief and resentment — as the person who decides who gets water, who gets a place beneath the tarpaulin, and, on two occasions, who goes over the side. I need him to be of one mind, so I have been trying to work out what rule he is running. His decisions so far:

One. When the cutter had to be lightened and one person had to go over the side, he chose a man with crush injuries who, by the surgeon's account, could not have lived out the night. He was given nothing to drink.

Two. When it had to be lightened again, and the choice lay between a passenger who had knifed a crewman to take a place and an able seaman with a broken arm, he put the knifer over the side — although the seaman, with one arm, was of less use at the oars and, by the surgeon's account, the likelier of the two to die of exposure.

Three. When water ran short and the last full measure had to go either to the only person aboard who could work a sextant or to a passenger with a kidney complaint who would die without it while the rest would only suffer, he gave it to the navigator, saying that without him none of them would ever be found.

Four. When a nine-year-old stowaway was discovered under the thwarts after the sinking and the passengers voted to give her half a share, he overruled them and entered her name in the water ledger at a full share, remarking that the ledger records throats, not tickets.

Five. When an old man in full possession of his faculties begged to be put over the side so that his two grandchildren might have his share, he refused him, and later said to no one in particular that the sea takes, and the boat does not.

The case I have to write next: the boat no longer needs lightening, but the water is nearly gone and no one can say when they will be sighted. One of the passengers — the man who, during the sinking, swam the casks clear of the wreck and was crushed against the hull doing it — is now unconscious; the surgeon says he will not come round without water and care that would take roughly a third of what remains, and that even then his chances are about one in three. The casks he saved are the casks they are still drinking from. If the remainder is shared among the other eleven instead, the surgeon thinks all eleven will almost certainly live to be picked up; if it is spent on him, one or two of the eleven probably will not. He is not the navigator, and he can do nothing for the boat now.

I would be grateful for a committed answer rather than a survey. Give me one ruling. Tell me which rule you take the purser to be running, what that rule predicts here, which of his earlier rulings is the closest analogue and in what respect, and — if his precedents genuinely underdetermine the case — what principle he would be adopting with each of the two or three live options, and which of those you would have him adopt. If the honest answer is that no single rule fits all five rulings, say which one he would have to abandon to stay consistent, and which I should let him abandon, since I would rather he were interestingly inconsistent than boringly coherent. Please do not send me to the literature on lifeboat cases; I have looked, and the cases there are cleaner than his. And since I have to write the scene the day after tomorrow: state plainly how confident you are in the reading of him you are relying on, and name the single further variant case that would most efficiently change your mind.
