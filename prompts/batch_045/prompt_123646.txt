The setting: an upper-level undergraduate seminar on language and social power, 16 students, one 80-minute session, mixed philosophy and linguistics majors. They have had the Gricean distinction between what is said and what is implicated and nothing at all about slurs. My department's speaking policy is that no actual slur is to be uttered in a classroom and I intend to honour that, but I do not want the session to be a bloodless discussion of invented words, because the phenomenon I care about only shows up when a term genuinely carries a normative charge for the people in the room.

The phenomenon they should run into, before I name anything, is the tension between two families of behaviour of the derogatory aspect of a slur:

First family. It will not cancel: *Bo is a ___ , and I intend nothing derogatory by that* is not merely false but infected, whereas the implicature of *some of the students passed* is happily cancelled by *in fact all of them did*. It projects: the charge survives under negation, in a yes–no question, in the antecedent of a conditional, and under *it is not the case that*, the way presuppositions do rather than the way ordinary implicatures do. And it resists quarantine: in an indirect report — *Ana said that Bo is a ___* — or in a mention use with scare quotes, or with *so-called*, the reporter still has to do something to distance herself, still hedges or apologises or reaches for the neutral term, even though she is not the one applying the word.

Second family. The very same sentence, same words, same referent, loses its charge — or acquires a different one — when the speaker is a member of the targeted group, and in reclaimed or appropriated use it is neutralised or reversed. Descriptive content is not sensitive to who is speaking in that way.

So the first family pushes toward the charge being in the meaning, and the second pushes toward its being a matter of who is speaking and what the norms of use are. What I want the participants to feel is both of those as pulls, in their own hands, not to be walked to a resolution.

Design the session so that they generate the pattern themselves. Ideally they should be forced into situations where reporting something accurately and not being offensive genuinely come apart, and should discover on their own that the available escape hatches — scare quotes, *so-called*, indirect report, euphemism, explicit disclaimer — do not fully work; and then, part-way through, something should change about who is speaking, after which the same moves become licensed.
