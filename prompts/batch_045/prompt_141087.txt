I am a PhD student in environmental policy, and I have been asked to write the standing protocol on when to buy more information for a regional water authority whose technical advisory committee I sit on. Several times a year the committee must choose whether to commit to an expensive and largely irreversible intervention — a treatment plant upgrade, a levee setback, a buyout of irrigators' water rights — and, before each such choice, whether to fund monitoring (sampling, modelling, a pilot) to reduce the uncertainty first. Good's theorem settles the easy part: for an expected-utility maximiser, information that is free and that changes nothing but the agent's beliefs cannot reduce expected utility, so the live questions are only whether the information is worth its cost and whether it would actually change what the committee does. What I need from you is a procedure, not an explanation of the theorem.

Please give me an ordered checklist the committee's staff could run on a monitoring proposal: the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as an adequate answer to each, the points at which the procedure terminates in a recommendation not to fund, and the points at which it must hand the question back to the committee on the grounds that it is a matter of values rather than of fact. Three recurring situations matter to me because I do not think the standard result covers them, and I want the procedure to say what to do with each rather than to ignore them. First, some of our measurements are not purely informational: the act of monitoring partly forecloses an option or locks in a path — exploratory drilling that commits us to a site, a pilot installation that constituents will then treat as permanent, design work begun early — so that collect-then-choose is not in fact available and the value of the information cannot be assessed independently of the intervention that produces it. Second, the committee will not update as the theorem assumes: it anchors on the staff's point estimate and moves only part of the way toward what the data say, and several of its members are avowedly ambiguity-averse and dislike learning things that would force a choice between options they currently treat as incomparable, which suggests that a strictly Bayesian value-of-information calculation may be advising an agent that does not exist. Third, some pilots do not merely sharpen a parameter; they reveal that a mechanism nobody had modelled is at work, and so expand the space of hypotheses rather than narrowing it.

Then apply the procedure to two live proposals: funding two years of nutrient monitoring before choosing between upgrading the plant and paying farmers to retire irrigated land; and funding a pilot of a water-trading scheme that the legislature will roll out statewide whatever the pilot shows. I expect the procedure to recommend against the second, which will be an unwelcome conclusion for a committee that likes pilots, so I need that argument stated in a form that cannot be waved away as pedantry. Finally, say plainly what a procedure of this kind cannot settle — in particular how it should behave when the reliability of the proposed monitoring is itself the main thing in dispute, so that there is no agreed likelihood with which to compute anything.
