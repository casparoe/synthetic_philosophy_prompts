hey — I'm putting together a problem set for a second-year module, and I need a variant of Dennett's Where Am I? built to spec.

The thing I want the case to test: that "where am I?" isn't just a cute way of asking "what am I?" The idea is that once you can move (a) the living organism, (b) the brain, and (c) the surroundings your experience presents as here independently of each other, the location question starts carrying information the identity question doesn't. To show that, I need one case where three accounts of where a person is each return a definite answer, and the three answers are three different places.

Specs:

- Keep the bones of Dennett's setup — brain taken out of the skull, kept alive in a vat, running whatever it's hooked up to by radio — but set the whole thing somewhere ordinary: a hospital, a lab, a normal street. No spacecraft, no top-secret missions, no cosmic stuff.
- The three accounts: (1) the organism view — you're a living human animal, so you're where that animal is. (2) the brain view — you're where your brain is, since that's the thing your consciousness depends on. (3) the experienced-here view — you're where your stream of experience presents as the place you're in, i.e. the place that shows up as here when you look around.
- Each one has to give a single determinate answer, and all three have to differ. None of them gets to say both places, or nowhere, or depends what you mean by where, pick a sense and I'll answer it. The disagreement has to fall out of the views given the facts of the case, not be written into the case: don't just tell me "the organism view says Halifax," show that the view delivers Halifax from what's in the scenario.
- No fission, no duplication, no uploading, no teleporting. There's exactly one candidate person throughout. The only bit of kit you're allowed to add on top of Dennett's own story is that the radio link can go to a machine surrogate — cameras, mics, wheels, hands — instead of to the original body.
- Don't stack the deck. The organism view shouldn't lose because the body is a corpse or a brain-dead husk, the brain view shouldn't lose because the brain is damaged or scrambled, and the experience view shouldn't lose because the subject is hallucinating or being fed a made-up world. Whatever pulls the three apart should be an ordinary working arrangement, not a malfunction or a death.
- One paragraph for the scenario itself. Assume the reader knows the Dennett story and nothing else about personal identity, so don't lean on machinery from the literature.

Then: for each of the three accounts, two or three sentences on what it answers and why. Then name the single feature of the case that's doing the work of pulling the three apart, and show what happens when you remove it — I want to watch it collapse back to a two-way disagreement, or stop being a puzzle at all. Then give me the strongest thing a defender of whichever view comes out of this looking worst could say against the case, and say whether that complaint is really about my scenario or about their own view. Finally, if you think one of the three handles it best, say which and why — just don't let that turn into "so the question was ambiguous after all," since that's the one move I've ruled out.
