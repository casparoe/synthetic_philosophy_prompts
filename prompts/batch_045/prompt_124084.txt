I work as a historical consultant on feature films and prestige television — a job that consists mostly of writing memos explaining why the cigarettes are wrong, or that the letter could not possibly have arrived that quickly. Over the better part of a decade of it I have become preoccupied with something my memos never touch, and I should like to know whether it has a name.

It goes like this. A production invents something: a scene that never happened, a friendship that never existed, a line never spoken. Everyone involved knows it is an invention, and frequently it was made precisely because it condenses something real — a structural truth about the person or the period that could not be dramatised literally. Audiences are then told, in press notes, in interviews, sometimes in an on-screen caption, that this element is invented. And it makes no difference. People come out of the film knowing the scene is fiction and go on picturing the person in it whenever they think of them; the invented scene becomes the image that arrives unbidden, and it becomes the standard against which later judgements of that person are measured — their courage, their cruelty, their sincerity. The true version, being thinner and harder to picture, does not compete. I have watched this happen with a wartime rescue invented to stand in for a more bureaucratic rescue that actually occurred, and with a deathbed reconciliation written for two people who never met. In both cases people who had taken the correction in still said, in effect, yes, but that is how it really was — where "really" is plainly not doing factual work.

I am not after a label; I want the carving. The nearest things I know of do not fit. I have come across the phrase "prosthetic memory" for memories of events one did not live through, acquired from films, but as I understand it that describes an addition to one's stock of memories and is neutral to approving about the acquisition, whereas what I am describing is a replacement, and one that I take to be a kind of loss. There is a large psychological literature on misinformation — on people's continuing to lean on a claim after it has been retracted, and on imagining an event making it seem more likely that it happened — but in my cases the belief is not corrupted at all: people will tell you cheerfully that the scene was invented and go on being governed by it. And "dramatic licence" is the trade's name for the practice, not for the effect.

So: is there an established concept for this, and if there is not, will you name and define it in a way that separates it from those neighbours and from ordinary falsehood about the past? I want its edges in both directions — what it excludes, and what your definition would wrongly sweep in. In particular I want to know whether it matters that the audience is not deceived; what to say about the case where the real person did the mythologising first, in memoirs or in self-presentation, so that the film transmits rather than substitutes; whether a composite character, standing in for several real people, is the same phenomenon or a different one; whether an avowedly fictional novel about named real people does it too; and whether the effect depends on the audiovisual medium at all, or whether a vivid anecdote in a popular history does the same work. If, on inspection, I have run three different things together, say so, and tell me which of them survives.

One further thing, since it is what actually worries me. The complaint people make in these cases is that the real dead have been wronged — displaced by a fiction wearing their name. I want to know whether that wrong is best understood as misrepresentation, or as something nearer usurpation, or as neither; whether the concept you settle on helps determine what correction is owed and to whom; and whether, when the image that has taken hold is a flattering one, there is anything left to complain about at all.

Please answer in continuous prose — no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables — in the manner of a considered letter of roughly a thousand words. I have no use for a reading list or a set of references; the carving is the thing.
