Hey — I write epic fantasy, hard magic system, three books out of a planned five, and I keep a series bible my editor pretends to have read. I've been poking at the fight in philosophy of science over laws of nature — Humeans vs. anti-Humeans — and I sort of get it and sort of don't. So: translate the whole dispute into writer talk for me, as faithfully as you can, and then tell me what didn't make it across.

Here's the vocabulary I actually have: canon vs. continuity error; the series bible vs. what actually made it onto the page; hard magic (rules the reader can learn and predict from) vs. soft magic (mysterious, atmospheric, never spelled out); retcons; "would that work in this world?", which is a question I ask about basically every scene; the outline I wrote before the book vs. the book itself; deus ex machina as the thing that gets you savaged in reviews; the Chosen One exemption, where one character gets to break a rule everyone else obeys; and reader trust, which is the thing I'm really protecting when I refuse to let a character do something cool.

So map both sides onto that. My guess is the Humean side comes out as something like: the rules of my magic system are nothing over and above the tidiest, strongest summary of what happens in the published books, and there's no further fact about whether a character "could" do the forbidden thing beyond what's on the page and what the best summary implies — which sounds like it means my series bible has zero authority and book-one-me has no say over book-five-me, which is uncomfortable. And the anti-Humean side sounds like: the rules are real constraints in the world that the events answer to, so a scene breaking one isn't just strange, it's impossible-in-this-world. But do the mapping properly rather than gesturing at it: what plays the role of the laws, what plays the role of the particular events, what plays the role of simplicity and strength in the Best System story, what plays the role of a miracle, and what plays the role of the counterfactuals I'm always asking ("what would have happened if she'd tried to...").

Then the part I actually want, the residue report:

1. What translated cleanly, with no squeezing.
2. What you had to distort to fit my vocabulary — and flag it when you're smuggling, rather than translating. One trap in particular: I have an obvious advantage physicists and philosophers lack, which is that I'm outside the world and I chose the rules. Don't let that become "so the Author of the universe is just the anti-Humean answer," because I know where that goes and it's not what I'm asking. Instead: what happens to each side of the dispute if you remove the author from the picture and keep only the writing vocabulary? Does one survive intact and the other collapse into it? And separately, is there stuff about my author-side situation the philosophy has no words for at all — that I can retcon, that the laws can change between books, that I can grant one person an exemption, that a rule can be genuinely true in the world and also be something I'm entirely free to revise?
3. What can't be said in writer talk at all, even approximately. And for each bit of residue, give me your honest read: is it a defect of the analogy (writer talk is about my choices and my readers, not about the world), or is it a sign that the philosophers' question was less sharp than it sounds? Specifically — is "could this happen in this world?" really the same question they're asking, or is mine a question about craft and reader trust that only wears the same clothes as theirs?

Last thing, and it's why I'm asking. In book four I want a character to do something nobody has done anywhere in the published books, but that nothing in the bible actually forbids. Does either position in this debate have anything to say about whether I've broken my own world — or is that precisely the case where both sides go quiet? And if they do go quiet, is that because the question is genuinely undetermined in my world, or because I've dressed up a decision about what my readers will swallow as a metaphysical one?

Tell me where I've got the philosophy wrong, too — I've definately got some of it wrong. Keep it concrete and skip citations; I'm not going to look anything up.
