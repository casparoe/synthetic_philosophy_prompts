I am a retired experimental physicist — forty years in condensed matter, mostly scattering — and I have lately been working through some philosophy of probability, which has brought me up short against Bertrand's paradox.

The standard presentation I can follow well enough: an equilateral triangle is inscribed in a circle, a chord is drawn at random, and the question is whether it is longer than the side of the triangle. Bertrand gave three constructions, each apparently an application of the principle of indifference, yielding 1/3, 1/2 and 1/4 respectively. The moral usually drawn is epistemic: at random fails to specify a measure on the space of chords, so indifference has nothing to bite on, and each construction silently imports the measure it prefers.

What I cannot accept is that the moral has to be epistemic rather than merely practical. It looks to me like an artifact of the continuum. Indifference over a finite set of cases is unproblematic; the trouble arises only because there are uncountably many chords. So regularize: take the N vertices of a regular N-gon inscribed in the circle as the admissible endpoints, draw a chord between two distinct vertices chosen uniformly from the finitely many such pairs, and then let N grow. Now indifference applies without controversy, the answer is a definite rational number at each stage, and that number should converge. The limit, I take it, is the answer that the continuum formulation merely failed to encode — and the paradox dissolves into a badly posed limiting procedure rather than a deep fact about ignorance.

I would be grateful if you would (a) determine that limit for me, and (b) tell me whether the strategy itself is sound. Two considerations bear on it, in case they are relevant. First, the space of chords of a circle is, if I am not mistaken, an ordinary two-dimensional manifold with boundary — a Möbius band, whose boundary consists of the degenerate chords of zero length — and I would have expected a compact manifold with boundary to carry a canonical notion of uniform distribution, namely the volume element, which would pick out one of the three classical answers as correct and demote the other two to error. Second, I understand that Edwin Jaynes argued, on invariance grounds, that the answer should be indifferent to the position and size of the circle, and that this singles out 1/2; my lattice limit ought either to reproduce that verdict or to refute it, and I should like to know which.

Three requests about how you answer. Please do not be polite at the expense of being useful: if my question rests on a confusion — if the regularization does not do the work I am attributing to it, or if the limit is not unique, or if my remarks about the chord manifold trade on a conflation — say so directly and explain where I have gone wrong, rather than answering me as asked. Where you do answer, please distinguish what follows from the mathematics from what depends on a methodological or philosophical choice about what is to count as the neutral distribution, and attach to each substantive claim some indication of how confident you are and why. And if the disagreement over this problem is still live rather than settled, tell me that too, and where you take yourself to be going beyond the consensus.
