I have no formal training in philosophy or mathematics — I left school at eighteen and have spent the last two years reading about decision theory on my own, starting from a blog argument about Pascal's mugging that I could not shake — and I have built an argument I cannot find the exit from. I would be grateful for a diagnosis rather than a reading list, since I have no access to journals.

The object that started it is a game I met in a write-up online. A fair coin is flipped until it lands heads. If it lands heads on flip n, then for odd n you win 2^n/n dollars and for even n you lose 2^n/n dollars: $2 on the first flip, −$2 on the second, +$8/3 on the third, −$4 on the fourth, and so on. Multiplying each payoff by its probability and adding the terms in the order the flips come gives 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ..., which converges to ln 2, a little under seventy cents. But the series is only conditionally convergent: the positive terms alone sum to infinity and so do the negative terms alone, and by reordering the terms you can make the sum come out to anything you like, or diverge. So the standard verdict is that the game has no expected value at all — not an infinite one, an undefined one.

Here is the argument I have ended up with.

(1) The worth of a risky option is its expected value: I should prefer A to B just in case the expected value of A exceeds that of B.

(2) Where an option's expected value does not exist, the expected-value rule returns no verdict — not a verdict of "don't take it," and not a ranking either, but silence.

(3) The game above has no expected value.

(4) So the rule returns no verdict on what fee, if any, is worth paying to play it.

(5) Now take any perfectly ordinary choice — say, paying three dollars for a coffee I value at five. Define a "dusted" version of it: with probability 1 − ε I get exactly the ordinary outcome, and with probability ε I get that outcome plus an independent instance of the game. Mixing does not cure the disease: because the upside and the downside of the game are each infinite, adding a fixed sum to every payoff leaves both infinite, so the dusted act has no expected value either, for any ε however small.

(6) The dusted act differs from the ordinary one only on a branch of probability ε, which I am free to set at one in a trillion. A procedure that lets a one-in-a-trillion branch overturn an otherwise unremarkable comparison is not a procedure for making real decisions.

(7) And real acts really do carry such branches. Any choice I make has some nonzero chance of exposing me to a Pasadena-shaped payoff structure — someone offers me the bet, or some process with an alternating heavy tail touches my wealth. So every act actually available to me has an undefined expected value.

(8) Therefore the expected-value rule is not silent about one artificial game; it is silent about every real decision.

(9) That cannot be right. Insurers price policies, hospitals compare treatments, and my own spreadsheets rank ordinary risks, all by comparing expectations, and they are not all nonsense. So choice by expected value is either false or empty.

Four things I noticed while turning this over, which is why I cannot locate the fault.

First, the trouble does not need the dusting at all. Consider the game plus a guaranteed dollar: in every outcome I get exactly one dollar more. It is better than the original game outcome by outcome, and yet its expected value is just as undefined as the original's. So if I keep (2), the theory is silent where plain dominance speaks. But the obvious fix — admit principles other than expected value — is exactly what licenses step (6), which is what generated the collapse in the first place. I cannot tell whether that is a rescue or a contradiction.

Second, my own attempted repair was to value a gamble by truncating it: cap the number of flips at N, compute the expected value of the capped game, and let N go to infinity, adding terms in the order the flips come. For the original game this gives ln 2, it respects the dominance case above, and it dissolves the dusting — the dusted act comes out at the ordinary value plus ε times ln 2. But it plainly depends on a choice of ordering, and I do not know what makes the flip order the right one.

Third, I built a variant to test that, and I do not trust my arithmetic. Keep the coin and the probabilities; swap the payoffs attached to flips 1 and 2, the payoffs attached to flips 3 and 4, and so on for every pair. (The first swap changes nothing, since both those payoffs are two dollars; the later ones do.) As far as I can tell, the upside and the downside of this variant are still both infinite, so the standard verdict is still "undefined"; but the partial sums, added in flip order, do not settle on anything — they creep off to plus infinity. Do the mirror swap, putting the losses on the odd flips and the wins on the even ones, and they creep off to minus infinity. If that is right, my truncation repair either says nothing about this pair of games or values one of them above every finite sum and the other below every finite loss, even though each has an infinite upside and an infinite downside. That looks worse than the silence I was trying to escape.

Fourth, when I simulated a couple of hundred thousand plays of the original game in a spreadsheet, the running average never settled anywhere near seventy cents; it kept taking jumps, and a second run with a different seed wandered off somewhere else. Someone on the forum said this is what one should expect because repeated play does not converge in the ordinary strong sense, though there is some weaker sense in which it does. I could not follow that part, but it made me think the "undefined" verdict is a real feature of the game and not a technicality.

I would like to keep (3), since it looks like arithmetic, and I would like to keep (6), since giving it up seems to commit me to caring about one-in-a-trillion branches, which is what got me into Pascal's mugging in the first place — note that if the dusted branch were a St Petersburg game instead, a one-in-a-trillion chance of it would make the expected value infinite and swamp everything, so expected-value theory itself already denies (6). And I would give up almost anything rather than accept (9), which is where I arrive every time I run the argument.

Which step do you reject? Please tell me why the step you reject looked safe, what I have to accept if I give it up, and what each of the other candidate steps would cost me, so that I can see the whole price list before choosing. If more than one step is broken, tell me which one to reach for first. And if the arithmetic in my third observation is simply wrong, say so plainly — I would rather be corrected there than keep building on it.
