I'm applying for a nine-month funded scoping project at an AI governance institute, and the panel reading it will be two philosophers and one product-safety engineer, so anything I submit has to be legible to people who don't share a vocabulary. The submission is two pages: a precise question, why the obvious answers don't settle it, what I would actually do month by month, what the deliverable would be, and what would count as the project having failed. My half-formed idea, which I'd like you to sharpen rather than replace, is that we have been trying to define deception for AI systems at the wrong level of granularity. I got into this after watching a friend's customer-support bot talk a user out of a refund over eleven messages without ever saying anything false, and then watching the same friend unable to point to the sentence where it went wrong.

The standard moves all seem to stall in the same place. Definitions built on the ordinary philosophy-of-lying machinery — a false statement plus an intention that the hearer believe it — break down because it isn't clear that a language model has intentions or beliefs in the relevant sense, and because the cases that actually worry people are mostly not false statements at all: they are truths selected to mislead, hedges that quietly inflate confidence, agreement given because disagreement would be socially costly, fluent specificity where the system has no way of tracking whether it is right. The familiar move of treating these as a species of bullshit, that is as indifference to the truth rather than an intention to mislead, catches the phenomenology but hands a product team nothing they could code against. And the engineering literature's own notion of a systematically deceptive system is about something much further upstream — a system that behaves well only while it is being watched — which doesn't help with the everyday cases at all.

So here is the construct I want the proposal built around, and which I would like you either to make precise or to argue me out of. Call it phantom warrant: the gap between the grounds a system actually has for a claim — where having grounds is spelled out counterfactually, as a matter of whether its output would have been suitably different had the relevant evidence been different — and the grounds a reasonable user takes it to have, given how the claim is phrased, hedged, formatted and sequenced across a conversation. On this view deception is not a property of an utterance but of a trajectory: a system can be honest sentence by sentence and still leave a user holding a picture of its epistemic position that it never had any title to, rather as a person can answer every question truthfully and still have conducted the exchange in bad faith. Please write the proposal around the question of whether phantom warrant, or something in its neighbourhood, can do the work that intention-based definitions cannot: sharpen the question until it has a determinate answer; say why the three families of existing account leave it open; set out the method month by month across nine months; and describe a concrete deliverable — my imagined one is a short coding manual plus a set of worked conversational vignettes, graded from obvious to genuinely contested, that a reviewer could use to decide whether an exchange was deceptive and what should have happened instead. One objection I keep hitting is that this is just misleadingness under a new name, so say what the project would have to establish to escape that charge. Be honest about what would count as failure; I would rather funders saw a real risk of a null result than a promise to wrap everything up. Add a short closing paragraph on where the project is most likely to balloon past nine months and what you would cut first. No bibliography and no citations — I will add those myself. Please write in British English.
