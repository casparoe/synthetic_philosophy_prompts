Setup: Szasz died in 2012, so he never saw any of this. Imagine you get to show him a thing that plausibly exists now: a phone app that watches a person continuously — typing speed and error rate, sleep regularity, how many people they call or text, changes in the timing and intonation of their voice — and compares that stream both to the person's own baseline and to a large population model. When the numbers drift it flags "early warning signs of relapse" for people who had a psychiatric diagnosis years earlier, and some clinics act on the flag: a phone call, maybe a dose increase.

The case: Dan, 34. One psychotic episode at 22, recovered fine, off meds for years, night-shift warehouse supervisor, no complaints about anything. The app flags him. Dan says he feels fine and tells the clinic to stop calling. The clinician writes down that Dan lacks insight, and treats that as confirmation the flag was right.

Two terms I want you to actually use (they're mine, not his):
- prospective diagnosis: nobody claims the disorder is present. The claim is that it's arriving, and treatment now is justified by a deviation that hasn't happened yet.
- the no-exit structure: inside the system, the only move available for denying the diagnosis is itself scored as a symptom of it, so no possible behaviour of Dan's could count as evidence that the flag was wrong.

Here's where I want you to get into the weeds. Szasz ran roughly two arguments, and this case pries them apart. Argument A is the lesion argument: disease is something that happens to the body, demonstrable independently of the patient's own values and of whoever happens to be in charge; "mental illness" is just disapproved behaviour wearing a medical costume. Argument B is the political one: the real content here is a moral and institutional relation — a person who hasn't asked for anything gets classified and managed, with the state behind it, and medical vocabulary launders that.

So: how would Szasz read Dan's case? Does the app finally hand him the thing he said was missing, a bodily, measurable, value-independent signal? Or does keystroke timing and vocal prosody fail his test for exactly the same reason mood and speech did — and notice that Dan's typing is something Dan *does*, not something that happens to his body. If A and B point in different directions here, which one is the real Szasz, and what does he have to give up either way?
