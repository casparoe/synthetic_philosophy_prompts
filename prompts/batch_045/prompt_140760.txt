I have just come out of a long and rather inconclusive argument with a friend about vagueness, and I would like an accuracy audit of the passage she was reasoning from, because I suspect it is doing more work than it should. The disagreement started over a practical case — whether insects ought to be treated as sentient, and whether anything follows from the fact that 'sentient' is a vague predicate — after which she sent me a pop-philosophy blog post on the sorites paradox and said it settled the matter. My paraphrase of the relevant passage runs roughly like this: vagueness is not a feature of the world but a feature of our language, and the puzzle is entirely about what to say about sentences such as 'Jack is bald' when Jack is a borderline case; epistemicists hold that classical logic and bivalence are non-negotiable, so that there is a fact of the matter as to exactly how many hairs a man must have before 'bald' stops applying, and that boundary is fixed with perfect precision by our usage of the word — we simply cannot find out where it lies, because our evidence about the fine detail of our own linguistic behaviour is far too thin, so vagueness turns out to be a species of ignorance rather than a feature of meaning; supervaluationists regard this as crazy, holding instead that there are many equally acceptable ways of making 'bald' precise and no fact of the matter as to which of them is the correct one, that a sentence is true when it comes out true on every acceptable precisification, false when it comes out false on every one, and otherwise has a third, intermediate status, 'indeterminate', which is why classical logic, and in particular the law of excluded middle, breaks down at the borderline; the payoff is that supervaluationism vindicates our ordinary tolerance intuition — the sense that removing a single hair cannot turn a man who is not bald into a man who is — whereas the epistemicist must simply bite the bullet and say that somewhere a single hair makes all the difference; this is why supervaluationism is the more popular view, although in the end the two theories are arguing about the same thing, since both accept that a sharp boundary of some sort exists and differ only over how to describe borderline cases, so that the choice between them is largely a matter of which description one finds less awkward and nothing much hangs on it.

My friend then added an argument of her own on top of this: if the supervaluationist locates the indeterminacy in the choice between admissible precisifications, then either the set of admissible precisifications is itself sharply bounded — in which case the sharp cutoff has merely been relocated one level up, and supervaluationism collapses into a more roundabout epistemicism — or its bounds are themselves vague, in which case one needs precisifications of precisifications and the view never gets off the ground. She took this to show that the closing sentence of the post is right and the whole dispute is terminological. I am inclined to think that closing claim is too glib: surely it matters whether borderline sentences are bivalent, and whether the inferences we ordinarily make with vague terms come out valid.
