I've spent a couple of years in a reading group on decision theory, and I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict, because the central argument seems not to have moved since the 1970s and I no longer expect it to be won.

Here is the specific version I care about. Take a large world: a finite population of agents, indexed 1 through N, each facing a one-shot binary choice between cooperate and defect against a partner drawn independently of anything the agent does. Cooperating costs the cooperator one unit and confers three on the recipient; defecting costs nothing and confers nothing. Read causally, defecting weakly dominates. What the large-world proposals add is that the outputs of any two agents' decision procedures are correlated to a degree given by a symmetric similarity kernel w_ij in [0,1], with w_ii = 1, decay under whatever distance the kernel is defined over, a long tail of partial similarities, and — in a world large enough — a non-trivial population of near-copies. The evidential rule I have in mind then says: choose the act that maximizes the similarity-weighted sum over j of w_ij times the utility of j's outcome, possibly after normalizing the weights and possibly after truncating them at some similarity threshold.

When I tried to make that precise I kept hitting the same thing, which I've started calling the altruism collapse. If the kernel has a sufficiently heavy tail, the unnormalized sum is dominated by the far tail and the recommendation reverts to defection, so normalization or truncation is forced. But once the weights are normalized they stop functioning as a measure of how much evidence j's choice carries about mine and start functioning as welfare weights, that is, as how much I care about j. At that point evidential cooperation looks like it has quietly become partial altruism toward one's near-copies with an acausal justification bolted on. The only way I can see to resist that reading is to insist that the instances weighted by the kernel are not other agents at all but other instances of me, so that the weighted sum is still my own expected utility — which seems to move the whole dispute into a theory of personal identity across instances rather than into decision theory.

Meanwhile the older dispute looks stalled in a different way: one side invokes causal dominance and the fact that nothing I do now changes what is already in the box; the other invokes the correlation and the fact that agents who act as it recommends end up better off; each side has a rehearsed reply; nobody moves.

My hypothesis is that this is two disputes run under one heading. On the classic cases I suspect the disagreement is about the point of the enterprise: whether a decision theory is an advisory discipline that takes the agent's situation as fixed and asks what to do now, or an evaluative discipline that ranks the decision procedures an agent might implement. On the large-world cases I suspect it becomes substantive but empirical: how much acausal correlation the actual world contains, which looks like a question for cosmology rather than philosophy. If so, the debate dissolves rather than resolving — one part deflated as a clash of questions, the other handed off to physics.

Is that right? Is the advisory/evaluative distinction doing the work I think it is, or is there a genuine residual normative disagreement about what counts as a reason for acting? Is the empirical turn real, or does relocating the dispute into the choice of a similarity kernel merely relocate the same disagreement into a choice of formalism — correlation between decision procedures may be a physical fact, but similarity over what space (source code, counterfactual outputs, behavioral dispositions, robustness under self-modification) looks conventional or at least theory-laden? And is the altruism collapse a real problem, or does it turn on my conflating weighting another agent's payoff in my objective with conditioning on another agent's act as evidence about mine? Tell me plainly if any of this is confused, if the question of how much correlation the world contains is not even well posed, or if the task as I have framed it cannot be done well; and mark which of your claims you are confident in and which you are guessing at. Please don't give me a literature review or tell me what to read — I have the reading group for that. Answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no preamble and no concluding summary.
