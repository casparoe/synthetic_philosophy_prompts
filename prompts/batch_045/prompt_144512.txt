Please act as the instructor for an advanced undergraduate seminar in contemporary political philosophy — you are a specialist in liberal egalitarianism, with Rawls and his critics as your main area — and audit the position below before I turn it in as my final paper. Before you begin the audit, spend a paragraph or two restating each of my commitments in its strongest form and noting what normally motivates it; I want you to work on the strongest version of each, not the sloppiest. I'm not asking for citations or further reading — I want your own assessment of whether this package hangs together.

Here is where I've landed on distributive justice. I hold:

(a) The right standard for the basic structure is the difference part of the second principle: social and economic inequalities are permissible only when they work to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged. I identify the least advantaged by an index of social primary goods — income and wealth, the powers and prerogatives of office, and the social bases of self-respect — and I don't want to run the index in terms of welfare, and I don't want to run it in terms of Dworkinian-style resources either.

(b) I read to the greatest benefit as a maximizing requirement, not merely as the weaker constraint that each inequality has to improve the worst-off's position relative to equality. A classmate says that with (b) my difference principle just collapses into maximin, so that (c) is redundant. I'm fairly sure that's wrong, but I can't say why.

(c) Maximin really is the correct choice rule for the parties in the original position, for the usual reasons: no reliable probability estimates behind the veil, a special concern for the worst outcome, and the strains of commitment.

(d) I reject luck egalitarianism. I don't think the point of distributive justice is to neutralize the effects of brute luck, and I don't think someone who is unlucky in talents, looks, or temperament is owed compensation as such.

(e) What makes the whole package attractive to me is the arbitrariness claim: nobody deserves their natural endowments or the social position they start from, so nobody has a pre-institutional claim to a larger share on that basis.

(f) I'm a statist about scope. The difference principle governs a self-contained scheme of social cooperation — a political society — and there is no global difference principle; what we owe people in other societies is governed by something thinner.

(g) But I also think nationality is morally arbitrary in the same way talent is, and that coercively excluding would-be immigrants is a serious injustice which they weren't born here does not come close to justifying.

(h) Methodologically I'm committed to wide reflective equilibrium: no fixed points, and considered judgments about particular cases are revisable in light of principle.

Two case verdicts, both on societies I've invented for the paper:

(i) Meridia assigns every sixteen-year-old, by public lottery, to a lifetime trade, and exit is impossible. The harshest trade is short-shifted, very highly paid, and publicly honored, and as a result the worst-off group in Meridia is better off than the least-advantaged group in any feasible alternative — and better off on the full index in (a), self-respect included, not just in income. I've concluded that my principle in (a) has to permit Meridia, and I bite that bullet even though the verdict revolts me.

(j) In Vantor, about four percent of children are born with a condition that leaves them unable to fill any social role and requiring continuous paid care. I think how Vantor treats this group is the first test of whether its basic structure is just, and I think the difference principle settles the question if you just apply it properly: this group is the worst off, so the arrangement must be set to their greatest advantage.
