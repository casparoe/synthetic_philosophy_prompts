Suppose you are advising a small technical research group — six to eight mathematically trained researchers, no empirical machine learning, about eighteen months — charged with making progress on the Löbian obstacle: the difficulty a reasoner faces in trusting a successor, or a later version of itself, when it cannot prove its own soundness.

Which open questions should they prioritize, and in what order? I want a dependency-aware agenda rather than a survey: which questions are upstream of which, which are tractable in eighteen months using logic and decision theory rather than experiment, which are neglected relative to their leverage, and what result or observation over the next year ought to move an item up or down the list.

One thesis I want arbitrated. A colleague argues that the obstacle is computational rather than logical: once the agent is given a bounded proof-length budget, the question becomes whether verifying a successor's certificate is cheaper than finding the proof oneself, so the Löbian difficulty is a special case of a verification-versus-discovery gap, and research on self-trust should be folded into research on bounded and probabilistic proof systems. If that is right, several standard items look misaimed; if it is wrong, say exactly where the reduction breaks.

Also say which questions you would drop as not worth this group's time, and why — in particular whether the distinction between trusting a later copy of your own code and trusting a deliberately modified successor matters for the ordering, and whether progress on any of this would in fact change how anyone builds a self-modifying system.
