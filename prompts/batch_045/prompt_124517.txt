I've been reading about Maxwell's demon on and off since the early Less Wrong days, and my understanding has stalled at the level of one-line answers: the demon acquires information, the demon must erase its memory, Landauer's bound of kT ln 2 per bit, the Szilard engine pays for itself, information is physical, entropy is a property of the map rather than the territory. None of those is wrong, but I want to know which one is actually load-bearing. So I'd like a ranking rather than a tutorial: no history of the debate, no citations, no tour of the literature — just your allocation of credit and the reasoning that separates the tiers.

Take as the baseline case the textbook Szilard setup: one molecule, a one-bit memory, kT ln 2 of work available per cycle, and the demon appearing to get it for free. Here are five candidate places where the bill might be paid:

1. Acquisition. Measurement itself is thermodynamically expensive: to see which side the molecule is on you need a signal distinguishable from the thermal background, and that costs more than the cycle returns.
2. Erasure. Measurement can in principle be done reversibly and for free; the dissipation is deferred to resetting the memory, at kT ln 2 per bit, and that is where the second law is saved.
3. Bookkeeping. The demon never breaks the second law at any stage; the law simply has to be stated over the joint system of gas, memory, and their correlations. On the modern feedback-control version of the bound, extractable work can exceed the usual free-energy limit in proportion to the mutual information gained, so the information behaves as a consumable resource rather than a line item billed at a particular step.
4. Substrate. Any controller is a physical system with a finite state space, a clock, and actuators. The demon's own hardware dissipates, runs down, or dies, and the flapper-valve and ratchet-and-pawl failures are the same moral in a simpler setting.
5. Map, not territory. There is no bill to localize, because the paradox was an artifact of attributing entropy to the gas itself. What the demon has is not a quantity of negentropy but a relation between its information and the world, and the appearance of a violation comes from misdescribing whose uncertainty is in question.

Allocate 100 points across these by how much of the real explanatory work each does, in a way that sums to 100, assign each a tier (S through F), and give one sentence of justification per entry. Steelman the two entries you rank lowest before you put them there. If you think two of the five are secretly the same move stated differently, merge them and reallocate. If the genuinely load-bearing move is not on my list, add it as a sixth entry and still make the total come to 100.

Then stress-test the allocation against three variants of the baseline:

B. No erasure inside. An external factory feeds the demon an endless tape of pre-zeroed bits, and spent bits are shipped out of the box unread. Erasure happens, but not to the demon and not to anything inside the box.
C. No measurement. The demon prepared the initial state itself and already knows which side the molecule is on; it gains no new information during the cycle and yet still seems to collect kT ln 2.
D. Fully coherent controller. The demon is a reversible quantum computer: it records the molecule's location as a coherent correlation, never collapses anything, never erases, and uncomputes its record at the end of the cycle.

For each variant, say which candidates drop to near-zero credit in that world, and say why the survivors survive. Finally, name the single variant you would keep as the cleanest test of whether an account is doing real work, and state, as a single number, how much of the total burden you would give to the claim that the demon's information is a finite resource that gets used up.
