I'm revising a paper on epiphenomenalism and first-person authority, and before I commit to the package in print I want an audit of whether the things I believe actually hang together. Here is the view as I currently hold it:

(1) Property dualism with an epiphenomenal clause: phenomenal properties are instantiated by certain neural states, are not identical with any physical or functional property, and do no causal work. My reason is the familiar exclusion thought — the physical account of the causation of any bodily movement is already complete, so there is no causal role left over for the phenomenal character of the states involved.

(2) Introspective phenomenal judgments — my present thought that there is a throbbing in my left temple — are genuine contentful beliefs, formed non-inferentially, and typically knowledge. I do not explain this by saying the experience causes the judgment; on my view nothing phenomenal causes anything. I explain it with what I call the covariance patch: the neural state that instantiates the experience also causes the judgment, and a psychophysical law links that neural state to that experience, so the judgment nomically covaries with the very experience it is about.

(3) Knowledge is safe true belief and nothing more. No causal relation, and no explanatory because-of relation, is required between the fact known and the belief; nomic covariance across nearby cases suffices. This is why I take (1) and (2) not to conflict.

(4) Phenomenal concepts are acquaintance-based: the experience itself partly constitutes the content of the judgment about it, and the experience is present to the subject in a way that is not exhausted by any representational or functional role. I rely on this elsewhere to explain why phenomenal concepts are conceptually independent of physical and functional vocabulary, and hence why the explanatory gap does not close.

(5) Content is causally relevant. My saying that my knee hurts is explained by what I believe, and not merely by the neural vehicle that carries the belief; beliefs do what they do in virtue of what they are about.

(6) The badness of suffering is constituted by its phenomenal character, and its moral relevance does not depend on its causal efficacy.

Two further case verdicts, both load-bearing in the paper:

(7) In the inversion case — a counterpart of mine, physically identical to me, whose colour experiences are inverted relative to mine, with no difference in functional role and none in behaviour — my introspective knowledge of my own current experience is unimpaired, and my judgments and utterances would be type-identical to my counterpart's.

(8) In the silent case — a being physically identical to me in whom the neural states that normally instantiate pain occur with no experience at all — the being's judgment that it is in pain is not knowledge. I take this to be a real possibility, not a façon de parler, and I also want to say that the difference between that being and me is not one that evidence available to a third party could ever reveal.

What I want is an audit rather than a tutorial. Tell me which of these pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like they are, rank the real tensions by severity, and for each say which commitment has to go or be revised and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set. Please discriminate: I assume some of the apparent alarms are false and I would rather not be told to abandon something I can keep. My referee claims the whole view stands or falls with (3); I think she has misdiagnosed it and that (4) is the soft spot. Settle that if you can, and tell me which single revision would clean up the most.
