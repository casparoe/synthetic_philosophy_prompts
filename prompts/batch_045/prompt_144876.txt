I'm an undergraduate in a philosophy of social science seminar, and this term's assessed pairwork is an adversarial collaboration rather than a debate: each pair has to hand in a single document that both of them can sign. My assigned partner and I are on opposite sides of the question of whether there can be laws of history, and after three meetings we mostly generate heat, so I want a model draft to work from.

My side is the Popperian line: there is no law of historical development; what look like laws are trends, which hold only given initial conditions that can lapse; the growth of scientific knowledge cannot be predicted from inside a period, so unconditional long-range forecasts of the course of human affairs are impossible; holistic prediction of whole societies is a confusion; what we can have is piecemeal, institutional knowledge of the kind that tells you which arrangements produce which unwanted outcomes. My partner holds that there are genuine, if conditional and mechanism-relative, large-scale regularities, that comparative-historical social science has already found some of them (she likes sequences linking fiscal strain, elite overproduction and state breakdown, and sequences linking population pressure to more extractive forms of political organisation), and that the Popperian arguments at most rule out unconditional prophecy and stage-theories, not the conditional generalisations she has in mind.

Please draft the document we should be able to sign, in three parts:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims. These should be substantive rather than platitudinous: I suspect we agree on more than we have admitted, for instance about the difference between extrapolating a trend and applying a law, about the Oedipus effect (that predicting a social outcome can alter it), and about what piecemeal institutional reform can and cannot deliver. If a claim is trivially true, cut it.

2. A table of the residual disagreements. For each one, give the formulation my partner would accept for her own position and the formulation I would accept for mine, side by side, plus a neutral statement of the difference. Where you think a disagreement is merely verbal — about what counts as a law, or as history, or as prediction — say which part is verbal and why, rather than manufacturing a real dispute.

3. A table of cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one of us. For each crux, one case, argument or body of evidence that both of us would accept as bearing on it, and our predictions written down in advance in separate columns.

Two things we invented in supervision that I want used as tests, not just decoration. First: ignorance-screened conditionals — generalisations whose antecedent includes a clause to the effect that the agents in the system do not know the generalisation and do not have high credence in its prediction, e.g. conditions C obtain, no relevant agent has been informed of this conditional, and outcome E follows within T. Does the screening clause do all the predictive work, so that the result is really a law about the uninformed, or is this just how every ceteris paribus law looks? Second: a sealed-forecast registry — an institution that scores pre-registered conditional forecasts of large-scale social outcomes, where half the forecasts are published and half are sealed until their outcome date. Both of us accept the results as evidence: does the published half score systematically worse than the sealed half, and does the sealed half beat chance? Write down what each of us predicts.

Also settle the scope question I keep dodging: does the argument from the growth of knowledge undermine all social prediction, or only predictions whose truth depends on what will later be known? Keep the whole thing under about 900 words, and write it so it could actually be handed in — no citations, no bibliography, no references to literature either of us would have to look up.
