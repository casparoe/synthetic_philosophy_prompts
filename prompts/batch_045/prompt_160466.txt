I'm finishing a dissertation chapter on Goodman's new riddle of induction, and before I circulate it to my supervisor I want to know what she is actually committed to, because her view is stated almost entirely as rulings on individual predicates rather than as a general criterion. Here is her track record as I have it from her graduate seminar on confirmation theory and from marginal comments on my drafts. Green and blue, applied to emeralds: projectible, and the generalization that all emeralds are green is lawlike and confirmed by its instances. Grue (green if first examined before 2050, blue otherwise) and its mate bleen: not projectible now, even though they fit the evidence exactly as well as green and blue do. The same pair with the cut-off moved into the past, say 1900: she says the puzzle never gets started, because the evidence no longer supports the two hypotheses equally, so the predicate is not in the running at all. Greavy, which I define as being green and weighing under one gram or being blue and weighing a gram or more: not projectible, with the remark that it mentions no time, place or individual, so whatever is wrong with it, it is not that it is positional. Greast, defined as green and west of the thirtieth meridian or blue and east of it: not projectible either, and when I pressed her she said the spatial and temporal cases stand or fall together. Finally, she has said in passing that a predicate defined wholly out of entrenched predicates can inherit their projectibility, but only if the definition is not doing the work — a remark I have never been able to reconstruct.

The cases I need verdicts on are these. First: a hundred and twenty years ago a small and self-contained tradition of gemmologists adopted, as their entire basic colour vocabulary, a pair of predicates I will write as G-star and B-star, where G-star is coextensive with greavy — a stone is G-star if and only if it is green and weighs under a gram, or blue and weighs a gram or more. Nobody in the tradition has ever used anything corresponding to green or blue. Over those hundred and twenty years they have projected G-star tens of thousands of times, always on stones weighing well under a gram, and every projection has come out true, including the standing generalization that every emerald is G-star. Next month they will examine the first emerald ever found weighing more than a gram. Is that generalization projectible for them, and what does it predict about the stone? Second: a condensed-matter group isolates a new phase and introduces a term for it ostensively rather than by reduction to entrenched vocabulary; stipulate that it marks a genuine natural kind with stable causal powers, and that it has been projected about a dozen times with no counterexamples. Is the generalization that all samples of it superconduct below 200 kelvin projectible, given that twelve is roughly the number of projections grue has enjoyed in the philosophical literature? Third: a predicate that a research programme projected continuously for two centuries, with most of its projections coming out true by that programme's own standards, but which we now take to pick out nothing real — I have been using releases phlogiston as my example, and if the actual history is messier than I assume, please treat it as stipulated. Was it projectible in 1780?

What I want is a prediction on each of the three, then the reconstruction: the two or three rules that fit her earlier verdicts equally well, stated precisely enough that I could put them in a footnote; what each predicts about each new case; which of the three cases is genuinely diagnostic and which only looks diagnostic; where her earlier verdicts leave her free to go either way; and if none of my cases separates the rules, the further case that would, ideally one I can construct without presupposing the disputed notion. She is on record as saying, when I tried the first case on her informally, that it is a question about whose language we are speaking, which I take to be either a deep point about whether entrenchment is a property of a predicate in a language or of a predicate relative to a community's practice, or a way of not answering me. Please say which candidate rule is the best guess with the reasons for preferring it, name the single verdict that would tell the rules apart, and tell me plainly if it turns out that the candidate rules agree on every case that can actually arise — that is the conclusion my chapter is edging toward, and I would rather find out now than in her comments.
