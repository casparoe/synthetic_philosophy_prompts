I'm one of the lay members on the ethics committee for a big volunteer-computing project — the old BOINC-style setup where people install a client and it crunches protein-folding and climate-model stuff in the background while their machine is idle. We've got roughly 180k active devices and, crucially, we're oversubscribed: the scheduler farms the same work unit out to three machines and discards the outliers, so the marginal device's causal contribution to the science is honestly a rounding error. A few minutes shaved off a run that takes eighteen months.

We've now had three meetings about the sign-up copy and I need something we can actually put to a vote on Thursday.

Current copy (A): "Every device you add speeds up the research."

Proposed replacement (B), the evidential-cooperation-in-large-worlds appeal, more or less in plain words: your own device makes no measurable difference on it's own, but your decision to run the client is correlated with tens of thousands of other people in a similar position — the sort of person who leaves it running is the sort of person whose counterparts leave it running — so switching it on is worth far more than its own clock cycles, because what you decide tells you something about what the whole crowd decides.

Option (C): drop the individual reason entirely and say "you'll be one of about 180,000 devices; together you are the supercomputer."

Complications:

- The comms team ran a scrappy survey (n=900, self-selected, so pinch of salt) where people read B and typed back what they thought it meant. About two thirds restated it as a causal claim — "so my laptop really does make it go faster" — and about one in six called it magical thinking. Comprehension didn't predict sign-up. So it's possible B works precisely by being misread.
- There's an option nobody likes much: switch off redundancy for a subset of volunteers and tell them truthfully that their result is used as-is. Tech reckons that costs us 8-12% of usable throughput in bad data. But it would make A literally true for those people.
- Our sceptical statistician member says that even granting the large-worlds argument in general, the correlation you get by sending 180k people the same email is the wrong kind of correlation: the versions of the argument that do real work need other people's behaviour to depend on your policy, not on you and them both being pushed by the same prompt from us. On that reading we'd be manufacturing a correlation and then harvesting it. I don't think she's obviously wrong and I've read enough comment threads to know I can't just wave it away.

Where I am: I'm maybe 60/40 that the ECL argument is sound in general and I one-box, but I've gone back and forth badly on whether it belongs in a sign-up flow, and I really don't want to ship a message that quietly recruits people into a philosophical view they never agreed to evaluate.

So: can you draft the memo I'll circulate? It needs a bottom line the committee can vote on — approve B, approve C, or something conditional — plus wording constraints if we go with B, a view on whether the "one device is a rounding error" internal note should go public on the site, and a point at which we revisit it. Give an honest go at the strongest version of whichever side you're rejecting rather than a balanced list. I've got about five minutes to read it out, so keep it tight, and don't cite anything — none of us are going to chase references.
