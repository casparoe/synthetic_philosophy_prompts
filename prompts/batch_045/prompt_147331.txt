I chair the heritage advisory committee of a town of roughly nine thousand people. Among other things we maintain about forty public markers and plaques, one permanent exhibit, and a short booklet used in the local schools. Around a dozen times a year we are asked to adjudicate a conflict of a specific kind: a community — a family, a neighbourhood, a nearby nation whose oral history touches our sites — remembers something about a person, event, or place, and the documentary record we can actually lay hands on either contradicts it, or is silent, or says something compatible in outline but different in point, attribution, or intent. We are seven volunteers, we meet for two hours a month, none of us is a professional historian, and every decision has to be written down on one page.

The friction is structural. The state heritage office, which has to approve any marker, will not let us assert as fact anything the surviving record contradicts; it will accept almost anything phrased as attribution. The communities whose memory is in question tend to experience that same phrasing — remembered by, according to tradition, oral history holds — as one more iteration of being disbelieved by people who hold the paper. So hedging is not a neutral fallback for us; it has a history, and sometimes the thing actually remembered is that history. I want a procedure, not a position statement on whether memory or history should give way, because I have to do this repeatedly and I would rather be consistently wrong in a way I can explain than right by instinct.

What I need from you is a decision procedure my committee can run on a proposal in under forty minutes: the questions in order, what would count as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops with a no, and — importantly — the points at which it stops and hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than evidence. The output of the procedure should be three things: what the marker may assert outright, what it may present only with its source attached (remembered by, recorded as), what it must omit; a single sentence of rationale; and a flag for the cases that have to go to a public meeting rather than to us.

Three things I want built into it, and I want you to say if you think any of them is a prejudice rather than a requirement.

First, I think there are three registers of conflict, not one: the record is silent where memory speaks; the record and memory make incompatible claims about the same event; and the two agree on what happened but disagree about who did it or why. There is, I think, a fourth that does not fit anywhere — cases where what the community remembers is not the event but the record's own bad faith about the event, and where the archive has in fact been shown on some other occasion to have been falsified or selectively kept. Tell me whether that fourth is genuinely distinct and what it does to the procedure when it is present.

Second, I do not think the two directions of giving way are symmetric in cost, even when they are symmetric in evidence. A correction entered in a catalogue is cheap, durable, and read by almost nobody; a correction to what a community says in public is expensive, partial, and may not survive the people who hear it. Should the procedure weigh that asymmetry, or is that a corruption of it — the point at which a committee stops asking what is true and starts managing a constituency? This is the part I expect to be hardest to settle, and I would rather have the tension stated than resolved with a slogan.

Third, we have a standing rule, inherited and never argued for, that we will not substitute a documented name for a remembered one on a marker even when we are confident the documented name is right — we will add, annotate, or put the two side by side, but not replace. I want to know whether that rule is defensible in principle or whether we are just being superstitious, and if it is defensible, where exactly it stops: does it cover dates, numbers of dead, intent attributions?

Then run the procedure on three proposals, showing your work at each step.

One: a founding plaque from the 1920s credits the town's establishment to a named settler in a named year. A neighbouring nation's oral history holds that the site was a seasonal gathering place used before and after that date; a single land-office map from two years after the recorded founding shows two structures already standing where the plaque says the settler built. Nothing in the record mentions the gathering place at all.

Two: a factory fire. The community has always said fourteen workers died; the coroner's register lists nine, and names nine. Oral accounts name the other five, describe them as itinerant workers hired off the books, and say their deaths were never recorded because the company's payroll burned with them. No payroll survives.

Three: a neighbourhood remembers that flood relief in the early 1960s was deliberately withheld from it, and says so on an unofficial marker it erected and maintains itself. The relief files show a delay of eleven days, an administrative misrouting, and no documentary trace of an intention to withhold from anybody. The same archives were shown thirty years ago to have destroyed a different set of files concerning the same neighbourhood.

I expect the procedure to return an answer on the third case that I will not like, and I want it stated without being softened. Finally, tell me plainly what the procedure cannot do: which of these disputes are not the kind of thing a checklist can settle, and what we should do with those.
