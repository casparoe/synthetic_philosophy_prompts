My friend and I spent about three hours last night arguing about whether a model fine-tuned on a poet's collected works and then told to write more poems like those is doing the same kind of thing the poet was doing, and we ended up shouting about Hofstadter's knob-twiddling picture of creativity, the one where a style is a region in a big multidimensional space and creating is wandering around inside it with your hands on the dials. His position was that this is all creativity ever was: you absorb a style, you twiddle, most of what you get is junk, occasionally you land somewhere good. My objection, and the thing I want the poem to actually be *about*, is that the knobs aren't given in advance. His answer to that was annoying and kind of good: any change whatsoever can be described after the fact as a knob being turned, so "discovering a new knob" is just hindsight, and my objection collapses into his view. I think that's wrong but I couldn't say why in a way that shut him up, and I've been turning it over since.

So: write me the poem that settles it, or at least performs it. I want a theme-and-variations structure — my suggestion is a sestina, since the rotating end-words are already a closed space being permuted, but if that's too much machinery then six variations of one fixed six-line blank-verse stanza is fine, as long as the *form* is what carries the argument. Put a short list at the top declaring the six knobs (things like register, line-length, the image-field the scene is drawn from, how far the speaker is standing from what they're describing, the scale of time in a line, whether the sentence is doing or remembering) and then make each stanza vary the theme by turning exactly one of them, so someone who knows the model can tell which stanza turned which knob from the poem alone. Two more constraints that matter to me: (1) at least one knob-turn has to drag another knob with it — the space isn't a grid, the dimensions are entangled, and that should be visible in the verse rather than asserted; (2) the last stanza or the envoi has to turn something that was *not* on the list, a dimension the base stanza didn't have, but it can't be a gimmick dropped in from outside — the earlier variations should have been visibly straining against it, so that when it arrives it feels like the only genuinely creative move in the poem and the other five suddenly look merely competent. And keep the metalanguage out of the poem itself: no knob, no space, no dimension, no creativity. If it works, the reader who doesn't know Hofstadter should just read a good poem about something small and concrete — I was picturing someone rewriting the same six lines about a kitchen table, or repainting the same doorway, but pick your own scene — and the reader who does know the chapter should come away with my side of the argument made in their bones.

The bit I really don't want you to resolve is the ending. My friend's last move, if he'd thought of it, would be to say that my new dimension is just a knob on a higher-level space that I hadn't named yet, and then we're off again, one level up. I'd rather the poem leave that open than answer it — let the last lines be genuinely uncertain about whether what just happened was a discovery or only a dial we hadn't noticed. Don't hedge into mush to achieve that, and don't turn it into a poem about poetry, please; about thirty-five to forty-five lines total including the envoi, and I want to be able to read it aloud at the end of our reading group without anyone feeling lectured.
