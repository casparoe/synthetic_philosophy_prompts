Hey — I run a fortnightly philosophy thing in the back room of a pub: about half are grad students in epistemology, the rest are a lawyer, a GP and two programmers who just turn up because they like arguing. Next session is on what the norm of assertion actually is, and instead of making them read a paper I want to sort them. So build me a "what's your norm of assertion?" instrument: 9 questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, scorable by hand at the table in under ten minutes, plus a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto leaves. Max seven leaves, and no footnotes or suggested reading — I don't want a bibliography, I want a quiz.

Sort by cases, not by labels: no question should ask anyone which norm they accept, and the trade names (knowledge norm, truth norm, belief norm, whatever) shouldn't appear anywhere except the key. Where a pattern of answers doesn't land cleanly on one leaf, give me a best-fit rule and say so.

Build the logical space before the names. Give me the three or four branching questions the tree really rests on, say how many answer combinations they allow, put the actual views into the cells, and for each empty cell tell me whether it's empty because it's incoherent, just unattractive, or merely nobody's bothered to defend it. Then flag any two leaves your instrument can't actually separate rather than pretending it can.

Two distinctions I want doing the real work, because they're what I keep going back and forth on:

(a) Is the fault in a bad assertion an epistemic failing — the speaker wasn't in a good enough position with respect to the truth — or is it more like an infelicity, the way a promise made without any intention to keep it is defective in a way that isn't about facts? That split should be near the top of the tree.

(b) Is the norm fixed entirely by the content and the speaker's situation, or does it shift with who's listening? I have a soft spot for a hearer-sensitive norm — the thought that you can be in a position to say p to one audience and not to another, even holding your evidence fixed — and I suspect almost nobody defends that out loud. If the cell is near-empty, put it in anyway and tell me why it's empty.

Leaves I want represented: the knowledge requirement; the truth requirement; a sincerity/belief requirement; some kind of reasonableness-or-warrant requirement; a norm about answerability rather than truth (you must be in a position to back it up if challenged, not necessarily in a position to know); and the hearer-sensitive thing above. Fill the rest of the seven with whatever belongs there.

Cases — use at least four of mine, fix them where they're muddled, and add your own wherever two leaves still don't come apart on anything:

- A hospital committee of five votes 3–2 to issue "the outbreak is under control." The two dissenters know it isn't; the three who voted yes believed it because they wanted to. Did the committee assert something improper, and whose state is supposed to matter?
- A text support bot outputs "your refund was issued on the 3rd." It's true and it came out of a reliable process, but ex hypothesi the thing has no beliefs and doesn't know anything. Is that a defective assertion, or not an assertion at all?
- I know the train leaves at nine, I'm trying to tell you so, but in a language I speak badly the sentence I actually produce means it leaves at six. Which proposition did I put forward, and is what I did wrong?
- Before the draw, purely on the base rates, I tell you "your ticket lost." Then it wins. What exactly was wrong with the first thing I said, and what should I do when I find out?
- A doctor tells a frightened child something she knows perfectly well to be true, in a tone chosen partly because it will calm her down. Compare that with the same doctor saying the same sentence to the child's specialist parent who already has better evidence than she does.
- I say p, which I knew when I said it; by the time it reaches you the world has changed and p is false. Defective or not — and does your answer depend on whether the norm is indexed to utterance or to uptake?
- Someone says "I think it's going to rain, but I have no idea." Is that even an assertion, and if it is a bad one, what makes it bad?

Last bit: give me a short note on where the instrument is most likely to misfire. Which two leaves does it genuinely fail to pull apart, which single question is doing too much work, and what one new case — or what answer from an actual person at the table — would make you rewrite the key. And say what would make you think the whole framing is wrong: that assertion isn't the kind of thing that has one constitutive norm at all, or that my hearer-sensitivity hobbyhorse is just a confusion of a norm of assertion with ordinary politeness. Also tell me where you're least confident about the mapping between cases and leaves, since a couple of my cases are probably doing something different from what I think they're doing.
