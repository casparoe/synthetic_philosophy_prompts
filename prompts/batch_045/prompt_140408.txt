Two colleagues on a subcommittee I sit on spent an hour arguing past each other, then turned to me to settle it, and I had nothing useful to say. The three of us have to give our county water district a single probability that a proposed recycled-water plant will overrun its budget by more than ten percent. The evidence base is a nine-hundred-page engineering appendix nobody can read in full, so each of us read a different slice of about a hundred and twenty pages, without first telling the others which. Our calibration on past forecasts is roughly identical. Hana came back with 30 percent, Yusuf with 55.

(1) Hana: we are peers in every relevant respect — same training, same access, no track-record difference — so conciliationism applies and we should each move toward the other, which for two equally reliable people means roughly splitting the difference and both landing near 42 or 43 percent.

(2) Yusuf: the usual argument for splitting the difference is that it is a cheap stand-in for something better, pooling our evidence and then each forming our own posterior from the whole; that is why the rule is trustworthy when it is. But here pooling is not expensive at all — I can tell you which sections I read and what they showed in ten minutes. You do not keep using a proxy once the real thing is on the table, so the proxy's authority does not transfer to a case like this.

(3) Hana: pooling only relocates the problem. Even after you summarize your sections, I have to decide how much to trust a summary of pages I have not seen, and that is itself a disagreement with you about evidence I cannot assess directly. Apply the equal-weight thought one level down and it says I should treat your reading of your sections about as seriously as I would treat my own. Conciliationism is not a substitute for pooling; it is what you get whenever any evidence has to be taken on another person's word, which is most evidence.

(4) Yusuf: taken seriously, that makes learning from others nearly inert — every report would drag me only halfway to the reporter's view, however strong her track record. Worse, it turns against the evidence you gathered yourself: the self who read those pages last week is just one more informant to the self deciding now, and if I split the difference with every informant, including my own earlier self, the rule stops pulling people toward the truth and starts pulling them toward ignorance. A view that tells you to distrust your own evidence-gathering cannot be the right one, which is why I am sticking closer to steadfastness.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the four moves, say whether it is right, partly right, or wrong, and why. Then say who is more right overall, and what, if anything, both of them are missing. Finally, state which single argument or consideration would most change your verdict, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
