I am a PhD student in philosophy, finishing a chapter on whether Hofstadter's superrationality, as he set it out in Metamagical Themas, is a genuine alternative to evidential decision theory or merely evidential decision theory plus an assumption about the other agent that it never earns. My working hypothesis is a dilemma: superrationality either collapses into evidential decision theory together with a similarity assumption, or else it has to become something quite different — a theory that assigns rational profiles to asymmetric games — and that other theory carries commitments the original argument never took on. I need a case that forces the choice, and I have not been able to build one that satisfies me.

So: construct a decision problem with the following features.

The setup. Two agents, a single interaction, no further dealings afterwards. Each faces a prisoner's-dilemma ordering over its own payoffs — defection dominates for each, and mutual cooperation beats mutual defection for each — but the stakes are lopsided by roughly two orders of magnitude: one agent's payoffs are denominated in millions, the other's in tens of thousands. Both payoff matrices are common knowledge. There is no communication once the problem is posed, no contract, no third-party enforcement, no repetition and no reputational shadow, no precommitment technology. No predictor better than a shrewd human being, no Newcomb-style boxes, no shared randomizer. And the two agents must not be alike in the way that usually does the work: no clones, no copies of the same code, no shared training or upbringing, no common-cause correlation between their deliberations. A human decision-maker and a deployed AI system, or two firms with very different internal cultures, are the kind of pairing I have in mind; each knows only that the other is rational and knows the setup. Keep it to one paragraph, and keep it realistic — real institutions and people, no aliens and no mailboxes on mountaintops.

The verdicts I need. Causal decision theory says defect, for both agents; show the reasoning even though it is short. Evidential decision theory says defect, or else says nothing at all, for both agents — under the evidential situation as I have described it, with no similarity between the deliberators for it to lean on. Superrationality says cooperate: for both roles if you can get that, and if you can get it for only one, tell me which and why. And the cooperation must fall out of the theory's own argument rather than being written into the scenario. In particular it cannot come from the familiar move that the two agents are running the same computation and must therefore reach the same answer, since the dissimilarity clause is there to remove that move. What is left, as far as I can see, is some role-indexed version of the argument — each agent asks what a superrational agent occupying the other role would do, given that the agent in this role is superrational — and that is precisely the extension to asymmetric games that the standard presentation does not supply. So state what has to be added, say whether the addition must choose between maximizing the sum of payoffs, maximizing the worse-off agent's payoff, and something like equal proportional sacrifice, say whether those rules give different instructions to the two roles in your case, and say what the theory delivers if it refuses to choose among them.

Then the audit. Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, the stake asymmetry or the dissimilarity of the agents, and show me the ablation: symmetrize the stakes and confirm that the case degenerates into the textbook twin case in which evidential decision theory and superrationality coincide, then restore the asymmetry and show exactly what breaks. Then give me the counterattack: what a defender of whichever theory comes out worst would say to stop the case from counting. One such reply should be addressed head-on rather than avoided — that superrationality here is a species of magical thinking, and that once the agents are not alike there is nothing left to ground the correlation between the two choices. I also want you to tell me if my specification of the evidential verdict is the weak point: if evidential decision theory can be made to say defect only by rigging the agents' credences, say so plainly, because that would be the most useful thing I could learn from the exercise.

Finally, be explicit about epistemic status. Mark the places where your scenario gets its verdicts from stipulation rather than from the theories, tell me what would change your mind about whether the case really forces the dilemma I described, and tell me where your own reasoning is most likely to fail. If the specifications turn out to be jointly unsatisfiable, say which one gives way and why, rather than quietly relaxing one of them.
