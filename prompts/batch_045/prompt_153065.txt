I'm writing a dissertation chapter on Newcomb problems, and I've become attached to a resolution that I want stress-tested before I build a chapter on it. I'd be grateful for an adversarial treatment.

The view, briefly. Take the canonical setup: an opaque box that either contains a large sum or doesn't, a transparent box containing a small sum, and a predictor with a long public track record of accuracy on cases like yours, whose prediction was made and boxed before you were told about the choice. My claim is that the standard dispute never specifies what kind of thing the predictor is, and that the correct verdict varies with that in a way neither causal nor evidential decision theory captures.

Call a predictor a *simulator* if its success depends on reproducing, or at least modelling, the very practical reasoning you are now engaged in: it takes roughly your evidence and your deliberation as input and returns your output. Call it a *proxier* if its success depends on some state of you that is not the upshot of that reasoning and that does not track it by mimicking it — a genetic marker, a scan taken years ago, a coarse psychometric profile, early childhood environment. My provisional verdict is one-box against simulators, two-box against proxiers, at equal track records. The reason: against a simulator, the correlation between your choice and the box's contents is not defeated by your coming to know the mechanism, because the mechanism is something you are presently instantiating, and the state that fixed the prediction is in part the process you are running now. Against a proxier, the correlation is the correlation of a symptom, and knowledge of the mechanism drains it of news-value about what you will do; what it tells you is news about features of yourself that you cannot now alter, which is not the sort of thing that can bear on what you should now do.

What I want is a map of the exposure of this view, and I'd like it ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with an honest statement of which of these a defender of my view would himself concede as counting against it, as opposed to what a critic would say.

Specifically, please press on:

1. Whether the simulator/proxier distinction can bear the weight I put on it, given that grain of simulation looks like a continuum. If a predictor simulates a coarse schematic model of you — your broad dispositions, not your occurrent reasoning — which way should it fall? If verdicts must be continuous in grain but my theory is binary, is that a reductio, or is there a principled threshold?

2. Cases that mix the two. Suppose the predictor first reads a proxy and then, conditional on that reading, runs a simulation of the kind of person with that profile. Or suppose it simulates you but seeds the simulation with the proxy fact. My own sense is that these are the cases most likely to break the view, but I can't tell whether they break it by showing the distinction is spurious or by showing I've misdescribed which feature does the work.

3. The move of deciding to become the kind of person who one-boxes, and the related move of delegating to a coin flip or to someone whose reasoning the predictor is known not to model. I take these to be the natural test cases, and I take the coin-flip case to favour me, since a proxier's correlation survives randomization and a simulator's does not. But I want to know whether the simulator's correlation really does evaporate there, or whether it merely shifts to a higher-order prediction of whether you will randomize, which would drain the test of its force.

4. Whether the view has a distinctive prediction my rivals don't. One I've toyed with: that a predictor's accuracy should be sensitive to how long the agent deliberates, if and only if it is a simulator, since fresh deliberation is input the simulator must accommodate. If empirical work on prediction of human choice showed no such sensitivity — or showed it for proxiers too — would that be decisive against me, or would I be able to absorb it?

5. Finally, the worry I take most seriously: that I have insulated the view. Whenever a case seems to favour one-boxing, I can redescribe the predictor as a simulator; whenever it seems to favour two-boxing, I can redescribe it as a proxier; and since I have given no independent test for which a given predictor is, the view threatens to become a redescription of my own intuitions rather than an account of them. I want to know whether that is a real defect, how I would go about supplying the independent test, and — if it turns out I can't — whether a view of this kind is allowed to be insulated in that way or whether it thereby forfeits its claim over the evidentialist and causalist accounts it was supposed to displace.

For what it's worth, the thing that would move me most is not a counterexample involving exotic predictors but an argument that the news-value of evidence cannot depend on which mechanism produced the correlation in the way I claim — or, conversely, a proxier case in which one-boxing is nevertheless clearly correct and clearly cannot be explained away.
