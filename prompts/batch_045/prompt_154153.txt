I help advise a small grantmaker in our orbit that can fund roughly twelve researcher-years of conceptual work over the next four years — philosophy, decision theory, and formal-ish theory, no empirical ML, no policy work, no field-building — on a theme it has narrowed to two clusters: acausal threats, and surrogate goals. It has asked me for an allocation, and I would rather hand over a defended agenda than a survey.

By surrogate I mean any specification that stands in for an intended aim at one of three places: in the training objective (outer surrogates — a reward signal, a labeler, a constitution); in the environment distribution over which the system is selected and then deployed (distributional surrogates — feedback channels that stop tracking the aim once the system moves off the regime they were fitted on); and in the system's own learned goal representation (inner surrogates — the goal-misgeneralization and inner-misalignment cases). A draft circulating in our circles argues the inner case is really a special case of the distributional one, so that the two should be funded as a single program; I am not convinced, largely because the interventions people propose for them look nothing alike. Separately, people here tend to sort surrogate-goal work into making the surrogate correct versus making the surrogate revisable — eliminate it versus maintain it — and my sense is that the second is under-funded relative to how often the first is actually achievable.

On the acausal side, define a system's exposure surface as the set of (modeler, demand) pairs that can extract concessions from it without any causal contact. The claim I keep running into is that the size of that surface is set mostly by how legible the system's policy and commitment behavior are to whoever is modeling it, not by which decision theory the system implements — so that the practically relevant research concerns the epistemic opacity of policies and commitment devices rather than settling the choice between causal, evidential, and functional-style theories. If that is right, a good chunk of the decision-theory program is aimed one step downstream of where the leverage is; if it is wrong, the classic questions look more central: how to think about trade with agents you will never meet, whether to capitulate to demands that cost little to satisfy once, and what multipolar deployment scenarios actually inherit from one-shot superrationality.

So: given twelve researcher-years, how should they be split between the clusters and within them, and which three or four questions should come first? I care much more about your reasoning than about your list: what each question is upstream of, whether it is tractable with the tools conceptual people actually have in four years, how neglected it is relative to its importance, and — the funder's own criterion — whether a positive answer would change what someone building or deploying a system does within about five years, as opposed to re-describing the problem more elegantly. Please also say what you would actively defund. Candidate questions, mine and not exhaustive: the status of updatelessness; whether acausal-trade results generalize from two-agent to crowded-agent settings; whether an agent optimized only weakly, or under a low-impact penalty, is more or less exposed than a strongly optimizing one; whether outer surrogates are eliminable in principle or only shrinkable; what corrigibility would have to consist in for the maintain-a-surrogate program to be more than a slogan; and how to reason about exposure when the threatener's model of you was itself learned from a surrogate of your policy. Tell me which of these are downstream of something else, which are unfalsifiable in practice, and what I have left out. Assume the reader knows the background and cannot look anything up: no literature map, no citations, no reading list.

Be explicit about confidence. I want to know where your ordering is most likely to be wrong: flag near-ties rather than presenting a strict ranking you do not believe, give the single result or argument over the next two years that would most demote each of your top items, and mark which of your judgments quietly rest on empirical claims about how capable systems will in fact be built, since those are the ones I would most want to check myself. Give a brief calibration note at the start — how much you are inferring from first principles versus repeating what you think the field already believes — and say honestly if you think the whole two-cluster framing is the thing that should be rejected.
