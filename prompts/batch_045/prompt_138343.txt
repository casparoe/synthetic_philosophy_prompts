I've got 16 people in an upper-level undergrad metaphysics seminar and a 75-minute slot, and I want an activity that makes them *feel* the gap between "there's no first-order disagreement left once you disambiguate" and "there's nothing at stake here." They keep hearing the verbal-dispute verdict as a deflationary one — as if showing that two speakers mean different things by a term settles the argument — and I want them to run into the opposite: a case where disambiguating genuinely dissolves the original question, and the argument *still won't stop*, because what's left is a fight over which concept we should be using, and that fight has real costs for someone.

Rough shape I have in mind, but design it properly: pairs, each student gets a card with a private purpose for a shared term (what they need the word to do, who gets what if it's applied), they argue a yes/no question, and the incentives are set up so each person individually does best by sticking with their own reading while the pair does best by coordinating — but they can only coordinate on one reading, and whoever looses that vote loses something concrete they can no longer say or do. The purposes should be close enough that they can't tell at first whether they disagree about the world or about the word. No real money. No lecture up front, and they shouldn't hear merely verbal, quantifier variance, or conceptual ethics until the debrief. Please skip the usual examples (statue and clay, is a hotdog a sandwich, botanical vs. culinary fruit) — give me a fresh case, or better, a short procedure for generating them so I can reuse the game next year. The debrief should map specific moments in play onto (a) why the dispute counts as verbal and (b) why that doesn't deflate it, plus the move to ontology: the sense in which both sides of a composition-style existence question can be right and still be fighting.

Sections, with rough lengths: The setup, 120 words; Rules and materials, 200; Timeline, 150; Scoring, 120; Debrief map, 300; What will go wrong the first time I run it, 150; What the game can't do, 120; Where this design is most likely to fail, 100. In that last one, tell me what would change your mind about the activity actually teaching what I think it teaches — e.g. if you think the payoffs smuggle in the conclusion that the residual dispute is substantive, say so and say what you'd change instead.
