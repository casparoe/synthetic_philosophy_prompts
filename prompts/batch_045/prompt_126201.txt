I've been put incharge of the toast at my predecessor's retirement dinner a week from Saturday and I'm badly stuck, so I want the whole thing written — call it six minutes.

Marlene ran Cedar Ridge High for thirty-one years: three hundred kids, ranch and timber families, the same handful of surnames going back four generations. I've had her job for two. The room will be about two hundred people split three ways: the town families, including her own; her professional crowd from the state ed department and the ed school; and roughly thirty former students in their twenties and thirties who drove back for it, a few of whom now talk about that school in the language of having survived something, and who think Marlene is the reason they could ever say it out loud.

The thing I keep hitting is her rule. She never closed a conflict in front of an audience. Whatever it was — a fight, a theft, a threat — the repair happened in her office with the people involved plus one extra person who had nothing to gain either way: a shop teacher, a bus driver, whoever. She called it the third chair, and she use to say the test for who gets to sit in it is "who doesn't need anything from this." Her reason, as I understand it, was that as soon as there are witnesses, whatever the witnesses think they're watching, it's a contest for their esteem, and the repair quietly turns into a fight about who looks better in front of them. Fists in one version of that, a settlement in another, a public statement in a third. She also said the third chair grows — the uninvolved party slowly ends up owning the conflict, and then everyone is performing for that party's sympathy.

So I can't do the "she taught us to walk away" material, because to half the room that's just a polite way of saying their grandfathers were brutes, and anyway she thought refusing to answer an insult was its own status move, not an exit from the contest. I can't do the "she taught us to come forward" material either, because she didn't think that was straightforwardly better. And I can't stand up and promise to keep the third chair, because I don't think I can: half of what crosses my desk I'm required to route upward now, and every fight reaches me with an audience of four hundred already attached to it, on a phone, from last night. There is no room with only the people involved in it anymore.

Three concrete problems. One: everybody wants the story about the two boys in 2009 who now run a fencing business together, including the part where Marlene never announced anything and the town was furious with her for it. Is that story tellable in this room, or does it work by making the town the villain? Two: there was a time she kept something quiet that should not have been kept quiet. The people involved are not in the room. I won't airbrush her into a saint, but confessing on their behalf to two hundred people feels like exactly the thing she was against — so tell me what the speech is allowed to say about it, if anything at all. Three: this is a toast. It's a public ceremony about a woman who thought public ceremony is what ruins repair, and it's delivered to an audience, by her successor, who is asking for their approval. Deal with that in the speech, or the whole thing is a hypocrisy — but no cute "she'd have hated this" line, please.

And under all of it: a toast has to say she was good, I think she was good, and I don't know how to say that without implying that one of those three ways of handling a slight is the grown-up one and the others are what you grow out of. The each-has-its-own-wisdom move is not available to me; it sounds like a school board email, and her brother will laugh out loud at it from the head table. Six minutes, mixed room, no saints.
