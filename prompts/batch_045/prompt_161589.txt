I teach a 3rd-year seminar on decision theory / formal epistemology — 14 students, one 90-minute slot, no real money (points, and the top scorer gets a chocolate bar). They've already had the Dutch book argument for sharp credences and basic expected utility. What I want is one runnable activity that makes them *feel* the difference between having a sharp credence and having an imprecise one — a set of probability functions, i.e. a lower and an upper probability for an event rather than a number. Three things I want them to walk into before I name anything:

**1. Imprecision is a behavioural difference, not a difference in how unsure you feel.** My plan: deal two different info cards for the same urn. Group K is told a fair coin was flipped and decided whether the urn has 4 or 6 red balls out of 10 — so the chance of red is exactly 0.5 and, if they defer to chances, their credence is a sharp 0.5. Group U is told only that the urn has 4, 5 or 6 red out of 10 and nothing more — so their set is everything between 0.4 and 0.6. Both groups will *say* roughly the same thing if you just ask how confident they are. Then I walk the price of a ticket paying 100 points if the drawn ball is red: 30, 35, 40 … 70, and at every price each student says buy, sell, or pass. My hope is Group K flips from buy to sell at 50, maybe with a single indifferent pass, while Group U has a twenty-point band where they simply refuse to move. I want that refusal to be something they do rather than something I describe, and I want the band to look visibly unlike a point of indifference.

**2. The cautious rule is menu-dependent and can throw away free money.** With the 0.4–0.6 set: bet X pays −10 if red and +14 if blue; bet Y pays +14 if red and −10 if blue. Each one has negative expected value at one endpoint of the set (−0.4), so each gets declined under accept-it-only-if-its-positive-under-every-probability-in-your-set. But X and Y together pay +4 either way. I want to run this two ways on two halves of the room: one half gets X alone, then Y alone, then is finally asked about the pair; the other half gets the whole menu at once and picks any subset. Same three gambles, different answers, and the debrief can ask which answer is the right one.

**3. Conditioning can make imprecision worse no matter what you learn.** Physical setup: 100 balls, exactly 50 red, run through a machine that splits them into two boxes of 50, with the manufacturer's guarantee that neither box ever ends up more than 60% red. Unconditionally the probability of red is exactly 1/2 — sharp, no interval at all. Then I offer, for free, to tell them which box the drawn ball came from, and they're allowed to decline the offer. Whichever box it is, their interval for red becomes [0.4, 0.6]. So they can see in advance that a free peek leaves them strictly less precise than they are right now. I want them to arrive at wait, should I refuse free information? on their own. (If there's a standard name for this phenomenon, tell me; I've half-remembered one but I don't want to bet on it.)

What I need from you:

- Actual rules, materials and a points payout scheme, plus a timeline. I was thinking roughly 55 minutes of play and 35 of debrief, but tell me if thats wrong.
- How to run the price walk so I get usable data on the board. Show of hands feels contagious — private slips, a fixed order, something else?
- A debrief that maps each moment (the pass band, the declined pair, the free peek) onto the philosophical point, and says where each moment could be resisted and what resisting there costs you. In particular I want the contrast with the Dutch book to land: the sharp agent's failure mode is sure loss, the imprecise agent's failure mode is forgone sure gain.
- What will predictably go wrong. My three worries: (a) this is basically Ellsberg in a costume and they'll file it under people are weirdly ambiguity averse and never reach the normative claim — is there a five-minute control round I can run up front that separates genuine imprecision from plain risk aversion, or at least tells me which one I'm looking at? (b) someone says just use the midpoint of the interval and I don't have a short reply ready; (c) they've all done stats and will try to hand me a confidence interval when I ask for a range.
- Be honest about what the game cannot deliver. Which part of imprecise probabilism has to come from me talking at them, because no box of balls and 14 people can produce it?

One last thing I'm uneasy about: every interval here gets manufactured out of sparse evidence, so what they're reacting to might really be missing data or disagreement between sources rather than indeterminacy as such. If you think that's fatal, say so and tell me how to source the intervals differently; if it's fine, tell me what to say at the end so it doesn't quietly do all the work.
