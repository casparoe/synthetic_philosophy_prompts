I teach a seven-week upper-level seminar on rational choice — fourteen students, mostly philosophy majors with a few from economics, meeting once a week for seventy-five minutes in a room with a whiteboard, a projector, two opaque cloth bags, a box of ping-pong balls, index cards and a coin, and no budget beyond one book token I am willing to buy for a raffle. We finished expected utility theory last week: the independence axiom, the Allais paradox, and the representation theorem to the effect that an agent satisfying the axioms can be described as maximising expected utility with respect to a single subjective probability measure over the states. In the session after next I want them to feel, before they are told anything, that it is the single-measure part that breaks: that a draw from a bag they can see contains five red and five black balls is not, for them, the same thing as a draw from a bag whose composition I have not disclosed, and that whatever the difference is, it is neither risk aversion nor a guess about the contents. I have run the plain version before — two bags, four binary choices, tally on the board — and it fell flat: a good half of the room said they would simply assume the second bag was fifty-fifty and get on with it, a couple treated my silence as evidence that I had stacked it, and nobody felt the pull of anything. So I would like a properly staged activity instead.

The mechanic I have been toying with, which you should either use or improve on, is a calibration bag rather than a forced choice: for each colour separately, each student writes down the number of balls of that colour they would want me to put in a second, transparent bag — which I then actually fill in front of them — such that they are indifferent between betting on that colour from the transparent bag and betting on it from the opaque one. A student who says four for red and four for black has thereby said, in their own hand, that the opaque bag is no better than a bag they can see contains four red when the bet is on red, and no better than a bag they can see contains six red when the bet is on black; their two numbers do not add to ten, and I want them to be the ones who notice that, and to find that they cannot make them add up however they try. Alongside that I want two further things, if you think they earn their ten minutes each: a token, purchasable for a small number of raffle tickets, that lets a coin flip decide which bag a student bets on rather than their choosing, and a split in which half the room is told that the opaque bag was filled by a classmate who wins the book token if the class loses while the other half is told it was filled by a mechanical process — the point of the split being to disarm the objection that suspicion of cheating explains everything, since a prior shifted towards thinking someone is out to get them predicts that they will prefer the transparent bag on one colour and the opaque bag on the other, not on both. If a two-stage step in which they commit to a policy in writing and are then invited to revise it is worth the time, add it; if it would muddy the session, say so and say why.

What I need from you is the runnable thing — the script minute by minute, what goes on the board and when, what each student writes on their card, how the raffle tickets are cashed in, and how I keep the students who calibrate early from anchoring the ones who have not yet written — and then the debrief: which moment in the room is supposed to map onto which part of the idea, and what I should say when the things I expect to go wrong do go wrong (the fifty-fifty move, someone shaking a bag, the student who filled it leaking it, the accusation that I am bluffing about not knowing, the one who refuses to gamble at all, the one who asks what happens if the bag is empty). Please also be frank about what the activity cannot deliver and I will simply have to say in words; my guess is that the gap between their numbers failing to add to ten and the formal apparatus of non-additive or lower-and-upper probabilities is where the staging stops and the lecture has to begin, but tell me if you think the real gap is somewhere else. They should not hear the name of the urn example, or the word ambiguity used technically, until the debrief.
