QUESTION 4 (25 points). A commentator writes: "The replication crisis is a p-value crisis. A p-value tells you how surprising your data would be if nothing were going on, but scientists read it as the probability that nothing is going on. Once you see that a p-value cannot give you what scientists want, the explanation for the crisis is obvious: researchers have been misreading their own statistics. Bayesian methods give you what you actually want — the probability of your hypothesis given the data — so a wholesale switch to Bayesian testing would largely dissolve the crisis." Write an essay that does all three of the following. (a) State precisely what a p-value is, and explain why it is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true. [5 points] (b) Assess the diagnostic claim: is the crisis best explained as a failure to interpret p-values correctly? Consider at least two other candidate causes and say what each would predict. [10 points] (c) Assess the prescription: would switching to Bayesian hypothesis testing or Bayesian estimation largely dissolve the crisis? Distinguish the different things a "Bayesian fix" might be fixing. [10 points]

STUDENT ANSWER. A p-value is the probability of getting a test statistic at least as extreme as the one observed, computed under a model in which the null hypothesis is true along with the other assumptions of the test — random sampling, the specified error structure, and so on. It is a claim about the sampling distribution of the statistic, not about the probability of a hypothesis: the probability of the data given the null is not the probability of the null given the data, and no amount of looking at the first quantity will give you the second. To get the second you need Bayes' theorem, which requires a prior probability for the null and a specification of what the alternative predicts. So the commentator's first point stands. A p-value near .05 may be strong or weak evidence against the null depending on how plausible the null was beforehand and on how well the alternative predicted the result that was actually obtained.

But the diagnosis is too narrow. If most of the hypotheses tested in a field are false, then even at conventional error rates the significant results in that field will be dominated by false positives, and the literature will consequently overstate how large and how well supported its effects are. This, rather than any misreading of p-values, is why the large replication projects keep recovering effects much smaller than the originals: if most published significant findings are false positives, then of course a better-powered replication of one of them lands near zero, and the original estimate was inflated rather than merely unlucky. Two further causes compound the problem. First, studies in many fields are badly underpowered, so a real effect has to be lucky to reach significance, and the lucky estimates are the ones that get written up — the significance filter. Second, researchers have a great many degrees of freedom in how they clean and analyse their data, and the pipelines that yield significance are the ones that get reported. Neither of these is a misunderstanding of what a p-value means, so better statistical education would help only at the margins.

The prescription is on firmer ground. Bayesian methods deliver the quantity the commentator wants, and Bayesian estimation with a skeptical prior centred on zero pulls noisy estimates back toward zero, which is just the correction the significance filter calls for. Bayesian inference also satisfies the likelihood principle, so it is not sensitive to the stopping rule in the way a p-value is: a researcher who peeks at her data and stops when things look good does not thereby inflate the evidence, and that removes one of the researcher's degrees of freedom at a stroke. And because the prior has to be stated, the analysis puts the researcher's commitments on the table instead of hiding them in a choice of controls. Reporting posteriors and Bayes factors under a default prior would accordingly remove most of the crisis. Not all of it, of course: a Bayesian who tries twenty priors and reports the strongest result can cheat as effectively as anyone, and no formalism stops a journal from preferring exciting findings.
