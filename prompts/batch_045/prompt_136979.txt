ok so here's what i want. Take the Suez crisis of 1956 as a case of a historical analogy misfiring, and run it through a set of tests i've been messing around with. Facts as i remember them, dont bother checking them, they're not the point: Nasser nationalises the canal company in july; Britain, France and Israel work out a secret three-way deal; Israel goes into Sinai in late october; Britain and France issue the ultimatum and start bombing; Eisenhower refuses to go along, the pound is under pressure and Washington witholds the IMF money; ceasefire early november; Eden is out by january. And Eden really was running on the Munich analogy — Nasser as Hitler, himself as the man who'd broken with Chamberlain over appeasement back in 1938 (i think the immediate issue was Italy rather than Munich itself, tell me if im wrong), and the lesson being that you dont buy off a dictator, because the concession only makes the later war bigger.

Here are the tests. First, separate role-casting from driver-matching. Role-casting is who plays Hitler, who plays Chamberlain, and whether that assignment is doing any real work or is just salience wearing a hat. Driver-matching is whether the mechanism that actually produced the outcome in 1938 — a rearming state with unlimited aims taking a concession as one step in a chain — is genuinely present in 1956. I think Suez is interesting precisely because the casting may be defensible while the driver is absent, and i want you to say which way each one cuts and why.

Second, the veto-player test. Munich had no actor whose consent was needed and withheld. Suez had one, and the analogy had no slot for it at all — british policy ran into Washington, not into Egypt. So ask what an analogy is for: is it a prediction device or an attention-allocation device, and does my test just reduce to the second?

Third, the discount problem. Eden invoked the analogy to justify a limited operation, but if Nasser really is Hitler then the lesson is closer to total commitment than to a Canal-zone police action. Is invoking an analogy at a fraction of its implications a reliable tell of misuse, or is most competent analogy-use partial anyway?

Fourth, lesson-drift. The lesson gets stated as a prohibition on the last mistake (dont appease) rather than as a causal claim, and then it ratchets — after Suez the lesson becomes never again without the Americans, which gets used both for and against Iraq in 2003. Say whether a framework like mine can tell a ratcheted lesson from a real one without hindsight.

So: do the mapping (who are the actors, what are the options, what does the analogy predict and what did it actually do), say which features of Suez it explains and which it renders invisible, and then turn it around and tell me what Suez does to my four tests — which one is doing the real work, which is redundant, and where they're silent. Two more things. Give me one invocation of the Munich analogy, or of the lesson of Suez, that your framework should rate as basically ok; if you can't, say so and admit it's just a machine for saying analogies that lost were misused. And the hindsight question head on: if Washington had backed Britain and the whole thing had worked, would your tests still call the analogy a misuse, and if not, what are they actually measuring?
