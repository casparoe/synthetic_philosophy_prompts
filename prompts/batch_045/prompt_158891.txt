hey — I write novels (two out, one in a drawer that won't behave) and the book I'm supposed to hand in next spring hangs a lot on a character I've been calling the Notary. She's a magistrate in a border town who, whenever she's asked to rule on something, refuses to give a verdict and instead reads out four statements — it is so; it is not so; it is both; it is neither — and then rejects all four, and then goes ahead and does whatever she was going to do anyway. I got the idea from reading about the four-cornered negation in Nāgārjuna a while back, the catuṣkoṭi, and I'd like to make it less cartoonish before anyone who actually knows this stuff reads it. I've managed to get an hour recorded with a philosopher friend-of-a-friend who works on Madhyamaka — she's fine being a sounding board, but she's one of those people who answers every question with a clarifying question, so I need to come in with real pressure points rather than "so what is emptiness, exactly". Also she's coming straight from teaching and won't have any books with her, so nothing that depends on looking up a passage.

Could you draft me eight or nine interview questions, each with the answer you'd expect her to give and then a follow-up aimed at that specific answer? Ideally one each on: whether the four corners are a logic with its own negation operator or just a checklist of views to be taken apart one by one (I expect the second answer, so press her on why four and not three or five, and on whether the "both" corner does any work of its own); the distinction between denying something without implying the opposite and denying-and-implying, and then what that leaves the refutations as — if nothing positive gets established, in what sense is she disagreeing with someone who says things have their own nature, and isn't there an old objection that if you hold no thesis you can't refute anything; the bit where emptiness is said to be the giving up of all views and anyone who turns emptiness into a view is called incurable, so whether my Notary is doing therapy rather than saying anything, and whether anything can be plain false as opposed to empty; the two-truths business, given that my character still sentences people — is "conventional truth" truth, or just what everyone has agreed to say, and are there conventional facts that simply aren't up for negotiation; my own pet addition, which is that in the draft the Notary sometimes admits a fifth option escaped her, "none of the four, and not even that" — would that be a refinement or a basic misunderstanding, given that "neither" already looks like a catch-all; the analysis of a walker and the walking into path already gone, path being traversed, path not yet gone, where I'm pretty sure the answer will be "we're not denying walking happens, only that it survives analysis", in which case what is the difference between surviving analysis and existing; whether emptiness is a fact about things or about our concepts and ways of talking, given how much of the material is about things' own-being; and the later split between those who thought you could argue with your own independent inference and those who thought you should only use consequences the opponent already grants — because if it's only the latter, the whole thing starts to look merely ad hominem to me.

And please commit rather than balance: tell me which parts of the Notary are faithful to the material and which are definately woo-woo I should cut, with reasons; where the scholarship is genuinely contested, pick the reading you think is right, and say what would have to be true for the other side to win you over. Don't hedge into "some interpreters say x, others say y, take your pick" — I can get that from the jacket copy.
