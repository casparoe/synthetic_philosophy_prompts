Quick sanity check before I build this into my dissertation chapter. I'm a third-year PhD student in political science working on government formation, and I'm modelling it as a two-party bargaining problem over portfolios: the disagreement point is the caretaker/status quo government, and the feasible set is what a coalition can deliver. In some of my cases one party also has a fallback coalition with a minor party, which strictly enlarges the feasible set, and I want the model to say that nobody is made worse off by that.

So I've decided to use the Kalai-Smorodinsky solution rather than the Nash one. The argument is: Nash violates monotonicity, in the sense that if the feasible set grows someone can end up with less; KS was designed to fix exactly that, and it keeps Pareto efficiency, symmetry, and invariance under affine rescaling of each party's utility. So axiom by axiom it looks like Nash with a bug fixed, and the only thing you give up is independence of irrelevant alternatives, which nobody really believes anyway — it's the same independence intuition that Allais-type examples kill in decision theory. Bonus: in a politics paper KS is easier to defend, because it hands each party the same fraction of the most it could possibly have gotten, which reads like a proportionality principle and doesn't seem to require comparing utilities across people.

Two questions.

One: is that argument actually sound? Everyone in my field uses Nash, nested Nash bargaining and all that, and I can't tell whether that's because Nash is genuinely better or just because it got there first and everyone copied it. If I'm wrong, tell me definitly which step is the bad one — I'd rather find out now than at my defence. Concretely: when the feasible set expands in my setup, does KS protect both parties, or only the one whose options expanded? Is there anything KS depends on that Nash doesn't, which should worry me given that my feasible set is estimated rather than known? And is the Allais analogy actually doing any work?

Two: is there a solution that keeps Pareto efficiency, symmetry, and invariance while satisfying both IIA and monotonicity? I've never seen one, but surely someone has either found one or shown you can't, and I want to know whether any such impossibility result is deep or just a corollary of the characterization theorems.

Give me a straight answer, please, not a survey of the options: commit to a verdict on which solution I should use in this chapter and why, tell me plainly if the axiom-counting argument is wrong, and flag which of your claims are theorems you're confident about versus normative calls where reasonable people genuinely disagree. Also say where you're unsure — I'm writing this on a train and can't check anything, so I'd rather have honest hedging than confident references I can't verify.
