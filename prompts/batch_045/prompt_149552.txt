Eighteen months ago I was passed over for a promotion I had spent four years working toward, and the panel told me, kindly, that the outcome reflected the strongest claim on merit. This spring I sat on the other side of a panel of the same kind, and we are now drafting the criteria for a search of our own; a colleague has proposed that once we have a shortlist of candidates who are all plainly capable of doing the job, we allocate the post by lottery. I have to take a view on that, and in trying to I have worked myself into an argument that I suspect contains a mistake.

My starting point is that meritocracy is the view that posts and rewards should go to the people who deserve them, and that desert here is a matter of what someone has made of themselves — the effort, the discipline, the hours — rather than the accident of being quick, or tall, or born to parents who read aloud. On that reading, most of the critical literature I have encountered looks like an extended complaint about measurement error: the tests are noisy, interviews encode class signalling, credentials proxy for parental income. That is a genuine complaint, but it is a technical one, and I cannot see how it gets anyone to the much stronger claim that the arrangement itself is illegitimate.

So I have tried to build a better argument, and I have been calling it the ratchet. Every unearned advantage a child has — a quiet room, a stable home, decent nutrition, a teacher who happened to notice them — is converted by schools, examiners and selectors into a credential. The credential is then read back as evidence of ability or effort, and because the next round of advantages is allocated on the basis of credentials, the conversion compounds: by mid-career the distance between two people is mostly accumulated accident, but it is inscribed in a CV, and so it presents itself to everyone, including the person who has it, as something earned. If that is right, meritocracy does not merely track desert imperfectly; it manufactures counterfeit desert. It launders luck into a claim of desert, and then recruits the loser's own self-respect to bless the outcome. There is even a historical corroboration: the word was introduced by Michael Young in a satirical dystopia of 1958, as a term of abuse, so people who defend meritocracy are defending something its inventor meant as a warning.

Put those together and I reach a conclusion I am not comfortable with: that competitive selection is a desert-laundering machine, worse than an open lottery, which at least does not pretend to be tracking anything, and that we should therefore replace it with lotteries among the qualified — the device the lottocracy literature proposes for legislatures, applied to hiring. Where is the false step? My own suspicion is that I have quietly merged merit with desert, and assumed that tracking desert is what selection for a role is even for. But if that is the error, what is the practice for, and does my colleague's lottery actually break the ratchet, or just hide it behind a threshold we still get to set?
