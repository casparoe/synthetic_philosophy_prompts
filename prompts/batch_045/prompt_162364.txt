Hey — imagine you're the outside reviewer for a small foundation that's decided to spend something like four million dollars over five years on the premise that a society's dominant moral emotion is something you can deliberately nudge, and that guilt is the better one to nudge toward. The board is already sold on that; your job is to tell them which questions to actually buy answers to first, before the money goes out the door, and which parts of their premise are assumed rather than established.

I dont want a survey of the guilt/shame literature or a reading list. I want a defended ordering: which open questions you'd fund, in what sequence, and why — judged on things like how much of the rest of the program depends on the answer, whether it's answerable in five years by people doing conceptual plus empirical work rather than needing a generation of actual cultural change, and how likely the answer is to change what they'd do with the money. Say explicitly what you'd deprioritize because its answer wouldn't move anything downstream, and what evidence in the first two years would push items up or down the list.

A few things I keep running into, which you should feel free to tear apart or replace with better ones: (a) the asymmetry that guilt seems to come with an exit ritual — confession, apology, repair — while shame seems to have no discharge at all except concealment or expelling the person, which would make the choice between them really a choice about institutions and rituals rather than about feelings; (b) the worry that guilt is more audience-dependent than it looks, since feeling guilty seems to require an imagined victim or judge, so the story about guilt as the internalized, self-directed, autonomous emotion may be overstated; (c) the possibility that guilt versus shame isn't the load-bearing axis at all — maybe what matters is whether the emotion points at something repairable, or whether it has any exit route, or something else, and the binary is crowding out a third family of responses (indignation, contempt, disgust) that the board should be spending on instead.

And take the title question seriously: can a culture choose here, or is that a category mistake about how moral emotions change? If you think the whole choose-your-moral-emotion framing is confused, say so and tell them what to fund instead.
