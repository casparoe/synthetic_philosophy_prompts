hi — I got roped into writing one question for the end-of-year test on a course I help out with ("how history works", first-year-ish, mixed bunch, mostly not philosophy or econ majors). I'm not an academic, I've just been chewing on this stuff on my own for years and apparently that makes me the local expert. The topic I want is path dependence: the idea that where we end up depends on the exact route taken, and that after a while you're stuck on the route.

Two things bug me about how this is normally explained. First, every version I've read leans on the same three stories: the keyboard layout, the video-format war, and the one about railway gauges coming from Roman cart axles (which I gather is probably invented). A student can pass by retelling those, and I want a question where retelling them earns nothing. Second, I think people run together about four different claims, and I want students forced to seperate them. My own working names:

- fragility: nudge the past slightly and you get a very different present
- lock-in: at some point going back costs too much, so the original route is now load-bearing
- functional redundancy: other routes would have delivered something that does the same job, so it only looks like the route mattered because you're tracking the wrong details
- erasure: the traces of the route fade, so even a load-bearing beginning can leave a present with no evidence of it in it

If two of those are secretly the same claim, or if I've got one backwards, say so in a note and fix it — I'd rather be corrected than set a question with a broken distinction in the middle of it.

What I want back:

1. One 45-minute essay question for students who've done the reading. It should be unanswerable by reciting famous examples, and it should make them say what would even count as evidence for or against "we're locked in", given that we only get one run of history and can't re-run it with a tweak. Please build it around a made-up case rather than a real one so nobody can look anything up — say two nearly identical river valleys where one early accident (a mill sited on the wrong bank because someone's horse bolted, whatever you like) compounds into one valley having a big city and the other staying farmland.

2. A model answer, roughly 700 words, at the level a strong student could actually hit.

3. A marking scheme out of 20: the specific moves a top answer must make, marks each is worth, and a one-line note on how students typically get each one wrong. I'd like one mark reserved for the Roman-cart point: does it matter to the lock-in claim that the story people tell about why the path was taken is false?

Last thing, and it's the bit I care most about: the model answer has to keep clear what's settled versus what people who actually study this genuinely argue about — e.g. whether "the more adopters, the better it gets for the next adopter" dynamics really lock things in or just make them slow to change, and whether the famous keyboard story is even true. Don't let it present a contested claim as the consensus, and have the scheme dock marks from a student (or from your own model answer) for doing that.
