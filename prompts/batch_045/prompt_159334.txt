I'm writing a term paper for an advanced seminar on the epistemology of other minds, and I'd like to use Mary Shelley's *Frankenstein* as a real case rather than as decoration — but I don't want to force it. My tentative reading is that the novel isn't dramatizing the sceptical question in its familiar form (how do I get from this behaviour to a mind behind it?) but something adjacent to it: call it *acknowledgment*. An agent can have all the evidence a mind-detector could want and still withhold the thing the creature is actually asking for, and it's that withholding, not a failed inference, that drives the plot. A friend in the seminar objected that this isn't epistemology at all — that detection is the philosophical problem, acknowledgment is just ethics, and I'm renaming the question to make a novel relevant to a syllabus it doesn't belong on. I want you to take that objection seriously rather than help me win the argument, and to tell me plainly if the distinction collapses under pressure.

Here's the shape of the reading I'm after. Start by fixing the distinction I'm reaching for: detection versus acknowledgment, what each would require, and whether one is genuinely an epistemic achievement and the other merely a moral one, or whether that partition is itself what my friend and I are both assuming. Then take the two directions the novel gives us. Victor's side: he is not short of evidence — he hears the creature speak at length, understands the argument, is reported as being moved by it — and still treats the mind in front of him as something he may dispose of; say what the correct philosophical description of that failure is, and whether "he detected but did not acknowledge" or "he never detected" is the better account of the text. The creature's side, which is the part I'm least equipped to handle: he acquires language by watching a family he cannot approach and has to fix what his own words mean with nobody able to correct him, so he faces the single-case problem from the inside while also having to read human faces he initially misreads badly. Is he in the position the classic argument from analogy describes — a subject generalizing from one known case — or in some worse position that the literature has no name for?

There's one further wrinkle I want you to take up, because it seems to me to be the thing the novel could actually contribute. In the standard thought experiments the behavioural evidence is presumed to be *unmanipulated*; here the behaviour belongs to a being whose capacities, and in a sense whose very testimony, are the artifacts of the person who must judge them. If an appearance is produced by an agent with an interest in how it reads — or, at one remove, if the creature's eloquence reaches us only inside Victor's narration, and Victor's interest in how the creature reads shifts from scene to scene — does behavioural evidence stop being evidence? I take that as a genuine argument-shape rather than a paradox, and I'd like you to say both what it shows about inference to the best explanation and where it would overreach (does it threaten testimony from other human beings too, and if not, why not?).

In the last section, distinguish how confident you are in the interpretive claims from how confident you are in the philosophical ones, and name the single point you are least sure of.
