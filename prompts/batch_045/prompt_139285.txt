For two years I have been escalating a dispute with my condominium association over a water-damage assessment. The sum originally in dispute was about thirty thousand dollars; I have now spent more than twice that on lawyers and experts. I recognise the shape of the dollar auction in this, but the textbook version has neither of the two features that I think are actually moving me: a bylaw under which the prevailing party recovers a set fraction of its costs, and an insurer on the other side that assigns a new adjuster to the file every few months, each of whom at first appears ready to settle. I keep deciding to go one more round, and I cannot tell whether what holds me in is the structure of the situation or my own inability to stop, so I want a clean model of it.

Please construct a thought experiment to these specifications.

1. Realistic and present-day, ordinary stakes, one paragraph, no more than 250 words. No demons, no predictors, no unlimited bankroll on either side; the counterparty's conduct must be consistent with a plausible type rather than stipulated to be irrational.

2. Many rounds. In the branch you describe, the agent ends up having spent a multiple of the original amount at stake. At every round, on your numbers, continuing beats quitting on expected value, using forward-looking considerations only.

3. The psychological drivers must be switched off and indeed reversed: no audience to justify oneself to, no reputational value sourced from outside the case, no desire for vindication, no loss aversion or break-even seeking, and every social and emotional pressure in the story pointing towards quitting. The escalation must occur anyway.

4. The engine must be the fee-shifting rule: the winner recovers a stated fraction of its total spend, so the expected gain from continuing rather than quitting rises with the amount already spent. Give the numbers — amount at stake, cost per round, recovery fraction, and the probability that the other side concedes at this round — and set out the continue-versus-quit comparison at an early round and at a late round. Explain why this is not the sunk-cost fallacy.

5. The concession probability must be earned rather than hoped for: build into the case a reason it stays roughly flat as the matter ages instead of falling — rotating decision-makers, reassigned files, periodic budget review — and say what happens to the case if a single decision-maker's resolve accumulates instead.

6. There must be no identifiable mistake point: at no round, given what the agent then knew, was quitting strictly better than continuing, even though in the branch you describe conceding at the outset would have been best. Derive this from your numbers rather than asserting it. State honestly whether entering was positive in expected value overall; if it was, say plainly that the trap lies in the shape of the distribution rather than its mean, and say what that implies for someone in my position.

7. The test I will apply is the inherited file: a stranger handed the matter at round t, with no memory of having chosen it and nothing to justify, must reach the same decision on your numbers. If they would not, the case has smuggled the psychology back in.

Close by naming the feature that is doing the work; giving the verdicts when the recovery fraction is set to zero and when it is set to one; and giving the strongest objection available to someone who holds that escalation of commitment is always psychological, together with why that objection fails on your numbers.
