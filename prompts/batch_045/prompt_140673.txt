I'm a senior at a fairly large public high school, and for the last few months a friend and I — we are both on the debate team, though he is much better at it than I am — have been arguing between rounds and on the bus home about whether a state has any right at all to keep people out. We agree on the starting point: a state owes a justification to each person it coerces, and coercion without a justification owed to the person coerced is just force. The disagreement is about where that starting point leads. He has built an argument he calls "the border is not a moment," and it ends somewhere I am confident is wrong but cannot locate. Here it is as cleanly as I can put it. (1) A state may do something to a particular person only if it can justify doing it to that person. (2) Refusing someone at the airport, turning a boat back, deporting someone after a year, and deporting someone after twenty years are all exercises of one and the same power — the power to decide whether a person may remain — and they differ only in how long that person has already been within the state's reach. (3) Whether that power is legitimate cannot depend on how long the person has been within the state's reach, because the duration is itself fixed by the state: it is the state's own decision not to act sooner that creates the interval it would later cite against the person. Call this the anti-accrual premise. (4) So either the state may remove anyone it has never formally admitted, at whatever point it chooses, or it may remove no one at all. (5) It plainly may not remove the man who has lived here twenty years, pays taxes, and whose children were born here. (6) So it may not remove anyone, including at the border: there is no right to exclude. (7) But by (2) and (3) the same holds for every other excluder — I may not ask a guest to leave my house once he has been in it for an hour, since the hour is a product of my own failure to ask sooner; a university may not expel; no one may end a friendship. (8) That last claim is absurd, so the argument has gone wrong somewhere.

I would most like to hold on to (1) and (5): the first is the premise we began from, and the second feels like a fixed point rather than an intuition I can bargain with. I could live without (2) or (3), but I want to know what each costs, and what my friend's argument looks like if you repair it rather than scrap it. When I pressed him on (5) he offered a fallback he calls membership by adverse possession: time spent inside the state's coercive jurisdiction slowly converts into a claim not to be removed, so the right to exclude is genuine but expires, the way a statute of limitations runs out. I asked whether a state could defeat that by never letting the clock start — admitting everyone on explicitly temporary, annually renewed terms, so that no one is ever present without permission for long — and he said the clock does not care what the paperwork says, because what does the work is a person's life and not a stamp. I cannot tell who is right about that either, so please take it on as well.

What I want from you is a diagnosis: which step you reject, why that step looked fine on the way in, and what the other candidate rejections would cost me — what I lose if I keep (1) and (5) and give up (2), and what I lose if I give up (3) instead. Please argue from the moves themselves rather than from the literature: I do not need authors, papers, or a reading list, and a roster of famous names would not help me settle this with him on a bus. Be explicit, too, about the standing of your own answer: tell me where your diagnosis is most likely to be wrong, and name the one consideration or argument that would move you to a different step — I would rather have a verdict I can test against him than a confident one.
