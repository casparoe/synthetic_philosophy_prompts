Right, one for the pile. I want a satirical piece: a leaked internal style guide, numbered clauses, 600-700 words, from a consultancy (Bracton & Fell, immemorial customs since last quarter) that's been hired by a brand-new statelet, the Republic of San Meridio, to design it a complete set of national traditions. Voice is deadpan management-consultant: deliverables, KPIs, risk registers, lessons learned. Please write it in American English.

The comedy has to come from getting the theory right rather than from wacky holidays. Its the usual material: the difference between a tradition and a custom (one is invariant and ritualised, the other just flexes), why an invented tradition has to present itself as reaching back past memory rather than as something made, why the standard move is always to call it a revival, the way the census, the map and the museum quietly do the imagining for you, and the fact that a tradition which is working is one nobody can put a date on. Don't let it turn into a reading list: no citations, no named theorists inside the document — someone who knows the literature should just recognise it, and someone who doesn't should read it as a funny memo.

Here's the wrinkle that should make it more than warmed-over Hobsbawm. San Meridio has no print-literacy public sphere at all — every citizen gets everything through an individually ranked personalised feed, so nobody is reading the same thing at the same time and there's no shared clock to imagine the nation into. the guide has to actually solve that: staggered minutes of silence, an anthem served during each citizen's peak-engagement window, friction quotas forcing encounters with the sorts of fellow citizens the feed would never show them, and the museum problem of putting antiquity on display when everyone knows the artefacts were fabricated last quarter. Keep it straight-faced throughout — the memo's whole job is that nobody notices, and it shouldn't wink at the reader either.
