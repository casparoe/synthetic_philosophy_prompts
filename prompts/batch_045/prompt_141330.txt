I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about the 2006 International Astronomical Union decision in Prague, and I need a third party. The facts, so you don't have to rely on memory: Pluto was found in 1930 and was counted as the ninth planet for decades, though its mass turned out to be tiny; from the 1990s on, astronomers found more and more Kuiper Belt objects, and in 2005 Eris turned up, comparable to Pluto in size and mass. A 19-member IAU committee chaired by Iwan Williams had offered three candidate definitions — cultural (roughly, a planet is whatever enough people call one), structural (big enough to be rounded by its own gravity), and dynamical (big enough to have swept other bodies out of its orbital region). A separate committee chaired by Owen Gingerich produced a draft that would have kept Pluto a planet and treated bodies like it as a subcategory; it was voted down. What passed requires a body to orbit the Sun, be rounded by self-gravity, and have cleared its orbital neighbourhood — which Pluto fails, so it became a dwarf planet. Many planetary scientists rejected the outcome outright and still work with a geophysical definition instead. New Horizons flew past Pluto in July 2015 and returned images of a geologically complicated world, which did nothing to settle the argument.

My friend is a deflationist about truth and thinks the case is a triumph for her view. Her claim: if the equivalence schema is all there is to truth, then nothing in 2006 was discovered about Pluto at all — the only question was which sentence the community should adopt — so the episode was a verbal dispute wearing the costume of an empirical one, and deflationism is precisely what lets us see that. I said that a theory whose only contribution is to reclassify a practice's central disputes as merely verbal is confessing, not explaining. I also pressed her on a case she couldn't handle: an astronomer says 'Pluto is a planet' in 1995, and says it again in 2008. Same speaker, same object, same non-linguistic facts, and yet something about the truth of what he says seems to have shifted. She said that just shows truth isn't the interesting notion here; I said that's the sound of a theory retiring from the field.

So I want a proper two-way case analysis, not a survey of positions. First, a disciplined mapping: who the actors were (the committees, the IAU membership that voted, the planetary scientists who dissented, the textbook writers and planetarium curators and schoolchildren), what the actual options were, and what counted as evidence for each — orbital dynamics, mass and shape, population counts, pedagogical and cultural considerations. Then run the theory through it. Assume I already know the standard moves and vocabulary — disquotationalism, Horwich-style minimalism, prosententialism, blind ascription, the scope problem, the appeal to truth's expressive role, the norm-of-assertion and aim-of-belief objections — and don't recite them at me; deploy them. Specifically: (a) Say what you think about the 1995/2008 ascription, and what your answer costs. If the schema has to be relativized to times, speakers, or meanings to give an answer, does that relativization quietly reintroduce the substantive machinery about propositions, interpretation, and meaning that deflationism was supposed to make unnecessary — the kind of objection associated with Hartry Field, that thinness about truth is bought by paying for it in the theory of meaning — or is that just an honest division of labour between two theories? Take a side. (b) Consider the cultural candidate definition, on which a planet is a planet if enough people say it is. Had something like that been adopted, would the deflationist be committed to saying that the truth of 'Pluto is a planet' is constituted by a social fact, and is that a collapse into a consensus theory or only an apparent one? (c) Deal with the ascriptions that no amount of disquotation reaches — that what the 1995 textbook said was true, that everything the resolution implies about Pluto is true, that my childhood belief about the ninth planet turned out to be false. Say whether the expressive function deflationists invoke really covers them, or whether these ascriptions are doing explanatory work in the episode that a thin notion of truth cannot do. (d) Here is a distinction I invented in the argument and want tested rather than indulged: I called it a charter dispute — the parties agree on every non-linguistic fact, but the resolution fixes which concept an institution will charter, and in doing so changes what is true. Say whether deflationism can mark that distinction, or whether it must dissolve it, and whether dissolving it is a cost. Finish by telling me honestly whether the episode confirms deflationism, is an anomaly for it, or is a case it never anticipated — and, equally honestly, where the theory is simply silent and you refuse to make it speak.
