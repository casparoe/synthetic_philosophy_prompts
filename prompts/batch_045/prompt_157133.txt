I'm putting together the preregistration for a follow-up to my undergrad thesis — I'm in the second year of a cog-sci PhD now — and I keep going in circles with my supervisor about the design, so I thought I'd ask here. Short version: we're running a fairly large multilingual online survey of philosophical case judgments. Gettier-style cases, a couple of trolley variants, some of those side-effect cases where someone brings about a bad outcome as a side effect and people say he did it intentionally. Around twelve languages, native-speaker translation plus back-translation, comprehension checks, the usual. My flatmate thinks the whole project is unfalsifiable and I'd like to be able to tell her why it isn't.

The working plan is to treat the whole thing as a sampling problem: analytic philosophy has been leaning on the intuitions of a very unrepresentative slice of humanity, so the fix is to sample properly and then aggregate. Weight the responses somehow — we can't decide between weighting by population size, weighting by number of languages, or just equally per site — and take whatever the global majority says as the verdict. If the global majority disagrees with what philosophers have been saying since the 60s, then the philosophers update their theories; that seems to me like the entire point of the critique. I also want to add a short debiasing preamble before each vignette, a neutral rewording that strips out the cultural framing — honorifics, names that signal ethnicity, anything that might trigger a local script — so that what we end up measuring is the intuition itself rather than the cultural overlay sitting on top of it. And if it still varies after all that, I guess we just report that knowledge and intentional action are culturally relative and leave it there?

My supervisor says the design has a conceptual problem rather than a statistical one, and that I'm presupposing the thing the literature is actually arguing about, but she won't tell me what she means until I work it out myself, which is driving me up the wall. So, concretely: is the weighting question even the right question to be asking, does the debiasing preamble do what I think it does, and what am I supposed to conclude from variation if it shows up — relativism, or something else? One more thing while I'm here: we have budget for a single extra between-subjects condition for control items, and I was going to spend it on a non-philosophical item that we already know varies across cultures, something like a risk-preference or trust question, on the theory that if it varies too then response style is doing the work. Is that the right control, or am I about to waste the condition?
