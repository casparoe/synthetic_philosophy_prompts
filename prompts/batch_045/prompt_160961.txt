I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on the epistemology of legal proof, and before I build an argument on the practice I want its implicit epistemology reconstructed and the underdetermination made explicit.

The practice, which I'll describe rather than cite: factfinders in the jurisdiction I'm writing about apply three standards of proof. Ordinary civil liability needs only preponderance, more likely than not. A short, fixed list of allegations—fraud, civil commitment, termination of parental rights, professional discipline—gets an intermediate standard, and the pattern instructions for it seem to me to add something beyond high probability: it is not enough that the opposing story be improbable, the factfinder must be able to say why it is false. Criminal cases require proof beyond reasonable doubt. Two features pull in opposite directions. The intermediate standard looks partly stakes-driven and partly allegation-driven: the same dishonest conduct is harder to prove when pleaded as fraud than when pleaded as negligence, with identical damages, and appellate courts have said the elevated civil standard is required by the interests and stigma at stake. Yet the same courts insist the criminal standard is one fixed threshold, the same for shoplifting as for murder, and that it is not raised for murder because more is at stake.

What view of knowledge or justification does this structure presuppose? I can see at least four readings, and I want to know which of them the practice strictly requires and which it merely accommodates. First, a pragmatic-encroachment reading: the bar for knowledge itself rises with what is at stake, so the grading tracks something genuinely epistemic. Second, a contextualist reading: knowledge is stakes-invariant and what shifts is only the standard that a context makes salient for the truth of a knowledge attribution. Third, an invariantist-plus-decision-theory reading: knowledge never moves with stakes, and the grading is entirely a rule for allocating errors under asymmetric costs, so a standard of proof is a decision rule and not an epistemic notion at all. Fourth, a reading on which the tiers differ in kind rather than degree: the upper standards require the elimination or explanation of the relevant alternatives, not merely a higher credence, so the practice presupposes that epistemic standing has an anti-luck or explanatory dimension alongside a credal one.

Please reconstruct the implicit theory; separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it is merely consistent with, and say where it is left underdetermined so that several theories would license it; tell me whether the fixed-threshold doctrine for criminal cases is really consistent with the stakes-driven doctrine for civil ones and, if not, which half has to give; and say what would have to change in the practice—the wording of instructions, the allocation of issues to standards, or what we ask factfinders to do—if the third or fourth reading were substituted for the first. I also want to know whether the practice is committed to the claim that one and the same body of evidence can be sufficient for a civil finding and insufficient for a criminal one, or whether it can be redescribed so as to avoid that commitment.
