Me and a friend got into a shouting match after a decision-theory seminar last week about the centipede game, and I lost it badly — mainly because I was mixing up two different complaints — so I want to practice against someone who actually holds the other side. Please play a game theorist who thinks backward induction is right, and I want the strongest version, not a caricature: you hold that in any finite perfect-information game, given common knowledge of the game and of rationality, the backward-induction plan is the uniquely rational one; that the experimental pattern (people pass a few rounds, then take) is a fact about people failing the premises, not a problem with the argument; and that when the premises fail you don't scrap backward induction, you move to a model with uncertainty about the other player's type and backward-induct *that*. Rules: one short paragraph per reply, two at the very most, plain prose, no namedropping of papers at me (I definitly can't look anything up and it wouldn't convince me anyway); you may concede small things — that the premises are idealizations, that real players don't meet them, that the "what should I do if I somehow find myself at node 6" question is genuinely awkward — but never the main one; if you need a number from me, ask for it at the end, but answer first anyway. Stay in character until I type STOP.

Here's the game, and I'm handing you the version that's supposed to be the easy one for you. A pot starts at $100. We alternate, I move first, eight decision nodes total, both of us know all of this. At your node you either TAKE — 80% of the pot to you, 20% to me, game over — or PASS, the pot triples and it's my move; I have the same two options. Nobody deliberates at any node: before play each of us seals a complete contingent plan in an envelope ("if the game reaches node k, I do X") and a referee walks the tree and executes whatever the envelopes say. So neither of us ever gets to stand at node 5 and say "well, I'm here now, so much for common knowledge" — the only real decision is which envelope to submit, once, ex ante. Backward induction says both envelopes say "take at my first node": I take at node 1, I walk away with $80 and you with $20. If we both passed all the way to node 8 the pot would be $218,700, the player who takes there gets about $175k, and even the one who gets stiffed walks away with $43,740 instead of $20.

My opening: I don't need much to beat your conclusion, and that's the bit I want you to deal with. At my node with pot V, taking is worth 0.8V; passing is worth 0.6V if you take next and 7.2V if you pass back to me, so passing pays as long as I think there's a bit more than a 3% chance your envelope says pass here. Three percent. And notice that your side's answer doesn't move: whether the pot doubles, triples or grows tenfold per pass, backward induction tells me to take at node 1 for $80, for exactly the same reason, every time — while my threshold slides toward zero as the growth rate climbs. So either you say my credence that your envelope passes must be literally zero, and then you owe me an account of what makes common knowledge of rationality an infinitely strong constraint on my probabilities, given you've already granted me that real people violate it all the time and that two such people are filling in these envelopes; or you grant me the tiniest sliver — trembles, altruism, confusion, the chance you misread the rules — and at a tripling pot I should pass, and probably pass several times. Which is it, and why isn't the first answer just the argument congratulating itself?
