hey — I'm giving a 40-minute talk in two weeks at our cognition lab's joint seminar with the philosophy department (they do it once a term), and I could really use a proper Q&A prep sheet rather than another round of "have you considered the rock objection".

The thesis: phenomenal consciousness is constituted by a higher-order representation, but I'm defending a deliberately deflationary version of what the higher-order state has to be. I don't require a judgment or belief that one is in some state; I require what I call an "availability-tracking" meta-state — a non-conceptual, action-guiding state whose content is roughly *this is currently available to me, I could hold onto it and use it* — and I let the whole thing be diachronic: what does the explanatory work is the tracked availability of the first-order state across a short window, not a punctate act of inner observation. Two extra moves I'm hanging a lot on. First, the "coldness" worry — the sense that higher-order accounts turn experience into a kind of self-observation — I answer by saying the meta-state is implemented in the very same perceptual systems as its target and inherits their format, so it doesn't add a second layer of content that the subject has to inspect; call it piggybacking. Second, and definately the most fragile part, I want to handle dreams, which I think is the nastiest case for any higher-order view: my move is "retrospective endorsement" — the dream isn't conscious while it's happening, it becomes conscious when, on waking, the memory of it is taken up by the same availability-tracking system, which is why we all talk as if there was something it was like to have had it. I'd rather say that than bite the bullet and deny that dreams are conscious experiences, but I'm not sure I've thought through what that move costs me elsewhere.

The room is about twenty people and I can picture who talks. Two-thirds are empirical consciousness researchers: psychophysicists and cognitive neuro people who work on metacognition and type-2 signal detection, run no-report paradigms and decoding studies, and who mostly think of themselves as adjudicating between global-workspace-style and integrated-information-style theories — they'll want to know what my view predicts about measurable metacognitive sensitivity and about where in the brain the meta-state lives, and they will not be impressed by armchair intuition-mongering. The philosophy side is three people: a self-representationalist who thinks the first-order state can represent itself and that I'm multiplying states for no reason, a disjunctivist/naive-realist about perception who is suspicious of the whole representationalist framing and thinks I can't even state what needs explaining, and one person who routes every question back to the explanatory gap. I already know the anti-physicalist objections I'd get at a general philosophy conference; I don't need those. What I need is what *this* room will actually say, in their vocabulary, ordered by how much trouble it makes for me — not by how famous the objection is.
