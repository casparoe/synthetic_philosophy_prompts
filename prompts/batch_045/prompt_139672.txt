I am writing a short critical note on imprecise probabilities, and I need a worked example to carry one step of the argument. The step is this: a set-of-priors representation cannot be combined, for free, with the obvious sequential decision procedure of conditioning every prior in the set and then maximizing minimum expected utility. I want a case that does real dialectical work, not one an opponent can wave away as a gerrymandered credal set. Please build it to the following specifications.

The shape of the case. One agent, one hypothesis H with two values, one experiment the agent is about to perform with two or three possible outcomes, and a decision whose payoffs depend only on H. Performing the experiment must be free, and its result must not itself affect anyone's payoff, so that whatever happens is purely informational. Keep the space small enough that I can check every computation by hand: two values of H, two or three outcomes. The setting should be ordinary, the sort of thing that comes up in a clinic, a workshop, a field trial, or a shipment from a supplier: no demons, no perfect predictors, no infinite sequences, no null-event or measure-zero trickery, and stakes an ordinary person could actually face. The experiment should also be the sort of thing any of us would unhesitatingly describe as evidence about H.

Specifications the scenario has to meet.

1. The imprecision has to be earned, not stipulated. Do not hand me a credal set. Describe what the agent knows: how the setup was produced, what the evidence is, what it leaves open. The set of probability functions compatible with that description is then the credal set. In particular, the agent's uncertainty should concern the evidential bearing of the experiment on H and not merely the base rate of H. Tell a story in which the agent has good reason to be unsure which way the evidence points.

2. The prior interval for H must be narrow, at most 0.1 wide, and narrow for a reason that is visible in the story. A point prior is permitted, and if you can get one, so much the better.

3. Genuine and robust dilation. After conditioning on the result, whichever result it is, the posterior interval for H must be strictly wider than the prior interval at both ends, by at least 0.2 at each end. A prior interval of 0.45 to 0.55 would have to widen to 0.25 to 0.75 or wider, and to something at least as wide under every possible outcome of the experiment. The agent must not be able to hope that the experiment comes out the helpful way.

4. The dilation must survive replacing the credal set by its convex hull. Show this explicitly, so that the effect cannot be dismissed as an artifact of a non-convex set.

5. Decision relevance, in the direction that is hard to get. Give the agent a small menu of acts: a bet on H, a bet against H, and a safe act with a certain modest payoff will do. It must be the case that before the experiment the maximin act is strictly better than the safe act, while after the experiment, under every possible outcome, maximin strictly prefers the safe act. So the agent would pay a positive sum to be forced to choose before seeing the result, and would pay a positive sum not to be told the result at all. The contrast I want alongside it: for every probability function in the credal set, the corresponding sharp Bayesian strictly prefers to look before choosing. Give me both sets of numbers.

The analysis I want attached to the scenario.

First, the computation: the prior interval, the conditional interval for each outcome, and the maximin values before and after, with enough of the arithmetic shown that I can audit it. Include an explicit check that no single probability function in the credal set reproduces the dilation, so that the effect is irreducibly a feature of the set.

Second, the diagnosis. Tell me which feature of the case is doing the work, and state it at the level of structure rather than at the level of the example: what the priors in the set must disagree about, and what they must agree about, for the effect to arise. Then remove it. Change one thing in the story so that the priors no longer disagree in that way, and show that both the dilation and the decision anomaly disappear.

Third, the honest note on the response. My expectation is that a defender of imprecise probabilities will surrender the decision rule rather than the representation, and will say that the defect lies in maximizing minimum expected utility after conditioning rather than in the set of priors. Say what that response costs, whether an alternative rule actually dissolves the case, and where the argument then stands. Consider specifically maximality in the sense of admissibility with respect to the set, and the policy of choosing a full plan ex ante and then updating without re-optimizing; if one of these avoids the problem, say which and at what price. If you think the right response instead is to reject the credal set that your own story generates, say that and defend it.

Last, two short items. Say whether the case can be turned into a sure-loss sequence of trades against the agent, and if it can, sketch the sequence; if it cannot, say what blocks it. And tell me which of the specifications above was hardest to satisfy jointly with the others and what you had to trade off to meet it, since that is where I expect the real cost of the example to be hiding.
