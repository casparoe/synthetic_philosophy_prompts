For the past month I have been the person my cousin calls when her violent ex starts asking mutual friends where she is, and twice I have had to decide what to say to someone I believed would use the answer to hurt her. That has made the Kantian murderer-at-the-door example newly pressing, and I have been trying to see how far the duty of truthfulness really extends. Here is an argument I built from the claim I take Kant to be making; each step looks fine to me, but the conclusion is monstrous, and I cannot tell whether the argument is invalid or valid with a premise I should give up.

The claim I start from is that a lie is an intentionally untruthful declaration to another person, and that it is wrong not merely because of the harm it causes but because it damages the common source of right; so the wrongness does not vanish when the hearer is a murderer or when the truth would be used to kill.

(1) The wrong-making feature of a lie is its damage to the common source of right, not its production of harm to some person.
(2) So the wrongness of a communicative act does not vary with whether the hearer deserves the truth or with the consequences of telling the truth.
(3) So, as far as the duty of truthfulness is concerned, what matters is only whether the speaker intentionally departs from what the speaker believes in what is declared to another.
(4) A declaration is not the only way to make another person believe something false: one can also do it with a literally true sentence expected to convey something false, with a deliberate omission where an answer is expected, or with a refusal whose meaning in the circumstances is that one is hiding something.
(5) If the duty protects the shared practice of communication, then any intentional production of a false belief in another through communication corrupts that practice as much as an outright false statement does.
(6) So the duty forbids not only lying but also misleading truths, strategic omissions, and evasive silences that are conventionally understood as answers.
(7) In the case I actually face, the murderer has asked me where she is and has said that he will treat my silence as confirmation that she is in the attic, and that any refusal or evasion will be taken the same way; in that context silence, refusal, and evasion are not ways of not answering, they are declarations that she is in the attic.
(8) A literally true but incomplete answer, such as saying that she is not in the attic, is forbidden as a strategic omission of the location he asked for.
(9) Since lying is forbidden, and every alternative to stating the true location is either a forbidden declaration of a falsehood, a forbidden evasion, or a forbidden omission, the only permissible answer is to state the true location.
(10) Therefore the duty of truthfulness requires me to tell a murderer where his victim is hiding.

I cannot accept (10). Which step do you reject, and why did it look fine? If the argument is valid, which premise is least costly to give up? I would rather keep (1) and (2) than (4) or (5), but I want to know what I am paying either way. Please also say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to reject a different step instead, or where your own diagnosis is most likely to fail.
