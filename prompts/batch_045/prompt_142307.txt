I've been working through Hofstadter's discussion of the location of meaning — the three-level distinction among the frame message, the outer message, and the inner message — and I want to test it against a case he doesn't use: the decipherment of Egyptian hieroglyphs in the early nineteenth century. The history I'll stipulate, so that nothing turns on contested detail: the stone recovered during Napoleon's Egyptian campaign carries a single decree in three scripts, hieroglyphic, demotic, and Greek; early-modern readers before it, Kircher pre-eminently, had read the glyphs confidently as direct symbols of ideas and produced fluent, edifying translations; Young made progress by treating the ringed cartouches as containers for royal names and recovering a handful of phonetic values; Champollion, leaning on Coptic, got the system in the 1820s, and it turned out to be mixed — phonetic signs alongside determinatives and ideograms — rather than the purely symbolic system the earlier readers had assumed.

What I want is a disciplined two-way analysis rather than a retelling. First the mapping: at each stage, which level is genuinely in hand and which is missing; what the scheme says a competent decoder should have been looking for at each point, and whether that resembles what the participants actually looked for; and whether the pre-Champollion readers had the frame message but lacked the outer one, or lacked the frame message as well — is the claim that these marks are writing the same claim as the claim that there is a message here for which I lack a decoder? Then press on two places where I suspect the framework is too clean. One is bootstrapping: the phonetic values were recovered partly by guessing what the inner message must say, a king's name inside a cartouche, and the outer message was then inferred from that guess, so the stone looks less like a delivered key than like a device for breaking a circle; does the outer/inner split survive when the receiver has to construct the decoder instead of being handed one, or is it an artifact of codes supplied by their sender? The other is the wrong-key case: a false outer message yielded a perfectly fluent inner message that nothing internal to the glyphs could have refuted, which suggests to me that the frame message is nearly empty and that almost all the epistemic work sits in the outer message. And if the frame message is what tells you there is a message, what told anyone here — my guess is that it was inferred from properties that are not themselves messages, the repeated cartouche-shaped rings and the three parallel scripts, in which case the frame message is not a message at all but an inference. Say whether that collapses the distinction or merely relocates it, and close by applying whatever you conclude: for a script that is still unread, the Indus Valley script, say, which level is the one actually missing, and what kind of evidence would settle the question?
