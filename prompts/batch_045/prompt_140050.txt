Last night I argued with a friend until well past one in the morning about a question I now have to settle in public on Saturday, and the argument convinced me of at least one thing: the question is real. What makes every *a* an *a*? More precisely: is there anything about a shape, considered on its own, that makes it an *a*, or is a glyph an *a* only in virtue of the slot it holds in a working system of letters that a reader already carries around?

On Saturday I am giving the send-off toast at the retirement party of Marta Reyes, who has hand-lettered signs in this city for forty-four years; the shop closes at the end of the month. There will be about seventy people there: her three apprentices; a gilder, a pinstriper, and the man who sandblasts granite; her husband, her two daughters, and four grandchildren; the owners of four of the storefronts she has lettered; and one friend of hers from the design school, who will be the only person in the room who has ever used the word ontology in earnest.

My friend Jonas holds that there is a core relational structure a glyph must realize in order to be an *a* — a bowl, a stem, an aperture, roughly the right proportions at x-height — and that style is whatever fills in the remaining degrees of freedom. I defended the view Marta herself holds: that nothing about a shape in isolation makes it an *a*; it is an *a* by holding the third slot in an alphabet, which means being parted where *o* is closed, lacking the crossbar of *e*, having no ascender to rival *d*, and being distinct from every other letter in the system at once; and that the judge of this is whoever reads it. Before we gave up we agreed on two further things. First, that you cannot identify the letter first and the style afterwards; the letter and the style arrive together or not at all. Second, that a machine which generated a novel letterform in the style of an existing typeface would not thereby have produced an *a*. Whether it had depends entirely on whether readers take it as one — uptake, not provenance.

What I need from you is a five-minute toast that takes my side of that argument without ever naming it, and that does the following.

It cannot praise her for approaching, reaching, or capturing the true *a*, or the letter itself, or any such thing. On my view there is no such shape for her to have come closer to.

It cannot fall back on the other available compliment either — that each of her letters was unmistakably and inimitably hers — if that is meant as a property of the glyph. On my view recognition is a relation between a mark, a system, and a reader, so any praise has to be phrased relationally.

It has to be a compliment, and it has to land for her daughters, who want to hear that their mother was superb at something and who will not enjoy being told that her achievement is not safely stored in the objects she made. On my view it is not stored there; it is stored in the practice of people who can still read a painted letter. The toast has to make that the generous version of itself rather than the bleak one: not that the work is fragile, but that the room is its custodian. And it should therefore end in something the room can do or say together, not merely in good wishes.

It should contain one close, concrete description of a single letter, written as if I were pointing at it. I would suggest the stencil *a* she cut for the municipal trucks: a double-storey form with a bridge across the top of the bowl, where the bridge is not really a bridge at all but the strip of metal left in place so the stencil would hold together, and which everyone in the city reads instantly and without effort. If you prefer, use the single-storey *a* on the bakery window whose tail runs into the *k*, or the hairline-serif *a* on the funeral home's granite — but use one of them, and let it carry the argument rather than ornament it.

No terms of art: no ontology, category, prototype, schema, contrast set, semiotics. The design-school friend should nevertheless be able to tell, by the end, which side of last night's argument the toast comes down on, while the grandchildren are neither bored nor puzzled. No mention of the argument, of Jonas, or of philosophy; no hedging and no apology for the oddness of the thought. It is a party.

Aim for seven hundred to eight hundred words, closing on a line the whole room can raise a glass to. If you judge that some one of these constraints cannot be met without my saying something I have just said I think is false, write the toast as well as you can and then add a short note saying which constraint you would drop and why.
