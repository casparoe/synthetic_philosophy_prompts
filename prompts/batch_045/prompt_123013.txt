I run a small, lightly modded sandbox server for about a dozen friends — the usual block-building survival game, where a decent cathedral or a fully enchanted pickaxe represents tens of hours of work — and we have been arguing about the ban policy, which keeps sliding into an argument about whether anything that happens there really matters. I have firm gut verdicts on individual cases that I cannot fit into one story, so I would like you to do the reflective-equilibrium work for me rather than to tell me what the literature supposedly says.

Here are the cases and my verdicts. (1) A spends months building a cathedral in a region where the server rules forbid raiding; B burns it down with a torch. I think this is a genuine harm to A and a serious wrong, not mere rudeness, and A's distress is not silly. (2) The very same destruction, but on an unclaimed plot in a region the server advertises as raid-allowed, and A knew that when building. Here I think nothing wrong has happened at all — that is just the game, and A should not be compensated. (3) B transfers A's unique pickaxe into B's own inventory; versus B uses a duplication bug, so A still holds the pickaxe but there are now two of an item whose entire point was that there was one. Theft strikes me as much worse; duplication strikes me as cheating, and only mildly annoying — yet what A cared about, its uniqueness, is destroyed either way, so I cannot say what the difference is. (4) B copies A's build block-for-block onto another server and passes it off as his own; I find this irritating but not a harm, because A still has hers. (5) In a haptic virtual-reality world, B makes an unsolicited sexual gesture at A's avatar, with force feedback but no contact between bodies; I think this is a real violation, clearly worse than verbal taunting, clearly not as bad as physically groping someone. (6) Killing a scripted quest-giver that respawns after a minute is nothing at all; permanently killing the companion creature A spent weeks raising is a real harm to her, even though the creature was never anything but code. (7) Taking a thousand units of currency in a game with an officially sanctioned real-money exchange feels like theft to me; taking the same amount in a game where trading for money is banned and no working grey market exists feels wrong, but I cannot tell whether I think it is theft, and this is the pair I keep changing my mind about. (8) The admins roll the server back from a backup ten minutes after the cathedral is burned and everything is restored; I still feel A was wronged, and I cannot point to the setback, since nothing was finally lost. (9) A owns a castle in a region of the map that no client has ever rendered — it exists only as rows in a database — and I have no hesitation calling it his. (10) The operator's terms of service say every item is the company's property, and this seems to me completely irrelevant to whether B wronged A. (11) The operator announces that the servers shut down in thirty days; destroying the cathedral now feels a little less bad to me, but only a little, and I don't trust that reaction.

My tentative principle is something like: a virtual thing is real in the sense that matters when it is the object of someone's ongoing project and is embedded in a practice in which others treat claims on it as valid; harming someone with respect to it is a matter of setting back that project, in proportion to how irreversible and how hard to replace the thing is; and a background norm of accepted risk can make the very same setback into no wrong at all. I suspect that principle quietly conflates two questions — whether virtual objects are real and when virtual harms are bad — and that cases (3), (4), (8) and (10) pull it in different directions, since some of my verdicts seem to track loss to the owner and others seem to track the violation of a claim whether or not anything is lost.

Please state one principle, or a small set of candidates, precisely enough that I could apply it without you in the room: give each clause together with the case that motivates it, and be explicit about which of my verdicts each candidate cannot accommodate and which you think I should overturn. Then tell me what your best candidate predicts about cases I have not listed — an item the owner had privately decided to abandon and never use again but has not deleted; a companion creature whose dialogue is generated on the fly and seems to remember past conversations; griefing that the victim never finds out about; and a griefer who turns out to be a contractor paid by the operator to make the world eventful. If the right answer is that no single principle covers all of this, tell me where the line between the genuinely distinct questions falls and what my verdicts have to look like on each side of it.
