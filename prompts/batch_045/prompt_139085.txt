I'm a senior at a public high school writing an independent-study paper on whether Mill's harm principle gives a workable standard for speech. I've read *On Liberty* once, I have no university library access, and I'd rather not be pointed at books and articles I can't obtain. What I actually want is an audit of my own position, because I can't locate the seam in it myself: every item below seems right to me taken on its own, but I have a persistent feeling that two or three of them are pulling against each other, and I genuinely don't know which one I'd abandon if forced.

Here is where I've landed:

1. Baseline: only harm to someone's important interests justifies restricting speech. Offense, outrage, discomfort, or feeling unsafe in the sense of feeling unwelcome is not harm. In particular there is no heckler's veto: if the only obstacle to a speech is that opponents would react badly, the right response is to police the opponents, not the speaker.

2. Security-cost exception: a school or a student group may cancel an event when protecting it against credible threats of violence would require disproportionate security. What is being prevented here is injury, not the content of the views. A friend tells me this is a heckler's veto in disguise; I don't think it is, but I'd like that checked rather than assumed.

3. Accumulation clause: many individually trivial acts — mass-reporting an account so that it is automatically suspended, a thousand separately mild but coordinated hostile messages — can sum to real harm. When they do, each contributor's act is regulable even though no single one of them would be, taken alone.

4. Verifiable falsehoods: claims about checkable public matters, such as how votes are counted or what an authentic video shows, may be restricted even when nobody can name a particular person whom the claim harmed.

5. Counterspeech as the default remedy: the answer to bad speech is more speech, and removal is the wrong default. At the same time, I think it is acceptable for a platform to invisibly reduce the reach of lawful but borderline posts — nothing deleted, no label, no notification to the poster; the post exists, but far fewer people are shown it. I'll call this quiet sunsetting. I prefer it to deletion because it seems less heavy-handed.

6. Platform neutrality: a large platform should not be permitted to remove lawful speech, and the fact that it is a private company is not by itself a moral justification. Yet I also defend the right of a small religious school to dismiss a teacher over speech the school regards as incompatible with its mission, and I'd defend a student club's right to choose its own members.

7. One case verdict: a synthetic video depicting a private classmate in a sexual situation, clearly labelled as fabricated, viewed by very few people, none of whom believes it is real, and causing her no measurable downstream loss, should still be a criminal offence.

Please treat this as a consistency check rather than a lecture, and please discriminate: I'd rather you tell me that one apparent clash is merely apparent than have all of them flagged. Work through the commitments pairwise, order the genuine tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad, and for each one say precisely what I would have to give up and what that would cost me elsewhere in the set. I'd also like you to flag any item that my own stated principle cannot actually deliver, and to tell me if one commitment is doing hidden load-bearing work for the rest — I suspect one of them is holding more up than I've noticed, and I can't tell which. Please don't simply restate Mill back at me.
