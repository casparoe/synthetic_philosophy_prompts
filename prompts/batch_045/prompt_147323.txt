Two colleagues in my department have asked me to referee a disagreement about a variant on Twin Earth, and since I work on adjacent material but have no settled view on this particular case, I would rather hand the job to someone with no stake in it.

The case, which I take to be new: Amara is an ordinary Earth speaker. Over roughly three years, without her noticing, the clear drinkable liquid in her environment is gradually replaced by the Twin Earth liquid — the proportion shifts by about one part per thousand per day, and no test available to her distinguishes the two. She uses "water" throughout, for drinking, cooking, and so on, and never detects a change. Call it the cross-fade case.

You asked me to referee, and I honestly couldn't. Two colleagues of mine — call them A and B — got into it over a variant on Twin Earth that I hadn't seen before. I'll reproduce the exchange as best I can and I'd like you to adjudicate it move by move.

A (externalist): The cross-fade raises nothing new. Reference is fixed by actual causal-historical contact, so the honest verdict is that during the fade her term simply becomes indeterminate — it doesn't determinately pick out the Earth stuff, the Twin stuff, or the mixture. That is a prediction, not a reductio. Vagueness and indeterminacy are ordinary features of natural-kind terms; there is no more reason to demand a sharp verdict here than in any other borderline case.

B: That misses what is distinctive. Ordinary vagueness is traceable to something — blur in our classificatory dispositions or looseness in the world. Here her dispositions are perfectly sharp, each candidate (the Earth stuff, the Twin stuff, any mixture) is equally natural, and the theory still has no verdict. An indeterminacy that lives purely in the theory's output is a defect in the theory rather than a discovery about the term.

A: Two replies. First, the demand that indeterminacy always be traceable to speaker-side or world-side blur is unmotivated: reference is a relation between a word and the world, and if the world's causal structure doesn't single out one candidate, the relation genuinely fails to hold determinately. Second, B is wrong that nothing is detectable. Put Amara at the end of the fade and ask whether this is the same stuff she called water three years ago: she will say yes. Which just shows she can be wrong about her own environment. Nothing follows about incoherence.

B: That is exactly the real issue. What externalism delivers here is systematic, undetectable error about the content of her own thoughts: a subject who is fully rational, reflective and attentive, who is nevertheless wrong about what she is thinking, and for whom no procedure could reveal it. A notion of content that permits this has stopped doing the work content attributions were introduced to do — rationalizing a subject's thought and action from her own point of view. The fix is to pry apart the reference-fixing story from the thing that explains thought and action: a speaker-accessible, narrow component carried by her inferential and recognitional dispositions. Those stay constant through the fade, which is just what we want, and it is no answer to say the reference story churns.

A: The two-component picture is unstable. Once you grant a narrow component constant across the fade, you owe an account of why the original Twin Earth intuition — that the Earthling and his duplicate mean different things — is anything more than a fact about reference, and on B's own view the duplicate's thoughts do not differ. And the self-knowledge charge is overstated: content is partly constituted by the subject's own judgments, which is what blocks wholesale error in ordinary cases. That machinery doesn't engage in the fade, but the fade doesn't obtain, so nothing follows about actual self-knowledge.

B: "Doesn't obtain" is not an answer, since counterfactual cases are precisely where we test a theory of content. And the constitution move cuts against A: if content is partly constituted by the subject's own judgments, then Amara's confident judgment that the stuff is unchanged should partly constitute her content — yet A's own verdict says it doesn't. One cannot invoke constitution to secure self-knowledge and then ignore it the moment the environment is what's moving. Besides, A already concedes that content drifts in the familiar slow-switching cases, so A cannot treat constancy as non-negotiable; the fade is only the continuous limit of a concession already made.

I have also wondered whether the case is simply ill-posed — whether, once the cross-fading begins, there is any such stuff as the water for her term to have been introduced to, so that the description never specifies a possible situation — and I'd like your view on whether that dissolves the dispute or merely relocates it.

Please go through the exchange point by point: rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification, and say who is more right overall. Then say what you take the disagreement to really be about — whether the cross-fade is well posed, whether theory-generated indeterminacy is a genuine cost, whether a narrow component can bear the explanatory weight B assigns it, whether constraints from self-knowledge are a desideratum on a theory of content at all — and what, if anything, both parties are missing.
