I've got a position I keep defending in arguments and I want it stress-tested. The claim: nothing can really ground itself, so every tangled hierarchy is only apparently a loop — there's always an inviolate level outside it doing the work (in Drawing Hands, that's Escher, or us looking at the page). I've started calling the thing I'm denying cyclic grounding: A supplies what's missing in B, B supplies what's missing in A, and the pair hangs together with no bottom and no regress. My argument is just that mutual dependence spreads a regress over two items instead of stopping it, and that fixed-point tricks (self-referential sentences, programs that print themselves) always smuggle in an encoder or interpreter from outside. My fallback test is the unroll test: if you can spread the loop out in time, or into two applications of the same rule, it was never cyclic — which is how I handle the flip-flop cases people throw at me, two gates each holding the other's output, by pointing at propogation delay and the physics underneath.

What would count against this? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and seperate the ones I'd have to concede from the ones critics just repeat. Is the unroll test doing all the work, and does it beg the question? And be honest about whether I've set things up so that nothing could ever count as cyclic grounding — whether I'd actually accept a system with no story to tell about an outside level, or just go hunting harder for one — and if I have insulated it, whether that's fatal or just shows it's a conceptual thesis rather than an empirical one.
