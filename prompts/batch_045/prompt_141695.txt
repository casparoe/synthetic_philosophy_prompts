Everywhere I've driven, speeding law has two layers: a hard number on a sign, and a catch-all that lets an officer ticket you for going too fast for the road, the weather, or the traffic even when you're under the number. And the number on the sign isn't really the number — everyone knows officers allow a margin over it, and speed cameras come with a stated tolerance because the reading itself isn't exact. I keep running into an argument about vague words like "fast" or "bald" where one side says there's a sharp cutoff we just can never locate (the "epistemic" view, I think?) and the other says there's no fact of the matter in the middle cases until someone picks a way of making the word precise (supervaluationism?). This looks like a real institution that has quietly taken a position on all of that at once.

So: does the two-layer setup presuppose that there is a determinate speed at which driving becomes too fast, with the sign as a merely useful proxy? Or does it fit better with the idea that the cutoff is made by stipulation and the penumbra left genuinely open? Does the published camera tolerance and the informal grace suggest the system thinks it is tracking a fact it can't quite see, or building one? And are the hard number and the catch-all even consistent — isn't the first bright-line and the second contextual?

Here's the version I actually want you to chew on. Suppose a city left the 30 sign standing but changed enforcement: each day it secretly draws the operative threshold from somewhere between 31 and 44, publishes only the range, and applies it by camera with no discretion at all. On the "sharp boundary we can't know" picture, every driver that day either did or didn't break the law and simply can't find out which. On the "no fact until you precisify" picture, there's nothing to be right or wrong about until the draw happens. Tell me which story the practice strictly needs and which it merely sits well with, whether the two pictures really license different enforcement or just different descriptions of the same enforcement, and what a city that genuinely believed the second story should do differently. Bonus: the edges of the range are drawn just as arbitrarily as the original cutoff — is that the higher-order version of the same problem, or a different one?
