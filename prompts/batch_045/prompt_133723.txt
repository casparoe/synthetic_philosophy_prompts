I am a software engineer by training, with no formal philosophy background, and I teach one section of a second-year course called Discrete Structures for Computing as an adjunct. I am writing this term's final and I want one question on infinity that cannot be passed by reciting the diagonal argument, restating the Hilbert's hotel story, or writing in the margin that the whole thing depends on which axioms you accept.

What my students have actually seen: dovetailing arguments for the countability of the integers and the rationals; the diagonal argument for the uncountability of the reals; the statement of the halting problem and the bare existence of Turing machines; and exactly one lecture on Hilbert's hotel, in which I made a joke about an autoscaling group that never needs to reallocate. They have had no philosophy of mathematics, and I do not want the question to presuppose any. Cantor, Hilbert and Turing are names they know; please do not require them to know any other name, and please make the question self-contained, with any term you use either defined in the question or already in my syllabus above.

I would like the question built around three moves, which I sketch below. Please turn them into a properly worded exam question rather than pasting my sketch in, and feel free to reorder or combine them if that makes for a better paper.

First: potential versus actual, and an honest accounting of where the paradox is supposed to be. I want students to distinguish a hotel that at every stage has finitely many rooms but can always be extended by one (my autoscaling analogy, or a lazily evaluated infinite list in a functional language, where the program is finite syntax and what it denotes is infinite) from a hotel that has all of rooms 0, 1, 2, ... at once; to see that the difference is a quantifier-order difference and that only the second hotel can house a countably infinite coach party, at no finite stage of the first hotel's life; to say what would have to follow for there to be a genuine contradiction here, rather than a clash between two explications of same number of elements, one of which is one-to-one correspondence and the other of which is the part-whole principle that a proper subset is smaller; and to register that the part-whole principle failing for infinite sets can be read off as a definition of infinitude rather than as a disaster.

Second: the trap I actually expect strong students to fall into. I have a half-formed hunch that the computable reals are countable, because there are only countably many programs, and yet that no program can enumerate them, and that this pair of facts is the crux of something. The version of it I expect on the exam paper goes roughly like this:

  Cantor's argument only shows that no computer program can list all the real numbers. But the set of computable reals is countable, since programs are countable, and a real that no program computes is not something we could ever specify anyway. So there is no real conflict: the reals are countable as far as anything we can actually get our hands on is concerned, and Cantor's theorem is a theorem about the limits of computation rather than about size.

I want the question to force students to sort this out precisely: to prove that there are only countably many computable reals; to prove that there is no computable enumeration of the computable reals, by making the diagonal construction effective relative to a computable enumeration; and then to say what follows, namely that countability and computable enumerability are different properties, that cardinality claims quantify over all functions and not only over rule-governed or programmable ones, and that combining countability of the computable reals with Cantor's theorem yields the existence of reals that no program computes.

Third: locating the finitist's cut. Please put a character in the question, defined entirely within it, who grants the natural numbers as a completed infinite totality and grants classical reasoning about them, but refuses to treat the collection of all functions from the naturals to the naturals, or equivalently the reals, as a completed totality over which quantifiers range determinately. I want students to identify exactly which premise of the diagonal argument that character is refusing; to say what the argument becomes once the refusal is in force, namely a schematic result about any purported rule or specification that we can always produce, by an effective construction, something it misses; to take a position on whether that is a weaker result or whether it was where the content of Cantor's theorem always lay; and to say whether the character has the resources to state her own view without quantifying over the very functions she declines to totalize. I would also like the question to press on an asymmetry my better students will notice on their own: the discomfort about the hotel is normally discomfort about concrete or physical infinity, whereas the diagonal argument is a piece of pure mathematics, so it is not obvious that a person who finds the hotel incredible owes any opinion at all about Cantor's theorem, and vice versa.

Deliverables, please:

1. The question itself, with a short preamble of three or four sentences that I can print above it to stop students from answering part one with a retelling of the hotel story. Parts labelled, marks shown against each part, 25 marks total, intended to be answerable in about 75 minutes.

2. A model answer of roughly 800 to 900 words, written in a register I could hand to a second marker as an anchor. If there is a standard name for a position or a move you use, you may mention it in parentheses so I can read further on my own, but the model answer must not depend on it, and I do not want any bibliography or references: everything must be checkable from the question and the answer alone.

3. A marking scheme as a table: one row per required move, the marks it is worth, and a single line on the specific way students typically get that move wrong or half-get it. Follow it with a paragraph drawing the A/B boundary explicitly, naming what an A answer does that a B answer does not, and a sentence on what a C answer looks like.

4. If any part of my sketch above is mathematically wrong or philosophically naive, please tell me briefly and quietly correct it in the question, rather than building the exam around my mistake. I would much rather find out now than at the marking stage.
