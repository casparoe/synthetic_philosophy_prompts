Here's the post's core argument, as close as I can reconstruct it (paraphrasing, but I don't think I'm changing anything):

The rule is simple: don't maximize expected money, maximize the expected logarithm of your wealth. And it isn't one option among many, it's forced — anyone not doing it is making a mistake you can prove. Two results pin it down. First: as the number of bets grows, a Kelly bettor ends up richer than someone following any other fixed rule, with probability approaching 1, and anyone on a different rule either goes broke outright or falls behind by so much that it amounts to the same thing. Second: there's no tradeoff here you could reasonably take the other side of, because betting more than Kelly lowers your growth rate and adds risk at the same time, while betting less than Kelly just hands the difference to whoever is on the other side of the bet. So geometric rationality isn't a temperament or a risk preference; it's what rationality means once you notice that wealth compounds. Maximizing expected value is a habit left over from one-shot puzzles, and the reason it misleads is that it averages over parallel copies of you that don't exist. The standard demo makes the point: a coin flip that multiplies your wealth by 1.5 on heads and 0.6 on tails has positive expected value — a big crowd playing it averages about 5% growth per flip — while essentially every individual who keeps flipping watches their own balance go to zero. Expected-value reasoning isn't merely aggressive there, it's answering a question nobody asked. One more reason to trust the rule: the fraction it spits out doesn't depend on how much money you have, since your net worth cancels out. That's the signature of a genuinely rational rule — any other utility function would change its advice as your bankroll changed, which is just arbitrariness. And because the rule is scale-free it generalizes past gambling: how much of the portfolio in one name, how risky a career move to take, how much to give to charity this year. Which is why fractional Kelly — betting half or a quarter of what the formula says, the way every fund claims to — is cowardice with a story attached: you pay in growth and get nothing back.
