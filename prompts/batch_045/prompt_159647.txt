Her position, which I am not asking you to adjudicate and which I will not say whether I share, was roughly this: that "right but early" is only ever a courtesy we extend to famous dead theorists; that if a long-run prediction has to be saved by pointing at something the theory never represented, the rescue costs the theory whatever content it had; and that the honest verdicts are therefore exhausted by two options — the mechanism is real but the forecast built on it was wrong, or the mechanism was never engaged in the first place. She added a distinction I have found harder to shake: a Malthusian mechanism that is overridden from *outside*, by something the theory says nothing about, is rescued in a different way from one that is relieved from *inside*, because on the second story the very pressure the theory describes is what calls forth the innovation that relieves it, so the theory's own dynamics produce the escape and it never really predicted a catastrophe at all. She also insisted that no theory can be "early" unless it carries a clock — a rate, a threshold, or a deadline — since otherwise there is no observation that would tell us we are living through a lag rather than a failure.

**Question 4 (as set).** In *An Essay on the Principle of Population* (1798) Malthus argued that population, when unchecked, increases geometrically while the means of subsistence increase at best arithmetically, and that societies are accordingly held near subsistence by positive checks (famine, disease, war) and preventive checks (delayed marriage, celibacy). In the two centuries since, both population and output per head have risen enormously across much of the world.

(a) Distinguish three verdicts on the theory: that its mechanism is false; that its mechanism is real but was overridden by a countervailing factor the theory did not represent; and that its mechanism is real but its trigger conditions were not met. Explain how, if at all, the claim that Malthus was right but early differs from each of them.

(b) The standard defence is that Malthus could not have foreseen the Industrial Revolution or the twentieth-century rise in agricultural yields. Does appealing to such a factor render the theory unfalsifiable? State what would have to be observable, and by whom, for "right but early" to be a substantive claim rather than a tribute.

(c) Does the demographic transition — the fact that richer societies now have lower fertility — support Malthus, refute him, or neither? Explain which part of his mechanism, if any, it bears on.

**Candidate's answer.**

(a) The post-1800 record does not refute Malthus, because refutation would require the mechanism he described to be absent, and the mechanism is not in dispute: before the Industrial Revolution higher real wages did produce faster population growth, which pushed wages back toward subsistence, and the positive check bound whenever harvests failed. So the theory can be wrong in only one of two ways: either the mechanism was overridden, or it was never triggered. These are quite different. If the trigger conditions — a fixed supply of land and an unchanging technique of cultivation — were never met, then Malthus was not early at all, since there is nothing for a theory to be early about if the situation it describes never arose. The charitable reading of "right but early" must therefore be the first of these: a real tendency, correctly identified, which a contingent historical development happened to mask. And because the mechanism is visible in the series that economic historians have assembled for the centuries before 1800, with wages and population moving together in just the way Malthus said, we can say that he identified a real mechanism ahead of the evidence available in his own day, which is what "early" means.

(b) The defence does not make the theory unfalsifiable. An escape clause is ad hoc only when it was devised after the fact purely to absorb an awkward observation, and the Industrial Revolution is not a postulate of that kind: it is an independent historical fact, established by sources with no stake in the theory. A theory rescued by an independently established auxiliary is thereby supported rather than emptied, and it remains falsifiable because it still predicts a definite relationship for any population that lacks the countervailing factor. What makes "right but early" substantive is precisely that the mechanism is independently measurable: we can go and look for the wage–population relationship in pre-industrial data, we find it, and that is the confirmation the claim required.

(c) The demographic transition supports Malthus rather than refuting him, because it is the same relation with its sign reversed. Malthus's claim was that income governs population; today we observe that richer societies choose smaller families, which is the same dependence of fertility on income running the other way under different conditions. A mechanism that can be observed with opposite signs in different circumstances is a real mechanism, and a theory that identified the relation at all deserves credit for it. What the transition shows is only that the relation is not fixed in sign, which qualifies Malthus's arithmetic without refuting his mechanism.
