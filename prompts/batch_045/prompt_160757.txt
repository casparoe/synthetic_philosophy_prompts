Compare two accounts of what it is for a property to be metaphysically fundamental.

The carving account: a property is fundamental just in case it is perfectly natural, i.e. it marks a genuine respect of similarity among things rather than an arbitrary or gerrymandered grouping. Whether it is fundamental does not depend on what is grounded in what.

The basing account: a property is fundamental just in case it figures in some minimal complete basis, that is, in some set of facts which collectively ground every other fact, none of whose members is grounded in the others, and from which nothing can be removed while preserving completeness.

The two accounts normally deliver the same list. To separate them, consider a possible world W whose total stock of facts admits two distinct minimal complete bases, one framed in particle-like properties and one in field-like properties, where the facts of neither basis are grounded in the facts of the other, both bases are equally economical, and there is no further fact of the matter available about which set carves nature at its joints. Treat this as a coherent metaphysical possibility and set aside what actual physics says.

Which account handles W better, and why? Concretely: does the right theory of fundamentality permit fundamentality to be non-unique, as the basing account seems to, or does the possibility of duplicate bases show that fundamentality cannot be identified with basing relations at all? Indicate how confident you are in the verdict you reach and identify the single premise you are least confident in.
