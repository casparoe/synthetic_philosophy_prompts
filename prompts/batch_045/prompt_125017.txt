I'm writing my final paper for a Philosophy of Social Science seminar on whether a long-run prediction can be "right but early" rather than merely falsified, using Malthus as the case, and I'd like your help with the part where I confront him with evidence he never saw.

The reading I want to defend is that the Malthusian claim is not the bare forecast that population will outrun subsistence, but a claim about a homeostatic mechanism: gains in productivity get converted into extra people rather than lasting wage gains, because higher real incomes raise net fertility. On that reading the demographic transition did not refute the mechanism so much as disable it, by reversing the sign of the income–fertility relation. So the question is about the historical Malthus — the author of the 1798 Essay, working with the preventive check, the positive check and moral restraint, and knowing nothing of what came after.

Suppose we hand him three posthumous facts: the very large increases in agricultural yields over the last century; the demographic transition, including below-replacement fertility in the richest societies; and the decline in the global population growth rate since its peak in the 1960s. For each, how do you think he would respond, and which of his responses would be a legitimate auxiliary adjustment rather than an ad hoc rescue? In particular: could he read falling fertility as the preventive check operating through a new channel — a quantity–quality trade-off in children, say — without emptying the theory of content? And is there any later development, such as constraints on the environment's capacity to absorb waste rather than on arable land, that he would claim reopens the window his mechanism was meant to operate in?

Please take the facts one at a time, and finish by saying which of the three he could not honestly absorb: that is what I need for the methodological conclusion about what "right but early" would have to mean.
