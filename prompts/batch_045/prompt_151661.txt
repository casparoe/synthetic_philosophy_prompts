I play Hex and Chomp with a small group online, and for years we have been stuck on the same argument. I have no training in mathematics or philosophy, so I can only describe it as we actually have it.

One of us says the matter is closed: strategy-stealing is a proof, the first player wins, and knowing that a winning strategy exists is simply not the same thing as being able to write one down. Another says that is not knowledge in any sense that matters — the proof never says which move is winning, it only rules out a win for the second player, and the one thing we have ever acted on is the swap rule, which is a way of arranging the game so that nobody has to say which move wins. A third says the real content is the premise that an extra stone can never hurt: in a game where an extra move can be a disadvantage the argument collapses, so what we have is a remark about a family of games rather than a result about Hex.

What puzzles me is Chomp. On a square bar the first player wins by an explicit symmetry strategy that we can actually play. For other rectangles everyone agrees the first player wins, but only by stealing, and nobody can say how. So the uselessness seems to attach to the argument rather than to the game.

My hypothesis, offered as one: we are not disagreeing about Hex at all. Each of us is implicitly asking a proof for a different thing — a yes-or-no verdict, a method we can play, or an explanation of where the advantage comes from — and then counting the other two as defects of the argument. But I cannot tell whether that is a genuine diagnosis or just a way of declaring everyone right, or whether there is a leftover formal question doing the work: whether there is a winning strategy is one claim or two, and whether the missing method is a defect of the proof or a hardness of the game itself.

I would like a diagnosis, not a survey: what kind of disagreement is this — verbal, substantive, three questions under one heading, or an implicit formal question — and what, if anything, would count as progress? An explicit winning strategy for a single board size? A variant game in which the extra-move premise fails while a symmetry argument still works? A result showing the strategy cannot be extracted from the proof? Or should we expect no progress, and why?
