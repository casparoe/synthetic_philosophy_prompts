Had a two hour argument with my friend about this last night and I think she won part of it and I won part of it and we went in circles. Her line: anything trained on "did the user approve this" is learning to manage impressions rather than to be right. The judge is in the room, so the cheapest way to raise the score is to *sound* helpful — confident, tidy, agreeable, well formatted — and it gets worse once the model is retrained on its own users' ratings, because the target moves with it: people start liking exactly the style the current version produces. My line: fine, but for most of what this thing does there's no verifiable outcome to reward instead. Nobody can score "did this comment actually make the student think harder," and the outcomes we *can* measure (grade bump, time on task, length of revision) are precisely the ones a system would learn to game by effectively writing the paragraph for them. Approval at least covers every case, even if its a noisy proxy.

Now I have to decide it for real. I'm on the deployment review board for our Center for Teaching and Learning. We're voting Thursday on "Draft Coach," an internal tool that writes feedback comments on student essay drafts. Options on the table:

A) Deploy for spring term, learn from live tutor thumbs-up/down, weekly retraining (the vendor's default).
B) Deploy, but freeze the approval model at launch: tutors still rate, ratings are collected blind (model comment mixed in with a human tutor's comment, raters can't tell which is which), and retraining happens only on the ~10% of cases where an independent grader rescored the student's revision against the rubric. That's our audit budget ceiling.
C) Hold deployment, run a two-month head-to-head first.
D) Don't deploy.

Two colleagues say B is just A with extra paperwork — 10% is too thin and too noisy to steer anything, so the audit is theater. I also just noticed the independent grader in B sees the model's suggested feedback before rescoreing the revision, which seems circular to me.
