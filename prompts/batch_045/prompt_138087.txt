The topic is quantum immortality, and I want to get past the version of the argument people have already had to death. The standard moves are familiar: under the Everettian picture there is always some branch in which the experimenter survives, so from the inside it can look as though survival is certain; third-personally nothing changes, most of the surviving amplitude is tiny, and so the quantum suicide setup gives no one else any evidence for anything; and there is a standing dispute, in the style of the self-sampling versus self-indication disagreement, about how to distribute first-personal credence across your own successors after branching. I've read all of that and I don't think more of it settles anything.

Here is the half-formed idea I want turned into a proposal. I suspect both sides are treating one scalar, the Born-rule measure of the live branches at a time, as the single quantity at issue, when there are really two claims being run together. The first is a continuity claim: at every time there exists at least one successor of me that counts as alive, even if its measure becomes arbitrarily small. The second is a measure-tracking claim: my first-personal expectation is proportional to that measure. Continuity alone supports no prediction I could act on, only the existential claim that some descendant survives; the interesting quantum immortality conclusion needs continuity plus something like measure-indifference about successor selection, the thesis that within one agent's successor set, what I should anticipate is insensitive to branch weight. A lot of people reject measure-indifference in the abstract and then help themselves to it the moment they say you should expect to survive.

The wrinkle I want to build the project around is what I've been calling the infirm-successor problem. Real dying isn't the idealized instantaneous quantum gun; it's a continuous hazard process under aging, disease, and small repeated risks. If measure-indifference is right, then the long-run content of my survival is fixed by whatever keeps the survival tail alive longest, and the tail is disproportionately populated by degenerate states: intact brainstem without cortex, locked-in states, extreme life-prolonging intervention, advanced dementia. And there is a dilemma nearby. The continuity claim needs a permissive enough criterion of survival that some descendant always qualifies, but the more permissive the criterion, the more the surviving tail consists of states most people would rank as worse than death; tighten the criterion and continuity itself can fail at some finite time, which drops the result into the relatively unexplored question of whether there is a last time at which any descendant of me satisfies it. I want to know which horn is worse for the view, and I don't think anyone has said.
