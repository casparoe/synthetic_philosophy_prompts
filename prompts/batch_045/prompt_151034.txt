For the past year I have been part of a small reading group — two value theorists, a decision theorist, a philosopher of language, and myself — working on whether "better than" is transitive. We are stuck in a way I find diagnostic rather than accidental, and I would like a structural account of why, not another tour of the standard arguments.

The case we keep returning to is one I built myself. Call a population P(k): 10^(3+k) people, each at welfare 100 − k, for k = 0 … 99. So P(0) is a thousand people with excellent lives and P(99) is 10^102 people each with a life barely above the neutral point. Compare adjacent steps: P(k+1) has ten times as many people, each of them worse off by one unit, and nearly everyone in the room judges it better than P(k). Compare the endpoints directly: nearly everyone judges P(0) better than P(99). Transitivity applied ninety-nine times says the opposite. No one dissents from either set of verdicts and no one has changed their mind about what follows from them.

I also built a fine-grained variant with the same endpoints: 9,900 steps, each multiplying the population by 10^(1/100) (about 2.3%) and lowering everyone's welfare by 0.01. Something interesting happens there — some of us find the step verdicts getting weaker as the steps shrink, others do not, and the difference seems to track how vividly one holds the endpoints in mind while making the local comparison.

The room has settled into four positions, none of which has moved in twelve months. One: transitivity is constitutive of a betterness relation, so the step verdicts are right and the endpoint verdict is a response to salience rather than a judgment. Two: nothing cycles at all; the relation is indeterminate over long distances, each verdict is sound relative to a precisification, and no single precisification spans the chain, so the step from local verdicts to a global ordering was never licensed. Three: betterness really is intransitive, we should abandon the idea that value supplies an ordering and think directly about pairwise choice, and then confront the cycling and money-pump worries honestly. Four: the chain equivocates — "a better outcome, all things considered" and "a better thing to bring about" and "better for someone" are different comparatives, and the argument runs on the slide between them.

I have two hypotheses, which I offer only as hypotheses. First, that the dispute is largely about what a comparative verdict is *for*: whether its job is to rank options against one another, in which case transitivity looks non-negotiable, or to say which of two presented things to prefer, in which case there is no reason to expect transitivity and the money-pump complaint simply changes the subject. Second, that the cycle is a symptom of value plurality rather than a fact about a single relation: each verdict tracks a mix of numbers, quality and equality, with weights that drift along the chain, so that "better than" behaves less like one binary relation on outcomes and more like a family of contrast-relative comparisons that cannot be composed.

What I want is a diagnosis with reasons. Is this a verbal dispute, a substantive one, several questions under one heading, or bedrock intuition that neither side can argue for? Is a hidden question doing the work — about vagueness, about the semantics of gradable comparatives, about what the concept is supposed to do, about whether values are commensurable on a single scale? Which of the four positions in the room is occupying which of those slots, and is any of them confused rather than merely opposed? Tell me which of my two hypotheses does real work and which is only a redescription of the standoff. And given your diagnosis, say what would actually count as progress here — a distinction between indeterminacy and genuine cycling, a formal result showing what structural assumptions force transitivity, something quasi-empirical about how verdicts behave as steps shrink and contrast sets are held fixed, or a change of question — or argue honestly that nothing will, and say why.
