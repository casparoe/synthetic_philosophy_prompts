I write a column on science and ideas, and my inbox is full of readers convinced that Gödel's incompleteness theorems settle the fate of artificial intelligence — usually in the more careful form in which the anti-mechanist conclusion is offered as a disjunction: either the mind outruns every formal system, or there are arithmetical questions we can in principle never settle. I would like a one-page decision tree, publishable as a graphic, that lets a reader work out which of these positions she actually holds.

Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names. Each node should be a single commitment, phrased so that someone with no prior stake can answer it without knowing where it leads: whether the argument is run on what human beings actually manage to prove under real limits of memory, attention and lifespan, or on an idealized capacity that abstracts from them; whether the reasoner must be able to identify which system she is and establish that it is consistent and sound; whether being an inconsistent or merely unsound system counts as a genuine escape or as a cost too high to pay; whether the reasoning generalizes to a machine programmed to perform the same self-application, so that a system could in principle come to accept the truth of its own undecidable sentence, or whether it secretly leans on something irreducibly human. Add one branch the standard treatments tend to skirt: whether the argument, if it works, also works against modelling a mind by a system whose axioms are allowed to grow over time, and note what happens to the tree if it does.

Give me the leaves labelled by claim rather than by author, with the familiar names in parentheses: Lucas–Penrose-style anti-mechanism, the mechanist who concedes that if we are a formal system we cannot prove which one we are, the mechanist who accepts that we may be inconsistent, and the deflationist who holds that nothing about minds follows at all. Mark any combinations of answers that nobody occupies and say briefly why — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored — and flag any pair of leaves your nodes cannot in the end separate. No history-of-the-debate preamble and no citations; I want the instrument itself.
