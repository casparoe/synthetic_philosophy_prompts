A cognitive neuroscientist being interviewed on a podcast summarized global workspace theory, and its bearing on the distinction between access and phenomenal consciousness, roughly like this:

Global workspace theory says that a mental state becomes conscious when the information it carries wins a competition for a brain-wide bottleneck and gets broadcast to the rest of the system — to memory, to language, to motor planning. That broadcast is what being conscious consists in; it is the difference between everything your brain registers without your noticing it and the one content you are aware of at a time. This is also why the theory handles the distinction philosophers draw between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness so cleanly: phenomenal consciousness is just the having of the experience, access consciousness is the very same experience being globally broadcast and made available, so access is where the explanatory work gets done and phenomenal consciousness is simply what is left over once you subtract it. The theory is not armchair either — it predicts the data. Masked words and unseen images are processed quite deep into the cortex but never ignite; when a stimulus does ignite, you get the late sustained activity in prefrontal and parietal cortex and the P300 wave, and at that point subjects can report the stimulus and are conscious of it on every measure available to us. So consciousness, in the only sense science can get a grip on, just is global availability, and what people call the hard problem is the insistence on an extra fact — the what-it's-like — that makes no difference to what the organism does or says. There is no room for such a fact in the theory, and the neural signature of reportability is the neural correlate of consciousness.

I may want to use some of this in a short seminar presentation, so I'd like to know how much of it I can repeat without wincing later.
