Write me a dialogue — say a page or so — between an origin essentialist and someone who thinks the necessity-of-origin thesis is just false. I want both of them good: no strawmen, real back-and-forth, and each of them should concede at least one thing that actually costs them.

The essentialist holds the Kripkean line: for any organism, originating from the particular gametes it in fact came from is essential, so a zygote grown from any other sperm and egg would not be me — there's no saying what I'd have been like, because I wouldn't have been. The opponent holds a closest-continuer view: in a counterfactual situation, "me" is whoever's body, psychology and life continues my actual life most closely, and which gametes did the originating is only one input among several.

Don't run them through the standard cases everyone's seen. I made up three I'd rather they chew on: (1) the same sperm and the same egg, but a single base pair corrected in the zygote at the one-cell stage — same originary matter, a slightly different genome; (2) the reverse case, a different sperm and a different egg whose combination happens to yield a nuclear genome identical to mine — different originary matter, the same "instruction set"; (3) a monozygotic split, where the zygote from those very same gametes divides at the two-cell stage and it's the other cell that gets implanted and goes on to live the life I actually live.

Make them answer each other's actual moves on each case — in particular, whether the essentialist really has to say the baby in case (1) is a different person, and whether the closest-continuer theorist can still explain why talk of origins feels so load-bearing when we say things like "if my parents had picked a different donor, I wouldn't exist". British English, please, and end with a last line beginning Verdict: that sums up in one sentence who had the better of it and why.
