Last night my friend and I got into a huge argument about whether LLMs understand anything. He's team stochastic parrot, I'm more on the fence, and after he went home I tried to write his argument down as fairly as I could. Now I think it proves way more than he wants it to and I can't see where to cut. Here it is:

(1) Everything anyone has ever had as evidence for saying a system understands something is behavioral — what it says and does, in the situations we've actually put it in and the ones we could put it in.

(2) For any finite batch of behavioral evidence E, it's possible in principle to build a system that produces exactly E while understanding nothing: a giant lookup table, a nearest-neighbor fallback over stored continuations, whatever. Call that a mimic for E.

(3) If a mimic for E is possible, then E can't rule out that the system producing it is a mimic.

(4) The total behavioral evidence we'll ever have about any real system — a person, a model — is finite. Nobody gets infinite evidence.

(5) So for any real system, there's a mimic hypothesis that our evidence can never rule out: for all we know my friend is one, for all we know the LLM is one.

(6) If you can't rule out that a system is a mimic, you're not justified in saying it understands.

(7) So nobody is ever justified in saying that anything understands anything — not LLMs, not my friend, not me about myself yesterday.

(8) My friend tries to stop short of (7): he says the chain only shows LLMs don't understand, and that for humans we get an extra premise, namely that other people are built like me and I know firsthand that I understand. But that extra premise either gives me more behavioral evidence (same build, so same behavior — which (5) already covers), or it's an inference to the best explanation from behaviour-plus-structure, and if that kind of inference is allowed for humans it's hard to see why it's banned for machines.

Here's the bit I keep getting stuck on, which I made up on the walk home. Imagine two systems, Ada and Bertha. On every prompt any human will in fact ever type they produce identical outputs. They differ only on "off-ramp" inputs nobody will ever actually give them — a million repetitions of one word, English transliterated into some invented alphabet, prompts written in a made-up base-64 scheme. On those, Bertha emits garbage and Ada keeps going fine. Bertha is just a curated table with a nearest-neighbor fallback; Ada has something that generalizes. Now: if understanding is behavior, Ada and Bertha are indistinguishable, so either they both understand or neither does. If Bertha's competence is a mile wide and an inch deep and that's not understanding, then whatever Ada has that Bertha lacks is the thing that matters, and it shows up only where no evidence will ever reach — which is exactly what (1) says can't matter. That can't be right either.

So: which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I have to choose I'd much rather keep (1) and (4) than (7), and I'd also like to keep that Bertha doesn't understand while staying agnostic about Ada, but I want to know what I'm paying for that. Also please tell me where your own answer is most likely to be wrong — what argument or consideration would actually move you off it.
