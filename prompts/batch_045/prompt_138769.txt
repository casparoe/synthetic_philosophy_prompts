I am assembling a short taxonomy of the escape routes from Aumann's agreement theorem — the ways in which that theorem's conclusion can fail while its assumptions remain, in some reasonable sense, nearly satisfied — and I should like your help scoring them.

Recall the result: if two agents share a common prior over a finite set of states, and at the actual state it is common knowledge between them that their posterior probabilities for a given event are a and b, then a = b. Sustained disagreement between honest, well-informed agents is therefore supposed to be impossible in principle.

Here is a variant I have been turning over. Two agents share a common prior over a finite state space. Both are exact Bayesians, both entirely honest, both know the other's information partition and the protocol, and there is neither a cost to communicating nor a limit on the number of rounds. The single departure from the ideal case is that the channel is coarse: in each round both agents simultaneously announce their current posterior, but the message space is a fixed grid — say the multiples of 0.05 in the unit interval, each agent reporting the grid point nearest to her true posterior. My tentative claim is that this does not, as one might first expect, open the door to agreeing to disagree. The announcements still converge and settle on a common value, but the agents' exact posteriors can settle on different values lying inside the same rounding interval, and neither agent can ever detect this. On this telling the resolution of the channel bounds the residual disagreement rather than defeating the theorem: the verdict becomes agreement to within the grid spacing, and agreement of that weak a kind is compatible with substantial real disagreement if the grid is coarse.

What I want from you:

1. Distribute one hundred points of explanatory credit across the following eight diagnoses of persistent disagreement among honest, well-informed experts, according to how much of the phenomenon each actually accounts for. Give a number for each, and one sentence of justification.

   a. The priors simply differ; there is no common prior for the theorem to invoke.
   b. Diminished awareness: the agents are not aware of all the relevant states or hypotheses, so there is no shared space over which a common prior could even be defined.
   c. Different propositions: the disputed sentence is given different truth-conditions, or different operational surrogates, by the two agents, so there is no single event whose probability is in dispute.
   d. Imprecise or non-probabilistic attitudes: credence takes the form of a set of probabilities, or of comparative confidence, so there is no single number to be common knowledge.
   e. Bounded rationality: the posteriors are not computable in practice; what gets reported is a heuristic, and updating on another's report is lossy.
   f. Channel poverty: posteriors are never exchanged as numbers at all — arguments, assertions, or observed behaviour are exchanged instead — or the exchange is capped at a small number of rounds.
   g. Infinitude: on an infinite state space or a continuum of hypotheses the exchange need not reach a fixed point after finitely many rounds, so disagreement survives at every finite stage.
   h. Insincere or strategic reporting.

   Some of these are not independent of one another: a pair of them may be two descriptions of one underlying mechanism, in which case splitting credit between them double-counts. Say which pairs you take to be redundant in this way and how you have handled that in your arithmetic.

2. Sort the same eight into three tiers: does real explanatory work; does work only in special settings; relabels the problem rather than explaining it.

3. Re-score them inside my stipulated variant, where a common prior, finiteness, honesty, exactness and unlimited rounds are all granted by assumption. Which diagnoses are switched off entirely, and which survive? If only the coarseness of the channel is left standing, say how much work it can do given that the residual disagreement is bounded by the grid spacing — is it a serious rival to (a), or a rounding-error footnote?

4. Check the claim itself. Is the argument for the grid-spacing bound sound, and where, if anywhere, does it break — with more than two agents, under a different tie-breaking rule, when posterior probabilities sit exactly on grid boundaries, when the two agents announce posteriors for different events, or when each learns from the other's actions rather than declarations? If the claim holds, give the smallest worked example you can construct that exhibits it.

5. Name the single item in your distribution that you would expect a specialist to contest most strongly, and say what one consideration would move you to redistribute points away from it.
