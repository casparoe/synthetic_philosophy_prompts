I teach an upper-level seminar that sits between philosophy of mind and animal ethics, and I am writing this term's take-home final. Candidates have ninety minutes and a fifteen-hundred-word limit. By the time they sit it they will have covered the usual material on the distribution question: the argument from analogy, the treatment of animal pain as an inference to the best explanation, the claim that sub-cortical or midbrain structures are sufficient for experience in vertebrates, the claim that flexible trade-off-sensitive behaviour is the real marker, and the precautionary reasoning that is supposed to carry us from uncertainty to practice. I want one question that cannot be passed by laying that menu out in good order.

Here is the case I want the question built around, which you should feel free to tighten but not to change in its essentials. Species M is a hypothetical social invertebrate with a nervous system of a few hundred thousand neurons. Intact members of M do everything that friends of invertebrate consciousness point to: they learn to avoid a cue paired with a noxious stimulus; they trade that avoidance off against other goals in a way that varies with how badly they need the goal; they reverse a learned response when the contingencies change; they show a pessimistic judgement bias after a period of unpredictable stress; and they will work for a substance that acts as an analgesic in vertebrates, doing so more after injury than before. Now a second condition. A small, pharmacologically dissociable cluster of neurons, call it the hub, supports sustained long-range synchronised activity that correlates with exactly the flexible behaviours just listed and with nothing else. When the hub is blocked, the animal still withdraws from noxious stimuli, still mounts the endocrine stress response, still acquires simple conditioned avoidance, still shows the pessimistic judgement bias, and still works for the analgesic substance after injury. What it no longer does is trade avoidance off against competing goals, reverse a learned response when contingencies change, or modulate its nocifensive behaviour by context. Call the first profile intact-M and the second blocked-M.

The crux I care about is this. Every theory a student might bring to the case predicts its own dissociation pattern, so the case looks decisive to whoever has already settled which theory they accept, and looks like a mere redesescription to everyone else. I want students forced to notice that, and then forced to say what would have to be added to the case for it to discriminate between theories at all. I also want the epistemic part to constrain the practical part, rather than the two running in sequence.

Please produce three things.

First, the question itself, in two linked parts, self-contained so that a student could answer it from the case as stated plus the reading described above, with no need to name authors or papers. Part (a) asks what the dissociation between intact-M and blocked-M does and does not show about whether blocked-M has experiences. Part (b) asks the student to suppose that a lineage closely related to M makes up a large share of the invertebrate biomass handled in agriculture, so that the number of individuals any policy touches is many orders of magnitude greater than the number of vertebrates it touches, and to suppose further that reasonable people who have examined the evidence all end up with credences somewhere between 0.05 and 0.6 that members of the lineage have experiences. Part (b) should ask what follows for how we treat them, and should require the student to name the decision rule they are using.

Second, a model answer of roughly seven hundred words that takes a definite position on both parts rather than surveying the options. It should be written in the voice of a very strong student, not of an instructor.

Third, a marking scheme worth twenty marks, built around five must-hit moves at four marks each. One move should concern the bridging premise from the behavioural and physiological profile to experience, including what kind of fact could support such a premise and why the premise is shaky even in humans, where report is available and still comes apart from the behaviour. One should concern whether the case can adjudicate between competing theories, and what would have to be true for it to do so. One should concern whether blocked-M is best described as a clean negative or as a degraded or partial case, and what follows about whether the distribution question admits of degrees and about where a sharp taxonomic cutoff would have to fall. One should concern the decision rule in part (b), with the marks going to the student's reasons for that rule rather than to the rule's name. One should concern the objection that whatever rule the student has chosen generalises to systems they would not want it to generalise to, and whether their rule has a stopping point.

For each move, add a one-line note on the way candidates typically fail it. Typical failure modes I have in mind include plugging a single theory into the case and reading the answer off; treating every behavioural marker as equally probative; helping themselves to a sharp line between taxa that merely needs discovering; closing the practical part with an unanalysed call for caution; and assuming that because report is our gold standard, the absence of report is evidence of absence. You may use these or better ones. Finish with two or three sentences specifying what separates an A script from a B script: my own view is that the B script does the two parts in sequence and never lets the epistemology do any work in the practical part, while the A script identifies which feature of the epistemic situation forces the choice of decision rule and says what would change its mind.
