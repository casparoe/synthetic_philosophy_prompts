Bit of background first: I'm a retired hydrologist — forty years of stream chemistry, mostly boring, occasionally not — and over the last year I've drifted into a philosophy reading group at the library. Last month we did a session on lying and I came away unable to say what I actually think, so I want to try it backwards: gut verdicts first, principle afterwards. And please answer in British English — organise, colour, that sort of thing.

Here's what I find myself saying, off the top of my head.

My neighbour's six-year-old shows me a drawing and asks if I like it. It's a mess. I say it's lovely. Not a problem at all; I'd do it again tomorrow.

A phone scammer pretending to be from my bank: I make up a sort code and give him that. Fine, and not even slightly wrong.

Telling my GP I have two drinks a week when it's more like ten. Not fine, but small.

The one that started all this: in the nineties our field crew told a landowner we were doing a conservation survey for the state when we were actually doing preliminary sampling for a mining company. Not fine, and I still feel sour about it thirty years on — worse than the GP one, and I don't think that's just about the size of the lie.

My wife is in hospice. She asked whether her nurse would be back in the morning; the nurse had said maybe, maybe not. I said yes. Fine, and I'd defend it against anyone in that group.

Letting something false stand: a new neighbour introduced me at a party as a professor. I'm not one. I let it go. Barely registers as a moral event.

But: in a methods section, writing a sentence that is literally true of my data while choosing the phrasing precisely so that readers will draw a false conclusion about sample size. That feels like a real lie to me, and worse than the professor thing, even though nothing I wrote was false.

And the bit I can't shake: someone in the group said Augustine's line is that what makes something a lie is the intention to deceive, not whether what you say is false — so a false statement you honestly believe isn't a lie at all — and that once it is a lie, it's a sin, full stop. On that reading my wife and the farmer come out the same, and my professor case doesn't come out at all. I think the first half is right and the second half is nuts, but I can't say why.

So: is there one principle — about what counts as a lie, and about what makes a lie permissible when it is — that gets close to all seven? I'd like it stated tightly enough that I can test it, and then: which clause is doing the work in which case? Which of my verdicts can it not accommodate, and which one would you have me drop? And run it on two I haven't decided: lying to a voice assistant I know transcribes into a shared family log, and telling a friend a truth he begged me not to tell him.
