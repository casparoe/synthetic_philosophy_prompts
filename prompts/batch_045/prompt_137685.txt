So I'm helping out on a first-year module with a philosophy-of-literature half and a film-studies half, and my seminar next week is on Blade Runner. My co-teacher is a hardline actual intentionalist — she thinks the meaning of a work is fixed by what the author actually meant, full stop, and that everything else is just interpretation history. We've done Wimsatt and Beardsley and the Hirsch-style move of splitting meaning from significance, and a bit of hypothetical intentionalism, and I want a case that puts real pressure on all three rather than another round of the same examples. Blade Runner looks perfect because there isn't one work and there isn't one author: there are something like seven versions floating around, the theatrical release had Harrison Ford's voiceover and the drive-into-the-sunshine ending pushed in by the financiers while Scott had no final cut at all, the Final Cut is apparently the only version where he did have control, Rutger Hauer is on record saying he slashed Batty's dying speech the night before shooting without telling Scott, Scott has said in interviews that the unicorn daydream plus Gaff's origami unicorn means Deckard is a replicant, Fancher has said he wrote Deckard as human and only wanted the possibility floated, and Dick's novel has Deckard plainly human and passing the empathy test. I've been playing with two views of my own and I can't decide between them, so I'd like you to try them on for size: call the first artifact accretionism, the idea that whatever ends up on screen is meaning-constituting however it got there, so studio notes, an actor's unauthorised rewrite, an accident of lighting, and a cut nobody had final say over all count equally, and the author is best thought of as just one causal channel among many; call the second authority-filtered intentionalism, the idea that only the intentions of whoever holds final creative authority over a given version count, everything else is noise or at best evidence, and later commentary from the same person is a separate act of criticism rather than a fixing of meaning. There's also a third option I keep circling, which is that meaning is version-relative and asking what Blade Runner means is like asking what a word means across two unrelated languages — in which case half the seminar question dissolves and I'd rather know that than pretend otherwise.

What I actually want is a reading that takes the film seriously and not just as a handy illustration. So: run those three options against the specific bits of the film's history — the voiceover and the imposed happy ending, Hauer's rewrite of Batty's last speech, the unicorn and the origami, Deckard's apartment full of old photographs, Rachael asking whether he ever took his own test, the eye-glow that Ford says was an accident, and Scott's decades-later interviews — and tell me which option handles which case best, and where each one gives an answer that feels wrong. Two sub-questions matter a lot to me. First, Scott's later statements: if a work is deliberately built to leave something undecidable, can an intention expressed years afterwards outside the work collapse it, or does that intention arrive too late to be the intention of the artifact rather than a reading of it — and notice that this is a problem for hypothetical intentionalists too, since the competent reader has to decide how much production history she's allowed to know. Second, the sequel: I'm fairly sure 2049 gives Deckard a daughter with Rachael and still refuses to settle it, and I want to know whether a later film by a different director can retroactively fix the meaning of an earlier one, or whether that's just the same accretion problem one level up.

Then, and this is the part I care about most, I want you to say where the film resists all of this and what it adds that the arguments alone don't. My hunch, which I half suspect is too cute, is that the film is itself about authority over a verdict: in-world, whether someone counts as human is settled by institutions — the Voight-Kampff test, Tyrell's designs, the state's records, Gaff's little paper figures — and the emotional question is whether that's the right place for the authority to sit, so a dispute about whose say-so fixes a text's meaning might be the same dispute in another register. If that's right, the film isn't just evidence in the intentionalism debate, it's a claim about what's at stake in it. But I'd rather you tell me if I'm forcing it: which scenes would you actually put in front of eighteen-year-olds to make that connection, which bits of the film genuinely refuse to be read that way, and how would you, as someone who knows the literature, honestly answer my co-teacher when she says that all of this is just a long way of saying the director gets the last word?
