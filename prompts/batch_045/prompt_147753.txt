I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for roughly fifteen years — Overcoming Bias and LessWrong in the old days, and their various successors since — so my working epistemology is basically Bayesian-plus: priors, updating, base rates, selection effects, calibration, Goodhart's law, the distinction between fitting signal and fitting noise. I also work as an ML engineer on ranking and recommendation systems, so I am fluent in a second vocabulary: training distributions, distribution shift, out-of-distribution generalization, train/test leakage, over- and under-parameterization, identifiability, causal identification from observational data, missing-not-at-random sampling, inverse reinforcement learning, hindsight credit assignment and hindsight relabeling of trajectories, performative feedback loops between a model and the distribution it scores. What I have essentially no background in is the philosophy of history.

I would like you to translate one debate out of that field into my vocabulary, and then tell me honestly what did not survive the translation.

The debate is about presentism: whether historians can avoid judging past agents — their beliefs, their actions, their institutions — by the standards of the historian's own time, and what precisely goes wrong when they fail to. Please do not first restate the debate in its own terms; I want the translation itself to be the primary object, with the historians' idiom appearing only where it resists translation.

To make the target less vague, here is a distinction I want preserved throughout. I suspect the single word presentism conflates at least three different failures, and I want them kept apart in your mapping:

(a) feature-space presentism — describing past agents with concepts, kinds, and distinctions that did not exist for them, as in calling someone a nationalist or saying that he was denying the science;
(b) loss-function presentism — scoring past actions against a standard of value the agents themselves did not share and could not have been expected to share;
(c) data-set presentism — treating as available to the agents evidence that only exists later: the outcomes of their choices, the subsequent course of events, the arguments of the next century.

For each, give me the closest object in the vocabulary I listed, say whether the mapping is exact, approximate, or a distortion, and name the corresponding failure mode in my world — so, for instance, tell me whether the classic complaint about Whig history is a special case of hindsight relabeling, a different failure, or two failures stacked.

Then the part I actually care about. I have a homebrew principle for deciding when a past agent's false belief excuses them, and I want it translated too. Call it the reachability test: an agent's false belief excuses their action if and only if no argument in their epistemic neighborhood — no line of reasoning that some contemporary of theirs could in principle have pressed on them, out of resources present in their world — would have moved a reasonable version of that agent. If someone could have talked them out of it, the excuse fails; if the error was unreachable from where they stood, the blame detaches from the agent and reattaches to the environment that produced them. Two wrinkles I want handled explicitly. First, what counts as available is going to depend on what was written down, copied, and preserved, so any blame assignment this test yields inherits the sampling bias of the archive — the strongest argument may have sat unread in a drawer and perished. Second, the test seems to entail that the better the unread arguments gathering dust in that drawer, the more blameworthy the agent, which strikes me as wrong, though I cannot say why.

Translate the reachability test and both wrinkles as literally as your honesty permits. Then give me the residue report: which components have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all, which survive only after you have quietly altered them, and — most importantly — whether the residue indicts my vocabulary or indicts my principle, or indicts the original debate. I specifically want your verdict on whether anything in the worry about presentism is irreducibly normative, or whether the whole thing dissolves into bias, variance, and leakage once it is stated properly. I would much rather be told that my vocabulary is missing something than be handed a tidy translation that has smuggled in the assumption that understanding a person is the same sort of achievement as predicting one.
