Hey — I'm a PhD student in philosophy (phil mind mostly, drifting into logic-adjacent stuff) and I'm running the grad reading group session next week on Gödel-style arguments against mechanism. I need someone to untangle a knot for me, and I'd rather you not just give me the headline dialectic again: I already have the usual moves (the theorems only apply to consistent, recursively axiomatized theories that contain enough arithmetic; Lucas says we can see the Gödel sentence is true so we can't be a system; replies: maybe we're inconsistent, maybe we can never know which machine we are, maybe our output isn't recursively enumerable because we revise ourselves). I want the bit underneath that, which every presentation slides over.

Here's how I've been setting it up, with two stipulations to block the easy escapes. First, stipulate that there is a fixed program P that exactly captures every sentence I will ever assert as a mathematical truth — total output recursively enumerable, consistent, no revisions — and that I've somehow been handed a full description of P. Second, consider a purely mechanical device I'll call the Gödel-swallowing agent: feed it any r.e. system S and it spits out G_S, the Gödel sentence for S. This device seems to perform exactly the move Lucas says shows we aren't machines, and yet it's obviously a machine.

Questions:

1. Given both stipulations, I seem to get a straight contradiction: if P captures all my mathematical assertions and I can see P is consistent, I can see G_P is true, so my assertions outrun P. Which disjunct of that setup actually has to go, and is the thing that has to go something with anti-mechanist significance, or just a self-reference artifact like the one that stops a machine from proving its own consistency? I suspect it's the latter but I can't say cleanly why.

2. What is the Gödel-swallowing agent missing, such that having it doesn't refute mechanism? My guess is that producing G_S is easy and mechanizable, while seeing that G_S is *true* requires seeing that S is consistent — so the Lucas argument is really only an argument about our knowledge of consistency. If that's right, does the argument then collapse into something circular (it assumes we have a non-mechanical capacity to tell, of arbitrary systems, that they're sound), or is there a version where it only needs that capacity for one system, namely me?

3. Related: everyone says you can just keep going — add G_P to get P', then G_P', transfinitely. Why doesn't that converge on arithmetical truth? I've heard something about needing to know which notations really denote ordinals, and about each limit step needing a fresh consistency judgement. I don't want the proof, I want the gut-level picture of why the iteration can never close, and why the missing ingredient at the limit is the same missing ingredient as in question 2.

Also, if it matters: I think there's a real difference between "for every system there's a truth it misses" and "there's a truth every system misses", and I want to know exactly where the Lucas argument needs the stronger one.
