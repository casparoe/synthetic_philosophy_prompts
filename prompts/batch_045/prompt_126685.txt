I direct the adjudication unit of a public agency that decides, several hundred times a month, whether an applicant qualifies for a support payment. The test we apply has three components, each vague and none of them defined anywhere I can find: the applicant's difficulties must be substantial rather than trivial; they must be long-term rather than temporary; and the help they need must be substantial rather than occasional. The components are also meant to trade off against one another, and how they trade off is left to us. Decisions are taken by caseworkers with two days' training, in about twenty minutes, must be justified to the applicant in a paragraph, and are treated as binding on later decisions. That last requirement has produced what a colleague of mine calls a diachronic sorites: each decision differs only imperceptibly from the one it cites, yet after two years the effective threshold has drifted somewhere none of us would have chosen knowingly.

I want a decision procedure my unit can actually run, and I want it built rather than borrowed. It should tell a caseworker what to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each question; where the procedure stops with a no; and where it hands the case to a panel because what remains is a question of values rather than fact. Four difficulties need to be designed around rather than dissolved. First, tolerance against consistency: if no small difference in an applicant's circumstances can be decisive, and each decision must be consistent with the one before it, the threshold moves; so the procedure needs anchoring of some kind — stored exemplar cases, periodic re-anchoring, and a rule for when a chain of decisions should be broken rather than extended. Second, comparing against classifying: my caseworkers are often confident that one applicant is worse off than another while unsure whether either one qualifies, and I want to know how much of the work can be done by such pairwise comparisons and where they stop working — I suspect comparability thins out at the margins and cannot be chained indefinitely. Third, the three components must not be collapsed into a single score, because the score would look more exact than the test is; say how they are kept apart and still brought to a verdict. Fourth, reasons: we cannot write to an applicant that the test is vague, and a reason that turns on a one-point difference reads as an insult, so I need to know which arbitrariness can be made legitimate by being announced in advance and which cannot.

Then run the procedure on three cases. (a) Difficulties clearly substantial and clearly long-term, but the applicant copes with a piece of assistive equipment that does part of the work of a person, so it is unclear whether the help needed is substantial — and the equipment is a fact pattern nobody anticipated. (b) A fluctuating condition, trivial on good days and substantial on bad ones, where the number of bad days a week is itself a matter of degree and the claim sits near the middle of the range. (c) An applicant marginally worse than someone refused twenty-four months ago and marginally better than someone awarded last month. At least one of these should come out as a no that I will not like. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do, and whether publishing it — which our transparency rules push us towards — makes things worse by teaching advisers exactly how far a case has to be moved.
