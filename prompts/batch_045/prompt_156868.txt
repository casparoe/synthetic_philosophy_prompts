I've been reading a well-reviewed recent book about attention and the media, and its chapter on Kierkegaard is the part I keep thinking about, because if it's right it makes Kierkegaard the first diagnostician of the attention economy and if it's wrong I'm not sure I can tell where. The chapter's account runs roughly as follows. Kierkegaard argued that the press had brought something new into the world: the public, a phantom collective that nobody belongs to but everybody answers to. Before newspapers, people belonged to real communities, a parish or a guild or a village, where you had to stand behind what you said because you said it to people you would meet again. Print let opinions travel anonymously, and once an opinion is detached from any speaker who has to own it, opinions pile up into an abstraction that can be more uniform and more irresponsible than any of the individuals involved. Kierkegaard called this levelling: the public grinds down every distinction of rank, talent and conviction into a single average, and it does it without anyone deciding anything, which is why it feels like fate rather than choice. His remedy was straightforwardly individualist: since the crowd is untruth, the only honest response is to withdraw from public opinion into your own private sincerity and stop reading the papers. And the chapter ends by saying that this is why his categories fit Twitter so uncannily, because the mechanism, cheap anonymous dissemination reaching everyone at once, is exactly the same one.

Two things in that make me suspicious, but I don't know enough to say why. The claim about Twitter feels like it overreaches, and the claim that his remedy was to retreat into private sincerity rings false to me because Kierkegaard published pseudonymously, wrote in newspapers, and spent years deliberately provoking a satirical paper that mocked him, which is not what someone who thought you should stop reading the papers would do. Still, I'd rather have my instincts checked than trust them.

So please audit the summary claim by claim: the public as an entity the press brought into being; levelling as a mechanical averaging of everybody into a mean; the individualist remedy; and the extension to social media. For each, tell me what a scholar of Kierkegaard would accept as fair, what they would call a simplification a popularizer is entitled to, and what they would call a misreading. Three things matter to me beyond the general verdict. First, is the press for Kierkegaard the cause of the public or only its instrument or expression, and does the difference change anything about the chapter's story? Second, is the single individual a matter of private inwardness and authenticity, or is it a category that does its work somewhere else entirely, and does either reading make the withdrawal conclusion follow? Third, the book never asks whether scale and anonymity are essential to the category: a few friends and I put out a small signed newsletter for a reading group, a few hundred subscribers, everyone knows who wrote what, readers write back by email and argue with us, and I want to know whether Kierkegaard's public could get a grip on that at all, and if so, what would have to be true of it for it to. Where you are reconstructing something the secondary literature disagrees about, say so rather than smoothing it over, and finish by telling me how confident you are in the audit as a whole and which single claim in it you are least sure of.
