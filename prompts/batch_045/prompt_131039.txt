My teammate keeps making the same move in every argument we have about AI: the mind is to the brain as software is to hardware, so what matters is the program, not the wet stuff its running on. He leans on it pretty obvs.

Here's how far he pushed it last week. I found this old dialogue in an anthology called The Mind's I where a guy argues with a book. The book is basically a giant lookup table of how Einstein would have replied to anything you could say to him, and you get the answer by flipping to the right page and reading it out. In the dialogue the book talks back in Einstein's voice. My teammate says: if the table is complete enough then obviously there's someone home — the book is the program, the guy turning the pages is the CPU, and anyone who denies it is doing carbon chauvinism. He then uses the same reasoning to argue that a system which just does cached lookup over a giant table of recorded human conversations would have someone home too.

I think he's smuggling something but I cant point at it, and Ive got three variants that make me more suspicious rather than less:

1. Make the table sparse instead of printed: generate each entry on demand with a deterministic function and never store it unless someone actually asks. Same input-output behavior, and the vast majority of the table never exists anywhere. Does that change the answer? If it does, what was doing the work before?
2. I think of a lookup table as just a program that got compiled, fully unrolled, and had every result memoized. Compilation isn't supposed to change which program is running. But if unrolling it kills the mind, then apparently the causal and temporal structure of the computation matters and not just the abstract function being computed — which seems to break the software/hardware picture he started from. Maybe it doesnt and Im wrong about that.
3. The book produces maybe one sentence an hour. He says speed is irrelevant. But if it's irrelevant in that direction, isn't it irrelevant in the other one too — something running a million times faster than us would conclude were the inert book?

So: audit the analogy properly. Give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the program, the hardware, the interpreter, the input, the runtime, the compiler, whatever else you need — then where the mapping breaks, and separate the breaks that actually matter for the conclusion he wants from the ones that are just decoration. Then give me your verdict on the book, yes or no, and if the analogy won't carry his conclusion say which of his premises you'd drop and what that costs him, and whether there's a better comparison that does the work he wants.

Take a real position and defend it. I don't want a survey of the options and I especially don't want a "well, it depends what you mean by conscious" dodge — pick the reading you think is strongest, commit to it, and tell me where you might be wrong.
