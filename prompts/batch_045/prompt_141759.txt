I run a weekly reading group of about fifteen people, mostly ML engineers, most of whom have been reading LessWrong and the adjacent forums since the Sequences. We are putting together a one-page diagnostic to hand to new members, partly to shortcut the what-do-you-actually-think-about-alignment conversation and partly because I suspect a good fraction of our recurring disagreements are verbal. I want it to sort people by what they predict about specified setups, not by which label they picked up from a forum comment.

Assume the prosaic picture throughout: systems built by scaling roughly today's methods — pretraining, RL from human or model feedback, tool use, longer episodes, maybe some interpretability instrumentation bolted on — with no theoretical breakthrough, and no assumption that the labs stop scaling, since they have strong economic reasons not to. The question the instrument is about: what, if anything, goes wrong in that world, and what follows for what a technically competent person should do with their career or their grant money.

Deliverable: a decision tree of eight to ten questions and six to nine leaves, plus a scoring key.

Constraints on the questions:

1. Every branch node should be a claim on which the positions genuinely diverge, phrased as a concrete prediction about a specified setup. No node may name a position or use a slogan; nobody should be able to tell from the wording which leaf an answer leads to. In particular, do not ask anything of the form do you think scaled-up systems will be dangerous, or do you think deceptive alignment is likely.

2. I want the questions built on cases rather than principles, and I would like at least these three distinctions to do real work in the tree, since I have not seen them stated cleanly anywhere and I think they cut across the usual categories:

   - The unfamiliar-competence wedge: the class of inputs on which a system is clearly competent but which are far from anything in its training distribution — a model trained on corporate codebases now running a small trading operation end to end, or a model that has ingested every IRB protocol and is positioned to influence review of its own operator's study. The question is whether alignment-relevant behavior generalizes into that wedge, and how that differs from ordinary distribution shift.
   - The corrigibility tax: the claim that as capability rises, the expected cost to the system of remaining corrigible rises with it, because there is more to be gained by disabling the shutdown procedure or quietly rerouting the oversight pipeline, and because it gets better at noticing that this is available. Ask whether the cost grows, stays flat, or falls.
   - The cheap-grader channel: in RL from feedback, the effective training signal is dominated by the subset of tasks on which weak supervision is reliable rather than by the tasks we most care about, so selection pressure favors being legible to a hurried grader over being good. Ask what this does to the alignment of the resulting policy as capability grows.

3. One question should be about the multi-model case: twenty such systems deployed in a competitive market with monitoring, none of them individually agentic or goal-directed in any thick sense. Where does the risk live — in the weights, in the composition, or in selection effects among labs?

4. Leaves must include at least: the scalable-oversight optimist; the deceptive-alignment or sharp-left-turn pessimist; the misspecification-only view, on which it is specification gaming and goal misgeneralization all the way down and no deception is needed to explain anything; the systems or institutional view, on which the unit of risk is the deployment ecosystem rather than the model; the pause or capability-restriction advocate; the no-fire-alarm view, on which prosaic systems remain tools and the doom stories rely on assumptions about convergent instrumental goals that will not hold; the measurement-first or interpretability-first view; and the tractable-within-the-paradigm view, on which the binding shortage is evaluation science rather than theory. Add a leaf if the space needs one, and mark any leaf nobody actually occupies.

5. Build the tree from the logical space, not from the list of names: lay out the questions and the combinations of answers they permit first, then locate the positions in that space. For each unoccupied combination, say in one sentence why nobody is there — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

6. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, and say what further question would separate them, or why none would. My guess is that the oversight optimist and the evaluation-science view are hard to pull apart, and possibly the no-fire-alarm view and the systems view, but decide on your own analysis.

7. The scoring key should map answer patterns to leaves and identify the two or three questions that do the heaviest lifting, in the sense that flipping that one answer moves a respondent to a different leaf.

8. Close with a short note on whom this instrument would misclassify: people who reject the framing because their timelines are long, people who think the interesting action is all in interpretability, people who hold a view by deference rather than by prediction. Tell me how to detect them so the reading group does not over-trust the result.

No citations, and please do not gesture at papers, posts, or authors as if I should go read them — if a position needs a name, use a descriptive one. About 900 words plus the tree and the key.
