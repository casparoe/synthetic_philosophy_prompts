I chair the training committee of a public benefits agency, and next week I owe the director a recommendation and two senior caseworkers a written answer to an objection that is currently blocking it. I would be grateful for your help in getting the structure of the disagreement right before I write either.

For roughly thirty years, decisions on the harder claims have been made by a small group of senior caseworkers. There are only a few pages of written guidance; the rest is something you absorb by sitting with them, watching which cases they treat as difficult, and being corrected. We are now bringing in about forty new caseworkers, trained largely by shadowing and by review of their draft decisions. So far their decisions match the seniors' on every case we have been able to check, including some genuinely hard ones. Two of the seniors still refuse to sign off, and they give two reasons.

The first is that no amount of checking could settle the matter. The newcomers might be applying a rule that coincides with the seniors' on every case we have looked at, on every case we are ever likely to look at, and diverges somewhere further out; and if that is possible, then the question whether they are really applying *the same standard* is not one that any evidence we could gather answers, and perhaps not one that has an answer at all.

The second is that even a guarantee of permanent agreement would not be enough. When the seniors call a decision wrong, they mean something more than that they themselves would not have decided that way, and they think that further element — the fact that the decision *ought* to have gone differently — is not recovered by any amount of agreement, however long it lasted and however hard the cases.

What makes me think there is something here worth taking seriously is that the objection seems, as far as I can tell, to apply to the seniors as well. Nothing in their own thirty years of decisions, and nothing in their own sense of what they have always meant, determines which rule they have been following; and yet they are not in the least troubled about themselves. That asymmetry may be where the answer lies, or it may simply be that they are being inconsistent.

Please tell me what the shape of their argument is, where it bites and where it overreaches, and what follows for what I should actually do: certify the new caseworkers, refuse to, or restructure the training. I need to answer the seniors in terms they will recognize as fair — I do not think their worry is silly — without conceding that nothing at all can be said about who is following the standard and who is not.
