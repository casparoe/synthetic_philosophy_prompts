ok, forget another hot take about whether the DSM is bad — I want the requirements list. Setting: you're advising a national health service that has to adopt one nosology for mood, anxiety and personality-type presentations, and the same list has to do five jobs at once: steer treatment in a fifteen-minute appointment, gate access to medication, therapy and disability benefits, let researchers run multi-site studies that compare like with like, travel across cultures, and be shown to the patient, who will read their own record and look the criteria up. Write the desiderata: what any classification in that situation has to deliver, with the case or consideration behind each one, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are just the preferences of one camp.

The wrinkle I want taken seriously is Hacking's: when you sort people into a human kind, they notice, they reorganise their behaviour and their self-understanding around it, and the kind itself shifts, so the classification is a moving target rather than a map of a fixed territory — very unlike his contrast case of indifferent kinds, the things that don't care how we classify them. And I want three complications folded in that aren't really in the standard telling:

1. Pre-emptive looping: the loop now starts before the clinician opens her mouth. People arrive already self-diagnosed from criteria they read online, so the category has been acting on them for months before anyone official applies it.
2. Meta-looping: it's now common knowledge that these categories are unstable and partly socially shaped, and that knowledge changes uptake too — some people discount the diagnosis, others use its instability to argue for or against a claim on resources.
3. Some categories look self-erasing (they thin out once the description is public and legible) while others look self-amplifying (they expand because they offer people a newly available way to make sense of themselves). A list that can't tell, in advance, which kind it's dealing with doesn't seem to me to have done its job.

Things I want adjudicated rather than assumed: is "carves nature at the joints / tracks a real mechanism" a genuine requirement or a prejudice of the biological psychiatry people? Is "patients should be able to recognise themselves in the description" a requirement or a prejudice of the service-user people? Does a classification owe non-maleficence under disclosure — handing someone their diagnosis shouldn't predictably make them worse? Does it need to be resistant to gaming by people with an interest in being in or out of a category? And is tagging each category with its own expected volatility (a sort of half-life) a real desideratum or over-engeneering?

Then the part I care most about: which pairs of your requirements can't be jointly satisfied once looping is real. I have a feeling full transparency to patients and stable predictive validity pull apart, and that cross-cultural invariance and sensitivity to local looping pull apart, but make the tension precise and say what has to give. And say which single requirement knocks out the most of the usual candidate approaches — the recent dimensional severity-and-traits models, mechanism-first approaches, purely pragmatic utility-first ones. A short scorecard at the end is fine, but keep the weight on the criteria.
