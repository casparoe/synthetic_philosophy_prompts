My friend and I got into a huge fight last night about whether her ex manipulated her into moving to another city, and I ended up defending him, which I'm not sure I should have. My line was that it was persuasion, not manipulation: he gave reasons — better job, cheaper rent, she'd been saying for a year that she was bored there — and I think people are allowed to argue hard for something they want. Her line was that he saved the big conversation for the end of a twelve-hour shift when she was wrecked, and had already booked the visit and talked to the landlord, so by the time they talked it wasn't really a decision anymore. I still said persuasion. We went round in cirlces for about two hours and eventually she told me to write down what I actually think the difference is, so I did, and now that it's on paper I want to know whether it holds together.

Here's the set:

(a) Persuasion works by engaging someone's capacity to weigh reasons. Manipulation works around that capacity — through emotion, pressure, timing, framing, whatever.

(b) But I really don't want to say that every emotional appeal is manipulation. A eulogy, a good speech, a friend telling me in a shaky voice that she's scared — those all move me through emotion and nothing has gone wrong. This is the one I'd be most reluctant to drop, because without it my account seems to swallow all of ordinary emotional life.

(c) What makes something manipulation is the intent to get your way by routing around the person's judgment. Not whether it worked, and not whether what you said was true.

(d) Manipulation isn't automatically wrong. Sometimes it's the right thing to do: talking a panicking person down by whatever happens to work, the bedside manner you'd want from a nurse when someone's in acute crisis.

(e) But it's clearly worse when the target is vulnerable — depressed, exhausted, grieving, financially dependent on you. Same tactic, worse act.

(f) "It was good for them" or "they'd agree with it now" doesn't make something not manipulation. It can make it less bad, though.

(g) Nudges — defaults, ordering, framing, opt-out organ donation — count as manipulation, because they work by not engaging deliberation. But I'm mostly fine with them when they serve the person's own ends and they'd sign off if you asked.

So: go through these and tell me which ones actually fight each other and which only look like they do, and why. Rank whatever you find by how bad it is, and for each one say what exactly would have to give and what giving it would cost me. Two specific things I want: whether my verdict on the ex survives my own (a) and (e), or whether (e) can be narrowed so that it does; and which single revision cleans up the most of this at once. My gut says there's a problem somewhere in the middle of the list but I genuinely can't locate it, so don't just walk through the items one by one — I want the joints.
