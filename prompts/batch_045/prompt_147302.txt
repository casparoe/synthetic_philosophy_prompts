**Question 3 (20 points).** In the bank-style cases we discussed, two subjects can have exactly the same evidence and yet we're inclined to say the one with little riding on being right knows, and the one with a lot riding on it doesn't. Two views were offered in response:

- **Contextualism** (in the version we read: *attributor* contextualism) — "knows" is a context-sensitive expression, like "tall" or "flat". Which epistemic standard is in force is fixed by the context of the person making the knowledge claim.
- **Pragmatic encroachment** (interest-relative invariantism) — the content and truth-conditions of "knows" do not shift with context. But whether a subject's evidence is good enough for knowledge partly depends on what is practically at stake for that subject.

**(a)** In your own words, explain how each view accounts for the pattern of judgments in the low-stakes and high-stakes versions of the case. (6 points: 3 per view)

**(b)** Amara looks up her balance on Friday and believes her rent check will clear. It won't: if it bounces she loses the apartment. She wrongly believes her parents would cover her, so she isn't worried. Her friend Ben, chatting about something else entirely and unaware of all this, says "Amara knows the check will clear." For each of the two views, say (i) whether Amara knows and (ii) whether Ben's claim, as he makes it, is true. Then say briefly how each view should handle the fact that the stakes are high even though Amara doesn't realize it. (8 points)

**(c)** One of these two views is *committed* to allowing that one and the same knowledge sentence — same words, same subject, same evidence, same circumstances — can be true as uttered by one speaker and false as uttered by another. Which one, and why? Why isn't the other view committed to that? (6 points)

Here's the student's answer, typed up more or less verbatim (I've kept their typos).

**(a)** Contextualism says that "knows" is context-sensitive, like "tall" or "flat": how good your evidence has to be shifts with the context of the conversation. So in the low-stakes bank case the standard is low and it's true to say the subject knows, and in the high-stakes case the standard gets raised and the same subject no longer counts as knowing. Pragmatic encroachment gets the same pattern of judgments a different way. It's an invariantist view: "knows" has the same content and the same truth-conditions no matter who is speaking. What changes is whether the subject's evidence is good enough, and part of what makes it good enough is how much is practically at stake for her — the more that rides on being right, the more evidence she needs. So both views explain why we're happy to say "knows" in the low-stakes version and not in the high-stakes version. They just disagree about whether the shift is in the word or in what it takes to satisfy the word.

**(b)** On contextualism, Ben isn't worried about anything and nothing in his conversation raises the standards, so his relaxed claim that Amara knows comes out true. Amara's own claim "I know the check will clear" also comes out true, since she isn't worried either. On pragmatic encroachment it goes the other way: a lot is riding on her belief being right, so her evidence isn't good enough and she doesn't know — which means Ben's claim about her is false even though he's just chatting. As for her not realizing what's at stake, I think PE has to go by the stakes as she sees them, since knowledge is a mental state and has to be something she could tell from her own situation, so strictly speaking she does know and Ben is right. That's a bit odd but it's where the view lands.

**(c)** Contextualism is the view that has to allow this. The standards a knowledge claim has to meet track how much is at stake for the person the knowledge is being attributed to — Amara's practical situation is what makes "knows" harder to satisfy here, not anything about Ben — so the same sentence can be true when Ben says it and false when someone else says it. PE avoids the consequence, since it keeps the content of "knows" fixed, so two speakers saying the same words about the same person in the same circumstances can't come out with different truth values. Honestly though this just shows contextualism is relativism: it says the very same proposition, that Amara knows, is both true and false, which can't be right. I'm not sure contextualism is really a seperate view from PE at the end of the day.
