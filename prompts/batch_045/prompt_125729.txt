Two senior engineers on my team spent most of a design review arguing about nine lines of legacy code in our payment capture path, and afterward they asked me to settle it. I couldn't tell whether they were even arguing about the same thing, so I'm bringing it to you.

The code sits at the top of `capturePayment`. It sleeps for 400 milliseconds before calling the processor, under a comment reading `PAY-1142 — do not remove`. Below it is a retry loop: on a timeout (and only on a timeout) it tries the capture up to three times, and each attempt generates a fresh idempotency key, with a comment above the loop saying the processor's own dedupe can't be trusted. The ticket referenced in the comment is from 2019 and was closed as stale without resolution. No test covers the sleep. Nobody currently on the team wrote either part or can say what the ticket was about. Latency on this path is something we're actively trying to reduce.

Their exchange, roughly as it happened:

(1) Maya: delete both. Four hundred milliseconds on every checkout is real money at our volume, no test fails when the sleep is removed, the ticket is dead, and in four years nobody has been able to state a mechanism by which it helps. Her general line: unexplained code is a liability, not a mystery to be respected, and every week it stays it teaches the next engineer that this is the service where weird stuff lives, which makes the next odd line harder to question too.

(2) Dev: this is exactly Chesterton's fence — the principle, which I'd never heard of, being that you don't tear down a fence until you know why it was put up, with Burkean roots in the thought that long-standing practices carry knowledge no individual possesses. He added that the asymmetry decides it: the upside of removal is 400ms, the downside is double-captured customers, which is money, support load, and a compliance conversation. The absence of a recorded reason, he said, is not evidence of the absence of a reason, and our CI has never exercised whatever 2019 condition motivated the line, so a green run tells us nothing.

(3) Maya: three objections. First, the principle was written for a world without undo; fences are hard to rebuild, and this isn't — we have version control, a feature flag, a 1% canary, and instant rollback, so the irreversibility that the whole Burkean apparatus trades on doesn't transfer. Second, the principle is unfalsifiable as she's seen it used: if nothing breaks, that is equally consistent with the line having been load-bearing and conditions having since changed, so no outcome ever licenses removal. Third, and relatedly, it licenses confabulation — hand Dev any arbitrary line and he can invent a plausible mechanism that makes it load-bearing, which is not the same as having a reason.

(4) Dev: two replies. The principle is a decision rule for acting under asymmetric and partly irreversible costs, not an empirical prediction, so demanding that it be falsifiable is a category mistake — though he conceded that Maya's confabulation point has teeth, because a just-so story is only better than no story if it generates a test. And it does here: if the sleep is a crutch masking a race in the processor's authorization-to-capture transition, then removing the sleep under load against the sandbox should reproduce the timeout. Two quiet weeks in production is weak evidence; a targeted load test is strong evidence. Then his main counter: "reversible" is a claim about code, not about state. The rollback is instant, but by the time anyone notices, money has moved twice and reconciliation is manual. The cost of reversal is detection lag times damage rate, not deploy time.

(5) Maya: she granted the state-versus-code point but said it cuts the other way — it's an argument for alerting on duplicate captures, not for preserving unexplained lines indefinitely. Then she proposed replacing the question "did someone have a reason?" with "what kind of thing is this?": a fence, which still keeps something out and is still needed; a crutch, which compensates for a defect somewhere else and is only safe to remove once that defect is fixed; or scar tissue, a workaround for a third-party bug that has since been fixed upstream and should just go. On her reading, these two lines have to be evaluated as one configuration rather than two independent decisions, because the sleep is arguably what makes the unsafe retry path unreachable: take out the sleep and you may start triggering, more often, the one path that can double-charge. So "remove the sleep" and "remove the retry" are not separate questions, and neither is safe alone.

(6) Dev: he accepted the three-way distinction but said you cannot classify a line from reading it in isolation — classification is precisely what the archaeology buys you, and the archaeology is cheap and bounded: git blame, the 2019 incident channel, one message to the contractor who wrote it. Hours, against the downside he described. On Maya's compounding-confusion argument in (1), he agreed with the diagnosis but said the remedy is a test and a comment, and that what she actually objects to is the team's documentation habits rather than this line.

One note on where I'm coming from: I've read neither Burke nor Chesterton and don't intend to, so please reason from the arguments as I've given them rather than from what either figure is supposed to have meant, and don't send me to the secondary literature.

Please adjudicate move by move. Open with a single sentence giving your overall verdict — who is more right, and about what — then take the six moves in order, rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a sentence or two of justification for the rating, and then say briefly what both of them are missing. No preamble and no restating the question.
