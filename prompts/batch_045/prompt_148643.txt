You are a philosopher of history who specializes in the epistemology of testimony, and you have working familiarity with how archaeologists, philologists, and ancient historians actually argue in each of the three cases below. Answer carefully and in stages: begin with two or three paragraphs of unstructured brainstorming in which you work out what you even take the question to be, and only then produce numbers. Do not give me a bare list of considerations for and against.

The three cases are: the Trojan War, King Arthur, and the Exodus. Background I will stipulate and do not need argued for: the Homeric poems were composed long after the events they narrate and no contemporary written record of the war survives; the mound at Hissarlik in northwest Anatolia holds a walled Bronze Age citadel with destruction levels, excavated from the 1870s onward; the earliest surviving texts naming Arthur postdate the period he is placed in by centuries, and the fullest early narrative of him is generally regarded as largely literary construction; no Egyptian record of the Exodus survives, and the earliest non-biblical mention of a people called Israel is a victory inscription of Pharaoh Merneptah, roughly late thirteenth century BCE.

I need a proposition with a truth value, so here is my resolution convention. Imagine a panel of thirty specialists in the relevant fields convening in 2050, with access to all evidence available then, each required to give a 0 to 1 score for each case under a fixed standard, the median score being announced as the verdict. The question I want a credence on is whether that median exceeds 0.5. The fixed standard has two versions, and I want numbers for both.

Version one, the Continuity Standard: the tradition counts as history only if there exists a chain of causally connected retellings running from people in a position to witness the events to the earliest surviving text, such that at least one core particular — a specific named place, a specific named group, or a datable event-type — passes down the chain with its reference intact, and that particular is not the kind of thing a later tradition could have reconstructed from the surviving landscape, from ruins, or from a plausible-sounding etymology. Call the second requirement the reconstructability filter, and the first the referential-anchoring requirement.

Version two, the Resemblance Standard: it is enough that some event of the right general type — a coalition war against a fortified coastal city; a war-leader organizing resistance in post-Roman Britain; a group of Semitic laborers leaving Egypt under hostile circumstances — occurred in the right region within roughly two centuries of the traditional date, with no requirement that any particular detail descended from witnesses.

Give me your credence on the median exceeding 0.5, for each of the three cases, under each of the two standards: six numbers. For comparison, my own are Troy 0.7 under Continuity and 0.85 under Resemblance, Arthur 0.1 and 0.35, Exodus 0.3 and 0.5.

Then decompose. I want the factors, with a number and one line of justification each, and an explicit statement of how they combine — multiplicative, noisy-OR, or whatever you actually think the structure is. The terms I would include, add or replace as you see fit: a base rate for how often migration-era and Late Bronze Age legendary traditions have any recoverable core; a transmission-fidelity term for how much veridical particular survives a purely oral chain of a given generational length, and specifically whether there is a compression floor below which nothing checkable survives at all; a reconstructability term for the core particular in question; a convergence term for independent non-testimonial evidence that points to the same particular; a determinacy term for whether the standard is well enough defined to be adjudicated at all rather than dissolving into a verbal dispute; and a genre term capturing how much the surviving text's literary and nation-forming purposes distort what it preserves.
