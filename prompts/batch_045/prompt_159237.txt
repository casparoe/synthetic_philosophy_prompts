I've been lurking around rationalist blogs and forum comment sections for the better part of fifteen years and this is the first time I've wanted to actually post a question instead of arguing in the thread, so here goes. Also, please don't give me reading lists or citations — I cant check them and I don't want them, I want arguments.

The view, stated stronger than I'd defend it out loud: I think the socialist calculation debate got settled on grounds that have since expired. The classic moves — that planners can't do rational economic calculation without money prices for factors of production, and that the knowledge needed to allocate resources is dispersed, local, partly tacid, and changes faster than it can be reported up a hierarchy — are, as I read them, claims about information throughput and feedback latency. Both are contingent facts about the technology of coordination, not necessities. And coordination technology has moved a lot since the 1930s. Cheap sensors, machine learning, and automated decision systems mean a planner doesn't need anyone to file a report upward; a lot of local knowledge can be inferred from the data people leave behind. So I want to retire "calculation is impossible" in favour of "calculation is costly, and how costly is an engineering question whose answer changes over time."

I also think the follow-on move people make when you push them — that markets aren't really a computer but a discovery procedure, that profit and loss is an error signal, that bankruptcy is how bad allocations get killed off, that entrepreneurship is about noticing things nobody put in the model — is the same point in different vocabulary. Those are all mechanisms for search under uncertainty, and search under uncertainty is precisely what modern computation got good at.

The thing I keep coming back to, which I havent seen anyone make, and which I think is where I'm most exposed: call it the sealed-station test. Suppose you have to allocate resources for a research station, or a Mars colony, that has no contact with any market economy — no prices to observe, no trades to train on, no market-generated data of any kind. If your fancy planning system's performance collapses in that setting, then ML-flavoured planning is parasitic on the price system rather than a replacement for it, and my view is a lot weaker than it looks. If it doesn't collapse, the Hayekian knowledge argument is in real trouble. I don't have a good answer either way, which is why I'm posting.

So: what would count against my position? I want the strongest version of the other side, ranked. Concretely:

- Defeaters that people who already disagree with me would themselves concede count against me, not just the things critics shout.
- Fatal vs. serious vs. merely awkward — don't flatten them.
- Which ones have arguably already been observed somewhere in the real world, and where.
- Whether I've quietly qualified my view into unfalsifiability, because "costly" and "depends on engineering" are doing an awful lot of work — and if so, the cheapest way to make it risk something real.
- At least two defeaters that are not the standard tacit-knowledge point and the incentives/soft-budget-constraint point. I've heard both, they havent moved me, repeating them won't help.

What would move me most personally: (1) an argument that markets do something that isn't computable even in principle because there's nothing well-defined there to compute — not "we can't calculate it" but "the question is malformed"; and (2) evidence that planning inside large firms scales worse than I assume, i.e. that big firms are leaning on externally generated market prices much more than it looks like from the outside.
