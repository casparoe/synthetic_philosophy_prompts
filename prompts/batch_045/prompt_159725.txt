I teach a first-year module on social epistemology, and at the end of term I'm running a fifty-minute session that pairs Miller's *The Crucible* with the recent work on echo chambers. I'd like a reading of the play through that literature — and I'd rather have three scenes I can actually teach than a survey of the play, so please be selective and concrete.

The frame I have in mind is this. Recent epistemology distinguishes *epistemic bubbles* from *echo chambers*. A bubble is a structure of omission: you simply fail to encounter the other side, because of how your informational network happens to be arranged, and the bubble is correspondingly fragile — exposure to outside sources tends to burst it. An echo chamber is a structure of active discredit: insiders are taught to distrust outside sources as such, so the chamber is robust to exposure, and can even be reinforced by it. A postgraduate in the seminar has been pressing me to add a third category, which she calls a *credibility loop*: what binds the group is neither mere omission nor distrust of outsiders, but the fact that the only evidence admitted for the group's central claims is the testimony of insiders, while insider standing is itself conferred by endorsing those claims. The source of credibility is therefore endogenous, and outside evidence has no route by which it could count. She adds a second complaint: the standard two categories are drawn for communities of sincere believers, and she wants to know what we should say when the central claims are known to be false by the people who originated them.

So my questions. Is Salem best described as a bubble, an echo chamber, or her third thing — or does it shift between them over the course of the play? Which scenes would you put on a seminar handout, and what exactly do they show: Danforth's handling of the depositions and of anyone who doubts the court; the treatment of Rebecca Nurse, whose standing makes her dissent inexplicable to the court except as something worse than dissent; Hale's withdrawal once the court rejects a confession he is willing to vouch for; Mary Warren's recantation under the girls' pressure; and the final act, where the town has turned against the court but the prisoners still press one another to confess — is that last scene the chamber's collapse, or its survival in miniature? And I want the reading to say where it breaks down. The accusers are not mistaken believers but liars, and Parris and Putnam have interests of their own, so an honest critic should say how much of Salem is genuinely an epistemic phenomenon and how much is a strategic or credibility-driven one that an epistemology of chambers isn't built to model. Finally, my colleague's claim: that what the play really adds is the discovery that a chamber can be sustained by people who mostly don't believe its claims — that sincerity is optional — and that the literature has no vocabulary for this. Is that a real contribution to the debate, or merely a reminder that literature is about motives while epistemology is about evidence?
