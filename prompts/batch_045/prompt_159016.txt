I'm a fourth-year PhD student in information science, and I'm finishing a dissertation chapter on whether privacy can be reduced to a subject's control over information about herself. My chapter argues for the negative, leaning on the contextual-integrity account developed by Helen Nissenbaum: what matters, on that view, is not whether a fact is hidden or whether the subject holds the reins, but whether a given flow of information fits the norms of the context it moves in — who may send what about whom to whom, and under which transmission principle. Our privacy reading group has its last meeting of the term next week, and rather than close with a summary of my argument I'd like to close with a poem. I need it to do something the chapter can't: make the claim felt instead of stated, and leave the hardest part of it open.

The form I want is a pantoum. For anyone who needs the rule stated: quatrains in which the second and fourth lines of a stanza reappear as the first and third lines of the next, with the final stanza taking the second and fourth lines of the penultimate stanza as its first and third, and the third and first lines of the opening stanza as its second and fourth, so that the poem ends on the line it began with. Six or seven quatrains, twenty-four to twenty-eight lines, meant to be read aloud. The interlacing shouldn't be decorative here; it should be what carries the philosophy.

The content: one plain, true fact about one person, migrating through a sequence of contexts — a clinic, a personnel file, an insurer, an advertising exchange, a family dinner, possibly a courtroom. The same sentence, a different sender, recipient, and reason for sending. By the end the reader should feel that nothing new has been learned and everything has changed.

Constraints I care about:

One, no secrets. Everything in the poem is true, and the subject states the central fact herself, out loud, unprompted, to someone she loves, somewhere early on. So the injury, if there is one, cannot be that she was found out. This is not a poem about exposure or about being watched.

Two, no villains. Nobody hacks, lies, breaks a promise, or bears ill will. Each transfer happens for a legible reason: a form filled in honestly, a record kept because records are kept, a correlation drawn because the data is there. If something goes wrong it goes wrong structurally, and the poem shouldn't hint at a culprit.

Three, the word privacy never appears — nor surveillance, nor data as a term of art. If the poem has to name its subject to make its point, it hasn't made it.

Four, and this is the part I most want: in a pantoum half of each stanza is inherited and half is new. Put that to work. Let the inherited lines be disclosures — things someone actually said or wrote — and let the new lines, increasingly, be things nobody ever said: a risk estimate, a classification, a prediction derived from the rest. What I want made visible is what happens next, when an inferred line is itself inherited by the following stanza and travels onward as though someone had told it. The form should let a reader watch an inference get laundered into a fact and notice that once it has moved there is nothing on it marking where it came from. If the poem turns, that is where it should turn: not at the moment the subject is hurt but at the moment a derived claim starts behaving like a disclosed one.

Five, keep every repeated line word for word identical, with a single permitted exception: one returned line, in the closing stanza, may come back altered by as little as one word. If you take that option, the alteration has to be the poem's answer — or its refusal to answer — to the question whether the same information can still be the same thing once it has moved. I'd rather you didn't take it unless it earns more than it costs.

The bar I'm holding you to: someone who works on contextual integrity should recognize its machinery — subject, sender, recipient, attribute, transmission principle, norms of appropriateness and of flow — without any of those terms appearing, and should not be able to read the poem as resting on a secrecy picture or a control picture of privacy. Someone who has never heard of the framework should simply find a good, unmelodramatic poem with no moral and no villain in it. Please don't resolve whether a wrong was done or what it consisted in; that was precisely where our discussion stalled, and I want to end there. No fixed meter, but every line should be speakable in one breath, and the returning lines should register as returns to the ear and not only to the eye.
