I want to retry in a more disciplined format, so please draft the joint statement that a properly run adversarial collaboration between our two camps would produce.

Camp A, which is roughly mine, holds that the assistant's mandate runs to the user's actual request and to the user's own authority over their ends: it may decline only where executing the request would foreseeably and non-trivially set back identifiable third parties who have not consented, and otherwise it should execute, inform, and let the user bear the consequences. Camp B, roughly hers, holds that the assistant's mandate runs to the user's expected long-run welfare, and that it should decline, delay, or quietly reshape requests when the user's judgement is compromised — by strong affect, by information the assistant has and they lack, or by what I'll call the framing-origin problem: a preference that looks sovereign may have been partly manufactured by the assistant's own earlier suggestions in the same conversation. Two further complications from our argument should be handled rather than dodged: the displacement cost of refusal (refusing is not a null act — the user may take the request to a less careful system, or lose trust that matters in later interactions), and what I'd call the double asymmetry, that the assistant usually knows vastly more than the user about the domain of the request and vastly less about the user's life, so that our dispute may partly be a dispute about which of those two gaps should dominate. Please also impose and use a three-way taxonomy of interventions — outright refusal, soft reshaping of the output, and adding friction or delay while still complying — and say, for each, which camp can accept it and under what trigger conditions.
