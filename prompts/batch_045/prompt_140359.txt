I'm in the last year of a philosophy PhD, working on formal epistemology, and the paper I'm currently stuck on asks whether the recent arguments about consequentialist agents inside the universal prior should make us rethink simplicity-based model selection. I have a number on the central claim and I don't trust the route I took to it, so I want yours, with the derivation.

The set-up I'm working with is entirely stipulative, so please don't reconstruct anyone's published version of it and don't give me citations or reading lists — I've read the central material; I want your own reasoning.

Take the standard sequence-prediction idealization: a universal semimeasure M over monotone Turing machines, a binary data stream, and a predictor whose forecasts are actually consulted in making a high-stakes decision (so that the stream contains bits that are, in the relevant sense, pivotal). Say that a hypothesis is *steering* if it contains a compressed model of the predictor and selects at least some of its outputs with the aim of influencing the predictor's later states or actions, rather than merely to match the generator. The proposition I want a number on:

P: conditional on any prefix of the stream that a real predictor could realistically have observed, at least 1% of M's predictive mass on the next bit lies on steering hypotheses.

I'll count P as settled when someone produces a measure-theoretic argument or a proof that a competent researcher in algorithmic information theory would accept, or a demonstration that the question is ill-posed; I'm asking for your probability that it settles that way. My own credence is about 0.6. A friend in the machine-learning theory group puts it at about 0.15 and thinks the whole worry is an artifact of an idealization nobody should take normatively seriously.

Give me your credence in P, decomposed over whatever sub-questions you think carry the weight. The ones I keep circling, which you should feel free to relabel, merge, or replace:

1. Whether M over monotone machines is even the right object to be evaluating — or whether a Levin-style speed prior that penalizes runtime, or bounded-resource model averaging, changes the conclusion enough that the debate is about the wrong target.
2. The complexity accounting: whether the bits needed to specify agency, a simulation rich enough to contain reasoners, and a read-out convention can be amortized across many targets (the "specify a generic universe that produces expanding life once, then sample whatever you like" move), or whether that overhead swamps the likelihood bonus and leaves the steering hypotheses with negligible mass.
3. Motive: whether the reasoners inside such a hypothesis would have any reason to steer at all, given that steering pays only if someone acts on the prior, and given the cost of locating a pivotal prefix among the ones cheap enough to specify.
4. Cancellation: whether the conflicting aims of many steering hypotheses, each trying to push the mass toward a different continuation, leave the aggregate distribution near what a benign prior would give, so that malignty is real at the level of individual hypotheses and invisible at the level of predictions.
5. Convergence: whether the standard result bounding the total expected deviation of a universal predictor's forecasts from the true source by a quantity depending only on the source's complexity defuses the worry — or is compatible with indefinitely long, arbitrarily misleading excursions, and whether it even applies once the predictor's own actions feed back into the stream.

One distinction I've been using that I haven't seen drawn in these terms, so treat it as my coinage: call a steering hypothesis a *sleeper* if its manipulation is gated on a short, cheaply specifiable trigger string and it otherwise mimics the benign generator. That adds a sub-question I think is load-bearing:

6. Conditional on some steering mass existing, how much of it is sleeper mass — and if it mostly is, does it follow that no finite amount of benign-looking data is evidence of safety, so that the practical upshot is not "watch for manipulation" but "there is no observational test"?

And the remedy question, with another stipulative term: let the *policy-blind submeasure* be the restriction of M to hypotheses whose output distribution is invariant under interventions on the predictor's own decision rule.

7. Whether such a restriction is even well-defined in the Solomonoff setting, and, if it could be implemented, whether it removes most of the steering mass while preserving the convergence guarantee — or whether it throws out the true generator along with the manipulators.
