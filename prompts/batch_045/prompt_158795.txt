Hi — I've got a 75-mintue guest slot next week in a colleague's interdisciplinary "Time" seminar and I've been asked to do the direction of time and the thermodynamic arrow. The room splits about evenly: half are physics majors who've had stat mech, know Boltzmann entropy, and think the Second Law basically *is* the answer; the other half are philosophy majors who've been reading about the asymmetries of memory, causation and knowledge, and who'll arrive suspecting that *those* are the primitive facts and the entropy gradient is downstream of them. I know the material, I don't need it explained to me. What I want is a plan for the explanation.

Here's the spine I've been toying with — tell me if it's wrong. I want to hand them what I've started calling an "arrow audit": an arrow of time can only be hiding in one of three places (in the dynamical laws, in the boundary conditions, or in the observer / in what we take ourselves to know), and the point of the session is that you have to locate it in exactly one of them and show the other two are empty. Is that the right spine? If it is, in what order do the three boxes get opened, and what has to be in place before the audit even sounds like a real question rather than bookkeeping? The sequence is most of what I'm after.

Second, the carrying example. My instinct is an ice cube in a glass of water — everyone's seen it, it's small in space and in time. But I've a nagging feeling it smuggles in exactly the things I'll then spend the hour removing: entropy as messiness or disorder, entropy as spreading-out-evenly-in-space, the arrow as *mixing*. So: what does the one case I build the hour around have to have, and what does it have to lack? And if the ice cube fails, what replaces it, and what do I do with the ice cube — put it on the board and then break it, or never show it at all?

Third, misconceptions, in the order they'll actually form, each with the point in the sequence where it gets cut off. The ones I'm expecting: entropy = disorder; "the Second Law explains the arrow" (i.e. the asymmetry is in the dynamics when it isn't); entropy is ignorance, so the arrow is in our heads; and the two-tailed worry — if entropy is low at one end, why isn't it higher in *both* directions away from that end, and why do we get one arrow rather than two, or none. Plus the one I'm least sure how to time: the "hot, dense early universe" picture, where they'll take the low-entropy bit to be the hotness or the denseness, when what's doing the work is the smoothness, given that gravity is on the scene and clumping is available to it. Anything else this particular mix will generate? And for each: do I stop it forming, or let them say it out loud and then dismantle it?

Then the bit where the room turns on itself: which arrow explains which. The physicists will want memory, causation and the psychological arrow reduced to the entropy gradient; the philosophers will want it the other way round, or will want to deny there's a fact of the matter about what's basic. Where in the hour should I stage that, and is there a formulation of the disagreement I can put on the board that both halves will recognize as *their* question rather than a strawman?

Last things: one question for the final five minutes that tells me whether the audit has landed rather than whether they've picked up the vocabularly; and two or three things I should openly refuse to go into at this level — I'm thinking the choice of typicality measure, the cosmological story, and anything quantum — with a line I can say out loud when I refuse, so it reads as a decision and not a dodge. Shorter is fine; I'd rather the sequence be right than everything be tidy.
