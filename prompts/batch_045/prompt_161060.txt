I'm teaching a first-year seminar this term on moral objectivity, and I have paired the philosophy material with Chinua Achebe's *Things Fall Apart*, which a good number of my students have already read at school. The discussion reliably collapses into a crude relativism — roughly: the Igbo had their norms, the missionaries had theirs, and neither is more correct than the other — and I want a reading that complicates that reaction in both directions rather than one more illustration of it.

The framework I keep pushing, and that I want you to work with, distinguishes three claims my students run together. First, the sampling point: the experimental literature's subject pool is drawn almost entirely from Western, educated, industrialised, rich and democratic populations, and the resulting picture of moral judgment — impartialist, individualistic, strikingly willing to treat impersonal lives as fungible — may describe an outlier population rather than the human baseline. Second, the depth point: what varies across cultures may be the content and application of norms rather than the underlying moral psychology, on the analogy with surface syntactic variation over a shared linguistic capacity, so cross-cultural difference is evidence against objectivity only if it runs deep. Third, the convergence point: one can keep a form of moral objectivity without naive universalism by asking whether a disagreement would survive better information, mutual comprehension and reflection, rather than whether everyone currently agrees.

So here is the reading I want. Work through the novel with those three claims in hand and tell me where each gains and where each loses. Concretely: the killing of Ikemefuna, which Okonkwo participates in while the boy calls him father and which leaves him unable to eat or sleep afterwards; the twins exposed in the Evil Forest; Obierika's unease at the punishment decreed for an accidental killing and at the oracle's demands generally; the position of the osu, the people dedicated to the gods and excluded from ordinary life; the egwugwu burning the church; and finally Okonkwo's suicide, the clan's refusal to touch the body, and the District Commissioner's reduction of the whole thing to a paragraph in a book about the pacification of the tribes of the Lower Niger. For each, is the conflict a surface difference over a shared moral psychology, a deep difference in the psychology itself, or something the distinction has no room for? Two traps I would like addressed head-on, since my students fall into both: our own horror at the twins and at Ikemefuna cannot settle whether the difference is deep, because those are exactly the cases our own culture has spent centuries moralising, so the intuition is not independent evidence; and Okonkwo is not a representative Igbo man, since the novel goes out of its way to make him excessive, and a critique built on warnings about sampling should itself be wary of inferring a population's moral psychology from one memorable individual.

Then the two questions I actually care about. Does the novel, as a novel, take a position on moral objectivity, or does it stage a disagreement it declines to adjudicate? And what does it add that the philosophical arguments do not? My own tentative answer is that its contribution is twofold: it makes the reader inhabit, from the inside, a moral world whose premises are not their own, and it shows the disagreement ending not in convergence but in conquest, so that the conditions under which the convergence test could even be run are precisely what coercion destroys. But I would like that sharpened and tested, including where it overreaches — where the tragic form, or Achebe's own aims, resist being recruited into this debate, and which two or three scenes I should put in front of the seminar in order to leave both the relativist and the universalist uncomfortable. I have the background reading covered, so I want your reading of the book rather than sources or further references.
