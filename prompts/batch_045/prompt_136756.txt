I was at my local rationalist meetup last weekend (were the kind of people who argue about epistemics and also about whether the old Sequences were correct about AI risk), and we got into a long argument about Kuhnian paradigm shifts and incommensurability. One friend — I'll call him Dave, he posts long comments on the forums under a pseudonym — keeps leaning on this analogy: a scientific revolution is just like a Gestalt switch or ambiguous-figure flip. You know the bistable drawings where you can see either a duck or a rabbit, or the Necker cube that seems to flip orientation even though the lines on the page never move: the ink stays the same, but your perceptual organization reorganizes, and once you've seen the duck it's hard to see the rabbit without effort. Dave says this is exactly what happens when a paradigm shifts: the observations stay the same, but the whole conceptual scheme flips, and because the paradigms are incommensurable there is no neutral observation language both sides can appeal to.

He's using it to license a pretty strong conclusion, though. He says that you can't rationally argue someone from an old paradigm into a new one, because the choice isn't a matter of weighing evidence by shared standards; it's a conversion experience, closer to therapy or religious awakening than to deduction. In practice he says this means a few things. First, it's a category error to judge a new paradigm by the old paradigm's metrics, like accuracy on old benchmarks or consistency with the old ontology. Second, peer review and funding should be maximally pluralistic and hostile to gatekeeping by old-paradigm experts, since those experts literally cannot see what the new people are seeing. Third, scientific revolutions aren't really truth-tracking; what looks like progress is mostly mob psychology plus a few lucky converts. He also says the usual list of theory-choice virtues — accuracy, consistency, breadth, simplicity, promise of future fruitfulness — is itself paradigm-relative, so it definately can't break the tie either.

I'm suspicious the analogy is doing too much work, but I don't trust my own kneejerk pushback. Can you audit it the way you'd audit a bad map or a broken correspondence? Please give me an explicit correspondence table: what in the ambiguous-figure case is supposed to play the role of the observations or data, the theory or paradigm, the flip itself, the inability to see both aspects at once, the role of expectation, history, and attention, and the fact that the figure was drawn to be bistable. Then separate the disanalogies that actually matter for Dave's three conclusions from the ones that are incidental. In particular, the ambiguous figure has a stable, neutral description of the marks — lines, curves, regions — that both duck-seers and rabbit-seers can agree on even when they can't see the other aspect, while scientific paradigms may or may not have such neutral residue. To make this precise, my friend and I invented a crude test we called the Rosetta-residue test: a shift is soft-incommensurable if there's a leftover vocabulary that still refers to the same entities and lets you translate old observation reports into new ones, maybe with changed truth-conditions or salience, and hard-incommensurable if it doesn't. Using that test, or a better one if you have it, where does the Gestalt analogy break: does the duck-rabbit flip model soft or hard incommensurability, or neither? Which features of the source — the figure is designed to have two pre-given interpretations, the flip is fast, private, and reversible, there's no notion of one aspect being more fruitful or accurate, the perceiver is fairly passive — does the analogy quietly import into scientific change, and which of those imports are doing the real argumentative work? Finally, does Dave's conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the Gestalt picture carrying it the whole time? If it fails, what would be a better analogy for a Kuhnian paradigm shift — maybe changing coordinate systems, replacing a map projection, learning a new natural language, switching from a command-line interface to a GUI, or some kind of large-scale code refactor that also changes the spec — and why would that better analogy support a different conclusion about rationality, gatekeeping, and progress? Feel free to be concrete and even a bit nerdy; I'd rather have an over-engineered answer than a slogan.
