I have just spent a thoroughly wearing weekend arguing with an old friend — she is a vision scientist, I am not — about Daniel Dennett's use of the colour phi phenomenon to argue against the 'Cartesian theatre' in Consciousness Explained. Her claim was that once you abandon the idea of a determinate moment at which a content becomes conscious, you have also abandoned the distinction between what a subject experiences and what the subject's reporting machinery ends up producing, so that Dennett's position is eliminativism under a friendlier name. I defended him against her, more out of stubbornness than conviction, and neither of us moved. The timing could not be worse: I have a stack of second-year scripts to mark on exactly this material, and after two days of that argument I do not trust my own sense of where the reasoning goes wrong. I would much rather the mark turn on what is on the page than on which side of a weekend quarrel I happened to have taken.

So I would like you to mark one script for me. The question as it was set, followed by the answer as submitted:

Question 3. In 'colour phi', a red spot is briefly presented at one location and, a short interval later, a green spot at a location some distance away, the pair being repeated in alternation. Observers report a single spot that travels from the first location to the second and changes from red to green partway along its path, even though no coloured stimulus is presented at any point along that path. Dennett argues that this raises a question about the temporal order of events in the brain, and introduces a contrast between 'Orwellian' and 'Stalinesque' revision. (a) Explain the contrast and the puzzle it is meant to answer. (b) State Dennett's own conclusion and the argument for it. (c) Raise one objection that you take to be serious, and say briefly how a defender of Dennett might reply.

Answer. (a) In colour phi the display contains only two stimuli, a red spot at one position and a green spot at another, presented in alternation with a short gap between them, but what subjects report is a single spot that moves across the gap and turns green somewhere in the middle. Nothing is ever presented along the path, so the intermediate content has to be supplied by the brain; the question is when it is supplied relative to the subject's becoming conscious of it. Dennett sets out two ways this could go. On the Orwellian story the subject is first conscious of the earlier, unrevised version of events, and the record is then rewritten afterwards, so that what is remembered is not what was experienced — as in Nineteen Eighty-Four, where the Party alters the past and the altered version is what everyone ends up remembering. On the Stalinesque story the revision is made before any of it reaches consciousness: the script is prepared in advance, as in a show trial whose verdict is fixed before the trial is staged, so that the doctored version is the only version anyone was ever conscious of. The puzzle both stories are meant to answer is how a content that depends on a later stimulus can appear earlier in the experience.

(b) Dennett's own conclusion is that there is no fact of the matter which of the two occurred. The question whether the revision happened before or after the experience only makes sense if there is a finishing line — a single place in the brain where the order in which contents arrive is the order in which they are presented to consciousness — and that is the Cartesian theatre he rejects. His positive picture is instead that conscious experience is distributed across the brain, a plurality of parallel drafts with no one of them privileged as the moment at which the subject becomes conscious. Because the two stories agree on every possible behavioural and neural measurement, and differ only over a relation to a finishing line that does not exist, there is nothing left for them to disagree about.

(c) The most serious objection I can see is that the two stories are not in fact indistinguishable. Suppose we had a reliable neural marker for the moment at which a content becomes conscious. Then we could check whether the neural activity that does the revising comes before or after that marker, and that would settle whether the revision is Orwellian or Stalinesque; so the indistinguishability is only a reflection of our present ignorance, and Dennett cannot move from our being unable to tell to there being nothing to tell. There is a more general worry of the same shape: the argument looks verificationist, inferring the absence of a fact from the absence of evidence. And a further worry follows from his conclusion. If there is no fact about when a content becomes conscious, then there is equally no fact about which contents a subject is conscious of at a given moment: the content of experience becomes whatever the reporting systems happen to produce, which is just the eliminativism his critics have always charged him with, and on his own premises he has no way of resisting the charge. A defender might reply that Dennett denies only the finishing line and not the reality of experience, but that reply is not open to him, since securing the reality of experience is precisely the work the finishing line was doing.
