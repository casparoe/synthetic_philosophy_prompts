I've been working through Frege's puzzle on my own, with no philosophy training, and I've come up against a version of it that I can't settle. As I understand the standard case: the morning star and the evening star are one and the same planet, so the statement that Hesperus is Phosphorus is, at the level of objects, an identity of a thing with itself, no less than the statement that Hesperus is Hesperus; yet the first can be a genuine astronomical discovery while the second tells you nothing you didn't already have. Frege's own answer, as I've reconstructed it, is that the two names differ in mode of presentation, and that the content of the statement is partly built out of those modes rather than out of the planet alone. The rival I keep returning to says there is nothing in the proposition beyond the object, and that what looks like a discovery is really my learning that two expressions of my language pick out the same thing: a fact about words, not about Venus.

Here is my wrinkle. Take a purely lexicographic case: I have never observed Venus, have met the two names only in a glossary, and learn the identity by being told that both name one planet. There, the about-words explanation seems to me to account for everything. But the same shape of explanation is available in the astronomers' case as well, since what they discover can equally be described as the fact that two independent ways of tracking the sky converge on a single object. So I cannot tell whether the lexicographic case is a degenerate instance of the puzzle or the model for every instance of it.

Give me a number: your credence that the informativeness of a true but non-trivial identity statement like the astronomers' case requires a semantic difference in content — something in the proposition over and above the object and the relation of identity — rather than being fully explained by metalinguistic, epistemic, or pragmatic facts. Decompose it, with a probability and one line of justification each:

1. that propositions have constituents finer-grained than the objects and properties they concern, i.e. that sense or structured content is on the right track at all;
2. that the discovery datum in the observational case is genuinely semantic rather than a fact about the subject's evidence or vocabulary, with a separate number for the lexicographic case;
3. that failures of substitution in belief reports — someone who accepts one sentence while refusing the other, where both names name the same thing — need that same apparatus, rather than hidden indexicals or implicature;
4. that one and the same apparatus has to explain both the identity datum and the belief datum, rather than a mixed view being available;
5. that the trivial side of the contrast really is cognitively empty in the way the puzzle needs, so that there is a clean datum to explain at all.

Then combine them into your headline number, saying how you combined them.
