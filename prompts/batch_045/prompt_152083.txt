hey — I'm in a small reading group working through the accuracy-first / epistemic consequentialist side of epistemology, and we got stuck on indirect normativity last week and I've been turning it over since. Here's where I landed; tell me if I've misread it. As I get it, the idea is a reply to the objection that a consequentialist standard just totals up accuracy across unrelated propositions, and so can't really respect that your evidence for one claim is separate from your evidence for another. The reply is supposed to be: fine, but accuracy was never the thing that answers your first-personal question "should I believe p?" — it evaluates belief states (or the dispositions that produce them) from outside. It's normative, just not directly. OK. But then I thought: if that's the shape of it, the real unit of deliberation has to be the policy, not the individual belief. What I choose isn't whether to believe p, it's which belief-forming policy to run. And then deliberation just becomes a plain expected-value calculation: list the candidate policies (strict conditionalizer, defer to experts, suspend on anything contested, whatever), score each one by expected accuracy given what I currently think — I actually started a spreadsheet with a Brier-style scoring rule — and adopt the argmax. Accuracy stays the fundamental standard, and it stays indirect in the sense that it doesn't whisper in your ear while you're in the middle of thinking; it just ranks the policies from outside.

My friend said that's exactly the move indirect normativity is supposed to block, but she couldnt say why, and I really don't see it — if accuracy is the standard, how is adopting the accuracy-maximizing policy not the rational thing to do? The bit that does worry me is that the expected accuracies are all computed from my own current credences, so the procedure feels like it might just hand my existing opinions back to me. I was going to patch that by scoring the policies with the credences I'd have after idealized deliberation instead — unlimited reflection, no cognitive or motivational biases, logical omniscience, the lot — so the ranking is at least the one an ideal deliberator would arrive at. But now I worry that fix is worse than the bug: once the deliberator is idealized like that, the policy framing seems to evaporate. An agent with no computational limits and no biases doesn't need a policy for managing its own future updating; there's no tradeoff left for it to adjudicate, so "what would idealized deliberation choose?" either has no answer or has an answer that tells a bounded agent like me nothing about what to do on Monday. So is my spreadsheet a clumsy implementation of a basically sound idea, or is the idea itself the mistake? Does swapping in idealized credences fix the self-certification worry, or does idealizing the deliberator make the deliberation problem collapse (or blow up) in a way I'm not seeing? And where does that leave the question I actually started with — should I believe p? Does accuracy have nothing at all to say about that question, or does it come back in once the policy-level stuff is settled?
