A friend of mine who's into artificial life — he has some silly Python thing that breeds little Turing-machine genomes — posted a long write-up about Hofstadter's typogenetics from Gödel, Escher, Bach, and then about a variant he coded himself. I don't know the book at all beyond its reputation, and I can't tell which parts of his post are the actual point of the chapter and which parts he's gotten carried away with. I'd paste the whole thing but its long, so here's the gist, mostly in his words:

"Typogenetics is the formal system Hofstadter invented to show that DNA is secretly doing Gödel's proof. You have strands built from the four bases A, C, G, T. A strand gets translated three bases at a time into an enzyme, which is a sequence of amino acids; the enzyme folds up and then acts back on strands — cutting them, deleting bases, switching to the other strand, moving left and right, entering copy mode and so on — and the result is new strands. That loop is what he calls the Central Dogma of Typogenetics. It's exactly Gödel's move: a strand can encode an enzyme that operates on strands, so the system can in effect talk about itself.

The isomorphism is then: strands are like numbers, enzymes are like statements about numbers (or rules for deriving them), and translation is doing the job of Gödel numbering. Which means a strand that brings about its own replication is the typogenetic version of a Gödel sentence — a self-referential object that the system itself can't settle. That's why Hofstadter spends forever on his famous self-replicating strand: it isn't a cute puzzle, it's the sentence that makes the system incomplete.

And this isn't just an analogy. Typogenetics is a genuine formal system with mechanical rules, and since it can simulate arbitrary computations it's Turing complete, so Gödel's theorem applies to it directly: there are facts about which strands arise that are true but can never be derived from the rules.

I built my own version where strands can grow without bound, I threw out the copy operation, and I added an operation that appends an arbitrary base. Unbounded strand plus append is basically a counter machine, so my version is Turing complete, and by Gödel the question 'does strand s ever produce a copy of itself?' is undecidable — every self-replicating strand in it is a true-but-unprovable fact about the system.

Which is also why this isn't just a game: DNA in a cell is a self-replicating strand in exactly this sense, so cells are literally instantiating a Gödel sentence, and any formal model of a cell inherits incompleteness."

The step I'm most suspicious of is the "it's not just an analogy" one, because that feels like exactly the move every pop-Gödel book makes and gets slapped for. But I half believe the thing about his variant, since encoding a counter machine in it does sound doable, and I've got no intuition about whether the self-replicating strand is really the Gödel sentence or just a quine with extra steps — someone once told me it's more like a quine, and that quining is Hofstadter's own term for programs that print their own source, but I don't know if that distinction matters here.
