I'm putting together a short position paper for a graduate seminar on consciousness — the paper is meant to defend a broadly Rosenthal-style higher-order thought view — and before I workshop it I want a consistency audit of the package I have ended up with, because I have the nagging sense that several of these claims were each borrowed from arguments that do not really live together. Here is the set, stated as flatly as I can manage:

(a) A mental state's being conscious is an extrinsic, relational matter: it is conscious just in case the subject has a suitable higher-order thought — non-inferential, assertoric, not itself introspected — to the effect that they are in that state. I want this to be a genuine reduction rather than a correlation: the theory should say what state consciousness consists in, without helping itself to phenomenal vocabulary.
(b) It follows, and I accept it, that there can be unconscious mental states intrinsically just like conscious ones — unconscious pains, unconscious perceptions — so nothing about the first-order state itself settles whether it is conscious.
(c) I also think consciousness does real work: what a subject does, says, and can rationally control depends on which of their states are conscious, and I do not want to be pushed toward anything epiphenomenalist.
(d) But I hold that higher-order thoughts cannot manufacture experience out of nothing. Call this the echo case: a subject's first-order pain stops while the higher-order thought as of being in pain briefly outlasts it; my verdict is that for that interval there is no conscious pain, because there is nothing there to be conscious of.
(e) At the same time I take introspective fallibility seriously and want the theory to permit it. In what I have been calling the mislabeled-discomfort case, a subject is plainly undergoing something and takes it to be nausea when it is in fact hunger pangs; I want to say they are consciously aware of the state and simply wrong about what it is.
(f) I take it as fixed that human infants and a great many nonhuman animals are conscious, and I do not want the theory to require them to possess concepts of mental states, or of themselves.
(g) I also want to preserve the transparency of experience: in ordinary perception the subject is aware of the scene before them rather than of their own sensory states, and I think a good theory should predict that rather than treat it as a special case.
(h) Finally, I do not want to slide into a first-order or self-representational theory — partly because I think (b) is a real and desirable consequence, and partly because I think first-order views leave no room for the difference the higher-order state is supposed to make.

I cannot tell which of these is load-bearing and which is along for the ride. Please audit the set: which pairs genuinely conflict, which only look as though they do and dissolve once the right distinction is drawn, and for each real conflict what the cheapest repair would be and what that repair would cost me elsewhere in the list. Three things I especially want addressed. First, I have been told that (d) and (e) are the crux and that I must choose between them; I would like to know whether that is right, and if there is a way to hold both, exactly what it commits me to about the content of a higher-order thought and about what the theory can then explain. Second, (f) and (g) both seem to push toward loosening what counts as a higher-order state — toward something more perceptual, or less conceptual — and I suspect that pressure travels backward and threatens (a) or (b); if so, tell me precisely where it lands. Third, I am unsure whether (b) and (c) can both stand, or whether a state that could exist unchanged while unconscious is thereby barred from doing the causal work (c) assigns to consciousness. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equal, and close by naming the single revision to one item that would clean up the most. I would much rather be argued out of one of these than given a survey of the field, so please spend your words on what my own commitments actually force me to.
