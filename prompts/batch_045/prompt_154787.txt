I'm the senior-class student representative on my district's Social Studies Curriculum Advisory Committee. I'm seventeen; I've taken AP Environmental Science and the world history course that this unit feeds into, and I'm the only member of the committee who is not an adult. We're finishing a revision of the ninth-grade "Humans and the Earth System" unit, which currently opens with the sentence "about 11,700 years ago, at the start of the Holocene." Two artifacts have to be finalized by the December meeting: a classroom timeline banner, which has room for exactly one start year printed next to the word Anthropocene, and a one-sentence "periodization statement" that opens the unit and that our state framework requires to rest on "documentable criteria."

Until this spring, our minutes recorded a plan to print whatever start date stratigraphers formally ratified, on the grounds that a unit of geologic time is a discovery rather than a decision and that a school district has no business picking one. That anchor is now gone. The working group's proposal — a lake-bottom section in Ontario with a 1950 boundary marked by bomb fallout — was voted down in 2024, and the international body's own statement afterward said the term will keep being used by everyone outside the official time scale anyway. So we either choose a number ourselves or print none, and roughly half the committee thinks the honest thing is to print none.

The options actually on the table are 1950, which has the most evidence and the most expert process behind it even though it failed as a formal unit, but which arguably dates the human takeover to a burst of postwar consumption in rich countries; 1610, the dip in atmospheric CO2 recorded in ice cores that some researchers attribute to the demographic collapse of Indigenous Americans after European contact, which connects to material already in the course but rests on a small signal and a contested reading of it; and roughly 1780, the industrial and steam-engine date, which matches how the course already narrates modernity but is really a European milestone rather than a global marker. There is also a "no single date" option: print a range or a bracket instead of a year.

Three things in the room are making this harder than a vote. Our department chair has proposed what she calls a "stated-rationale convention": the banner prints a year plus a short clause naming what that year was selected to mark, so that the number is visibly a choice under a stated criterion rather than a fact students take on authority. One of our science teachers argues that no single year is scientifically available to us at all: boundaries get fixed at one reference locality and the marker shows up at different times in different places, so "one number for every classroom in the district" is a pedagogical convenience that misdescribes the evidence. And a petition with about two hundred student signatures asks for 1610 on the grounds that 1950 "assigns responsibility to everyone and therefore to no one," while two teachers reply that using a measurement to make a claim about blame is a category error unless we say explicitly that that is what we're doing. The assistant superintendent wants whatever we print to be defensible as neutral.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate to the committee before the December meeting. It should give one concrete recommendation — a year, or a refusal to name one — with the exact wording for the banner and for the one-sentence statement, and defend it in terms the history teachers, the science department, and the administrator could each actually respond to. It should say plainly what kind of thing we think we are deciding: whether the start of the Anthropocene, for our purposes, is a measurement we are reporting, a periodization we are choosing for a teaching purpose, or something else, and therefore who has standing to decide it and what the petition is really asking us for. Give the strongest argument on the side you reject its full weight, not a sentence of dismissal.

At the end, add a short note on confidence: say how confident the memo is in its recommendation and in each of the premises it leans on, and single out the one point it is least sure of. I'd rather hand the committee a document that flags its own weak joint than one that sounds more settled than it is. Please keep the whole thing under about nine hundred words.
