I've been going down a rabbit hole on the algorithmic fairness impossibility stuff and I want to use a real case to test how much the results actually explain. The case is the 2016 ProPublica investigation of COMPAS in Broward County, and the fight that followed: ProPublica's story that black defendants who were not rearrested got flagged as higher-risk at roughly twice the rate of white defendants who were not (and the mirror-image gap the other way for people who were rearrested), the vendor's reply that the scores were calibrated and so the differing error rates were not evidence of bias, and the Wisconsin case a little later where the state supreme court let the score into a sentencing while telling judges to treat it cautiously. I know the broad shape of these events, so don't retell them; analyse them.

Here is roughly where I am. The impossibility results say, crudely: if the base rates of the outcome differ between groups, then a score cannot in general be both calibrated within groups and equal in its false-positive and false-negative rates across groups, barring degenerate cases. So on the face of it the episode is a perfect illustration — one side complaining in the language of error rates, the other defending itself in the language of calibration, and the theorem saying no, you can't have both. But that reading makes the theorem sound like it predicted the fight, and I suspect it is doing less work than that. So: give me a disciplined mapping first. Who exactly is the decision-maker the theorem is about — the judge, the county that bought the tool, the vendor who set the threshold and the score levels? What are "the options" in the Florida pretrial setting, and what counts as the evidence each side was actually appealing to?

Then the parts I keep tripping on, which I want you to deal with straight rather than smoothing over. One: the error-rate numbers ProPublica reported required collapsing a ten-level score into something like "high risk" versus not, and the theorems I have seen are mostly stated for binary classifiers — so where does that cut point enter the argument, and can a defender of the tool say the conflict is an artefact of a dichotomisation the tool never made? Two: the outcome being predicted was recividism as measured by rearrest, and it is only observed for people who were actually released, so the joint distribution the theorem quantifies over is not the one anybody can estimate from the data — plus the population of scored defendants is itself downstream of who got arrested and charged in the first place. Does the result survive being applied to a distribution you can't see, or does it become unfalsifiable? Three: the theorem is silent about accuracy. A weak classifier can look calibrated without much difficulty, and by most accounts COMPAS was only modestly predictive in Broward. So is the trade-off the interesting fact here, or is it second-order to the fact that the score was barely better than chance, in which case the case is an anomaly for the theory rather than a confirmation of it?

Last thing: the theorem is standardly read as "you must choose which criterion to satisfy," but the episode suggests the choosing gets done by whoever controls the threshold and the deployment, not by anyone reasoning about fairness. Say whether that is a case the results never anticipated, and what the episode should make someone who believes the results do revise.
