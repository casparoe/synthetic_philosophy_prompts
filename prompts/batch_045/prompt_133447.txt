I come to philosophy late, after a career in experimental physics, and this term I am helping a colleague grade take-home exams for an upper-division course on the philosophy of mind. I would like an independent assessment of one answer before I write my comments, so please do not take any steer from what you imagine I think of it.

The question set to the students was this:

Consciousness is sometimes described as a user illusion — a streamlined interface onto the brain's largely unconscious activity, rather like the icons and menus on a computer desktop. Assess the following argument: if consciousness is such an interface, then there are no phenomenal properties, that is, no intrinsic qualitative character that experience has and that neuroscience cannot capture; and the widespread conviction that there are such properties is itself explained by the interface. In your answer, distinguish the claim that consciousness is an interface from the claim that phenomenal properties do not exist; say whether the first supports the second; and identify the strongest objection to the position that results from accepting both, together with the reply its defenders usually give.

The answer I have been given reads as follows:

The user-illusion picture begins with a platitude that nobody should dispute. Most of what the nervous system does is inaccessible to introspection: the reconstruction of a stable visual scene across saccades, the filling in of the blind spot, the control of gait. Conscious experience is the small, simplified digest of all this that the organism is given, in the way that a desktop icon stands in for a great deal of machine code. Two things follow. First, there is no Cartesian theatre. Nothing in the brain needs to receive the finished show, because the digest is produced by the same competitive, parallel processes that produced the behaviour, and the user to whom it is handed is a central executive in the prefrontal systems, where deciding gets done. Second, the items presented in the digest are not constituents of the neural processes they summarise; they are the interface's way of marking distinctions the organism has to act on.

From here the inference to illusionism is short. If what the interface displays is a convenience rather than a discovery — in the way that a stick half-immersed in water is not straight, whatever it looks like — then the qualitative properties we seem to encounter are not really there. Dennett's claim, then, is that we labour under a positive illusion of phenomenal properties: introspection represents us as enjoying intrinsic, ineffable qualia, and in doing so it misrepresents us.

The most serious objection is the familiar one that illusion is self-defeating here. To be under an illusion at all you must be conscious, so there has to be conscious experience for the illusion to be an illusion of. The standard reply is that the illusion is cognitive rather than experiential: we form the judgement that our experience has an intrinsic qualitative character, and that judgement is false. A false judgement needs no appearance that is itself qualitative in order to be made.
