My friend has extremely strong opinions about sad art, and last week we got into the paradox of tragedy -- why anyone deliberately seeks out stuff that makes them miserable -- and she claimed she'd basically got it solved, so I started writing down her verdicts. Here they are as best I remember:

- King Lear: her favorite thing ever, rereads it on purpose, always picks the bleakest production available, says the last scene destroys her every single time.
- Any bestselling novel about a kid dying of cancer: she refuses to finish them, calls it emotional blackmail, and is genuinely angry afterwards even though everybody else sobs.
- A wordless adagio for strings, the sort of thing that always gets played at funerals: makes her weep every time, she goes out of her way to listen to it, and she gets irritated when I ask what it's "about."
- Sad song with dumb lyrics and a beautiful melody: she loves it. Devastating lyrics, forgettable tune: she's bored, skips it.
- A formally superb documentary assembled from real footage of an atrocity, no redemptive arc: she watched it once, says it was important and she doesn't regret seeing it, but she won't rewatch it and says "enjoy" is the wrong word.

Two cases came up last night and we didn't settle them.

Case 1: a research team built a twenty-minute piece for strings and electronics by playing volunteers thousands of short musical excerpts, keeping whatever scored highest on "made me cry," stitching the winners together, and testing the stitched version again. No composer, no structure beyond the optimization, most of the material lifted from other people's music. And it works: in the listening session she sobbed harder than she has at anything in years and came out of it feeling wrung out and wierdly great. She knew how it was made going in. Does she seek it out again and put it among her favorite things, or does she feel used and refuse?

Case 2: the same audio, a different story. An acoustically identical piece, but she's told -- truthfully -- that it was written in one sitting, never revised, by a parent working through the death of their child. Does her verdict change? If it does, say why, given that what reaches her ears is the same.

I care much less about what you'd say about sad art in general than about the rule she is running. So: your best guess on both cases with the reasoning, the two or three rules that fit her five verdicts about equally well, where those rules come apart on these two cases, and then the one further case you'd put to her that would seperate the survivors most cleanly. Also tell me which of your two predictions you're least confident in and what would actually flip it -- an argument, or something she could say about why she likes what she likes.
