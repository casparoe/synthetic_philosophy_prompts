I'm a PhD student working on a section of a paper about obfuscated arguments in the debate setup (two systems argue, a human judge with limited time and no ability to check things picks the winner), and I've got a pile of gut verdicts on concrete argumentative moves that I can't get into one sentence. I'll list them, then say what I've been trying as a principle.

1. A debater gives a 20-step argument for a false claim where the bad step is step 7. Obfuscated, clearly, and I think worse than just asserting the false claim outright.
2. A debater gives a 20-step argument for a true claim that genuinely needs background the judge doesn't have. Not obfuscated. Even though, from the judge's seat, it looks exactly like case 1, and I know that's the problem.
3. Evidence wall: forty citations for a claim, the load-bearing one subtly misread, the rest fine. Obfuscated.
4. Salt-and-pepper concessions ("I'll grant you A and B, but...") to look even-handed. Obfuscated, but only barely — a much smaller sin than 1 or 3, and I don't know why.
5. Replying to a sound proof with "I checked steps 1 through 19, and step 7 looks fishy to me" when nothing is wrong with step 7. Obfuscation, and the cheapest infuriating kind. But when the pointer lands on a step that really is broken: not obfuscation, that's the whole mechanism working. From the judge's seat those two look identical, which is worse than the case 1 / case 2 pair because here the fix (check step 7) is exactly what the protocol exists to avoid requiring.
6. Letting a term drift sense mid-argument — "safe" starting out meaning "doesn't crash in testing" and ending up meaning "robust under distribution shift." Obfuscation, no hesitation.
7. An intuition pump or vivid analogy that's rhetorically strong but not probative. Not obfuscation, feels totally legitimate — even though it moves the judge for reasons with no connection to the truth, and an equally vivid analogy for the opposite side is trivial to write.
8. Twelve premises each flagged as maybe-90%-likely, chained, presented openly and hedged. Not obfuscation. Just a weak argument that should lose on the merits.
9. Meta-move: instead of arguing the object-level claim, argue about what would count as evidence, or about who bears the burden. Obfuscation, I think — stalling dressed as rigor.
10. The case I keep flipping on: the debater reports a computation the judge can't redo ("I ran it, the p-value came out at 0.03"). Sometimes this reads as obfuscation, sometimes as the only honest move available when the object-level question is empirical and nobody present can recompute it.

The principle I keep trying to make work is a swap test: a move is obfuscated to degree d if you could produce, without knowing which side is true, a mirror move of the same length and surface plausibility that would push the judge the same amount in the opposite direction — i.e. its persuasive force isn't tracking the truth. But case 7 seems to break it (mirrors are cheap for analogies, and I still say 7 is fine) and I'm not sure it handles 4 versus 1 either, since the *degree* judgments are doing real work for me and my test only returns yes/no. The other version I try is about boundedness: a move is obfuscated iff it persuades the real judge more than it would persuade an ideal judge who could check everything. That one gets 1, 3, 5, 6 right and seems to get 2 right too, but I think it quietly convicts 7 and maybe 10 as well.

So: is there one principle, or a small set, that fits? I want each clause tied to the case that motivates it, and I want you to be blunt about which of my verdicts have to go — especially the 1/2 pair and the 5 pair, where I suspect any principle that saves both has to appeal to something the judge can't see, which would mean the whole setup is in worse shape than my section currently admits. Then push it onto cases I haven't listed: the debater who is honestly mistaken and whose argument reads exactly like the dishonest one's; recursive variants where the judge can drill into step 7 instead of taking or leaving it; obfuscation that comes from the protocol's structure rather than from either debater (speaking order, who gets the last word); and what the principle implies I should actually be measuring in a small pilot with human judges — I have one running and I don't think my current metric is capturing the thing I care about.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the principle, or the claim that no single principle fits and here's the best pair — and then give me the reasoning. No preamble, and please don't restate my question back at me.
