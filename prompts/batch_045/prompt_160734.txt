Hi — I'm a journalist, I cover science and ideas (mostly AI and cognitive science), which means I spend my days around people who are very sure about things and my evenings translating them for readers who aren't. I need help with a wedding toast and I've concluded it's actually a philosophy problem wearing a writer's-block costume.

The groom, Sam, is an old friend: a committed impartial consequentialist of the sort who has actually rearranged his life and given a serious chunk of money away because of it, and who at the same time holds, completely without irony, to one particular project that he says is what makes his life his — AI safety work, which he describes as his ground project and would defend against any reallocation of him whatsoever. I sat in on a seminar where he argued that the integrity objection only bites if you think reasons are reasons for someone, and that he doesn't think that, and then he went home and kept working on the very thing the impartial view is supposed to be unable to accomodate. The bride, Nora, is a violinist; she is not a philosopher and has approximately zero patience for being described as a good outcome. Half the room will be Sam's philosophy and EA crowd, who will hear every echo; the other half is her family — musicians and high school teachers from a small town — who will hear the tone and nothing else. Last night at the rehearsal dinner the best man tried a bit built around the idea that of all possible partners Sam could have searched he'd found the optimum, and it went over very badly with everyone. I don't want to make a warmer version of the same mistake. I'm also pretty sure I have to avoid the register Williams is talking about when he imagines a man who can save only one person from a wreck being told that it is permissible for him to save his wife: the wrong toast here is the one where affection gets a justificatory thought bolted onto the front of it and everyone can hear the bolt.

Here's the thing I actually want the speech built around, and the thing that's making me nervous. They have an anniversary ritual: every June each of them writes the other a one-page case for us in their own professional idiom — he writes something like an expected-value memo with confidence intervals, she writes a theme and variations, or a set of variations on a theme they picked the first year — and they read them aloud to each other and to nobody else, ever. It's the best thing I know about them. But I have a horrible feeling that the moment I say, out loud, in front of a hundred people, why the ritual works, I will have produced exactly the thought that shouldn't be there: that an explanation of the mechanism by which his memo is affectionate turns it back into a memo, and that any third-personal description of a marriage offered from inside it is going to land that way no matter how warm the adjectives are. My hypothesis, which I don't trust: it isn't the maximizing vocabulary that's the problem, it's the explaining, and I can talk about the ritual only by talking about it the way she does rather than the way he does. Tell me if that's wrong.

So: please be a professional philosopher who also writes for general readers and has stood up at a few of these. Before you write anything, brainstorm properly — I want to see the notes, don't skip them — about what makes a thought one too many in a room like this: is it the content of the thought, the audience, the fact of saying it aloud, the fact that it's a thought about something that shouldn't need a thought, or something about who is doing the thinking? Then list the specific moves I might make in this toast that are that failure in a nicer suit. Then write me a four-minute toast that does real structural work with the ritual, says something true about Sam's integrity and about Nora's patience with it, and lands as affection rather than as a lecture or a conversion or a sneer. No jokes at her family's expense, no reassuring anyone that love is really commitment to a cause, and if you think there's a version of this that has to use the word utility somewhere to stay honest with Sam, say so and show me where.
