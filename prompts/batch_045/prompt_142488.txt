I work on model evaluation and forecast aggregation for a prediction-market platform, and I keep getting stuck on a claim about markets that I cannot translate into my own terms: the family of results usually called no-trade theorems, the agreement result usually associated with Aumann, and the escape routes that get offered for them — heterogeneous priors, ambiguity or Knightian uncertainty, transaction costs and no-trade intervals, noise and liquidity traders, limits to arbitrage, the fragility of common knowledge. I know all of this only second-hand and I have no way to check a reference, so please do not lean on citations, titles, or who said what; I want the structure of the arguments and nothing that requires a library.

My vocabulary, in which I am fluent: proper scoring rules and their incentive properties; calibration and refinement as separately evaluable virtues; aggregation by linear and logarithmic pooling; cost-function automated market makers with bounded worst-case subsidy; inference from order flow under adverse selection; distribution shift and performative effects, where predicting a thing changes the thing; and the machine-learning habit of treating persistent disagreement between models fit to the same data as a resource — diversity of inductive bias — rather than as noise to be engineered away. I am also used to asking of any two predictors whether their disagreement survives a disclosure loop: show each of them everything the other saw, including the other's current forecast, and see whether the gap closes.

What I want, first, is a faithful restatement in that vocabulary of three things: the core result, that mutually recognized gains from trade cannot arise purely from differences in information when priors are shared; the work done by the shared-prior assumption and by the nearby idea that all disagreement should be traceable to differences in evidence rather than to differences in how evidence is weighted; and each of the escape routes, as a claim about what has to be true of my traders for volume to be nonzero. Then I want the residue report: what translated cleanly, what had to be bent, and what has no expression on my side at all.

Three places where I expect the translation to fail, and I would like you to press on them rather than smooth them over.

First, the shared prior. Two forecasters on my platform can both be well calibrated and still disagree for as long as anyone has measured, in the same way that two models with different inductive biases can fit the same training set equally well and diverge off distribution. Calibration, as I use it, is always calibration relative to some information set, so joint calibration and persistent disagreement are not in tension. Is the shared-prior assumption therefore closer to a ban on diversity that cannot be traced back to information than to anything about shared data? And is it best read as a constraint on rationality, or as a claim about the causal history of the disagreement — about how these two agents came to have the views they do? My suspicion is the latter, and that most of its apparent force as a rationality requirement is borrowed from that.

Second, the conclusion. My platform's automated market maker takes the other side of any order at a posted price, and its loss is bounded by construction. As far as I can tell that makes it a noise trader by definition, one whose only function is to absorb the other side. If that is right, does the theorem have any bite at all on a subsidized mechanism, or does it degenerate into a statement about how much information a given subsidy purchases? And if so, is the puzzle of why there is volume genuinely detachable from the question of whether prices aggregate information — the Grossman-Stiglitz-flavoured worry that fully revealing prices would destroy the incentive to produce the information they reveal?

Third, the distinction I have started using internally. Call a disagreement signal-driven when each side would attribute it to the other having seen something they have not, and weight-driven when both sides grant they are looking at the same evidence and differ in what they make of it. My hypothesis is that the no-trade result is often presented as ruling out both, but that the escape routes partition along this line: some of them let weight-driven disagreement generate trade, others do not, and the epistemology of markets — whether trading is a discovery process or a way of transferring money from the badly calibrated to the well calibrated — comes apart depending on which. Test that: state the distinction precisely enough to be used, and tell me whether it does any work in the translation or collapses into something the literature already has under a different name, which you should describe without naming its authors.

Finish by telling me what the residue means. Is there anything these arguments assert that my vocabulary cannot express — some role for common knowledge, or for the difference between a posterior being publicly posted and it being common knowledge, that has no counterpart in how I think about ensembling and aggregation? Or does the residue point the other way, at a result that is a coherence constraint on a class of models and therefore compatible with any volume I could ever observe? If it is the latter, say so plainly, and say whether I should treat that as a defect in the result or as exactly what a coherence constraint is for.
