You are a social epistemologist and a formal theorist of collective judgment. You have been retained to mediate an adversarial collaboration between two camps in the debate over information cascades and rational herding, and to draft the joint statement both would sign. Work slowly and carefully: before you write the document, spend some time brainstorming the structure of the disagreement yourself — list what each camp is actually committed to, which of their apparent conflicts are about the world and which are about words, which modelling assumptions are doing the work, and what kind of result would genuinely embarrass each side. Only then draft.

The two camps:

- The Fragility camp holds that herding on the observed actions of others is a real and structural failure of collective learning: private information gets stranded, a cascade that locks onto the wrong option resists later contrary evidence of a kind that would have sufficed earlier, and deliberative, electoral, and market institutions should therefore be deliberately redesigned — through sequencing, disclosure timing, and incentives for dissent — to slow or break cascades.
- The Efficiency camp holds that the appearance of failure is largely an artifact of knife-edge modelling assumptions: once you allow endogenous timing, costly but acquirable private evidence, graded or noisy signals, reputational and market feedback, and repeated interaction among players who know the game repeats, collective learning is substantially self-correcting, and institutional tampering aimed at disrupting herding typically destroys a cheap information channel and leaves groups worse off than they were.

Your brief:

1. Agreed claims. Number them and give between eight and twelve. These are claims both camps can sign: about what the canonical sequential social-learning model establishes when its assumptions hold; about the difference between behavioral herding (correlated action) and an informational cascade (action that has become insensitive to the agent's own private signal); about the difference between the rationality of an individual action given the public history and the epistemic efficiency of the group's outcome; about the fact that an institution which publishes a running tally of discrete choices is by design discarding part of the evidence held by later movers; about what would have to be true for a cascade to be broken by subsequent evidence. Each claim must be substantive rather than diplomatic: if a claim is one whose denial neither camp would bother defending, cut it.

2. A disagreements table. Columns: the issue; the proposition the Fragility camp would word for itself; the proposition the Efficiency camp would word for itself; and a classification of the residual disagreement as factual, normative, or verbal. Neither column may be a strawman. Each entry must be phrased so that its own side would be willing to sign that exact sentence as a statement of its view, not merely as something an opponent attributed to it.

3. Verbal disputes, identified explicitly. Say which parts of the long-running argument are disputes about words rather than about the world — for instance whether cascade denotes an informational state or merely correlated behavior, whether rational here means Bayes-rational given the public history or truth-conducive in the long run, whether fragile is being used to mean slow to converge or prone to locking in error — and for each, state the substantive question underneath it, or say honestly that there is none and the debate should drop that thread.

4. Cruxes, with a prediction registry. For each crux give: the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one camp; one concrete case or test that both camps accept as bearing on it; what each camp predicts about that case; and what outcome each camp would count as a genuine blow to its own position rather than a case it can accommodate after the fact. Restrict your cases to settings whose facts are common knowledge — a committee or jury that votes aloud in a fixed order, a run on a bank, the adoption of a consumer technology standard, choice between two similar-looking restaurants on an unfamiliar street, an idealized urn-drawing experiment whose parameters you specify yourself — and specify the parameters yourself where they matter. Include at least these cruxes: (a) whether the knife-edge assumptions of the canonical model — coarse public binary actions, an exogenous fixed order, private signals of equal precision, a single pass with no option to wait or to pay for better information — are load-bearing or merely simplifying; (b) whether familiar real-world herding episodes are better explained by informational cascade or by preference- and reputation-driven conformity, and what observable difference would distinguish the two explanations; (c) whether the right benchmark for saying that the group does well is asymptotic efficiency, rate of convergence, or the tail risk of a locked-in wrong cascade; (d) whether reputational herding and informational cascading are one mechanism described at two levels or two distinct mechanisms that call for different remedies.

5. A joint verdict on three institutional proposals, with each camp's prediction recorded: Blind First Round, in which every member records an independent judgment before any tally is published, followed by exactly one revision round after the tally is released; Informed-First Ordering, in which the member with the highest-precision private signal is deliberately placed last rather than first in the sequence; and a Contrarian Bounty, a reward for members whose final judgment proves correct while having been in the minority at the time they gave it. For each proposal, give the mechanism by which it would help, the mechanism by which it would hurt, the parameter regime in which each camp concedes that it is net-positive or net-negative, and whether any of the three can be jointly endorsed or jointly rejected by both camps together.

6. A short closing section, co-signed, stating what a genuinely decisive study or natural experiment would have to look like, and what interim practical recommendation both camps would accept given that no such study exists.

Constraints: argue from first principles and from the structure of the models themselves. Do not cite, name, date, or attempt to quote any published work, author, or experiment, and do not construct a bibliography or suggest further reading. Where you need an empirical claim, flag it explicitly as an empirical claim and say how it would be tested rather than asserting it on authority. Prefer exactness to diplomacy: after reading your document, the residual disagreement between the camps should be sharper than it was before, not smoothed over. Aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,600 words, in the formal register of a memorandum that two research teams are prepared to put their names to.
