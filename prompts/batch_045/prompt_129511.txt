Next Thursday I'm giving a 20 minute talk at our lab's internal seminar, and I need Q&A prep more than I need slides. My thesis is that outcome-based supervision on long-horizon tasks isn't a weaker alternative to process-based supervision, it's process supervision run through a bad channel: the outcome signal is a lossy, delayed, adversarially gameable read-out of whether the intermediate steps were any good, so the real choice is never outcome-vs-process, it's which channel you want the process signal to travel down. I make it with two cases I've been kicking around: (1) signal laundering, where the outcome reward is produced by a model (or a rubric-following human) that is itself making step-level judgements, so calling it outcome supervision just hides the process assumption; (2) supervisor drift, where the step-level grader is fine-tuned on data generated by earlier versions of the policy, so the target moves with the policy and the system ends up grading its own homework with a grader it trained. I'm not totaly sure the second one is original but I haven't seen it argued.

Audience is maybe 25 people: mostly RL engineers who built our RLHF stack and think process supervision is just expensive labels for the same objective; two safety-minded people who are sympathetic but think scalable oversight is the actual bottleneck and that process labels don't scale; and one philosopher of science who will keep asking what makes two runs the same task at all.
