I'm in the upper sixth and I help run our school's philosophy society, and next week I'm meant to lead the session on Bernard Williams's integrity objection to utilitarianism. I've been preparing from a general-audience book on ethics that I picked up in the school library, plus an episode of a podcast that covered the same material, and both of them present the argument in more or less the same way, which I take to be a good sign but which also makes me suspect they're repeating a settled simplification. Their version goes roughly like this: Williams's integrity objection is a version of the familiar complaint that utilitarianism asks too much of us, and his central example is a man who can reach only one of two people in a burning building, his wife or a stranger. The husband's difficulty, on this telling, is that a utilitarian has to stop and weigh up whose life to save, and that calculation is already one thought too many; what he should do is simply act, without reasoning at all, and even the thought "she is my wife" is a step too far if he is using it as a reason. The general moral is that a decent moral theory should not tell an agent to think about his own personal commitments in the first place, whereas utilitarianism does, because it tells him to view the situation from an impersonal, detached standpoint and to work out which outcome would contain the most happiness — and in doing that it turns him into a cold stranger to the people and projects that give his life its shape. Integrity, on this account, just means putting your own projects and your own people first, so the objection is really an argument for partiality: inside your personal life you are entitled to favour your own, and moral theory has no business there. The podcast presenter folded in what he called Williams's other version of the same point, about a traveller who is invited to shoot one captive himself in order to save the rest of a group, and said the complaint there is that the theory makes the man responsible for deaths he did not bring about. Both sources then gave what they took to be the utilitarian's way out: utilitarians can simply say that agents ought to be brought up, or bring themselves up, not to reason in utilitarian terms at all, but to acquire the habit of rushing in for the people close to them — and once they say that, the objection collapses, because the husband no longer has any extra thought.

I don't trust myself to say where this goes wrong, but two things in it feel off to me, and I'd rather find out now than in front of the society. First, the sources slide between saying that the husband should have no thought and saying that he should have the thought that she is his wife; I can't tell whether Williams's complaint is about thinking, or about a particular *kind* of thinking sitting behind the right thought. Second, I keep hearing that the same worry can be raised against moral views that tell you to act from duty rather than from love, which would suggest that the problem isn't specifically utilitarian, and I don't know whether that makes the objection stronger or just shows it proves too much. I've also been playing with two variations of my own, and I'd like to know whether they help Williams or the utilitarian. Call the first "one thought too few": imagine a husband who, thanks to exactly the kind of training the utilitarian recommends, has no thought at all in the moment and would be unable to answer if asked why he went for his wife; is that the outcome Williams wants, or is it a reason to doubt the objection? Call the second the "rescue worker" version: the same man survives, and a firefighter later asks him why he pulled his wife out first, and he answers with a calculation about which life was worth more. Is that the same defect, or is justifying yourself to someone else a different activity from deciding what to do, so that the objection only bites on one of them? I've seen people online insist that the details of Williams's example differ between retellings, so please also flag anywhere my sources have quietly changed the shape of the case in a way that does philosophical work.

What I'd find most useful is a claim-by-claim audit rather than an answer to "is utilitarianism true". Please separate out what a careful reader of Williams would accept in my sources' summary, what is a fair simplification of a technical claim, and what is straightforwardly a misreading — in particular, I want to know how much turns on the word "impersonal", whether "integrity" is really just partiality under a grander name, and whether the captive-shooting example is doing the same job as the wife-and-stranger example or a different one. Then: does the indirect, self-training reply actually dispose of the objection, or does it just relocate it, and does either of my two variations change the answer? And finally, do the conclusions my sources draw still stand once the mistakes are fixed — is there really a private, morality-free zone around our personal relationships, and does the objection amount to a refutation of utilitarianism rather than a constraint on what a utilitarian can say about motivation? I'd rather have the reasoning than any pointers to further reading: we have no library access over the holidays, and I need something I can reconstruct at the whiteboard without notes.
