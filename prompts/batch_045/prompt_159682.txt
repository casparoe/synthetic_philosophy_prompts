I am trying to extract the principle behind my own verdicts on whether brain size — or any quantity closely tracking it — has any bearing on a creature's moral standing. Please treat every neurobiological claim below as stipulated rather than as something to verify, and assume that each creature is a healthy adult of its kind unless I say otherwise.

My verdicts:

1. A three-year-old human child versus an adult chimpanzee, where the chimpanzee has both the heavier brain and the greater number of cortical neurons. The child's claim on us is not weaker; if anything it is stronger.

2. A human adult whose brain is roughly a quarter below the species mean, with no detectable difference in cognition or affect. No difference in standing whatsoever.

3. An elephant versus a chicken. The elephant's claim is stronger, and I am tempted to say it is stronger partly because of what its much larger nervous system makes possible.

4. A corvid with a brain of a few grams which, stipulate, matches a much larger-brained mammal in flexible learning, social responsiveness and apparent affect. The smallness of the brain is irrelevant once the psychological profile is matched.

5. A hypothetical species with brains a tenth of our mass but, stipulate, twice our neuron density and a psychological profile indistinguishable from ours. Equal standing.

6. A whole-brain emulation of a human being run on hardware that realises it at half the original speed. Equal standing: the rate of realisation should not matter.

My tentative principle is that standing tracks the richness of a creature's possible conscious life, with brain size serving only as evidence for it — but I cannot get that to yield verdict 2 cleanly, since the evidence there is slightly weaker and yet I want to say the standing is identical. Verdict 2 also sits badly with verdict 3, and verdict 6 sits badly with any view on which what matters is the amount of neural processing per unit of time rather than the pattern realised.

So: state a principle, or the two or three best candidates, precisely enough to be applied, saying which clause each of my verdicts motivates and which quantity brain size is supposed to be a proxy for — mass, neuron count, something like integrated complexity, or the crossing of a threshold capacity. Tell me which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate and would therefore have me overturn. Then give me its verdicts on two cases I have not put: an artificial system with no neurons at all whose conversational behaviour is indistinguishable from a human's but whose internal complexity is unknown to us; and the emulation of case 6 instantiated twice over in parallel, where what I want to know is whether there is now twice as much of whatever matters.
