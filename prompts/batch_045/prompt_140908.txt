I'm in the fourth year of a PhD in philosophy, working on comparative philosophy of language, and I'm writing a short commentary on the White Horse Dialogue (one of the chapters of the Gongsun Longzi attributed to Gongsun Long, the one that argues that a white horse is not a horse). My supervisor thinks my whole framing is anachronistic, which may be right, but I'd rather find out by using it than by dropping it. My working thesis is this: the argument, reconstructed carefully, is valid but only trivially, and the philosophically interesting question is not its validity but what I've been calling the export problem — whether anything at all follows from the thesis about names for what one may do with an actual animal, or say in a court, or write into a statute. My own machinery, such as it is: call a term colour-indifferent if for any object, whether it falls under the term never turns on that object's colour (horse, on the usual reading), and colour-committed if it does (white horse); the dialogue's genuine observation, on my reading, is that a colour-indifferent term and a colour-committed term cannot name a thing in the same way, and that its controversial move is the slide from that observation about names to a conclusion about horses.

What I want from you is a dialogue — roughly 900 to 1,200 words — to open the paper in place of a literature survey. Two unnamed participants, equally competent and equally stubborn, stuck in the bar of a conference hotel after a panel on the School of Names, neither of them a caricature. The first holds that the dialogue contains a real and still underappreciated point about modified sortal terms: that the text's move from what horse names (roughly, the shape or the kind) and what white names (the colour) to the conclusion about the compound is close to a point about intensionality and about the non-identity of a subclass with its superclass, and that the obvious retort — that riding a white horse is riding a horse — simply changes the subject from what the term names to what falls under it. The second holds that the point, once made precise, is either a notational variant of the set of white horses not being identical to the set of horses or a straight equivocation between the is of identity and the is of class-inclusion, and that its appearance of depth is largely an artifact of a classical Chinese with no copula and no articles, plus a rhetorical tradition that rewards paradox.

Run them against two cases I've been using as probes, and make sure the cases actually do work in the argument rather than getting wheeled in as set pieces. First, my ferry case: a fictional port city with an ordinance reading No person may bring a horse onto the ferry; a woman leads a white horse aboard, is charged, and argues that she has complied, since the white horse is not a horse; the magistrate is unpersuaded. Second, my database case: a breeding registry whose schema has a species column and a colour column, where SELECT on species = 'horse' returns 1,200 rows and SELECT on species = 'horse' AND colour = 'white' returns 30, from which someone concludes that in this registry, at least, the white horse is not the horse — and where the retort is that the instance-of relation is not the identity relation. I'd also like them to test the reading against the separability claim familiar from the hard-and-white material in the same corpus, the thought that sight delivers the white and touch delivers the hard and neither delivers both, and to say whether that helps the white-horse argument or quietly undermines it by showing what a real metaphysical thesis would look like. I want genuine back-and-forth rather than two monologues: each should be able to state the other's strongest version of the case to the other's satisfaction before replying, and each should concede something real at least once, without either winning by stipulating a definition of is. By the end they should have located the precise point at which their readings diverge and said what kind of evidence would settle it — a newly excavated parallel text, a consideration from comparative syntax, a decision about what a theory of names is for, or nothing at all. The audience is a mix of philosophers of language and sinologists who read no Chinese, so no unglossed technical vocabulary and nothing that depends on the original wording, but please don't flatten the argument to make it accessible.
