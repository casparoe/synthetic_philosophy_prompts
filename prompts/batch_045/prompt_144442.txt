I am in the lower sixth, and over the summer I worked through the early chapters of *Gödel, Escher, Bach*, where the MU-puzzle is used to introduce the idea of jumping out of the system. I am now writing my EPQ on what that move actually consists in, and before I go any further I want to fix the standard I will hold accounts to. So: write me the desiderata for a theory of jumping out of the system — what any adequate account has to deliver, with the case or consideration that makes each item a requirement rather than a preference.

The system, so that we agree on it: alphabet M, I, U; a single axiom, MI; four rules — (i) if a string ends in I you may append a U; (ii) you may double everything after the M; (iii) any occurrence of III may be replaced by a U; (iv) any occurrence of UU may be deleted. Target string: MU. I already know the usual solution, counting I's and working modulo 3, and I can follow the argument; what I am asking about is the shape of that move, not for the move itself.

The purpose, since adequacy is relative to one: I want a theory good enough for two jobs. First, to tell me, given some rule-system and a target, whether there is any point looking for an outside argument or whether brute search is all there is. Second, to tell me when stepping outside the rules is a genuine insight and when it is quietly changing the subject.

At minimum, anchor each requirement to a case from this list, and add cases of your own where mine are unhelpful:

1. MIU with rule (iii) replaced by "II may be replaced by U". MU now falls out in two steps. One symbol destroyed whatever made the original argument work, so an account should say what the invariant was tracking, and whether finding a conserved quantity is the whole of jumping out or only one species of it.

2. MIU with the extra rule "U may be replaced by III", and MIU with the extra rule "any single U may be deleted". Neither makes MU derivable, and I think for the same underlying reason, although the second looks, misleadingly, as though it should help.

3. The target "M followed by 2^40 I's". It is derivable in forty applications of rule (ii), no search will ever write it out, and a two-line argument with a schema and an induction settles it. So a theory has to handle certificates of possibility as well as certificates of impossibility, and explain why the two directions are not mirror images of each other.

4. A second system I made up: symbols -, p, q; axiom schema, for any hyphen-string x, "x p - q x -"; rule, from "x p y q z" infer "x p y- q z-". Stepping out here means finding a reading — p as plus, q as equals, hyphen-strings as numbers — after which every theorem is a true sum, the system is obviously consistent, and strings like "-- p - q -" are obviously not theorems. Is this the same move as the modulo-3 one, or a different one that merely gets called by the same name?

5. Someone who "solves" MU by adding the rule "MI → MU". That is cheating in a puzzle, yet abandoning a framework is sometimes held up as the insightful move in science or in politics. A theory should supply a criterion for telling the two apart, or explain why no such criterion can be had.

6. The regress. My modulo-3 argument is itself a proof, carried out in a system with some arithmetic in it, so I have only jumped from MIU into something else; and there are systems whose derivability questions no system settles, by the analogue of the halting problem. So the theory must not entail that every puzzle admits an outside argument that closes it. Say which requirement gives way.

Then: which requirements are non-negotiable and which are the tastes of a particular camp? For instance, must a theory explain how a person actually comes up with the jump, or only certify it once found? Must it tie jumping out to self-reference and incompleteness, or is that an optional extra? Flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — the ones I most suspect are "a theory should let us recognise a genuine jump mechanically" against "recognising one is itself a creative act", and "every system can be escaped" against "some questions are undecidable in principle". Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate accounts.

Only after that, and briefly — a short paragraph each — score three candidate accounts against your list: jumping out as finding a conserved quantity; jumping out as ascending to a meta-system in which the object-system is itself an object of study; and jumping out as reframing or abandoning the frame altogether, the Zen-flavoured reading. Keep the weight on the criteria, which is where I need the help.

Please take a definite position: give me your own list with reasons, and tell me which candidate requirements you are rejecting and why, rather than laying out the options and leaving me to choose. Write in British English. And do not cite anything or point me towards further reading — I cannot get hold of journals, and a bibliography is no use to me.
