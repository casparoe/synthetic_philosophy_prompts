I'm on the strike committee of a mid-sized public-transit workers' local — about 900 members, mixed: drivers, depot mechanics, cleaners, some dispatch. A few times a year we have to decide whether to ask the membership to authorize strike action, or whether to back someone else's action, and right now we do it by arguing in a room until someone calls for a vote. I want an actual procedure we can run in a week with what we've got: no economists, nothing our organiser can't dig up in a day, and it has to fit on one page our members can read and argue with, not a paper. Our lawyer handles the purely legal side (ballots, notice periods, what our category is barred from doing outright), so I only want the moral and political test.

I've been reading about the just war criteria — last resort, proportionality, legitimate authority, reasonable chance of success, and the rule about not deliberately targeting non-combatants — and they feel like the right shape for this, but they were built for something else and I want them adapted, not borrowed. So please write the procedure: the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, what we do when the number we'd need just isn't available, where the procedure simply stops with a no, and where it has to hand the decision back to us because it's a fight about values rather than a question of fact. Be honest about which steps are judgement wearing a hi-vis vest.

Two things I want you to actually solve rather than wave at. First: a strike is normally coercion, not speech — it works by costing the employer money untill they concede. So I want a step that asks whether the party being costed is the party who can give us what we're asking for, and when the answer is no (our employer is a body that can't settle our claim without someone above them signing off, or we're walking out over a national policy they have no say in) the procedure needs to say something more useful than "then it's just expressive, go home." Second: most of our action costs third parties more than it costs the employer — commuters, hospital patients, contract cleaners on somebody else's payroll. Give me a distinction between harm that is doing the work (the leverage) and harm that's spillover, and a rule for how much spillover is too much when we can't put a figure on it. Also one step on the members who don't want to do it: what the procedure owes them, and what it says about asking non-members to respect a picket line.

Then run it, briefly, on three:

1. A 48-hour stoppage over rostering and rest breaks. The employer can concede, the grievance route has genuinely been tried twice, a ballot would pass easily, and the spillover is commuters plus a city-wide event that weekend.
2. A sympathy strike to back a cleaners' local at a completely different employer who are running their own ballot. Our employer can give them nothing, the spillover is huge, and our members will definately want to do it — solidarity is more or less the point of having a union. If the procedure comes out no, say so plainly, say which step kills it, and say what would have to be true for it to come out yes. This is the one I expect to hate your answer to.
3. A one-day walkout over a government policy that has nothing to do with our contract, no ballot held yet, employer can't concede and wouldn't if it could.

At the end: what the procedure can't do. I'm thinking of the walkouts we're asked to bless after they've already started, work-to-rule and go-slows that aren't formally strike action, and action whose real point is recruitment or public opinion rather than the demand on the table — if the procedure can't even classify those, say so. And flag the two or three steps that are really votes in disguise, and who should take them: the committee, the whole membership, or just the members in the affected depot.

Keep the whole thing under 900 words: ordered steps first, then the three cases, then the limits. No theory jargon and please don't name-drop philosophers at us, our members will switch off.
