I work in operations research at a hospital network, and for three years I have been the person who maintains the solver that assigns operating rooms, surgical teams, and recovery beds for the following day. The model is a mixed-integer program, NP-hard by an easy reduction, and yet branch-and-bound closes it to optimality on most nights inside a two-hour window. The failures are a recognizable minority with a particular near-tie structure in the data, and on those nights we ship a solution with a gap we cannot close. This month the same question arrived from two directions in one week. A vendor told our chief financial officer that a quantum optimization service will shortly make this class of problem exactly solvable at scale. Days earlier, one of our data scientists argued in a design review that we should stop maintaining the exact model at all, on the grounds that the problem is NP-hard and exactness is therefore a mirage. I have to answer both people, and I noticed that I do not have anything I would call a theory of what infeasible means. I have habits of speech.

So before I answer either of them, I want the specification. Write me the desiderata that an account of computational feasibility would have to meet to be fit for my purpose: a notion good enough to guide go/no-go decisions about algorithms inside a real pipeline. Should we keep paying for exact methods, or is that money better spent on heuristics? Does a quantum service change the answer, and by how much? Is a three-percent gap a failure or a result? A notion good enough for that, not a notion good enough to prove theorems about.

Give each requirement with the case or consideration behind it, and sort them into the ones you take to be non-negotiable and the ones that are preferences of a camp. I have started a list of things I think such an account must be able to do; tell me which of these are genuine desiderata and which are borrowed prejudices of one research tradition:

1. Say something about the regime I actually live in, where instance size is bounded and known in advance, so an exponential procedure with a small base may beat a polynomial one with a bad exponent and a large constant forever, without the theory being able to call either infeasible.
2. Distinguish the cost of getting an answer from the cost of getting a certified optimal one, and handle the shape of the returns we observe: the first ninety-seven percent of the gap closes in minutes and the last three percent costs a hundred times the compute.
3. Handle amortized feasibility, where an enormous one-time cost — tuning a formulation, learning a branching policy, compiling a cut library — is repaid across nightly runs of the same instance family, so that the honest accounting is over a distribution of repeated use rather than a single call.
4. Be usable epistemically, before I know the complexity class. In practice I never have a hardness theorem for my exact problem; I have one for a neighbor of it, or five years of failed attempts by competent people, or a vendor's assurance. The account has to say what such evidence licenses me to conclude.
5. Be stable under reasonable changes of encoding and formulation — and here I mean something stronger than unary versus binary, since two ILP formulations of the same problem, or the same problem on trees versus on general graphs, differ by far more than a polynomial in practice — while remaining sensitive enough to instance structure to tell me why Tuesday is hard and Thursday is not.
6. Be closed in the way working engineers rely on: if I compose feasible procedures, or reduce my problem to one I have already solved, the result should be feasible. Whatever notion you give me, I will use it as a subroutine.
7. Not collapse the worst case, the typical case, and the adversarial case, and specifically not let a worst-case monster constructed in a hardness proof stand in for the instances a hospital generates.
8. Grade approximation: it should be able to tell me when there is a fast procedure for a guaranteed near-optimal answer, when near-optimal is as hard as optimal, and when the hardness result is really a signal to relax the objective or remodel the world rather than to give up on solving it.
9. Price a speedup. It should say why a quadratic improvement leaves my situation essentially unchanged while an exponential one would transform it, and it should be able to do that without depending on unresolved questions about what quantum polynomial time contains.
10. Say something about the search for algorithms as a computational cost in its own right — automated tuning, portfolio solvers, synthesized code — and about the difference between there being an algorithm and there being one we know, including procedures that are non-uniform or per-instance in a way asymptotics ignores.

Then do the part I care about most. My suspicion is that some subset of these cannot be had together: that a notion fine-grained enough to be empirically adequate at fixed instance sizes cannot also be robust enough to prove things with or invariant under machine model, and that closure under composition pulls against sensitivity to constants and structure. If that is right, say exactly which requirements conflict and why the conflict is real rather than a matter of taste. If I am wrong and the list is satisfiable, say so. Either way, tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate accounts, and give me a short scorecard — a few lines each — for the obvious contenders: identifying the tractable with the polynomial-time, parameterized and fixed-parameter notions, average-case and smoothed analyses, approximation-graded accounts, and purely empirical benchmarking of the kind my team already does.

Commit to a position. Do not hand me the menu and leave me to choose: tell me which requirement you would drop or weaken, what notion survives that pruning, and what I should therefore say to the vendor and to the data scientist, with your reasons. Mark where you are stating something you know with confidence — the closure properties, what is and is not known about quantum speedups — and where you are extrapolating from how practitioners in fact behave, since I would rather have your reasoning with its seams showing than a survey.
