I sit on the committee that reviews our hospital's diagnostic and screening protocols, and in preparation for a discussion about lowering a diagnostic cut-off I have been reading a chapter on the concept of disease in a bioethics reader we were handed. I cannot tell how much of its overview to trust, so I wanted an accuracy audit I could act on. The chapter's summary of the naturalism-versus-normativism debate runs roughly as follows:

Two families of answer dominate the debate. Naturalists, above all Christopher Boorse, hold that health and disease are descriptive, value-free notions: health is the normal functioning of an organism's parts, where normal means statistically typical for the species, and a disease is any internal state that depresses some part's functional efficiency below that species-typical level. So whether a condition is a disease is an empirical question about how common it is in the relevant population, and has nothing to do with what anyone values or wants. A consequence the naturalist accepts is that calling someone diseased carries no implication that anything ought to be done about it; disease and illness, in Boorse's terms, come apart. Normativists object that disease is inescapably evaluative: a condition counts as a disease only when we judge it bad for the person who has it. Since that judgement is not empirical, they conclude, disease categories are ultimately social constructions — maps of what a society happens to disvalue rather than discoveries about bodies. Jerome Wakefield's harmful dysfunction analysis is usually presented as the canonical statement of the normativist alternative: a disorder is a dysfunction that a society disvalues. The decisive objection to naturalism is the reference class problem: Boorse has to fix a comparison class — age, sex, and at one point race — and any such choice is a choice we make, so the supposedly scientific half of his theory is a value judgement too. Naturalism never recovered from this, which is why most philosophers of medicine today are normativists of some sort.

What I would like is a claim-by-claim audit: which sentences a defender of each view would accept as stated, which are tolerable classroom simplifications, and which misrepresent the position — including cases where the chapter uses a technical term (normality, dysfunction, value-free) in something other than the theory's sense, or slides between two claims a theory keeps apart. Then I want to know whether the chapter's bottom line — that the reference class problem refutes naturalism and that normativism has carried the field — survives correction of the details.

The reason this is not academic for me: our committee is weighing a proposal to lower the cut-off on a continuous measurement (think blood pressure, blood glucose, a tumour marker), so that the number of patients counted as having the condition rises substantially while nobody's physiology changes. I want to know whether, once the summary is corrected, the two positions actually diverge here. Is the naturalist committed to saying the threshold question is purely factual — either the new cut-off tracks subnormal part-function or it does not — while a normativist or a hybrid theorist must treat it as partly a value judgement about net benefit, including the harms of labelling and of treating people who would never have had symptoms? And does the corrected naturalist have anything principled to say about a case where a single subsystem functions below the species-typical range but the bearer's overall survival and reproduction are above it — say a hereditary variant that weakens one clotting factor in a population where thrombotic strokes are the leading cause of death, so that the variant is locally common and protective? Or is the answer genuinely contested within naturalism?

For what it is worth, my own hunches are that the equation of normality with frequency cannot be right, yet the chapter is written by a professional, so I do not trust my suspicion that the social-construction conclusion is stronger than most normativists would endorse. Please argue for the analysis rather than pointing me to readings: I have no library access at the moment, and I need the shape of the arguments, not references.
