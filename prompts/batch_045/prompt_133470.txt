I am a professor of philosophy, and I sit on our university's Committee on Enhancement and Academic Practice. Our remit is deliberately narrow: we decide only which interventions the university health service will provide or prescribe at university cost, never what students may obtain privately. In four years we have ruled as follows: we approved stimulant medication for a student with a diagnosed attention disorder, and approved extra examination time for a student with the same diagnosis who declines medication; we declined the same stimulant for an undiagnosed student preparing for finals, noting explicitly that our ruling was about provision, not permission; we approved an antidepressant for a student with a diagnosed depressive episode and declined the same drug for a student with subclinical low mood who wanted more drive; we declined a cochlear implant for a congenitally deaf student who is a fluent signer and does not consider herself disabled, funding interpreters and real-time captioning instead, while approving an implant for a student who lost his hearing at nineteen and does not sign; we approved, narrowly and on grounds of risk to patients, a wakefulness agent for a healthy senior medical resident on night rotations with no sleep disorder; and we approved without discussion reading glasses for age-related presbyopia in faculty and elective laser correction for mild myopia in a student who manages perfectly well with glasses.

Next week's request is this. A healthy thirty-four-year-old doctoral student with no diagnosis asks us to provide, at university cost, a newly licensed sleep-compression agent — it does not improve cognition; it simply delivers a full night's restorative sleep in five hours rather than eight, with no measurable health cost on the evidence before us. She is the sole carer of a four-year-old and wants the three recovered hours for her dissertation. She has declined a reduced teaching load, which would take her past her funding deadline, and says the childcare subsidy available to her would not help, since the bottleneck is hours after her daughter is asleep rather than money.

I want three things from you. First, the rule, or short list of rules, that best describes our record, together with at least one substantially different rule that fits it equally well: I suspect a diagnosis-based reading, a welfarist reading, and a "change the environment before the person" reading all survive the precedents, and I want to know exactly where they diverge. Second, your recommendation for the new case on grounds of consistency with what we have already done, with the closest analog identified and an explicit statement of the principle we would be adopting under each ruling — including whether the therapy/enhancement distinction is doing any real work in our decisions, or whether we have in fact been tracking something else, such as welfare, positional advantage, risk to third parties, or the availability of an environmental fix. Third, the further case you would place in front of us to tell the surviving rules apart. Argue from our record rather than from the literature: I do not want a survey of the enhancement debate, citations, or reading suggestions, since nothing we decide can rest on them.
