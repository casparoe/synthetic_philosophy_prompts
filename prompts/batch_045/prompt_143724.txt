I am marking a take-home exam for an upper-division seminar on the limits of self-knowledge. The question concerns Williamson's anti-luminosity argument and its bearing on the KK principle. Below is the question as it was set, followed by one student's answer. Please grade the answer.

The question (20 marks)

The following definitions were supplied to the students.

- A condition C is luminous if and only if: for every case α and every subject S, if S is in C in α, then S is in a position to know that she is in C in α.
- KK: for every proposition p, if S knows that p, then S knows that she knows that p.
- Say that a condition is non-trivial if there are possible cases in which a subject is in it and possible cases in which she is not.

(a) Explain the difference between KK and the claim that the condition of knowing that p is luminous. Does either entail the other? (5 marks)

(b) Set out the argument for the conclusion that no non-trivial condition is luminous. (7 marks)

(c) What further premise is needed to get from that conclusion to the falsity of KK? Is that premise one the defender of KK could reasonably reject? (4 marks)

(d) A hedge. Where S knows that p, let the margin of her knowledge be the amount by which her total evidence for p exceeds whatever amount is required for knowing that p — informally, how far above the threshold her evidence sits. Consider:

KK_δ: for some fixed δ > 0, if S knows that p with margin at least δ, then S knows that she knows that p.

Does the anti-luminosity argument refute KK_δ? Does the hedge preserve what KK was wanted for? (4 marks)

The student's answer

(a) KK says that knowledge iterates: necessarily, if S knows that p, then S knows that she knows that p. The luminosity of the condition of knowing that p says something weaker and more general: whenever S knows that p, S is in a position to know that she knows that p. The two differ in two respects. First, being in a position to know is weaker than knowing. S may be in a position to know that q without knowing that q, because she has never considered the matter or has not bothered to form the belief; so knowing that q entails being in a position to know that q, and not conversely. Second, luminosity is a thesis about conditions generally rather than about one proposition: the question whether a condition is luminous can be raised about being in pain, being rested, being cold, or anything else. KK is instead a schema about knowledge of knowledge. Given the first point, KK entails that the condition of knowing that p is luminous; the converse fails, since being in a position to know that one knows that p falls short of knowing that one knows that p. So the anti-luminosity result, if it holds, is stronger than a refutation of KK: it refutes a claim that KK requires.

(b) Suppose for reductio that some non-trivial condition C is luminous: S is in C in some case α0 and not in C in some case αn. Because C is non-trivial, we can arrange a series of cases α0, …, αn along some dimension — the light slowly failing, a sleeper slowly waking — in which adjacent cases differ only very slightly, and in which the transition out of C occurs somewhere in the series. By luminosity, since S is in C in α0, she is in a position to know in α0 that she is in C. Now the key premise: being in a position to know requires a margin for error. A capacity that returned the verdict C in one case while failing to return it in an arbitrarily similar case would be maximally discriminating, and no capacity of ours is like that. So if S is in a position to know in αi that she is in C, then S is in C in every case sufficiently similar to αi — that is, in every case in which her total evidence is in effect unchanged — and in particular in α(i+1). This is a modest externalist requirement, not a sceptical one. Since S is in C in α1, luminosity applies again at α1, and then at α2, and so on; by induction S is in C in αn. But by construction she is not. Hence no non-trivial condition is luminous.

Two features of the argument deserve emphasis. Its power comes from its generality: it assumes nothing about what evidence is, or about whether the mental supervenes on the physical, and it turns on nothing beyond the need for a margin. And the construction does not depend on which series we happen to choose. Since the series was arbitrary, the same reasoning establishes, of every case in which S is in C, that she is in C without being in a position to know that she is. Luminosity fails not merely somewhere along the series but at every point of it: there is no case in which a subject is in a position to know that she is in a condition she is in. That is why the conclusion is so much stronger than the platitude that self-knowledge is fallible.

(c) The luminosity result does not by itself refute KK. Take C to be the condition of knowing that p. Non-luminosity yields a case in which S knows that p while not being in a position to know that she knows that p; to conclude that she does not know that she knows that p, one more premise is needed. The premise needed is the factivity of being in a position to know: necessarily, if S is in a position to know that q, then q is true. Given that premise, S's failure to be in a position to know that she knows that p is a failure to know that she knows that p, and KK is false. Without it the argument would have no bite: a case in which one is not in a position to know that one knows that p could still be a case in which one knows that one knows that p. Could the defender of KK reject the premise? Not plausibly. Factivity is part of the point of the operator, and surrendering it would leave being in a position to know with no claim to be a knowledge-like notion at all.

(d) The argument does refute the hedge. Let C_δ be the condition of knowing that p with margin at least δ. C_δ is itself a non-trivial condition, so it falls under the general result: there is a case in which S knows that p with margin at least δ but is not in a position to know that she is in C_δ. By the bridge premise from part (c), she does not know that she is in C_δ. Now the consequent of KK_δ is simply that she knows that she knows that p, and to know that one is in C_δ is just to know that one knows that p and that one's margin is at least δ; so if she does not know that she is in C_δ, she does not know that she knows that p either. KK_δ therefore fails in the same way KK does and for the same reason: however far inside the condition the subject is placed, the condition is not luminous, and the hedge buys nothing. It is fair to add that the hedge is immune to the naive version of the argument: run the argument on the unhedged condition of knowing that p and the case it produces may well be one in which the subject's margin is smaller than δ, in which case KK_δ has no application there. But the general result is what defeats it.
