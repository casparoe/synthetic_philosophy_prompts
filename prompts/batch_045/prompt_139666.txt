I sit on the parent and carer advisory panel to the working group revising our national diagnostic manual, and I have been asked to draft our position on the one proposal that has split the group: whether to admit Developmental Demoralization Syndrome (DDS) as a formal category. I should disclose why I was asked. My sixteen-year-old daughter was among the first patients given a provisional DDS diagnosis at the pilot clinic, she has since become a visible member of the online community that has organized around the label, and I believe — perhaps conveniently — that the diagnosis helped her. The working group votes in the spring. Before I write anything, I want the philosophical question argued properly, not settled by committee compromise.

DDS is proposed for ages fourteen to twenty-one: at least six months of pervasive loss of initiative, withdrawal from school and peer contact, and a reported sense that nothing one could do would matter — provided the picture is not better explained by a depressive episode and organic causes have been excluded. Two features make it contentious. The criteria rest partly on the young person's own reported experience rather than on observable behavior alone. And in the two clinics where it has been piloted, the number of adolescents meeting criteria roughly tripled in three years, while the presentation changed: early cases were described as inert and near-mute; later ones are articulate, arrive already self-diagnosed, and usually reach the clinic after sustained contact with the online community. Half the working group reads this as a real and previously invisible condition finally being detected. The other half reads it as a category manufacturing its own instances.

I have been reading Ian Hacking on the looping effects of human kinds: the thought that a classification applied to people can feed back on the people classified, who then alter their conduct, their self-understanding and even their experience in light of it, so that the kind is a moving target in a way that a classification of an indifferent natural kind is not. My colleagues draw contrary conclusions from this. Some treat it as decisive against official recognition: we would be bringing a new way for young people to be into existence and then counting the outcome as a discovery. Others reply that the loop is already running — the label circulates, the community exists, and formal recognition at least brings explicit criteria, oversight and eligibility for services — so looping is an argument for recognition under stricter governance rather than against recognition.

Write me an essay of about 1,200 words that decides this. Assume I already know the loop framework: situate it in at most a short paragraph, then get into the difficulty. Specifically: say whether the fact that a kind is loop-prone — unstable, partly self-confirming — is any reason at all to withhold official recognition, given Hacking's own insistence that such kinds can be genuinely real while being historically contingent, and given that many classifications we now regard as uncontroversial were once contested; then say whether formal recognition changes the stakes of the loop in a way mere informal circulation does not, since official status brings insurance codes, school accommodations, research funding and a public identity. Engage the pilot data: what, in principle, would distinguish detection from manufacture here, and does the answer matter for the verdict? Consider the comparison some parents make to the neurodiversity framing — that a name can convert private failure into a shared, actionable condition — and whether that counts in favor. Finally, tell me whether my own stake is a reason to discount my judgment or a kind of evidence I am entitled to use.

Do not end on a non-committal note. Recommend for or against formal inclusion, or recommend a specific third option with real teeth, and defend it as the position our panel should adopt; then state the strongest objection to your own recommendation and why it fails. I do not need references or further reading — I need the argument.
