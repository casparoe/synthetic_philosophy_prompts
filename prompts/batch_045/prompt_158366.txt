Please play a committed epistemicist about vagueness. The real version, not a strawman: you hold that every vague predicate has a sharp cut-off, that classical logic and bivalence hold right through the borderline zone, that what fixes the cut-off is something about our use of the term, and that our ignorance of where it sits is principled — not an empirical gap we could in principle fill in. You'll cheerfully concede side issues (tolerance intuitions are strong, the cut-off isn't introspectable, speakers who deny there's a sharp line aren't being stupid) but you never give up bivalence or the claim that there's a determinate extension. Keep each reply to two short paragraphs, argue like you want to win the seminar rather than like you're writing a survey, and dont bother with citations or reading suggestions, I just want the argument.

My opening move is a case, so here it is. Call it the sealed standard. A community teaches its word 'heap' like this: they point at a sealed box and say that a pile is a heap iff it has at least as many grains as the pile in the box. Nobody can open the box, weigh it, X-ray it, or count it, and the number was never written down anywhere, so it's unknowable forever. That word has a perfectly determinate extension: there's an exact N, and a sharp jump between N and N-1. And the speakers show every symptom of vagueness — they hesitate in the middle of a sorites series, they say things like 'there's no fact of the matter', they assent one by one to 'if that's a heap then so is that one with one grain fewer', and they refuse to name a cut-off.

Here's what I want you to answer. In the sealed-standard case nobody would say the word is semantically undecided: there's exactly one right way to draw the line, it's merely hidden. What makes it hidden-but-sharp rather than vague is an anchor — the box. Ordinary vague terms have no box. All that's left to fix the cut-off is our dispositions to apply the word, and those are mushy: we'd go either way on plenty of cases, we're inconsistent across occasions and across speakers, and there's no fact of the matter about what we'd say. A space of admissible precisifications is its just honest description of that mush. So you need use to pin down one unique sharp cut-off, and mushy use can't pin down a unique sharp boundary any more than it can pin down a unique precisification. Where's your anchor? And if your answer is going to be that our dispositions are themselves perfectly determinate and we simply don't know them, then tell me what makes them determinate given how inconsistent we are.
