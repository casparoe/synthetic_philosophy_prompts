I'm writing about self-amending constitutional rules and have built an argument whose conclusion I cannot accept but whose fault I cannot localize. I'd be grateful for a diagnosis.

Consider a system S whose constitution contains the amendment rule R: "Any provision of this constitution may be replaced by a two-thirds vote of the assembly." R is itself one of those provisions.

(1) A power is a legal power only if some rule of S settles whether a purported exercise of it counts as an exercise of it; where nothing settles that, we have a bare fact, not a legal act.
(2) So if R confers a legal power, then the assembly's enactment of an amendment A under R is assessable by S's criteria of validity.
(3) Let A repeal the constitution in its entirety and replace S with a new system S*. The criteria that would have to assess A are annihilated by A; at the time of assessment there is nothing left by which to assess it.
(4) An act that destroys the conditions of its own legal assessability is not a legal act. So A is not authorized by R: it is a revolution, and S* has no legal continuity with S.
(5) Hence no legal system can legally authorize its own replacement, and every amendment rule is, to the extent that it purports to do so, void.

But (5) appears to prove far more than that:

(6) The same reasoning covers every alteration of S's criteria of validity, not only wholesale abolition: adding a new source of law, transferring legislative competence to an external body, admitting new member units, or lowering the amendment threshold itself. On (5) all such clauses are legally inert.
(7) And it covers the reverse case, since a clause entrenches a provision against future amendment only by altering the criteria under which later amendments count. So entrenchment is void too, and a constitution can neither open nor close itself: it is, by its own lights, immutable.
(8) That collapses the distinction between amendment and revolution, and renders unintelligible the claim that some constitution was legally replaced under its own rules.

Of the premises I would most like to keep (1) and (2), and I would sooner give up (5) than either of them. But every repair I attempt generates the mirror-image absurdity: if I weaken (1) so that a power can be legal while partly unregulated, I seem committed to saying that a constitution may validly authorize anything whatsoever, including the abolition of its own courts and the disenfranchisement of a minority.

Name the step I should reject and explain why it looked sound. Then set out what the two remaining live positions — a constitution cannot authorize its own abolition, and a constitution can authorize anything, including its own abolition — each have to pay, and say which of them leaves the amendment/revolution distinction intact. If the fault lies in when validity is taken to be assessed, be explicit about what that temporal indexing commits me to, including whether an act's legality can be settled by standards that no longer exist.
