My reading group worked through Appiah's book on honor and moral revolutions (The Honor Code, I think it's called, around 2010 — the one that goes through duelling, British abolition and footbinding) and we've been arguing about it for three weeks, so I'm trying to settle it on my own. As I understand the fight, there are three stories: the one where reasons did the work (abolitionists assembled testimony about the Middle Passage, pressed the inconsistency with Christian and liberal principles, and eventually the arguments simply won); the skeptical line people attribute to Williams, that moral argument only bites on people who are already half-moved and that the real engine is always something else, social or economic; and Appiah's, where what changes isn't belief but honor — a practice stops being respectable among the people whose respect you need, and then it collapses much faster than conviction ever moves. A friend who reads economic history says all three are wrong and it was profit: the sugar islands were getting less profitable, the Haitian revolution made the whole business look like a liability, free labour was turning out to be more productive. I find the honor story attractive and also slippery, and I want it tested rather than admired.

So: take Britain ending the slave trade (1807) and then slavery in it's colonies (1833, with the owners paid off — roughly twenty million pounds as I recall, then the apprenticeship arrangement — I'm going from memory here, so correct me gently if I've got the sequence or the sums wrong, but please don't let the whole answer hang on a date). Run all three accounts, plus my friend's, through it properly. I definately don't want a summary of Appiah's chapters or a timeline of abolition; I want the mapping: who exactly the actors and the audiences are, what options each of them actually faced, what counts as "the argument" versus "the interest" versus "the honor community" in this case, and what each story predicts about who converts first and in what order. The bit I keep getting stuck on, and the bit I most want chewed on, is a structural difference between Appiah's own cases: when duelling ended, the people whose honor turned were the people who duelled — honor had required the practice and then stopped requiring it — whereas in British slavery the people who actually owned slaves mostly never conceded that they were dishonorable, they got compensated instead and went on thinking well of themselves. The shift looks like it happened among people who were never practitioners at all: MPs, voters, churchgoers, and (if I have this right) the big consumer boycott of West Indian sugar in the 1790s, pushed hard by women who owned no slaves and had no material stake either way. So the honor mechanism seems to work on the audience rather than the agent. Is that still an honor explanation, or has it quietly become reputation-management — an interest in honor's clothing? And if there's a genuine difference between stopping because you'd lose face and stopping because it costs you money, say what it is and where it shows up here.

Here's a test I invented to try to seperate them, and I'd like you either to use it or to tell me why it's a bad test. If honor is doing the work, the change should travel along the lines of whose good opinion people depended on — regiments, professions, congregations, trading and patronage networks, marriage circles — rather than along lines of who read the strongest pamphlet; if argument is doing the work it should track exposure to the evidence; if interest is doing it, it should track exposure to the money. So which pattern do we actually see in the British case, or is the record too messy to tell? And a related thing someone else threw in: ridicule. The satirical prints and caricatures, the way abolitionists made slave-trading and later slave-owning faintly absurd. I can't work out whether ridicule is a fourth mechanism, or just honor's delivery system, or just argument in a louder key. What I want at the end is a verdict running in both directions: which features of this episode each account explains and which it visibly misdescribes (for instance, does the interest story have anything to say about the gap between abolishing the trade and abolishing slavery, or about the owners being paid?), and what the episode does to each theory — confirmation, awkward anomaly, or a kind of case the theory was never built for. Be willing to say where an account is silent instead of stretching it to speak, but don't hedge everything into mush: if you think one of them is basically right, say so and take the hit from the strongest objection on the other side. No bibliography and no citations — I'm doing this on a train and just want the argument.
