The situation: I'm applying for a four-month summer research fellowship — small stipend, no teaching, and at the end you give one 30-minute talk to the department. The area is the epistemic regress problem: foundationalism vs. coherentism vs. infinitism. This got personally relevant over the last few months because my younger brother has fallen into one of those online "but how do you *really* know anything" rabbit holes, and every argument with him ends up in the regress: he says every justification needs a justification so nothing is ever justified, and I want to have something better to say than "well, some things are just basic". What bugs me is that none of the three standard positions really matches what I want to say, and I think part of the reason is that the whole debate is carried on without anyone saying what kind of structure a body of reasons even is. I did two courses in set theory and logic before I swtiched to philosophy, and that's clearly colouring how I'm seeing this.

So here's the half-formed idea I want made into a proposal. Everyone agrees that justification has some shape — linear, circular, or endless — but nobody says what makes two shapes the same or different, so the three positions may not be as separate as they look. My suggestion: model a subject's reasons as a directed graph (propositions or reason-states as nodes, "r supports p" as edges) and then import distinctions that graph theory and non-well-founded set theory already have — well-foundedness, cycles, infinite descending paths with no minimal element, branching, reachability from a finite set of starting points — and use them to see which of the three positions actually come apart and which collapse. Two things I suspect fall out of this. First, a local-indistinguishability worry: a finite inquirer can only ever traverse finitely many edges, so a genuinely infinite non-repeating chain and a very large loop may be indistinguishable from the inside, which would make infinitism and coherentism much closer cousins than the literature treats them. Second, infinitism's standard objection (finite beings can't have infinitely many reasons) may be an artifact of thinking of reasons as a list rather than as something generated by a rule, so I want to separate having all the reasons from having a finite schema or procedure that generates endlessly many reasons on demand — I've been calling this capacity infinitism, but rename it or bin it if it's junk.
