I'm in the second year of a PhD in genome biology, and I've noticed that everyone around me says things like the genome is a code, a blueprint, a program, a set of instructions — and that the same people will, ten minutes later, insist that all of this is only a manner of speaking. Two conversations last month made the wobble matter. At journal club a postdoc I respect killed a discussion about whether a particular noncoding variant disrupts a regulatory instruction by saying that DNA isn't really a code at all: it is a molecule with a shape and a charge distribution, and information is a façon de parler we keep because it is convenient, so the only literal content in the room is in the head of whoever is reading the gel. A week later an undergraduate I help supervise turned in an essay arguing that since the genome is the organism's blueprint, exhaustively sequencing it would in principle let us read off the organism — which I also think is wrong, but possibly wrong for an entirely different reason, and I couldn't say why in a way that would have helped her.

So I don't want a verdict on the biology; I want the menu. Please lay out the distinct precise claims that 'DNA is a code' — together with its cousins 'the genetic code', 'blueprint', 'program', 'recipe', 'instruction', and, differently again, 'the histone code' — could be making, ordered from the thinnest reading that is nearly a tautology to the strongest one that would earn real metaphysical weight. For each, say exactly what it asserts: that there is a mapping from codons to amino acids; that the mapping is arbitrary and therefore convention- or decoder-dependent; that the arbitrariness is itself evidence of a designer; that a sequence has representational content in virtue of a proper function fixed by selection; that a sequence carries causal or control specificity of the sort that makes one factor a switch for another; that a sequence encodes a procedure with conditional structure rather than a static structure; or something about what is in the head of the interpreter. Then sort the readings: which are trivial, which are substantive and defensible, which are straightforwardly false, and which one my postdoc and my undergraduate are each most likely invoking. In particular, can the two of them be mistaken for unrelated reasons — that is, does rejecting the blueprint reading commit me to anything like her eliminativism, or is there a reading on which the mapping is real and arbitrary-but-conventional while the blueprint's isomorphism claim is simply false, and another on which even that is too strong?

Two cases I keep circling and would like handled explicitly under whichever readings they bear on. First, in mitochondria and in a handful of other lineages a few codons are read differently than in the standard table, and synthetic biologists have added further ones: if a code is partly a matter of which decoder shows up, does the variant-code case strengthen the conventionalist reading, embarrass it, or leave every reading indifferent? Second, one and the same stretch of DNA can serve as a transcription template in one cellular context and as a binding site or structural element in another, which makes me suspect that whatever 'instruction' means it cannot attach to the sequence alone. I'd also like to know which reading, if true, would make histone code talk literal rather than merely analogical, since that usage seems to me to be doing something different from all the others — and if none would, say so plainly. Where you give a verdict, tell me what would move you off it: which further consideration would make you concede that information talk really is eliminable in practice, or that the design-flavoured reading has something going for it after all; and mark the step in your own reasoning where you are least confident.
