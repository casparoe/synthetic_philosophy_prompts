hey — I need a proposal written up for a 12-week senior thesis chapter on the Allais paradox and the independence axiom. About 7000 words, one supervisor meeting a week, philosophy department, and I'm doing this on my own (no running subjects, no data collection).

Here's my half-formed idea. Everyone treats the Allais pattern as a violation of independence and then argues about whether to keep independence and explain the pattern away as a presentation effect, or drop independence and rebuild decision theory around probability weighting / rank-dependence instead. I think that framing hides something. Independence seems to do two different jobs in the two arguments people actually give for it. In the representation theorem it's the condition that lets you write preferences as an expected sum. In the money-pump and you-would-reverse-yourself arguments it shows up as a condition on what you'd want once you learn which branch you're in — a sequential or dynamic thing. I suspect those aren't the same condition, and that the Allais preferences can break the first while satisfying the second, which would mean the argument that actually bites (you'd be exploitable, you'd plan badly) may not touch them at all.

The other bit I keep circling: in the standard case nobody says whether the 89/11 draw gets resolved before or after the draw inside the 11% branch. The options are presented as one flat list of outcomes. But you can describe the very same lottery flat, or as a two-stage thing where you first find out whether you're in the 89% branch and only then, if you're not, face the second draw. If those two descriptions pull different rates of the Allais pattern, that says something about whether probability weighting is doing the work or something more like anticipation / planning is. I want the chapter built around that, roughly.
