hey — I'm on this slightly odd working group that's supposed to write internal guidance for a research ethics committee, and we've gotten stuck on the part I assumed would be easy. We have to say how much moral weight to give different kinds of system, and we keep swinging between a binary (sentient / not sentient, everything above the line counts) and a graded picture (degrees of sentience, degrees of moral status). Insects are what broke us: our current paperwork treats them as basically not worth counting, but nobody on the committee can say why a fly belongs on the thermostat side of the line rather than the mouse side, and "smaller and simpler" obviously isn't an argument on its own. A couple of jurisdictions have recently started naming cephalopods and decapods in welfare law, which is fine, but it just moves the line without telling us what the line is tracking.

So before we read any more theories I want the spec, and to make it concrete here are the things it has to cope with. Imagine we had two instruments: a "nociceptive integration index" from 0 to 100 that measures, purely from the outside, how much of a system's downstream processing is reorganized by a damaging stimulus and how long that reorganization lasts; and a "counterfactual richness" score for how many distinct states the system can be in and how finely its behaviour discriminates between them. Now suppose we found a fly that, after a leg injury whose nociceptors we've pharmacologically silenced, keeps tending that limb for days; a colony of fifty thousand insects that solves problems no individual can solve but where no individual carries any representation of the colony's state; an organism that can be split in half with both halves surviving and independently learning; and a system — biological or silicon, your pick — that works to escape a damaging stimulus, learns to avoid the setup that produced it, and generalizes that avoidance, but whose "unpleasantness" pathway has been rerouted so the behaviour survives while the affect apparently doesn't. Give me the requirements list: what a graded account has to get right, with the case behind each requirement, and sort my provisional list into real desiderata vs. mere committee convenience — (a) having status versus how much status, (b) intensity versus duration versus moral weight, (c) interpersonal comparability of the scores, (d) continuity with no sharp line versus thresholds a committee can actually administer, (e) giving an ordering even when the evidence is thin, (f) not making everything hostage to verbal report or to one anatomical feature, (g) handling collectives, split subjects and the aggregation question (how many insects per mouse), (h) saying whether positive states count and whether a thermostat is genuinely at zero or just below our resolution, (i) not being wildly revisionary about the paradigm cases.

I most want the parts where the list fights itself: which pair of requirements you suspect can't be jointly satisfied, and which single requirement knocks out the most popular accounts — my guess is it's the one about being usable under radical uncertainty, but I want your argument rather than my hunch. A table is fine, keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on scoring the theories, and then give me two or three lines on how three rough families of view (a simple capacity-for-valenced-experience view, a neural-or-informational complexity threshold view, and something like an integrated-information style view) do against whatever survives your own winnowing.
