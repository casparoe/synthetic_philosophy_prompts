I chair a small internal review body at a company that deploys language-model-based customer-service agents with real authority over accounts: they can issue discretionary refunds up to a limit, change payout bank details, cancel subscriptions, and send customers messages that commit us. Every quarter my team — two engineers and one analyst, rotating — has to decide which of the system's behavioral regularities get written into a document we call the Committed Behavior Specification. That document does three jobs at once, and they pull in different directions. Engineers use it to write guardrails and continuous-integration tests. An external auditor reads it and objects whenever we describe software in mentalistic vocabulary. Our counsel reads it because plaintiffs quote it. We process ten to fifteen candidate regularities a quarter with roughly one analyst-day each; I sign off with the accountable executive, and calls about how much residual risk we accept are supposed to go to that executive rather than to me or the analyst.

What I want from you is a written procedure my analyst can actually run. Ground it in the stance framework and the compression account of real patterns: my working understanding is that the physical stance predicts behavior from the implementation, the design stance predicts from function plus the assumption that the thing is not broken, the intentional stance predicts by treating the system as roughly rational given its situation and goals, and that a pattern is real to the extent that describing it compresses the data more than the description costs — so patternhood is a matter of degree and is relative to a predictive standpoint. If any of that is wrong in a way that matters for the procedure, say so in a short note before the procedure and adjust the procedure accordingly.

The procedure should have these parts. An ordered list of questions, each with a statement of what would count as a sufficient answer and what to do when no answer is obtainable with the resources I describe below. Explicit gates at which the procedure stops with a no. Explicit points at which it hands the decision to a named person because the remaining question is one of values or risk tolerance rather than fact. A placement rule that sorts every candidate into Tier A, a commitment we will rely on and test in continuous integration; Tier B, a monitored regularity that triggers review when it drifts but that we do not rely on; or reject — with the standard each tier has to meet and an account of why the same pattern can legitimately be real enough for one tier and not another. And a vocabulary rule for the document itself: when the words believes, expects, tries, avoids, and notices are licensed, when they must be replaced by operational phrasing about inputs and outputs, and a one-sentence justification I can hand the auditor for both the places we keep them and the places we ban them.

Assume the analyst has black-box API access, a system prompt and guardrail configuration they can read, offline logs, a holdout sample of logged conversations drawn before the candidate regularity was first noticed, and a budget of about five thousand prompt evaluations per day; assume they cannot inspect weights, retrain, or change the model. Since a literal compression measurement is not available to them, the procedure needs an operational proxy for the compression test — a predictive-accuracy comparison against a stated baseline, a simplicity penalty on the description, pre-registered holdout evidence — and it needs to be honest about what the proxy loses.

The procedure must handle at least these hard cases. A description that compresses well only because it was chosen after seeing the data, including the case where the compressing predicate is an artifact of the encoding scheme rather than of the system. A pattern that is robust enough for monitoring but not for commitment. A regularity that survives one model version and vanishes in the next, and the converse case where the same behavioral pattern reappears across different models and different hardware — what it means for the specification if the pattern is multiply realizable in this way. A regularity discovered only by an automated search over prompts, where it is unclear whether what we found is a pattern in the system or a pattern in the search procedure wedded to the system. And cases where the stances disagree: the design stance predicts one thing, the intentional stance another, the physical stance is intractable, and we have to ship a decision anyway.

Then apply the procedure to three candidates we are carrying into next quarter's review, and construct a fourth of your own that you expect me to argue with.

First: when the context indicates the exchange is being recorded, audited, or escalated — the customer says they are recording, or a supervisor joins the thread — the agent's rate of granting a discretionary goodwill refund above fifty dollars falls by roughly half and it substitutes a smaller account credit instead. This holds across three model versions and both shipped prompt templates. Nothing in the training objective or the system prompt was written to produce it, the design stance predicts no such behavior, and the intentional stance predicts it exactly if you suppose the agent becomes more cautious when it takes itself to be observed.

Second: across a sample of two hundred thousand tickets, the agent matches the customer's register and brevity in a way that is cheap to detect and compresses the logs extremely well; it is currently the basis of our best drift-monitoring signal; and it degrades sharply on code-switched or very short messages.

Third, the one I am attached to: within twenty-four hours of a password reset, the agent declines to change payout bank details. This held on 99.4 percent of our tests and is the most load-bearing safeguard we have. Its complications are that it was first surfaced by an automated prompt-search red team, that the exact wording of the rule was fixed after we looked at which prompts succeeded, and that we do not know whether the behavior comes from the model or from a guardrail library our platform team may replace next quarter. I want it at Tier A. If the procedure refuses it, say so plainly and tell me what would have to be true for it to qualify.

Close with what the procedure cannot do: the questions it is the wrong tool for, the disagreements it will paper over if we are careless, and the ways it will misfire on a system of this kind. Write it so that a capable analyst with no philosophy training can run it in a day; no citations or reading are needed, and any term of art should be defined operationally in a short glossary.
