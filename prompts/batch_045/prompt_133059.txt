I'm a graduate student in early modern intellectual history, and I'm on the committee that runs our department's author-meets-critics series. Next month we are hosting the author of a recent monograph on Galileo and the interpretation of Scripture — I'll leave her and the book unnamed here — and I've been asked to supply the first round of questions together with a note on where she is likely to push back, so that the session doesn't just become a set of prompts for her to restate each chapter. I have read the book once and discussed it with two people who liked it less than I did, so I have a rough sense of where it is vulnerable, but I'm not confident I can turn that into questions that actually tighten rather than inviting another chapter summary.

The view, as I take it, is that Galileo is badly served by the familiar two-books framing — the idea that nature and Scripture are two divine revelations that cannot really conflict, so an apparent conflict is always a sign of exegetical error. She argues that what he defends in the Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina is narrower and stranger: the plain sense of a passage is presumptively correct even about matters of nature, but that presumption is defeated, locally and only locally, by a genuine demonstration. The defeat is not a licence to demythologise; her preferred formulation is that the principle is a scalpel, not a solvent. Most of the textual evidence she offers comes from his handling of the two miracle stories about the sun — Joshua's long day, and the shadow going back on the sundial. As she reads him, he does not treat those passages as merely phenomenal language about how things look from down here; he argues that the lengthening of that day is far less disruptive to the whole order of things if the daily motion belongs to the earth than if it belongs to an outermost sphere that carries everything else along with it — I may be garbling the details of the argument. She takes this to show that he thought the astronomical content of the text was genuinely answerable to physics while its theological point was not, and she uses that to argue that he is more conservative than his admirers want: on her account he never says Scripture is unreliable about the natural world, only that a demonstrated natural fact can override its plain sense in the specific passage where the two collide. There is also a chapter claiming that this puts his position in the 1610s in continuity with the older accommodationist tradition rather than breaking from it, and a shorter one suggesting that Bellarmine's stance in the letter to Foscarini is much closer to Galileo's than the trial narrative allows.

What I'd like from you is six to eight questions for the session, each with a sentence or two on the answer she is most likely to give and a follow-up keyed to that answer. I want at least one question on the gap between her principle and what Galileo could actually deliver: if the presumption is defeated only by demonstration, and the argument he took to be his demonstration — the tides — is simply wrong, then by his own rule he was entitled to nothing more than the hypothetical treatment the Church said he could have, and I want her to have to say whether the rule gives any guidance at the moment it is needed or only retrospectively, once we already know which proofs survived. I want at least one question on the accommodation-to-appearance problem: the standard move is that Scripture speaks the language of the senses, but in the Joshua story the senses report exactly what the text reports and he still needs the miracle to be a real event, so how does one tell an accommodated passage from a literal one without already knowing the physics, and once you have evacuated the physical content, is anything left for the miracle to have consisted in? At least one question on the continuity claim, and at least one on what her reading implies about why the condemnation happened at all — whether she thinks the trial was fundamentally about a hermeneutic or about something else entirely. The last question should ask what she would count as evidence against her own reading: what in the texts, or in the record of the proceedings, would make her give it up. One further thing: in the two recorded sessions of hers I have listened to, she gets out of hard cases with some version of the reply that this is a problem for the exegete rather than for the principle, so I'd like at least two questions where that move is more expensive than it sounds, with follow-ups that close it off.

Because I will be asking these in front of people who know the material better than I do, please also flag, briefly and for each anticipated answer, which of its historical claims are uncontroversial among historians of early modern science and which are genuine live disputes — I would rather cut a question than have it rest on a reading of the 1616 proceedings or of the condemnation that most specialists would reject, and I don't want anything contested handed to me as settled. Finally, if my summary above misdescribes what someone holding her view would really be committed to, tell me that before you give me the questions, rather than writing sharp questions for a position nobody occupies.
