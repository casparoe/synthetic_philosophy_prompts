I'm the tech lead on a six-person team building the scheduling brain for a regional home-care agency — every morning it decides which of about 400 clients get a visit that day from whoever actually showed up, and seperately it reads free-text messages from clients and decides whether to escalate something to a family member or a nurse. My own grandmother is on the client list in one of the pilot counties, which is sort of how I ended up volunteering to write the part of the design doc we call "the values section." We hit one of these decisions about once a sprint, there are ~30 of them stacked up in the backlog for the pilot, and the reviewers are me, two engineers, a PM, a nurse manager, and the agency's counsel — nobody with philosophy training, and counsel has to be able to defend whatever we write in one page. Two hard constraints: we can only get a human on roughly 3% of decisions (the agency won't pay for more, and past that point the product doesn't save them money), and every decision needs a short logged reason string, which I've noticed is often not the real reason the model did what it did.

What I need is a procedure, not a list of principles. Something my team can run in an hour on a backlog item, and that outputs one of three things: a rule we can actually put in a spec, a "defer to a named human" instruction, or a "don't ship this decision at all." The part I keep getting stuck on — and the reason the usual AI-ethics advice hasn't helped — is that most of our hard cases aren't "what's the right thing to do" but "who gets to say what the right thing is." The client's stated wishes, the family's wishes, what the nurse manager thinks is clinically indicated, what the agency is willing to be liable for, and what the county contract requires can come apart, and I've started calling the points where they come apart value handoffs. So I want the procedure to force us to name, for each decision, whose values are authoritative, what happens when two of them conflict, and what the system does when the spec runs out — abstain, escalate, or pick a default and log it. The same build also ships in two counties with visibly different norms about family notification, so the procedure has to tell us what's fixed in the spec, what's a config knob, and who is allowed to turn the knob.

Give me the ordered questions, what counts as an adequate answer to each, where the procedure just says no, and where it has to stop and hand the question to a person — and be explicit that those last two are different moves. Then run it on three backlog items: (1) 40 clients, 25 slots, and the choice between prioritizing whoever is worst off today, whoever deteriorates fastest without a visit, and whoever's missed visit is most likely to turn into a hospital bill; (2) a client messages that she's stopped eating and says "please dont tell my daughter," and the system has to decide whether to notify anyone; (3) a case where the honest answer is that we shouldn't ship the decision at all, or that our 3% deferral budget can't cover it — tell me which, because I'd rather hear it now than at the pilot review. I also want the part where you're honest about what the procedure can't do: the cases where any rule we write is just one engineer's intuition with a reason string bolted on, and how I should document those so we're not pretending otherwise. One thing I remember from a philosophy elective is that people distinguish building ethical rules in top-down from getting them out of data, and a friend who works on alignment keeps telling me I should make the system track moral uncertainty — if either of those actually changes what goes in my spec, say how, and if they're unusable at my granularity, say that too. Answer in American English please, and no reading lists or citations — I need something I can paste straight into a design doc.
