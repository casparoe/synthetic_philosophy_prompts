I run a monthly rationality reading group — a dozen or so people, mostly working programmers with two philosophy graduate students mixed in, nobody with formal logic beyond having written a few proofs — and in two weeks I have them for two hours with a whiteboard on the Lucas–Penrose argument against mechanism. What I need from you is a plan for the session, not an explanation of the argument for my own benefit.

Some facts about the audience that should shape the plan. Their priors are strongly physicalist, and they have all encountered Penrose's quantum-microtubule material, which has taught them to treat the whole topic as a known-bad argument; I expect them to arrive disposed to pattern-match it to dualist woo and stop listening. They think fluently in terms of Bayesian updating, embedded agency, and self-reference, so they will be quicker than a normal audience on the diagonalization and slower on why anyone thought the conclusion mattered. One or two of them will bring up Löb's theorem and want to know whether it is the same phenomenon in a different costume. At least one will say that large language models now prove competition-grade mathematics problems, and take that to settle the question.

Here is what I want you to work out for me.

First, the dependency structure: the order in which the components have to be introduced, and what actually depends on what. Do they need the second incompleteness theorem at all, or only the first? Must they be given the notion of a computably axiomatized theory, or can that be gestured at? Where exactly does the gap between consistency and soundness have to be opened up, since Lucas's move needs me to see that a particular sentence is true rather than merely unprovable? And where does the mechanist thesis have to be stated precisely — do I make them commit to output-equivalence with some Turing machine, or to knowable output-equivalence, and does that choice have to be made before or after the diagonal argument is on the board?

Second, the example that carries the session. I have been toying with a sealed-envelope device: a box that prints arithmetic sentences, a complete specification of the box sealed in an envelope I am permitted to reason about but not open, and the question whether there is something I can see that the box cannot print. Tell me whether that should carry the two hours or whether something else does the work better, and in either case say what the example must contain and, equally, what it must not contain in order not to smuggle in the conclusion.

Third, the misconception map, with each misconception placed at the point in the sequence where it should be defused rather than collected at the end. I can anticipate several: that incompleteness says there are truths mathematics cannot reach, period, rather than relative to a fixed axiomatization; that the argument, if it worked, would establish something non-physical about minds; the slide from I can see that the sentence is true to I am not that machine, which seems to require both that I know which machine I am and that I have some warrant for my own soundness; the quick rebuttal that humans are inconsistent anyway and so the argument dies, which I suspect relocates the argument rather than killing it; and the modern rerun about language models, which I think confuses a claim about all machines with a claim about current architectures, but I would like that handled properly rather than waved off.

Fourth, one objection I specifically want a place for, because it is the sort of thing my group produces and I cannot tell whether it revives Lucas or begs the question. The objection is that I do not need to be told which machine I am. Suppose I know only that I am one member of a computably enumerable family of machines, indexed by the natural numbers. Then the union of everything those machines can prove is itself computably enumerable, so there is a single machine whose output dominates everything I can produce, and I can in principle diagonalize against that machine without ever learning my own index. That looks to me like a genuine strengthening of the anti-mechanist case, and it also looks like it might presuppose exactly what is at issue. Give me the honest verdict on it, and tell me where in the session it belongs so that it deepens the discussion instead of derailing it into a recursion-theoretic tangent.

Fifth, what to leave out at this level and say openly that I am leaving out. My instinct is to drop the derivability conditions, the mechanics of encoding syntax, and the full proof of the fixed-point lemma, and possibly to set Penrose's quantum story aside entirely while noting that I am doing so. Correct me if I am cutting something load-bearing.

Sixth, a closing check: one question or short exercise that would show me they have the logical structure rather than the atmosphere — that they can say what would follow if the argument were sound and what would not. Which brings me to the framing I am unsure about. I want the session to end with the group able to say whether not a machine is a claim with any behavioral or observational consequences at all, or whether it is precisely the sort of posit our tradition distrusts, a mysterious answer to a mysterious question that would look the same however the facts turned out. Should that be the closing frame, or does it need to appear much earlier to keep the programmers from concluding, by minute twenty, that they are being asked to take dualism seriously?
