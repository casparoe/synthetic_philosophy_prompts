I am a retired experimental physiologist — forty years of single-unit and field-potential work, evoked responses, signal averaging, a good deal of time spent arguing about what a waveform can and cannot be taken to show. In retirement I have been sitting in on a philosophy department's reading group on action and free will, and it has become plain to me that the people in that room are running two different arguments through the same experiment. I would like a document that would force them into the same argument, and I would like it written in American English.

What I want is the joint statement that two parties could sign at the end of a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Call the first the antecedent-initiation camp: it holds that neural activity identifiable well before a subject reports the urge or intention to move shows that the conscious intention is a late index of a process already underway, that what we call making up one's mind is in the first instance something the agent's own neural machinery settles, and that the experience of intending reports this settlement and may shape what follows but does not originate the action. Call the second the conscious-control camp: it holds that the interval between the reported onset of intention and the movement, together with the residual capacity to abort a movement already in preparation, gives conscious intention genuine causal authority, and that the early premovement negativity indexes preparation, readiness, or a bias toward acting rather than the settling of anything that deserves to be called a decision. Please characterize each camp in its strongest form before you do anything else; I have no interest in a document that only one side could sign because the other was described badly.

The deliverable should have four parts.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims. I want six to ten of them, and I want them non-vacuous: not the observation that the mind-body problem is hard, but claims with enough content that a skeptic about the experiment would learn something from them. I expect at least these to be candidates: what the premovement negativity is measured from and what it can be measured against; that a prepared movement can still be called off late; that Libet-style tasks use arbitrary, self-paced, motivationally weightless movements in which there are no reasons to weigh and nothing at stake, which may matter a great deal if the mechanism of interest is deliberation; that no instrument reads off the onset of a decision directly, so any claim about when a decision occurs inherits whatever operational definition was smuggled in; that the reported time of intention is a metacognitive report with its own noise sources; and that which philosophical conclusion follows depends on which notion of free will is in play. Add or drop as the collaboration honestly would, but say why each claim is on the list.

Second, a table of the residual disagreements. One row per disputed proposition; in each row, the formulation of that proposition that the antecedent-initiation camp would accept as its own view, and the formulation the conscious-control camp would accept as its own. Steelman both. For each row, mark whether the disagreement is about a mechanism, about what a term refers to, or about what would count as an explanation of agency at all.

Third, the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side — and for each crux the concrete test both camps agree would bear on it, with each camp's prediction written down in advance. I have views about which cruxes matter, and I would like you to treat mine as proposals rather than instructions:

One crux is what the early premovement negativity actually indexes: the settling of the decision, or a stochastic accumulation toward a threshold whose crossing time is set partly by when the movement happens to occur rather than by when the agent settled anything. The test I would propose varies the cost of acting early against the cost of acting late, and includes blocks in which an unpredictable external event creates a decision that must be made on the spot rather than a movement that merely happens; the question is whether the onset of the early signal tracks the deadline structure or the reported intention. Both camps should say what pattern they expect and what pattern would embarrass them.

A second crux is the error budget for the reported time of intention. In my old field one did not report a latency without decomposing its variance: reading error on the display, a systematic pull of the report toward the event that follows it, and reconstruction from memory after the fact. I want that decomposition attempted for the report of when one became aware of intending, and I want each camp to state which component it needs to be large and roughly how large. The natural tests are catch trials on which the prepared movement is unexpectedly blocked, and trials in which attention is briefly captured at the moment of the report; each camp should predict the direction, and where possible the rough magnitude, of the shift.

A third crux is the abort. Is calling off a prepared movement itself an intentional action with a neural precursor of its own, or is it simply the non-occurrence of a threshold crossing, with nothing corresponding to the exercise of a veto? The test is whether the final stretch of premovement activity distinguishes an instructed or self-initiated abort from a go above chance, and whether that stretch predicts the abort in advance rather than merely accompanying it. Both camps should record what they expect to find and what they would concede if the finding went the other way.

A fourth crux is scope: whether whatever mechanism the paradigm isolates is the same mechanism that operates when an agent weighs reasons between two outcomes that differ in ways the agent cares about. Both camps should say what result in a reason-bearing version of the task would surprise them, and both should say in advance how much of their view they would retract if the effect simply failed to generalize.

There is also a framing crux that I suspect is partly verbal: whether the fact that the neural precursor belongs to the agent's own machinery makes the claim that the brain decided before the agent did misleading, or whether that fact is simply irrelevant to the causal question. Please handle this one in the fourth part.

Fourth, a short closing section. Identify which components of the dispute are merely verbal — say which, say why, and propose the disambiguated vocabulary that would let both camps restate their position without talking past each other — and which are substantive and would survive even perfectly clean data. Then name, for each camp, one result that would in fact make it change its mind. I am not asking you to decide who is right; do not adjudicate the substantive disputes. I am asking you to make the residual disagreement exact.

Two further requests, both from habit. Please note, at the points where it matters, which of the shared tests would be underpowered or where a null result would be uninformative rather than reassuring; a collaboration that agrees to a test it could not interpret has agreed to nothing. And please flag any place where the empirical question is in reasonable shape and the philosophical reading is doing all the work, or the reverse. Finally, do not supply citations, references, or a bibliography: I have the library, and what I want from you is the argument.
