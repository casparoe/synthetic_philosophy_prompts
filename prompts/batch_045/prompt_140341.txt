THE QUESTION (Question 4 of 5; answer in no more than 900 words; 30 marks)

(a) [10 marks] Set out, in the version of the doctrine associated with Luis de Molina, the distinction between God's natural knowledge, His middle knowledge, and His free knowledge, and explain why the second is held to be logically posterior to the first and logically prior to the third. Say what a counterfactual of creaturely freedom is.

(b) [10 marks] Explain the grounding objection to Molinism.

(c) [10 marks] Does Molinism succeed in reconciling divine providence with libertarian freedom? Defend your answer against the strongest objection you can state.

THE ANSWER (as submitted)

(a) Molinists divide God's knowledge, in the logical order of His deliberation about creation, into three moments. Natural knowledge is His knowledge of all necessary truths and of every possibility; it is prevolitional, in the sense that its content does not depend on any act of the divine will, and it could not have been otherwise. Free knowledge is His knowledge of the actual world, of what will in fact happen; this is posterior to the decree, since its content is settled by God's choice to create this world rather than another. Between the two stands middle knowledge, scientia media: God's knowledge of what every possible free creature would freely do in every complete set of circumstances in which it might be placed. It resembles natural knowledge in being prevolitional and therefore not up to God, and it resembles free knowledge in having contingent content.

The ordering is what is supposed to do the work. If God's knowledge of creation were only natural knowledge, He would know what could happen but not what would; if it came only by way of the decree, He would know what will happen because He has already settled it, and that settlement is hard to distinguish from determinism. Because He knows antecedently to any decree what each creature would do in each circumstance, He can survey the worlds available to Him and select one in full knowledge of its entire history, including every free choice in it, without causing any of those choices. It was this feature that made the doctrine contested in the sixteenth-century debates about grace: Molina's critics objected that on his account grace is efficacious because God foresees that this creature would freely consent to it, rather than because grace itself determines the will.

(b) A counterfactual of creaturely freedom is a conditional of the form: had creature S been placed in the complete circumstances C — circumstances which in fact never obtain — S would have freely done A. Its truth does not depend on whether C ever arises, and God's knowledge of it is not inferred from His knowledge of the actual world. This is what marks middle knowledge off from simple foreknowledge: the Ockhamist's God knows future contingents, but what He knows there is what will actually happen.

The grounding objection, as pressed by Hasker, is that nothing makes such counterfactuals true. Not any actual event: the circumstances never obtain, and so there is no act of the creature, in time, for the conditional to be about. Not a divine decree: on Molinism these counterfactuals are true antecedently to and independently of God's creative will. And not any state of the agent antecedent to the choice: to posit a condition of the agent robust enough to settle what she would do is to reintroduce the very determinism that Molinism was devised to avoid. The objector's conclusion, in effect, is that there is nothing here for God to know — that an omniscient being can no more know a truth which has no foundation than He can know a falsehood. The Molinist reply, I take it, is that this pictures God's knowledge on the model of our own. We come to know such things, if at all, by inference from evidence, and where there is no evidence we are at a loss. God's knowledge is not discursive: He knows every truth immediately and non-inferentially, and so the absence of anything from which the counterfactual might be inferred is no obstacle to His knowing it. And if the objection were sound it would tell equally against simple foreknowledge of future free actions, whose truth is no better founded; an objection which proves that much proves too much.

(c) I shall argue that Molinism does reconcile providence with libertarian freedom, and that the strongest objection to it has nothing to do with freedom.

The heart of the reconciliation is that knowledge is not causation. God's knowing that S would do A in C is not what makes S do A; if anything the dependence runs the other way, since what makes the counterfactual true is what S would do. Molinism therefore need not say — and explicitly denies — that God causes free choices. A free act is self-determining, and a choice which God had caused would not be free in the sense the Molinist requires. What God brings about is the circumstances; what the creature brings about is the choice. So the truth of God's foreknowledge is no more of a threat to freedom here than it is for the Ockhamist, and Molinism improves on Ockhamism by giving God something to work with before He creates.

Nor is His providence thereby weakened. Since for every creature and every complete set of circumstances God knows exactly what that creature would freely do, He can strongly actualize whichever world He pleases: He need only place creatures in the circumstances in which He foresees that they will freely do what He wills, and the whole history of that world follows. Providence is thus as complete on Molinism as on any account on which the decree is efficacious, while the creature's freedom is untouched, because nothing in the story makes God's knowledge a cause of what He knows. Indeed, given how complete His knowledge of these counterfactuals is, He could, had He wished, have actualized a world containing free creatures and no moral evil at all; that the actual world contains evil shows only that He permitted it, not that He could not have arranged otherwise.

The real objection is about sovereignty rather than freedom, and it has been pressed in the literature since Adams: on Molinism the counterfactuals are true independently of God, so the range of worlds from which He may choose — and therefore the shape of His providential options — is fixed by something He did not determine. Whether this is a defect turns on whether divine aseity requires God to be the source of every contingent truth, and Molinists have argued that it does not. I conclude that Molinism succeeds in the reconciliation the question asks about, at the cost of a conception of sovereignty on which much that God must work with is simply given to Him.
