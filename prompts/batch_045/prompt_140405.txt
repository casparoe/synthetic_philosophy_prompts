I am writing a short position piece on population ethics, and before I commit any of it to print I would like an audit of whether my commitments actually hang together. Here is the package, stated as neutrally as I can manage.

(1) The asymmetry: the presence of suffering is bad; the presence of happiness is good; the absence of suffering is good even when there is no one whose suffering it is the absence of; and the absence of happiness is not bad unless there is someone who is thereby deprived of it. I hold this as a claim about which outcomes are better than which, not merely as a claim about what we have reason to do.

(2) A subject-affecting constraint on value: nothing is good or bad unless it is good or bad for someone, and only those who exist at some time can be the someone. I hold this generally and not only in population ethics; it is why I remain unmoved by appeals to the value of, say, beauty no one ever experiences.

(3) There is no duty to bring happy people into existence, and in bringing them into existence one is not benefiting them.

(4) Antinatalism is false, and so is the claim that it would be better, all things considered, if our species came to an end. A good human life is a great good, and that there be a long human future matters.

(5) Procreative beneficence holds even where the choice determines who exists. If a couple will have a child and can, by conceiving now rather than in three months, bring it about that a numerically different person comes to exist, and one candidate life would go substantially better than the other, they ought to choose the better-off one. This assumes that whatever doctrine governs the choice between creating and not creating also governs the choice between creating one person and creating another. I am less confident of that than of anything else here.

(6) Reducing existential risk is among the most important things we can do, and a substantial part of the case for saying so is the sheer number of good lives that would otherwise never be lived.

(7) Methodologically: I treat transitivity and acyclicity of the better-than relation as fixed constraints I am unwilling to give up, and I discount intuitions about comparisons between populations of different sizes, since those strike me as sensitive to framing in a way that fixed-population intuitions are not.

What I want is an audit rather than a survey. Which of these pull against which, and how severely, ordered rather than listed as if equally bad? Which pairs merely look incompatible and dissolve once one is stated precisely? For each genuine tension, what is the cheapest revision, and what does that revision cost me elsewhere in the set? If one single revision dissolves the most, name it. Where a fix consists in splitting one of my claims into two readings that I have conflated, say which reading survives and what the other was doing for me. And if the set can be made coherent, tell me what the resulting view is committed to about adding happy lives to the world, since I suspect I have not faced that verdict yet. Please argue from the structure of the commitments themselves; I do not want citations, a bibliography, or a reading list. I genuinely do not know which of (1) through (7) I would give up.
