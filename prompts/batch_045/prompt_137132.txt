I sit on the joint clinical ethics committee of a children's hospital network. We review roughly eight protocols a month in which patients who cannot consent — newborns, sedated children, children with cognitive impairments — will be put through procedures that hurt, and we must return a written determination of no more than a page, signed by a lay member of the committee and by hospital counsel. Our board has now instructed us to adopt as our working standard a lexical-threshold negative utilitarian view: below some intensity, pains and pleasures are commensurable and can be traded against one another at some rate; at or above that intensity, pain has lexical priority, so no quantity of pleasure, health benefit or other good that the protocol produces can compensate for it, and the protocol cannot go forward unless the pain itself can be removed or brought below the line.

Please turn that into a procedure we can actually run in a fortnightly meeting, by people who have no training in the theory. It needs to settle, in the order we would ask them: how we locate the threshold when all we have are ordinal reports — nurse and parent ratings, behavioural scales — rather than cardinal units of suffering, and what we do when the same procedure falls on both sides of it depending on who is rating; what belongs in the ledger at all, apart from the pain itself — dread beforehand, the memory of it afterwards, the distress of parents at the bedside; how to compare protocols that trade intensity against duration, or pain now against pain prevented later; and, once we are above the threshold and trading is forbidden, how to choose between two protocols that both involve above-threshold pain — fewest patients over the line, lowest peak intensity, or something else — given that aggregation is no longer available to us. Mark where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision back to us because the remaining question is one of values rather than fact.

Then run it on three protocols now pending: (i) a few seconds of severe pain, unanaesthetised, for each of about two hundred newborns, where an anaesthetic would not fit the ward's workflow and the alternative is a sedative that removes the pain but carries a small risk of a fatal complication; (ii) six months of repeated, individually moderate pain for forty children, with substantial health gains and with children afterwards reporting pride and relief; (iii) a choice between two protocols, both above the threshold, one exposing thirty children to a higher peak of pain briefly and the other exposing a hundred and twenty to a somewhat lower but still above-threshold pain. At least one of these will come out somewhere we will not want to go; show that plainly. Close by saying what the procedure cannot do, and where it will systematically misfire.
