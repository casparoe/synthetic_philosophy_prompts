hi — I run a tiny newsletter on space policy for non-specialists (mostly written at 1am, typos and all) and I keep getting stuck on one thing. Please play the role of a professional philosopher who also happens to know a lot of game theory, and actually brainstorm for a bit before you commit to a take — I'd much rather watch you try on two or three theories and throw one out than get a tidy answer immediately.

Here's the thing. The same astronomy community that spends decades and hundreds of millions building ever bigger listening arrays treats anyone who wants to beam a message outward as a reckless show-off: there's a pretty firm unwritten rule now that listening is respectable science and transmitting is something you don't do unilaterally, without everyone agreeing first. Meanwhile we've been leaking for a century — early warning radar, tv carriers drifting off the planet — and in the 1970s we deliberately fired a short message at a distant star cluster and bolted picture-records onto probes leaving the solar system. On top of that, any halfway competent civilization with a decent telescope could already read our atmosphere or catch us transiting the sun, with zero cooperation from us.

So what does that listen-don't-talk norm actually presuppose? I want the implicit theory reconstructed, and I want to know which bits the practice strictly needs and which bits it merely sits well with. Specific things I want you to get into:

1. Reconstruct the asymmetry. Is it a risk asymmetry (a reciever is safe, a transmitter is not), a value asymmetry (listening gains information, talking gains you nothing you couldn't get later), or something more like professional jurisdiction — scientists shouldn't unilaterally expose everybody?

2. Find the inconsistency, if there is one. If mere existence-detection is the thing that triggers a strike — the dark forest picture from Liu Cixin's novels, all hunters hiding in the dark, chains of suspicion, the fear that the stranger could blow past you technologically any decade now — then we're already cooked and the taboo on messaging is theater. If it isn't, the danger has to live in something more specific. I want you to work with a three-way distinction I've been toying with: a signal can reveal that we exist, where we are, or what we're like / what we might do. Which of those is the norm implicitly treating as the dangerous one, and does that choice even survive the dark forest logic?

3. The self-undermining bit. The search only ever succeeds if some civilization out there violates the very restraint we're following. So continuing to fund the search presupposes something about them: irrationality, non-strategic motivation, immunity to predation, post-biological indifference, something like that. Spell out which, and then face the darker version: the more convinced of the dark forest you are, the less reason you have to listen at all.

4. Take my pet idea seriously and then criticise it. Suppose a genuinely zero-risk version of the search is available — no emission at all, just combing archived sky surveys for weird transit shapes or waste-heat megastructures. If that's on the table, the asymmetry looks perfectly coherent, and the live question moves off physics and onto consent: who may speak for the species when the risk is spread over everyone alive and unborn and the credit goes to a handful of researchers? And note that if the worst happens, the people who would otherwise have existed simply won't — so the obvious we're-risking-our-grandchildren framing may not even work, and the norm may need some story about collective agents or about what's worse for nobody in particular.

5. Tell me which rival views the practice quietly rules out — that hiding is futile, that we're not worth the fuel, that cheap talk can build trust faster than silence can.

6. Finally, what would have to change in practice if we swapped the implicit theory for a loud-is-safer view, or for a full dark forest view? Be honest if the current setup is a half-measure that gets the worst of both worlds: we shout by accident, we whisper on purpose, and we keep a listening program that the hypothesis itself says will never hear anything.
