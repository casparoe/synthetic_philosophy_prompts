I'm a controls engineer — I mostly build state estimators and LQG controllers, so the vocabulary I actually think in is state-space realizations, similarity transforms, observability and detectability, minimal realizations, Kalman filtering, the internal model principle, that sort of thing. I know essentially nothing about ML interpretability beyond what leaks out of blog posts, so please don't hand me a reading list or assume I've read any particular paper (I don't have access to papers right now anyway) — just work from the ideas.

I got into an argument at a wedding with a friend who does interpretability. As I understood her: she claims the activations of a trained net genuinely represent features of the world — there's a direction in a layer that is, say, "the board is in this state" or "there's a curve here", and the rest of the net reads it and acts on it. I said this sounded like astrology to me: with a few hundred dimensions and enough labeled examples you can find *some* direction that correlates with anything you like, so of course a linear probe "works". She had three replies I couldn't answer. First, the probes generalize: fit on one distribution, tested off-distribution, the readout still tracks the feature. Second, interventions: push the activation along that direction and the model's behavior changes in exactly the way it would if the feature had changed, which she says shows the net is using it and not merely encoding it. Third, she says nets pack far more features into a layer than there are dimensions, features that are only nearly orthogonal, so the "direction" story is messier than I'm imagining — which to me sounds like compressed sensing with a lossy decoder, but I'm not sure the analogy holds.

Could you restate this whole disagreement — the realist side and the skeptical side, the probe-vs-intervention argument, and the more-features-than-dimensions business — as faithfully as you can in my vocabulary, and then tell me what didn't survive? Two cases I'd like translated specifically, because I think they're where the two vocabularies come apart. (1) A transformer trained to predict legal next moves from transcripts of a 9x9 board game; a linear readout from the middle of the net recovers the board almost perfectly. Now fine-tune the same model on a variant of the game with different rules but identical move notation: the readout still recovers the board, but the direction has rotated about thirty degrees from the old one, and a second, weaker direction now partly tracks the old board state too. In my world a similarity transform is a non-event — same system, new coordinates — but here the "coordinates" are supposed to be meanings, so I can't tell whether the rotation is a non-event or a real change. (2) A classifier trained on images where the label depends on shape and the background colour is random and irrelevant to the task. Colour is decodable from layer six with high accuracy, but zeroing that direction changes nothing downstream, and there is no path by which it could affect the output. In my vocabulary an unobservable state just gets deleted from the minimal realization; is that the right translation, and does it mean the net doesn't represent colour?

Also: I have a suspicion my own vocabulary is rigged. For me a state estimate is about the plant state partly *because* of its job in the feedback loop, so if I start calling activations "representations" I may be smuggling in a use-theoretic theory of content and then congratulating myself for having translated something. Tell me if that's what's happening, and tell me which parts of the interpretability debate have no counterpart in state-space terms at all — I'm especially wondering about misrepresentation (a filter can just be wrong; can a net?), about whether two nets trained on the same data have "the same" representations, and about the fact that nobody ever specified what the coordinates were supposed to mean. Then give me your view on the residue: does it point to something my framework is missing, or to something less substantive than the interpretability people think they're arguing about?
