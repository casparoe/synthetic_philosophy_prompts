I'm a PhD student in legal philosophy, and this year I'm the graduate-student member of the Governance Committee of my academic association, a learned society of roughly four thousand members. Article IX of our constitution sets the amendment procedure: a two-thirds vote at the annual business meeting for ordinary amendments, plus ratification by a referendum of the full membership for any change to Article IX itself.

Two questions have come to a head before our meeting later this month.

First, that referendum requirement was itself added in 2019 by a two-thirds business-meeting vote alone — the very procedure it now declares insufficient for changing Article IX. One member argues the entrenchment is therefore void and that we may amend by a bare two-thirds vote; the Secretary replies that a rule about its own amendment can authorize the procedure that creates it, and that the member's argument would make entrenchment impossible in principle.

Second, a petition carrying a large minority of signatures asks us to place a replacement constitution on the spring ballot, adoptable by a simple majority. It is framed not as an amendment but as a refounding — repeal the old document wholesale, adopt a new one — so that Article IX never applies. The new text contains a self-application clause (every provision, including the amendment rule and that clause itself, is amendable by majority vote) alongside a clause forbidding any future amendment that would make any provision harder to amend.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate. State the recommendation in the first paragraph, in a form the Committee could adopt as its own: should the petition go on the ballot and, if so, under which threshold, and what should we do about the 2019 entrenchment. I need an actionable bottom line — who does what by when — not a survey of positions. Take the refounding argument seriously: if repeal-and-replace differs in principle from amendment, say where the difference lies and where it runs out; if it does no work, say why. Address what the two clauses in the proposed text jointly produce — a fully self-amending rule of change, a permanently locked one, or something unstable in between — and whether that is a defect in the proposal or a feature. Flag which of your reasons hold whether or not the entrenchment is valid. Keep it under about nine hundred words and plain enough for the two colleagues on the Committee with no philosophy background, and close with the strongest objection to your own recommendation, stated as sharply as you can make it.
