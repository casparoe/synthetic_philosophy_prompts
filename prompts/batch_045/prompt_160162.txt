I spent most of last evening arguing with a friend about whether any empirical science could ever tell us what an experience is like, and we ended in a stand-off that I now think was really about a practice rather than a theory. My friend's side was the familiar one: there is something it is like to see red; that something is, supposedly, intrinsic to the experience, available only from the inside, and in principle detachable from anything behavioural, functional or physical — hence the inverted-spectrum worry, that a creature could match me in every physical and functional respect while my red and green are swapped, and hence too the worry that complete physical and functional knowledge would still leave something out. I could not refute it. What I kept insisting instead is that there is a mature, technologically successful science that behaves as if the worry were empty, and neither of us could say precisely what that science is committed to. I would like you to reconstruct its implicit theory of experience.

The practice is psychophysics, and the colour and hearing sciences that grew out of it. As I understand it, it has these features.

First, it does not, in the first instance, ask subjects to introspect and report qualities. It asks them to discriminate, match and order: say whether two patches differ; adjust a comparison field until the two look identical; say which of two tones is louder; rate how similar two odours are. From judgements like these — largely forced choices, and largely about stimuli rather than about experiences — the field constructs a space: a small number of dimensions, a metric, a distance function. In colour, three matching dimensions, a chromaticity diagram, and then a re-scaled space in which equal distances are supposed to be equally noticeable, plus formulae for colour difference used in industry. In hearing, equal-loudness contours. In olfaction and taste, multidimensional scalings of similarity ratings. The dimensions are then labelled with the names of qualities, and the space is treated as a map of experience.

Second, the practice pulls in two directions at once. On the one hand it denies that matching is identity of the stimulus: two lights with quite different spectra can match, and the same patch shifts in appearance with the surround and with the observer's state of adaptation, which is why colour-appearance models were later built on top of the matching space. On the other hand it treats matching and discrimination as constitutive: a difference no observer, under any adaptation or surround, can see is not a difference in colour; and the number of dimensions along which a subject can be made to discriminate is taken to be the number of degrees of freedom their colour experience has, so that a dichromat's colour experience is described as a two-dimensional space and a normal one as three-dimensional.

Third, it aggregates across subjects. It fits smooth functions to pooled data, defines a standard observer, and treats individual variation as noise or as a parameter to be modelled rather than as something that threatens the enterprise. And it uses the resulting numbers for thoroughly practical ends — colour management across cameras, displays and presses, hearing-aid fitting, quality control in paint and food — where they plainly work.

There is one further wrinkle: the field's own toolkit includes cases where discrimination and experience seem to come apart, since forced-choice performance can exceed what a subject will report seeing, and blindsight and related phenomena are standard fare in the surrounding literature. So the practice both relies on discrimination as its basic datum and possesses reasons to doubt that discrimination is experience.

What I want from you is the implicit theory of consciousness, or of qualia, that this practice presupposes, and I would like it kept separate from what the practice merely fits well with.

Does the practice presuppose something like a relational or quality-space view — that what an experience is like is nothing over and above its position in a network of similarity and difference relations to other experiences and to stimuli, so that a properly constructed quality space is not a proxy for qualia but a measurement of them? Or is the practice, strictly speaking, neutral: could someone who holds that qualia are intrinsic, private and ineffable accept every technique described above, on the grounds that the space maps the psychophysical link, or the stimuli, and never the qualia themselves?

Which commitments are strictly required by the practice, and which are optional extras? In particular: does it require that indiscriminability behave like a distance — symmetric, transitive, additive — and if so what happens to it when it does not? Does it require that similarity relations be comparable across subjects, and what inside the practice is supposed to underwrite that comparability, given that its raw data are one person's forced choices at a time? Does it require that verbal reports reliably track similarity even if they do not track what it is like, and how does that sit with the rhetoric of privacy and ineffability?

What does the practice rule out if we take it at its word? Does it entail that the inverted-spectrum hypothesis is empirically empty, on the grounds that no measurement the field could make distinguishes the two subjects? Does it entail that any difference in experience unaccompanied by a difference in discrimination, matching, report, or downstream use is no difference at all? Does it entail that the residue in the Mary-style case is either an illusion or a species of ability?

Where is the practice underdetermined — which of its features could the intrinsic-qualia theorist and the relationalist both endorse while giving different readings of them? Metamerism looks like a case in point: one side will say it shows the field has given up on the stimulus and is in the business of appearances, the other that it shows appearance just is the equivalence class. Say who has the better reading of what the practice actually does, and whether the practice settles the matter at all.

Finally, what would have to change in the practice if the intrinsic-qualia view were true and psychophysicists took it seriously? Would they have to abandon forced-choice discrimination as the basic datum, stop pooling across subjects, stop treating the number of discriminable dimensions as the number of degrees of freedom of experience, or add some independent calibration of introspective report? And conversely, if the relational view is right, is there anything the practice currently does that it ought to stop doing — the standard observer, the pooling, the labelling of dimensions as though they named something intrinsic?

One constraint: I have no library access and I do not want a literature survey, a reading list or citations, and I would rather you did not lean on the names attached to these views. Reconstruct the theory from the practice as I have described it, tell me which parts of it are forced and which are merely convenient, and say where a rival theory would license exactly the same procedure.
