I teach a third-year undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of medicine, and I want to replace my usual handout with something the students actually have to work through: a classification instrument that takes a person from their verdicts on concrete cases to the position in the naturalism/normativism space that those verdicts commit them to. I am asking you to build the instrument, not to argue for a position.

The deliverable is a decision tree with exactly ten branch nodes and between eight and twelve leaves. Every branch node must be phrased as a question a thoughtful undergraduate can answer without knowing any of the theoretical labels — a case or a concrete claim, never a restatement of someone's theory, and never a question about what the word disease means. At most one node may be about meaning at all. The leaves should be named positions: the biostatistical naturalist, the selected-effects or harmful-dysfunction hybrid, welfare-based and strong normativists, the pragmatist or institutional-kind view that treats disease ascriptions as tools rather than discoveries, the eliminativist who thinks there is nothing for the term to track, and any further position you think occupies a cell I have left out. Name each leaf by the commitments that define it and one sentence on its most awkward consequence. Do not attach author names, papers, or dates to any position; I do not want a bibliography, and I want the handout to be checkable without a library.

Please build the tree from the logical space before placing anyone in it. The axes I want used are: whether disease ascription requires a failure of a part-level function, and whether function is read historically as selected effect or forward-looking as current causal contribution to survival and reproduction; whether it requires the bearer to be worse off, and whose evaluation settles that — the bearer's own, the species-typical life history, or a counterfactual version of the bearer; whether statistical typicality within a reference class matters, and whether the reference class is fixed by the theorist, by age, or by the species at its prime; and whether there is one underlying property here or a cluster of related but separable concerns. Show me the combinations these axes allow, then locate the actual positions in that space, then list the combinations nobody occupies and tell me for each whether it is empty because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.

One further axis is mine and I want it included. Disease ascriptions appear to do at least four jobs: explaining a bearer's symptoms, licensing intervention, excusing or allocating the sick role, and authorising a claim on institutional resources. I want one branch that separates the leaves by which of these jobs they treat as primary, and each leaf to say which job it handles well and which it handles badly. A view that explains everything except why anyone should treat the person is doing something different from a view that guides treatment but predicts nothing, and I want my students to see that.

The cases below must all appear somewhere in the tree, in whatever order serves the structure. Several are invented so that students cannot bring in a memorised verdict:

A trait present in roughly eighty-five percent of adults by middle age, painless, reducing maximal oxygen uptake by about twelve percent, and associated with slightly higher lifetime reproductive success.
A physiological response that does exactly what that part of the organism was shaped by selection to do — conserving salt and water — but which, in a high-salt environment, raises blood pressure and shortens life.
A symptom-free thirty-year-old whose only finding is a marker that raises the ten-year probability of a serious event from one percent to four percent.
An abnormality discoverable only at autopsy in people who died of something else, and which would never have produced symptoms.
Infertility in someone who does not want children and reports no distress about it.
Congenital absence of one kidney: rare, no current functional loss, no elevation of risk provided contact sport is avoided.
A cluster of disabling fatigue and pain that reliably produces disability, with no identifiable mechanism and normal results on every available test.
A pattern of child behaviour that is statistically typical given the child's setting, which a school system identifies and treats, after which the identified children's own self-description changes and the measured prevalence rises.
Senescence: universal among those who live long enough, progressive, and a decline in virtually every capacity.
Mastitis in a dairy cow and rust in a wheat crop, as a test of whether one concept is doing work across human medicine, veterinary medicine, and agriculture.

Finally, three things I will use in the seminar itself. First, flag any pair of leaves your ten questions cannot separate, and for each such pair give me the eleventh case that would separate them. Second, tell me which two nodes are most likely to be answered inconsistently by the same student, because those are the ones where the position the tree assigns is unstable and where I want to spend class time. Third, tell me how the assignment changes if the student takes the reference class to be typical-for-age rather than typical-for-the-species-at-twenty-five, since I expect half the class to do this without noticing.
