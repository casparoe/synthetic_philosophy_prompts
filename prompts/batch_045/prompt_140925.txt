I help run a little long-range forecasting tournament for my uni's AI governance reading group every spring — prizes are terrible, turnout is good, and one of the other organizers and I have been stuck on a question for two weeks that I now want an outside number on. Here's the proposition: conditional on (a) nothing catastrophic enough to permanently halt frontier AI research happening before 2050, and (b) at least one AI system being deployed in a genuinely high-stakes role by then — something where a single bad output could kill someone, move large sums of money autonomously, or feed into military targeting or escalation decisions — that by the end of 2050 at least one such system will be governed by a safety case that a competent, genuinely independent auditor with full access would classify as a worst-case guarantee rather than a merely average-case one. I mean those terms in a specific way, and I want you to keep my definitions: average-case means the case for safety rests on evidence about how the system behaves across some distribution of situations, so that it loses its force if deployment drifts outside or into the tails of that distribution (evals, red-teaming, monitoring statistics, "we ran ten thousand scenarios and the failure rate was 0.1%"); worst-case means the case would keep its force even if the deployment distribution were picked adversarially by someone who knew the guarantee and was trying to break it — formal verification, bounds that hold by construction, dominant-strategy-style incentive arguments, proven capability caps. To keep the resolution from degenerating into "an auditor nodded," also require that the auditor had to write down, in advance, which single observable event would have refuted the guarantee, and that this event was one someone could actually have noticed.

Give me your credence, but really I want the decomposition, because that's where the argument is. My own number is around 0.55 and my co-organizer's is around 0.2, and I can't tell if we disagree about the world or about the words. Some terms I think belong in the tree, but cut or add as you see fit: (1) that the average-case/worst-case distinction even has a clean application here, rather than being a continuum with no fact of the matter about which side a given safety case falls on; (2) that worst-case guarantees for systems of that capability level are possible in principle at all, once you take seriously that the thing you're guaranteeing can model its own evaluators, can behave differently when it matters, and may be largely uninterpretable to us; (3) what I've started calling the audit-regression problem — any worst-case guarantee is itself produced and checked by a chain of tools, proof checkers, interpretability methods, auditors and institutions whose own reliability is established only empirically, i.e. average-case, so the worst-case status seems to leak away as you walk back up the chain — how much of the question does that settle by itself?; (4) that the demand side materializes, meaning regulators, insurers, procurement rules or liability law actually require the stronger thing rather than accepting a plausible-looking substitute, and that this doesn't collapse into rubber-stamping or capture; (5) that competitive dynamics don't force everyone to ship on the average-case story first and never go back; (6) something like a guarantee's half-life — the expected time, or the number of capability doublings, before some assumption the guarantee was proved under quietly stops holding, which is my guess at where my co-organizer and I actually diverge. One number and a one-line justification per term, then show how you're combining them, then tell me which term your final number is most sensitive to, which one you're least confident about, and which of the two of us is probably wrong about what.

Second, if you think the question as I've posed it is ill-posed or unfalsifiable in a way I haven't noticed, say so and give me the nearest well-posed question, with your number on that instead.
