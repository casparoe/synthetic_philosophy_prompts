I'm doing a 900-word piece on self-knowledge — the "do you actually know your own mind" angle — and I need one concrete case to hang the whole thing on. Not a famous example from the literature, something I can put in front of general readers in a single paragraph. So build me one.

The setup is a disagreement between two pictures of introspection. Call the first one detectivism: introspection is a kind of inward perception, with a fixed reliability profile — good for vivid, occurrent, here-and-now states, bad for standing traits and for the causes of your own behavior. Call the second one the transparency or agency picture: you don't inspect an inner object to find out what you believe or intend, you settle it by answering the outward, world-directed question ("is it true?", "shall I?"), and theres something second-rate about turning the detective's eye on yourself instead.

What I want is the reverse of the case everyone reaches for. Don't give me a pain or an itch, where detection obviously wins. Don't give me a plain factual belief, where looking outward obviously works. I want a case where the state in question is genuinely attitude-like — a belief, an intention, a wanting, a suspicion, a resolve — so the transparency picture is supposed to have authority over it, and where the subject runs the transparency procedure honestly and comes out with the wrong answer about herself, while the detectivist story gets it right.

Constraints, becuase I know how these things go:

- Realistic, one paragraph, under about 150 words. A newsroom, a clinic, a hiring meeting, a family kitchen. No lab, no scanner, no electrodes, nothing measuring anything.
- The subject should have no motive to hide anything from herself and nothing to gain from the answer she gives. No wishful thinking, no self-deception, no guilt, no axe to grind. If the case only works because she's repressing something, it's a dud.
- At the moment of the report nothing has happened yet. She hasn't acted, nobody has observed her, there's no track record. The detectivist verdict has to be reachable from what's introspectively available to her right then, not from what she later does.
- The verdicts have to fall out of the two pictures, not be written into the scenario. No special powers, no unusual neurology, no "she's just a very perceptive person."
- Both sides have to be talking about the same state. If your case only works because it isn't really a belief at all but some implicit or sub-personal state, I don't think it works — that feels like relabeling rather than a counterexample. Tell me if you disagree, but don't build it in.

After the paragraph I need:

1. The procedure the subject actually runs, and why the transparency picture says that procedure delivers knowledge here.
2. The introspectable items the detectivist points to instead, and why they're supposed to be the better guide in this case.
3. Which single feature of the case is doing the work — and what happens if you remove it. Does it collapse back into a case where transparency gets it right?
4. The pushback. How does a transparency theorist say I've cheated? Is the "that's not really a belief" move legitimate here, or is it a dodge?

It's going in a piece where a philosopher gets a reply sidebar, so the case has to be able to take a hit.
