Hey — I'm a novelist (three books, none of them about philosophy) and I've let myself be talked into giving one of those festival talks on the theme "is the world getting less violent," which is roughly the question my new novel circles around without ever actually asking. I've drafted it as a sort of essay-lecture and I'd really like someone to poke holes in it before I stand up and say it out loud in front of four hundred people. Please be blunt. I'd much rather be corrected in private than on stage.

Here's the draft as it stands — more scaffolding than prose at this point:

**1. Start with the unit, not the trend.** Everyone argues about whether the trend is real. I want to argue about what's being counted. Candidate units: violent events; victims; deaths; pain-years; years of life lost; violations of consent; "amount of force a given social arrangement requires to stay stable." These don't move together. A world with fewer killings and more long prison sentences could be worse on any of the last three and better on the first. So "less violent" needs a unit before it needs a dataset, and I don't think there's an obvious way to pick one — or rather, I think the unit gets chosen by what you were afraid of in the first place.

**2. The denominator is doing more work than anyone admits.** Per capita is the礼貌 default, but per capita of what? Two worries. (a) Dilution: if you add a lot of peaceful people to the population, the rate drops without anyone getting safer. That feels like it shouldn't count as progress for the person who was already there, and I want to say the honest version of the question is "is a given person safer," not "is the average lower." (b) Exposure: most interpersonal violence happens between people who actually encounter each other. Someone in a fourteenth-century village met a few hundred distinct people in a lifetime; someone now meets thousands. If you normalise by encounters instead of by heads, the decline might look much bigger — or much smaller, depending on whether modern encounters are padded with low-risk ones. There doesn't seem to be a privileged exposure measure, and that worries me more than the dilution point does.

**3. My actual thesis, which I'm calling the hydraulic view.** Maybe what declines isn't violence but violence's visibility in a given category. Get good at suppressing one form and the same dispositions get routed somewhere else: the duel becomes the lawsuit, the pogrom becomes the housing policy, the beating becomes the sentence. What's conserved isn't a quantity of harm but something like a society's willingness to impose suffering to keep its arrangement stable. I like this because it explains why every era's reformers think they've won and why their grandchildren find some new atrocity. I don't like it because I can't say what would refute it.

**4. Latent violence.** Related idea I'm fond of: a peace held up by a credible threat of force still has the violence in it, the way a dam has the water in it. If deterrence is doing the work, counting only realised events is like measuring a dam's strength by how much water got through. So maybe the right measure is counterfactual — how much force *would* be needed — and of course that's the measure we can least easily take. (Suspicion: this collapses into "all state power is violence," which makes the whole thing trivial. I can't tell if I've walked into that.)

**5. Is there a fact of the matter?** Two options. The realist says: yes, there's some real quantity of violence-stuff out there and our instruments are just blunt, so the question is well-posed and hard. The carving view says: no — "violence" is a cluster we carved out for moral and legal purposes (to blame, to punish, to license a state response), and asking for a grand total is a bit like asking whether there's more "weather" this year. The cluster has no common metric because it was never held together by a metric; it's held together by a narrative shape, which is presumably why a novelist got asked to talk about it.

**6. The bit I'm stuck on.** The carving view has a consequence I can't shake: if the concept is our carving, then a society could become less violent purely by relabeling — reclassify deaths as accidents, as medical events, as administrative outcomes — and the numbers would improve while nothing improved. That feels like cheating. But if I say it *is* cheating, I seem to be committed to there being a fact of the matter after all, which is the thing I just spent the talk denying. So either I give up the carving view or I give up the intuition that relabeling is cheating, and I genuinely can't see which.

What I'd like from you:

- Take the four conceptual moves separately (the unit problem, the denominator stuff, the hydraulic thesis, latent violence) and tell me which ones are actually load-bearing and which collapse into something boring — e.g. does the hydraulic view say anything more than "kinds of harm change over time," and is that a fatal problem?
- On latent violence specifically: is the collapse-into-triviality worry I flagged real, and is there a version of the idea that escapes it?
- On the per-encounter denominator: is there an obvious reason that's a bad measure that I'm missing, or does it survive?
- On section 6: is there a way out of the dilemma, or is one of the horns actually fine to bite? I'd especially like to know whether the "relabeling feels like cheating" intuition is doing legitimate work or whether it's just my residual realism talking.
- General: is any of this interesting to someone who thinks about these things for a living, or is it all old hat dressed up in new words? And flag anywhere I'm confidently wrong rather than just vague.

Its fine to tell me the whole thing needs seperate reworking. I'd rather know now.
