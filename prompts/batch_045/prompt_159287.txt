I'm in eleventh grade, and I run my school's math club — twelve or so people, mostly tenth to twelfth graders, two of them on the competitive programming team, one doing an independent project on the Collatz conjecture, the rest there because they like puzzles. In three weeks I have a forty-minute slot and a whiteboard, and I want to spend it on why anyone would refuse the law of excluded middle, treated as a live question rather than as a historical curiosity. Nobody in the room has had any formal logic; what they do have is proof by contradiction, from the argument that the primes never run out and from the irrationality of the square root of two, plus Cantor's diagonal argument if I need it. Assume nothing beyond that. What I want from you is not an explanation of intuitionism but a plan for explaining it: the order the pieces have to come in and what depends on what, the single example that should carry the whole arc, and the misconceptions I should expect, each with the point in the sequence where it gets headed off.

Here is where I keep getting stuck, and why I'm asking instead of just sketching it myself. My first instinct was to open with the standard chestnut, the two-line argument that some irrational a and b give a rational a to the b, but I've decided against leading with it: it makes the whole position look like a parlor trick, and I'm told this particular case now has a constructive proof as well, which would only muddy things. My candidate instead is a real number I define in front of them digit by digit — the number between zero and one whose nth binary digit is 1 just in case 2n+2 is a sum of two primes, and 0 otherwise — followed by the question whether that number is rational. It looks like an ordinary object with a definite construction, and yet, if I have this right, the claim that it is rational or irrational is not something a constructivist will assert, even though nobody thinks the answer depends on what we decide. Tell me whether that example can carry the full forty minutes, what else it would need in order to, and what it crucially must not have: I don't want the constructivist to come across as a pedant who has found one more thing to refuse, and I don't want anyone leaving with the impression that constructive mathematics is just classical mathematics with some theorems crossed out.

Two further things I'd like built into the sequence. First, the distinction I think has to be in place before the refusal can look like anything other than ignorance: for each individual even number, a constructivist is happy to assert that it is a sum of two primes or that it is not, because there is a finite check to run; what is not assertable is the single global claim that every even number is or that some even number is not. So the refusal does not track how much we happen to know, and I want the session to make that land rather than merely state it — though if I have the contrast backwards, say so and rebuild the plan around the corrected version. Alongside it, tell me where to place the move that this may really be a dispute about what the connectives mean rather than about which statements hold: raised too early it deflates the room into thinking the two sides are only quibbling about words, and raised too late it never arrives at all. And tell me where to absorb the objection my programmers will raise the moment they hear any of this — that if a constructive proof is just an algorithm, we may as well keep classical logic and tag the constructive proofs as a bonus. Then the misconceptions, in the order they will actually form, and which ones I should invite rather than defuse: I'm guessing that some statements are held to be neither true nor false, that this is really skepticism about what mathematicians can know, that it is finitism about how many numbers there are, and that proof by contradiction is simply banned outright — the last of which I think is the one worth getting exactly right, since proving a negation feels to me quite different from extracting a positive claim from a refutation of its own refutation, but I can't see how to put that in fifteen words at a whiteboard. Finish by giving me one question to end on that would show me the idea has landed rather than that they can repeat a slogan, and by naming one thing I should openly refuse to explain in forty minutes and say out loud that I am refusing it.
