I lead a monthly discussion group for non-specialists at my public library — a mix of curious adults and a few interested teenagers, none of whom have any background in logic or philosophy of science — and next week I have a fifty-minute slot on Hempel's raven paradox. Last night I had a long, inconclusive argument with a friend about the paradox, and it showed me that I can state the puzzle but not teach it. Her position was that a green apple really does confirm the hypothesis that all ravens are black, because that hypothesis is logically equivalent to the claim that all non-black things are non-ravens, so any observed non-black non-raven is confirming evidence; what she called the feeling that the apple is irrelevant is, she said, just a psychological bias toward looking at ravens. I objected that if she were right, an ornithologist could test the hypothesis by staying in her office and logging every non-black object she happened to see, which no working scientist would count as a test of the raven hypothesis; she replied that the absurdity of the office-logging scenario is merely practical and not logical, and that the paradox really shows our ordinary notion of evidence to be incoherent. I then accused her of conflating a positive instance of the hypothesis with an instance of a merely equivalent formulation; she accused me of special pleading on behalf of intuition. Neither of us convinced the other, and I do not want the library group to inherit that stalemate as a shouting match.

So please plan the session for me rather than explain the paradox to me again. Give me the order in which the ideas must be introduced and why — what depends on what, where the group has to be brought to see the equivalence between all ravens are black and all non-black things are non-ravens, and where the intuitive resistance should be given its full force rather than explained away. Tell me the one concrete scenario that should carry the whole session, what it must have and lack in order to do that work, and whether the carrier should be the plain green apple, my friend's office-complement-log scenario in which a researcher logs non-black non-ravens all day, or something else you would choose; I am especially unsure whether the office-complement-log device helps by making the impracticality vivid or misleads by turning a point about relevance into a point about laziness. List the misconceptions the audience will predictably form and tell me exactly where in the sequence to head each off: I expect at least that a green apple is simply irrelevant to the hypothesis, that the paradox is only a verbal trick with no real content, and that observing one black raven proves the hypothesis, but add the ones I have not thought of, including any that my friend and I modeled in our argument. Specify the check I can use at the end to see whether the group has understood the paradox rather than just learned the slogan that a green apple confirms it, and say what I should openly refuse to explain at this level and why.

Because my friend and I could not settle the disagreement, I also want an epistemic check on your plan: as you give it, say how confident you are in what you claim, distinguishing if you can between your confidence in the logical and philosophical points and your confidence in the pedagogical judgments, and single out the one point in the plan or in the reconstruction of the paradox that you are least sure of. I do not need a hedge on every line, but I do want that explicit confidence statement and one clearly flagged uncertainty.
