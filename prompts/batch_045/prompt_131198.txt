I've somehow ended up on two hiring committees this year — one at my day job, one for a volunteer board I'm on — and both have gotten really weird over the last twelve months. Candidates are obviously running everything through an LLM, our own team is now seriously discussing running the applications back through an LLM to screen them, and everyone involved hates it and nobody wants to be the first to stop. At the same time my partner runs a tiny bakery and we've watched local ad auctions turn into the same shape: everyone bidding up, nobody actually gaining. So I've been reading around multipolar traps, "Moloch", races to the bottom, and I've decided I'd rather work on it than just be annoyed about it.

I'm applying for a small independent research fellowship — about $20k, six months, roughly one day a week — and I need a two-page proposal. Here's my half-formed idea, which I want you to turn into something fundable.

The popular framing treats all of these as one phenomenon: a collective action trap where everyone would prefer to stop and nobody can unilaterally stop. I think at least two different things get lumped together there. First, traps that run on choice within a fixed set of players — I escalate because escalating pays me, and a commitment device, a norm, or a regulator could in principle fix it. Second, traps that run on selection: nobody is choosing anything, the units that don't escalate simply get filtered out (the firm that doesn't cut costs goes under, the applicant who doesn't use the tool doesn't get an interview, the shop that doesn't raise its bid disappears from the page). From inside it feels like a choice, but the mechanism is differential survival. I suspect the second kind needs a totally different remedy set — you can't coordinate your way out of it, you have to change the selection environment: the variance, who exits, what actually gets selected for — and that a lot of real-world "can't we all just agree not to" efforts fail precisely because people misdiagnose a selection race as a choice race.

There's a related thing I keep tripping over: in a lot of these cases somebody is making money off the escalation (the platform, the test-prep industry, the vendor selling the screening tool), so "everyone would prefer to stop" is just false, and I want the diagnostic to be able to say where the surplus is going and who the profiteers are.

What I want in the proposal:
- the question stated tightly enough that it has a determinate answer in six months, not a gesture at a big topic
- why the standard framing leaves that question open
- the method: what I'd actually do with roughly 25 days of work, including two or three worked cases (the LLM hiring thing, ad auctions, and maybe academic publishing metrics or college admissions) and what exactly I'd compare across them
- what the deliverable is — I'm imagining a diagnostic someone actually stuck in one of these situations could run: what to measure, what to look for, and which intervention follows from which diagnosis
- what would count as the project failing, honestly stated
- an honest scope check: is this too big for six months at one day a week, and if so what's the version that does fit

Don't bother with a literature review or citations, and please don't invent any references — I'll fill those in myself. Write it plain, my voice-ish, not grantese. I'd definately rather have something a bit opinionated than something safe.
