I've been reading the rationalist blogosphere more or less continuously for about fifteen years, and one habit that scene drills into you is suspicion of a vivid scenario that seems to do your thinking for you. I now teach an upper-level seminar on philosophical method to a mix of philosophy and cognitive-science majors, and I'm writing the take-home final. I need one essay question on intuition pumps — how thought experiments persuade and mislead — together with a model answer and a marking scheme. Ninety minutes, targeted length 1,200 to 1,500 words, closed book, no library and no web: students may not look anything up, so nothing in the question may depend on a source, a citation, or an author's exact wording.

The problem I keep hitting is that my students arrive already able to recite the cautions. They can tell me that intuition pumps work by framing, that they can smuggle in irrelevant detail, that Dennett — who coined the term — distinguished pumps used as tools of persuasion from pumps used as engines of discovery and warned that they can be abused, that a pump is supposed to be kicked away once the argument stands on its own, and that verdicts can shift with wording, order, or framing. A question that asks for any of that is a question I can grade without reading it. I want the crux instead.

Here is the apparatus I've been working out in my own reading notes, and I want the question built on it. First, a taxonomy of jobs a thought experiment can do: a coherence pump, used to make a scenario conceivable so that a possibility premise can be asserted; an extension pump, used to elicit a verdict about whether a concept applies in a case, and thereby test or refine an analysis; and a premise pump, used to lend pre-theoretic support to a substantive claim. Each has a different characteristic failure mode, and I want students to see that a single "intuition pumps can mislead" warning lumps together three different risks.

Second, two diagnostics. The mirror test: hold the pump fixed and invert the single feature suspected of doing the work; if the verdict does not move, that feature was not load-bearing. The blank control: a scenario matched to the pump in structural complexity, weirdness, and narrative shape but stripped of the target content, to check whether the response is a generic reaction to strangeness rather than a response to the target property. When the verdict is produced by such a general-purpose reaction, call it pump capture; when it is produced by features outside the claim the pump is meant to support, call it pump leakage.

Third, and this is the part I actually want the question to force: the load-bearing/incidental split is not free. Deciding which features of a scenario are doing the work looks like it requires a prior theory of what the target property is — which is the very thing the pump was supposed to help settle. So the diagnostic appears to presuppose the conclusion, and the student has to say whether it can be run in any non-question-begging way. Candidate moves I'd accept include using a pump negatively, to break a sufficiency claim, rather than positively, to establish one; using it only as an internal-consistency check on a theory the arguer already accepts; triangulating across independently built pumps whose incidental features are disjoint; and reinterpreting what a pump measures, as a fact about our concepts rather than about the world. There are also traps: inverting a feature often changes several things at once, so a shifted verdict may be caused by something the inversion introduced; and some pumps only generate a verdict as one member of a pair, so there is nothing to mirror.

Please deliver four things.

1. The question as it would appear on the exam paper, self-contained, defining any of the above vocabulary the students need and naming one specific thought experiment they must work with — chosen from the small set that appears in every introduction (the trolley switch and the footbridge, the Chinese room, the experience machine, teletransportation, the violinist, Mary the color scientist, the utility monster, brains in vats). Add one sentence to me, not to the students, saying why that pump is the best one to run the diagnostics on. Require students to write down, before they report any verdict, which features they are treating as load-bearing and what result would support or undermine what — a pre-registration clause — and flag for me which sub-question a memorized summary cannot touch.

2. A model answer of 900 to 1,100 words, written as the strongest student in the room would write it: it must take a position on whether the diagnostics can be rescued from circularity and say which of the three pump-jobs survives the rescue and which does not.

3. A marking scheme out of 25, organized as must-hit moves with the marks each is worth, a one-line note for each on the way students typically get it wrong, and an explicit statement of what separates an A from a B. Include a policy for a student who argues well against the model answer's own classification: what earns full credit, and what does not.

4. An epistemic note on your own answer, which matters to me because I intend to argue with it before I finalize the exam. Tag each substantive claim in the model answer with a confidence level in brackets, and close with a paragraph of at most 120 words naming the single claim you are least confident about, and saying what would raise your confidence in it and what would lower it. In the marking scheme, tell me which of those claims I should permit students to attack for full credit.
