I'm a novelist — this is my third book, so I should know better than to wander into philosophy — and I've got a chapter that keeps going wrong, so please help me out.

The set-up: my narrator's younger sister has drifted into one of those online wellness-to-conspiracy communities over about two years. The narrator keeps sending her articles, studies, patient-registry data, the usual, and every single one comes back used against her: the source is corrupt, the journal is captured, the statistic is a trick. Nothing lands, and the sister is, annoyingly, good at arguing. I want to get the mechanism right rather than write her as stupid or brainwashed, which would be lazy.

So I read a long piece in a Sunday magazine that lays out what it calls the two-way distinction philosophers and cognitive scientists now use. I'm paraphrasing from memory but this is close to what it said:

"An epistemic bubble is just a gap. The people around you happen to share your assumptions, so you never run into the other side — an accident of who you know and what you read, made worse by recommender algorithms that feed you more of what you already clicked on. Nothing has been done to you, so a bubble pops the moment real counter-evidence gets in: show a bubbler the facts and she updates more or less like anybody else. An echo chamber is different in kind and much worse. There the group doesn't merely leave out the other side, it actively discredits it, teaching you in advance that anyone outside is bought, stupid, or evil. That is why facts don't work on these people: you can't get evidence past a filter whose entire job is to reject evidence. So a chamber is held shut by distrust rather than by ignorance, which is also why its members are often perfectly rational — they're reasoning well from a trust structure somebody else built for them — and why the only real fix is to rebuild trust in outsiders rather than to argue."

My friend who teaches philosophy said the distinction comes from a paper by someone named Nguyen, maybe in 2020, but I don't care about the citation, I care about whether the magazine got it right. A couple of things smell slightly off to me: the word "filter" seems to put back the very thing the contrast is supposed to remove, and "perfectly rational" sounds like the sort of thing a magazine adds to make a paragraph feel clever. But I can't tell where.

What I'd like: go through that summary more or less sentence by sentence and tell me what someone who actually works on this would accept, what they'd wince at, and what is just wrong, and say whether the final claim about the remedy survives once you've fixed the rest. Sending my narrator's sister more material doesn't bounce off this group, it feeds it.
