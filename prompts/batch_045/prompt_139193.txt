I've been following the rationalist-blog conversation about recursively self-modifying agents for years — the tiling-agent literature, the Löbian obstacle, the probabilistic and reflective-oracle replies to it — and I still can't tell whether the obstacle is a load-bearing wall or a scar left by an over-strict formalization. So I want your credence on the substantive question, with the derivation in the open rather than a bare number.

Setup, so we don't talk past each other. An agent A reasons in a formal system T — say, something at least as strong as Peano arithmetic, equipped with the usual provability predicate. A wants to build a successor A-prime and, before building it, wants a license to trust it: a derivation, inside T, that what A-prime proves or does is safe. If A-prime reasons in the very same T, then the general form of that license is: for arbitrary P, if A-prime proves P, then P — that is, T proves box-P implies P schematically. By Löb's theorem (if T derives box-P implies P, then T derives P outright), T can grant itself that license only by already proving every P, which it can't do for the interesting ones. So the parent cannot, from within its own system, certify a successor that reasons exactly as it does. That is the obstacle in its standard form.

The proposition I want a number on is that the obstacle is dissolvable in the sense that matters for building a genuine recursive self-improver: that there exists in principle a design in which (i) each generation can certify the next well enough to be willing to build it, (ii) the certification does not require the parent's system to be of strictly greater consistency strength than the child's, so that nothing degrades or runs out across an unbounded chain of self-modifications, (iii) the problem is not simply outsourced to an external verifier that is itself never tiled, and (iv) the guarantee that survives is strong enough that you would actually switch the thing on. Give me your probability that such a design exists, decomposed. If you think the proposition is ill-posed as written, say which clause makes it so, answer the nearest well-posed variant, and flag what you changed.

My own number drifts between 0.25 and 0.45 depending on how I read clause (iv); a friend who is more optimistic about the probabilistic approaches sits near 0.8. More than the headline I want the decomposition. These are the sub-questions I think carry the weight — split, merge, or add as you see fit, and give each a number with a one-line justification:

1. Formalization artifact. How much of the bite comes from demanding proof, or provable soundness, as the license, rather than something weaker that would still be adequate? Concretely, consider a comparative-dominance license: I do not need to prove my successor's conclusions are true, only that acting on them yields expected utility at least as high as acting on my own conclusions. Probability that switching to that style of license dissolves the obstacle rather than relocating it, on the grounds that estimating the successor's expected utility still requires trusting its model of the world?

2. Capability versus consistency strength. The descending-strength escape lets each parent use a system of strictly greater consistency strength than its child and certify the child's weaker system, so the chain always moves downward. Probability that capability — more search, better heuristics, larger memory, richer world-models — can be decoupled from consistency strength well enough that an indefinitely long chain of strictly weakening systems still gains in everything we care about, i.e. that we never run out of strength to spend.

3. Infinite versus finite tiling. Perhaps unbounded tiling is not needed: a bounded number of trust-transfers might reach a reflective fixed point, or the returns to further self-modification might be bounded in practice, so the descending chain never has to be infinite. Probability that some finite chain suffices for everything that matters.

4. Probabilistic or approximate trust, and the compounding of error. The probabilistic-tiling and reflective-oracle line replaces proof with trust that degrades by some epsilon per generation. Probability that cumulative error over an indefinite chain stays acceptable rather than being the thing that kills you, and that epsilon-grade trust is enough for the certification to be worth anything at all.

5. The unmodeled escape. Probability that there is a route none of the above captures — something about the structure of the requirement, the nature of self-reference, or an assumption baked into the standard framing (for instance, that parent and successor must be distinct agents with distinct proof systems at all) that makes the obstacle evaporate.

Combine those into your headline number, and then tell me which single term the number is most sensitive to, which term you are least confident in, and — the part I care most about — what one piece of evidence or argument, if you actually saw it, would move the number furthest. Finally, if you think my friend at 0.8 and I are not really disagreeing about the headline but about one of the sub-questions, say which one and why.
