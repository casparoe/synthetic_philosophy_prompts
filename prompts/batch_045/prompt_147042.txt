I was reading a long post on some rationality-adjacent blog — a guy I follow, not a philosopher, but he writes carefully and this is apparently the thing he's best known for — about something he called Kavka's toxin puzzle, which I'd never come across before. I want to know how much of his version to trust. Please don't give me citations or a reading list; I just want to know which bits are right and where it quietly slips.

His setup: a billionaire offers you a million dollars if, at midnight tonight, you form the intention to drink, at noon tomorrow, a glass of stuff that will make you pretty miserable for a day and do no lasting harm. The money is for the intention alone; you never actually have to drink it. His argument that you can't collect: intending to do something requires believing that you'll do it, and you can already see now that once the million is banked your only reason for drinking will have dissapeared and all that'll be left is the day of misery, so you can't now believe you'll drink it, so you can't now intend to.

He then draws two big conclusions. First, that this shows intentions just aren't the sort of thing you can decide to have — you can no more choose an intention than you can choose a belief. Second, that this is a serious problem for decision theory, because decision theory treats agents as choosing whichever intention maximises expected utility, which he says is the same reason it can't handle Newcomb's problem.

Then he gives what he calls the obvious workaround: get at the intention sideways. Announce it publicly, or bet someone you'll go through with it, or resolve to become the kind of person who does what they said, or just drink the stuff anyway out of pure spite once you've been paid. He says any of these disolves the puzzle, and that people who keep treating it as deep are only refusing to notice that the setup quietly assumes your future self has no reason left to act.

There's also a footnote where he says you can run exactly the same puzzle with believing instead of intending — billionaire pays you for believing at midnight that you'll drink tomorrow — and that this proves the puzzle was never about intention in the first place, only about self-prediction.

My instincts, for whatever they're worth (I'm not a philosopher either, I just found this linked somewhere): the sideways workaround feels like cheating but I can't say why, the footnote struck me as the cleverest bit in the whole thing, and the decision theory stuff I genuinely can't judge. So: where is he right, and where exactly does he slide? Does the sideways reply actually answer the puzzle or does it change the subject? Is the belief version really the same puzzle, or does swapping in belief lose something that matters? And do the two big conclusions survive once whatever's wrong has been fixed?
