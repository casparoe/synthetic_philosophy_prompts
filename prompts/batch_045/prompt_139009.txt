I work on a listings site — apartments, mostly — and my team built a classifier that labels users as satisficers or maximizers from their search traces: how many listings they open before they stop, whether the one they take is the best of what they looked at, whether their stopping point moves when we shuffle the order of the same set. It mostly agrees with our gut: one user opens four listings and takes one we'd score 72 out of 100, another scrolls all forty and takes the 91. First one's a satisficer, obviously.

A collegue on the econ side of the building says the classifier is measuring nothing, and he gave me an argument I can't find the hole in. Written out:

(1) Call a subject an optimizer when there is a utility function over options, fixed before she sees the menu, such that in every menu she could be shown she picks something with maximal value. Call her a satisficer when she inspects options one at a time with an aspiration level and takes the first one that clears it, lowering the bar if she runs out of options.

(2) Take any satisficer with aspiration level α. Define u so that u(x) = 1 if x clears α and 0 otherwise. Then in any menu containing at least one option that clears α, whatever she takes is u-maximal, because everything she would take scores 1 and everything else scores 0. So her choice behavior is optimizing behavior.

(3) If the menu contains nothing that clears α she lowers the bar, and at the moment she chooses there is some effective level α'. So she is maximizing u-sub-α'. Between (2) and (3), every satisficer's choice in every menu is a maximizing choice, full stop.

(4) The translation runs backwards too. Any optimizer is a satisficer with a moving bar: set the aspiration to the best value seen so far and accept only improvements. Search with an adaptive bar just is optimization once you charge for each inspection.

(5) If each description can be recovered from the other, the two hypotheses have the same empirical content, so no datum — search length, order effects, response times, what happens when we drop one more listing at the bottom of the pile — could ever tell them apart. My classifier is reading tea leaves.

(6) And it isn't special to satisficing. The same move works for any stopping or choice rule you like: take-the-best, tallying, elimination by aspects, pick the first one with a photo, even a rule that ignores the options entirely. For each there is some pairing of an objective with an environment that it is optimal for, so no rule is ever better or worse suited to a situation than any other — only better or worse relative to an objective somebody hands you afterwards.

(7) Push one step further and a rock rolling downhill is an optimizer, since it minimizes potential energy. So she is optimizing can never be a discovery about anybody.

(8) Therefore the distinction I have been building a product on is empty.

I can't accept (8), and honestly it's (6) and (7) that make me suspicious — if an argument hands me those for free I assume it is cheating somewhere in (2) through (5). I just can't say where. My guess is something goes wrong with fixed before she sees the menu and with the fact that α' in (3) gets set after she has seen what is in the menu, but he says that is a detail he can patch: let the utility function take the whole menu as an argument, or take the option's rank within the menu, and the translation goes through again.

One more thing that bugs me. Even in the easy case, the u I built doesn't say which of the listings clearing α she will take, only that she will take one of them — and which one depends on the order we show them in, which is the main signal my classifier runs on. He says add a tie-breaking term to u. I can't tell whether that helps him or hurts him.

If I have to give something up I would rather give up (8) than give up that my classifier tracks something real, but I want to know the price. So: which step do you reject, why does it look fine when you first read it, and what does rejecting it cost me? Specifically — if the fix is to make the utility function menu-dependent, is that still optimizing in any sense that could ever be tested, or have I just renamed satisficing? Does it matter that the optimizing description fits the choices but not the search path? And is there anything I could actually go measure on the site — a pattern in the traces, a manipulation of the ordering — that would tell me which description of a given user is the true one, or is that question itself badly formed?
