I should say first that I have no academic background in philosophy. I left school at seventeen, I have spent twenty-two years supervising the night shift at a printing plant, and about four years ago I read Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments on my own, after hearing the phrase the impartial spectator in a radio discussion. I have now read it three times. I am writing because I have become convinced it contains exactly the piece I am missing in some volunteer work I do, and because I cannot get from the book to anything I could hand to another person.

The volunteer work: I am one of six appeal reviewers on a large volunteer-run discussion forum for a hobby, roughly forty thousand members and about sixty moderators. When a member is sanctioned they may appeal. Two reviewers read each appeal; if they disagree it goes to a four-person panel, whose decision is final unless the board reverses it for procedural error. The only materials are written ones: the member's appeal, the moderator's note, quoted excerpts of the posts in question, and sometimes message logs. Our options are to uphold the sanction, reduce it, overturn it, or uphold it with a note on the member's record; we cannot make a sanction heavier. I personally read about thirty appeals a year, and I have twenty-five minutes budgeted per case. My fellow reviewers are volunteers with no training and no interest in moral philosophy, and I cannot ask them to read anything.

Two things I have worked out on my own, which you should take as given and build on rather than argue with.

First, what I call the staged-spectator problem. Smith's machinery seems to assume that the spectator is handed the situation and then imagines his way into it. In an appeal, both sides are selecting, ordering, and phrasing the material that the spectator is invited to imagine, and they are doing it under strong incentives. Length of membership, standing in the community, a hard-luck story, a well-turned apology, a clever turn of phrase, a photograph: none of these bear on whether the member's reaction was proportionate to what provoked it, but all of them change what I find myself able to sympathize with, and I have caught myself deciding cases on exactly that basis. I do not want a general caution to be careful. I want a step in the procedure that is aimed at this, with something to do.

Second, a confusion I keep watching us fall into. Smith, as I read him, separates the question whether a sentiment is proper — proportionate to its object, such that a well-informed spectator could go along with it — from the question whether the agent deserves resentment or gratitude for acting on it. Our panel runs these together constantly: we decide the member's anger was understandable, and three sentences later we have concluded that no sanction was warranted at all. I want the procedure to keep the two findings apart, and to say which of our four dispositions each finding supports.

Two further constraints. The spectator in Smith is meant to know all the circumstances and yet not share the agent's actual passion; tell me how to build that into something a tired volunteer can do in twenty-five minutes without asking them to perform a psychological miracle. And I have noticed our panel being pulled toward whichever party has more standing in the community, which I take to be close to what Smith says about the corruption of our sentiments by admiration of rank; the procedure needs to catch that in us, not only in the parties.

What I want from you is a written procedure, not an essay. It must be in plain language, arranged as numbered steps in the order they are run, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each step and with an explicit stop-with-a-no where the answer is not sufficient. After each step give me, in parentheses or in a bracket, one or two sentences naming which part of Smith's account that step is meant to carry, so that I can judge whether the translation is faithful rather than merely plausible. Please do not give me quotations or citations: I cannot check them and I do not want them. Argue from the idea itself. Where the procedure depends on a fact about the forum that I have not told you, name the assumption instead of guessing silently.

Please structure your reply in these sections, with roughly these lengths:

1. What the procedure is for — about 100 words.
2. The procedure — about 700 words: the numbered steps in plain language, each with its sufficient answer, its stopping point, and the bracketed note on which part of the account it carries.
3. The staged-spectator test — about 200 words: the step from section 2 that is aimed at my first worry, set out in enough detail to actually run.
4. Three appeals — about 650 words, roughly 200 each. Run the procedure on each of the three cases below and end each with the disposition it returns and one sentence on why it lands there.
5. What had to be dropped — about 200 words: which of Smith's distinctions you flattened or threw out to make this usable, and what we lose by that.
6. Where it hands off, and what it cannot do — about 200 words: the points at which the question is one of values and must go to the board or to a person, and the respects in which the procedure will misfire no matter how carefully it is run.

The three cases.

Case one. A member of eleven years' standing, well liked, several thousand posts, wrote a withering reply to a newcomer's first project thread — technically about the work, but phrased so that several people winced. The newcomer has not posted since. The appeal says the member's mother was in hospital that week, that he did not intend it as an attack, and that he has since apologised privately and been told the apology was accepted. He has attached the apology. My instinct is to overturn with a note. I want to know whether yours is different and why.

Case two. Two long-standing members give flatly contradictory accounts of a private message exchange in which one of them is said to have threatened the other. The logs exist but are partial: the platform retains them for ninety days and this appeal is five months old, so we have one side's screenshots and the other side's denial that the screenshots are complete. Both appeals are well written. Neither party has obvious standing over the other. I do not know what the procedure should do here and I suspect the honest answer is that it should stop.

Case three. A member posted, in a general thread and at length, a denunciation of another member for a genuinely offensive remark the other had made in a different thread the day before. The denunciation was accurate, was not obscene, and named the offender, who had already been warned and was in fact banned the following day. Our own rules forbid personal attacks in general threads, and the member was suspended for a week. I argued against that suspension and lost. I still think the panel was wrong, and I think the person did something the community needed. If your procedure upholds that suspension I want it to say so plainly and to tell me whether I am wrong, and if I am wrong, which of my two confusions I am committing.

One last thing. In at least one of the three cases the procedure should return an answer I will not like. Do not soften it, and do not add a paragraph explaining that I might reasonably disagree.
