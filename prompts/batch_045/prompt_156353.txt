Posting this because I keep getting the same question in Q&A after talks and never have a good answer. I'm a philosophy PhD student, dissertation on artifacts and authorship, and I've been leaning on forgery cases as examples for years. Here are my gut verdicts, which I now suspect don't add up:

1. A 1930s forgery of a Dutch golden age painting — canvas and pigments aged properly, fooled the experts for decades, now exposed. I think it's worth a tiny fraction of the real thing and I'd honestly rather look at a good reproduction of a real one than at it. But I can't point to anything I'm missing when I stand in front of it.
2. A bronze cast pulled in 1985 from the artist's own 1912 mold, sanctioned by the estate, edition of eight, same foundry, never touched or seen by the artist. I'm fine with this one. Not equal to a lifetime cast, but definitely a real work, worth something close to what it sells for.
3. A print made in 2015 from a photographer's own 1971 negative, by her own printer, editioned, estate stamp on the back, not signed by her. This one I'm not fine with. It feels like a document or a relic rather than a work, and I wouldn't pay anything like what I'd pay for a print she signed in 1971.
4. A painting that turned out to be about 70% overpainted by a nineteenth-century restorer. Still the original, still belongs on the wall — but my admiration now feels like it's aimed partly at the wrong person and I can't shake that.
5. A violin attributed to a famous Cremonese maker, then shown to have been made by a pupil in the same shop from the same templates and the same stock of wood; it sounds better than any authenticated example. As an instrument nothing got worse, so the price collapse looks to me like a fact about collectors rather than about the object — except that I do feel a loss, and I'm not sure that's just snobbery.
6. A pianist mimes on stage to a recording of her own earlier performance; the hall is moved. I think what's wrong here is the deception, not the sound: billed in advance as playback of her own 1975 recording, I'd have been fine with it, maybe more moved.
7. A large sculpture the artist never touched — fabricated by a shop to her drawings and specs, signed by her, edition of three. Totally genuine to me, no issue at all; that's just how some work gets made.

My rough rule has been: what you're responding to is the fact that a particular person made a thing at a particular time, not how the thing looks, and how tight that causal connection has to be varies by medium — very tight for paintings, looser for bronzes and music. Which I've started to think is just a fancy way of restating my verdicts, especially since (2) and (3) look structurally the same and I treat them differently.

So: can that principle be repaired into something that actually earns these verdicts, or should I trade it for a better one? State precisely what you'd give me, tell me which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and which one I should drop, and run it on two cases I haven't listed — a cathedral rebuilt after a fire from the original plans with a scattering of the saved original stones, and a digital work whose only original is a file plus a certificate. Also please say what would change your mind: which argument, or which finding about how people actually respond to objects, would make you abandon the principle you hand me, and where your diagnosis is most likely to fail.
