hey — I run a fortnightly reading group, mix of philosophy grad students and ML people, about 14 of us, one 90-minute session, and I want to do something on that cluster of questions around which computations matter morally. Not "are LLMs conscious" — that'll just turn into a shouting match. I want them to feel the *attribution* problem: the bit where you can't just look at a running system and read off how many computations are happening in it, or which of the physical processes in there are the ones that count.

Rough thing I sketched, please fix it or throw it out and build something better: the room is some kind of compute ethics board for a data center, and on the whiteboard there's a single number — how many morally significant subjects are running in this facility right now. It starts at 1. Each round I read out an operational change, everyone votes on whether the number goes up, down, or stays, and writes down the rule they used. At the end we apply the group's own rules consistently across all the rounds and see what the number has become. I also want them to privately guess the order of magnitude of the final count before the reveal, so they feel the gap between their case-by-case rules and their global intuition.

The cases I want to use are the boring engineering ones, because that's where it gets sharp, and I'll define each on its card so nobody needs to know any of it in advance:

- serving the same model to 5000 users in one batched forward pass (same weights, applied to many different inputs at once)
- KV cache reuse: earlier token states get stored and re-read for every later token instead of recomputed
- activation checkpointing: throw away intermediate results during training and recompute them later
- triple modular redundancy: run everything three times on separate hardware and take a majority vote, to catch bit flips
- shadow deployment: a new model version runs on live traffic next to the old one but its outputs are discarded
- speculative decoding: a small draft model guesses ahead, the big model verifies, and most of the draft's work gets thrown away
- the same checkpoint restored on a machine on another continent and left running

I want at least one round where the disagreement becomes visible in the room without anyone having to say anything — bodies, not words. And no lecture up front: nobody should hear words like functionalism, substrate-independence, or type/token until after the reveal. There's a small pot, coffee or chocolate, and I definately want a forfeit tied to it.

Give me: the rules and the actual card text, a timeline with minutes, what I write on the board and when, how the reveal works, and a debrief that maps each round onto the place where the sequence could have been resisted and what you give up by resisting there. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I'm worried about the ML people relitigating whether speculative decoding really works that way, and about someone going "obviously none of this matters, it's just matrix multiplies." And be honest about which part of the problem the game can't actually stage, so I know what I just have to tell them.
