Last night I had a long argument with a friend about finks and masks, and I would like a case built to order to settle it.

Her position was that the conditional analysis of dispositions — roughly, that a thing is disposed to give response M to stimulus S just in case it would give M if S were to occur — is only ever troubled by philosophers' contraptions: a sorcerer who would quietly de-fragilise the vase at the instant it is struck, a demon who intervenes at the moment of testing. Her claim was that no disposition we actually ascribe in the real world fails the conditional test, that finks and masks are artifacts of the examples, and that the analysis therefore needs no repair or at most a footnote. I took the opposite side: that an ordinary piece of engineering, or an ordinary piece of biology, can fail the test just as badly, and can do so in a way that the two standard repairs cannot jointly handle. We have a bet riding on whether the failure is local or structural, so I need a case, not a survey of the literature.

Please construct a scenario meeting all of the following, then show that it meets them.

Constraints on the scenario:

1. Mundane setting, one paragraph, no diagrams. A kitchen appliance, a car, a building's wiring, a hospital device, a patch of ordinary physiology — nothing science-fictional. No sorcerers, demons, omniscient agents, nanobots, or exotic physics. Whatever interferes must be the sort of thing engineers, or natural selection, actually build into a system.
2. Do not stipulate that the disposition is present. The object must earn the ascription independently: from its design or function, from the specification it was built to, or from its behaviour in nearby variations of the case. In particular, include a control variation — the same object, with the stimulus very slightly weaker, very slightly later, or very slightly different in some other single respect — in which M does occur. The control is my reason for thinking the disposition is there rather than lapsed, so it has to be part of the case rather than an aside.
3. In the target situation, the counterfactual if S then M must be false: S occurs, M does not.
4. No fink. The disposition must survive the episode intact; immediately afterwards, and indeed throughout, the object is still the sort of thing that responds to S with M. If your case works only because the stimulus destroys the disposition, it is the wrong case.
5. The preventer must be what I have started calling a constitutive mask: internal to the object, switched on by S itself or by the object's own prior exercise of the very disposition in question, doing exactly the job it was designed or selected to do, and operating under conditions that are themselves perfectly normal. A vase packed in foam, or a machine guarded by a bystander, will not do: there the interferer is extrinsic and idle, and everyone knows how to handle it.
6. The hard clause. The preventer must operate through the disposition's own causal basis: the same bit of mechanism that would produce M is what switches the preventer on. The point of clause 6 is to block the move of holding the basis fixed while S occurs and reading off the counterfactual; I want a case where basis and preventer are tangled together, so that the tangle itself is doing the work.

What I want after the scenario:

- Three verdicts, each with a couple of sentences of reasoning: the simple conditional analysis; the repair that holds the categorical basis fixed, or holds it fixed for a suitable stretch of time after the stimulus; and the repair that restricts the test to normal or ideal conditions. For each, say whether it returns disposed or not disposed, and say whether it agrees with the intuitive verdict and with ordinary ascriptive practice — would an engineer, or a physiologist, still describe the object as having the disposition in that situation?
- Which single feature of the case is carrying the weight. Then remove it: if the preventer were extrinsic, or abnormal, or merely adjacent to the disposition rather than built out of its own basis, what would the case collapse into, and would one of the two repairs then succeed where it previously failed?
- The strongest reply available to someone defending the simple conditional analysis, together with an honest assessment of what that reply costs. I expect the reply to be some version of the disposition simply is not there at that moment, and I want to know whether that reply is ad hoc: does it force the object to acquire and shed the disposition over and over in the course of ordinary operation, or to have it only relative to a phase or a set of background conditions that have to be specified case by case?
- An explicit note on whether anything in the case smuggles in the verdicts rather than generating them from the mechanics, since that is exactly what my friend will look for.

Finish with a single machine-readable line in this form:

\answer{<kind of case, three words or fewer> ; <which analysis, if any, gets the verdict right: simple | basis-fixed | normal-conditions | none>}
