I'm a science journalist working on a feature about what AI companies actually mean when they say a model is "aligned", and I've been sitting in on a bunch of safety briefings over the past few months and I keep running into a split in the practice that I can't get my editor to see as a problem.

Here's what I mean. On the one hand, these labs spend an enormous amount of effort on the training side: preference fine-tuning, persona and character training, synthetic feedback generated from things like "what would a helpful and harmless assistant do here". All of that seems pointless unless what matters is what the model is disposed to do in situations nobody has tested yet — i.e. unless what matters is, in some sense, what the model is trying to do. On the other hand, they spend just as much on input and output classifiers, hard refusal rules, monitoring, red-teaming, eval suites that only ever look at the tokens coming out, and endless patches for the last jailbreak. All of that seems pointless unless what matters is just the output. And the same people will talk about a model scheming, or concealing what it can do, in one sentence, and then insist in the next one that it's a next-token predictor and there's nobody home.

My editor's line — and its getting under my skin a bit — is the aviation analogy. We don't require that the autopilot want to land the plane safely; we require that it land the plane safely, and we certify it by testing the aircraft, not by interviewing the autopilot about its values. He calls that "output certification", and he calls the training-side stuff "character certification". His question: if output certification is good enough for aviation, why isn't it good enough here, and isn't the whole character-training project a category mistake?

So what I'd like from you:

What does the pipeline as it actually exists, both halves of it, presuppose about what we're asking for when we ask for alignment? Does it commit the labs to there being something like an agent with ends in there whose ends have to match ours, or is all of it compatible with a flat "only the outputs matter" view? Is alignment one problem, or does the pipeline look the way it does because it's quietly solving three or four different ones — working out what we even want, getting it into the thing, telling whether it's actually in there, and keeping it there after capabilities jump? Which parts strictly presuppose which theory, and which parts would several mutually incompatible theories license equally?

Two more things I want straight. First: if a lab gets asked for a version with the refusal layer stripped off for some customer, everyone I've talked to treats that as an obviously safety-relevant change — but on the pure "it's a next-token predictor and only tokens matter" view, what exactly is the difference between shipping that and shipping the normal one? Second: if my editor is right and we went full output certification — no talk of goals, no character training, certify the behaviour the way you certify an aircraft — what would we lose, what would we have to build instead, and is there anything in current practice that suggests that trade was already considered and rejected?

I'm not after a reading list or citations, I want the implicit theory reconstructed, the places where it's internally inconsistent flagged, and the places where the practice is genuinely underdetermined and my editor and I are both just guessing.
