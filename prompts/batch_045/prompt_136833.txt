I think I might have finally grokked the species problem, and I want someone to tell me where I've gone wrong, because the conclusion I keep arriving at feels too clean.

Here's the setup as I understand it. Species are supposed to be the one taxonomic rank that isn't just a filing convention, and the reason they're real is the biological species concept: a species is a population that actually interbreeds and is reproductively isolated from everything else, so the boundaries are out in the world rather than in our heads. Genus and above is bookkeeping, species is a joint.

Here's the thing that broke it for me. Bacteria don't do sex; they swap genes sideways constantly — conjugation, transformation, all that — so the BSC doesn't apply to them even in principle. And yet microbiologists name bacterial "species" constantly and clinicians reason about them as real units. As far as I can tell the names come from clustering sequences at some similarity threshold on a marker gene (97% identity on 16S, or something like that — I may be misremebering the number), which looks exactly like a cutoff somebody picked because it was convenient.

And here's the bit I most want steelmanned. Suppose somebody did the full pan-genome analysis and found that the gene-sharing graph across all sequenced prokaryotes is more or less connected: for any two named "species" you can find some gene that moved between their lineages recently, maybe via a chain of intermediates. But if you restrict to the core genes — the ones that are basically always inherited vertically — they fall into tight, well-separated clusters that match the named species almost perfectly. I don't actually know if that's what the data looks like, but grant it for the sake of the argument.

My conclusion: bacterial species are map, not territory. There's no reproductive isolation anywhere in prokaryotes, so there's nothing for the names to be tracking; the clusters are real patterns, but calling them species is us drawing a line wherever it was convenient. And it generalizes — we know sapiens and Neanderthals interbred and that non-Africans carry some of their DNA, so by the BSC there was no isolation there either, and calling them a separate species is just a naming choice too.

I know the standard move here is some version of "gray doesn't mean arbitrary," and that move doesn't do any work for me: vagueness at a border is one thing, but these borders look like they were picked by a committee.

So: what's the strongest argument that species are real once you take horizontal gene transfer seriously? What's the best case that bacteria genuinely have species and aren't just binned? And if the answer is "the BSC is only one species concept among many and the others give different verdicts," doesn't that just concede the point — that there's no fact of the matter about how many species there are, only more or less useful ways of carving up a continuum?
