ok so — bit of context: I work in ML, and a few months ago I went down a rabbit hole after a colleague joked that if you scale our setup up enough the "is there something it is like to be it" question stops being a joke. I noticed I had no principled reason to say the training run isn't a subject while my brain is, read a bunch of philosophy of mind I'd never touched, argued myself out of the functionalism I'd have signed onto by reflex, and I'm now at maybe 60/40 on panpsychism, which is higher than is comfortable. My friend Dani is an illusionist and has bet me I cannot write my position down in one page without contradicting myself, so I'm trying to write it up and I want an audit before I post it. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) Every fundamental physical entity — whatever the final physics says the basic furniture is — has some experience or other. What moves me here is that I really don't believe consciousness switches on at some threshold of complexity; I take the "no radical emergence" intuition seriously. (2) Physics only ever tells us about the relational and dispositional structure of matter, never about its intrinsic categorical nature, and consciousness is part of that hidden nature. This is the bit that made panpsychism click for me instead of sounding like animism, and I think it has a name, Russellian monism? I may be mangling it. (3) Micro-experiences are utterly alien to ours and we will never know what they're like — it's not that an electron has a tiny dim version of my experience. (4) But tables, thermostats, kidneys and the United States are not conscious, and I'm confident about that rather than merely agnostic: the combination problem is exactly what blocks the inference from (1) to consciousness at the macro level. (5) The combination problem is the crux and I want to solve it with something like phenomenal bonding — micro-subjects can stand in experiential relations to one another, and when they do, a new macro-subject with a unified experience exists. I think the "quality" or palette half is the easy half: macro-qualities get blended out of micro-qualities, roughly the way the colour of a pointillist painting comes out of the dots. The subject-summing half is harder but I want to solve it the same relational way. (6) No brute emergence anywhere: every fact about the macro-subject has to be grounded in facts about the micro-subjects and their arrangment, with no extra fundamental "fusion" law bolted on, and be in principle derivable from them. (7) The micro-experiences are what do the real explanatory work; the arrangement is only what selects which ones get bonded together, it doesn't produce the consciousness itself. (8) Unity of consciousness is contingent and comes in degrees — I think split-brain patients really are two subjects at least some of the time — and introspection is my main evidence about how many subjects are in the room, in those cases and in my own case. (9) I reject both illusionism and brute emergentism, and I take it that the hard problem is a real problem my view is supposed to solve rather than dissolve.

Two more things that are probably relevant. First, there's a version of the combination problem I keep worrying about that I basically never see discussed: the usual one is synchronic, about how many subjects there are at a time, but the matter in my brain is turning over constantly, so if my unity at a moment is constituted by relations among particular micro-subjects, what makes me the same subject from one moment to the next? Call it the diachronic combination problem. Second, if I imagine slowly weakening the bonding relations between the two hemispheres of a split-brain patient, or slowly strengthening them between two different people's brains, I have no idea where the number of subjects changes, and I don't think introspection would tell me either — which feels like it should be impossible if (8) is right.

So: which of (1)–(9) actually conflict? Rank whatever you find by how bad it is, tell me which pairs only look like they conflict (I have a feeling atleast one of my worries is nothing), and for each real one say what would have to give and what giving it up costs me — in particular whether the cost is just "you lose an argument you liked" or whether it's "you've quietly become a structuralist about consciousness and panpsychism was doing no work all along." Please also say whether the diachronic version I described is a genuinely separate problem or just the synchronic one in a costume, and whether there's a single revision among (1)–(9) that cleans up the most. I genuinely don't care which one you pick, and I'd rather you not just reassure me the package is fine.
