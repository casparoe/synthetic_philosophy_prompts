Posting this here because I keep going in circles with a friend from a formal epistemology reading group I tagged along to — she's the technical one, I'm mostly there for the wine.

As I understand the "imprecise probability" / set-of-priors idea: instead of representing your uncertainty with a single probability function you use a set of them, so instead of a sharp number you get something like an interval — P(green) somewhere between 0.2 and 0.5 rather than P(green) = 0.35. My objection, which seems to me obvious but which nobody has managed to shoot down to my satisfaction, is that this is just ordinary Bayesianism with extra steps. Take the set, put some weighting on the functions in it — uniform over the extreme points if you've got no better idea — average them, and out pops a single perfectly ordinary additive prior. Everything the interval was telling you, you can read off the average, and then you conditionalize and maximize expected utility like a normal person. Refusing to actually pick numbers starts to look like superstition about precision, or like you want to keep your options open so you can never be wrong.

Here's the sort of case I keep testing it on: a machine is going to paint a ball green or blue. It was built either by factory A, whose machines paint green 40% of the time, or by factory B, whose paint green 80% of the time, and I have no clue which. The 50/50 average gives P(green) = 0.6, so I should pay up to $0.60 for a ticket that pays $1 if the ball is green. The interval [0.4, 0.8] seems to be saying... roughly the same thing? Both approaches tell me to take the bet at $0.50 and to turn it down at $0.90. So where does any of this actually bite? Her replies so far haven't landed: she says the weighting is arbitrary and nothing in my evidence picks one out, that your interval can get *wider* when you learn things (which sounds to me like she's just recomputing the average wrong), and something about paramaterizations that I didn't follow at all.

So: what, if anything, is genuinely lost in collapsing the set to its average? I'd really like a case with numbers I can check by hand where the two approaches disagree about what I should believe or what I should do, not just about how tidy the notation is. And I'd like to know whether whatever answer you give is only about describing what people actually do — is this connected to that Ellsberg urn experiment where people supposedly behave weirdly? — or whether it's a claim about what it's rational to believe and do, regardless of what anyone does.
