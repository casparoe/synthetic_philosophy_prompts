I'm helping put together a casual interview series on forgotten angles in AI history, and our next guest is an invented philosopher—let's call her Professor L—who defends a view near to Turing's own: that the way to machine intelligence is not to hand-code an adult mind but to construct a minimal child machine and then educate it, using rewards and punishments, so that the education process does most of the work, in rough analagy to evolution plus learning. I need eight to ten interview questions for her that don't just let her restate that view.

Here's the spin I want, so we don't end up with another yawn-worthy chat about the Turing test: suppose we actually build such a child machine now, but the only thing available to educate it is a narrow curiculum of short logic puzzles, and the experimenter's rewards are the only feedback. Professor L has to say how much of the resulting intellectual character comes from the original hereditary material versus the education process, and she tends to reply that the education process is what really matters, not the initial programming. I want questions that make that reply harder to just assert. At least one question should tackle each of these: (a) whether the analogy to evolution commits her to saying the experimenter is doing something like artificial selection rather than teaching, (b) what happens if the machine's built-in structure is so minimal that it can't even represent the reward signal at the start, (c) the objection that the whole proposal just pushes the hard problem back to designing the education process, and (d) whether the child machine would have to be conscious or have something like feelings for the reward-and-punishment setup to do any real work. For each of the four, give the most likely reply from a defender of this Turing-inspired view and then a follow-up that responds to that reply. Also add one closing question about what would count as evidence that the education process had failed, not just that the machine was stupid. Keep the questions chatty, like for a conversational podcast, not a journal colloquium, and it's fine if a couple of them ramble a little.
