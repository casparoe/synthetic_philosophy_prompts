I'm in a small upper-level seminar on the philosophy of punishment — we've done retributivism, deterrence, and a week on expressive and communicative theories — and I keep getting stuck on the gap between the clean theories we read and the mess of the actual capital-sentencing scheme in the state I'm from. So I'd like to reconstruct the implicit theory of a real practice rather than of an argument. I've written out the features carefully so you don't have to know anything about the jurisdiction, and I don't want citations or reading suggestions; I want the argument.

The scheme, as it actually operates: the statute's purpose section is written in purely backward-looking terms, reserving death for the worst offenders on the basis of what they deserve, and it expressly says the legislature does not rely on deterrence because the evidence for it is inconclusive. At the penalty phase the jury must find at least one aggravating circumstance beyond a reasonable doubt, then weigh any mitigating evidence, for which there is no burden and no standard, and may sentence to death only if the aggravating circumstances outweigh the mitigating ones. Whether the defendant is likely to be violent in the future is itself a statutory aggravator, and the state may call a psychiatrist to say so. The alternative sentence is life without parole, which the state has and uses constantly. Once the direct appeal is over, new evidence bearing on guilt is cognizable only if it also shows a constitutional violation at trial; a showing of factual innocence is not enough on its own, however convincing. The clemency board, separately, does recommend commutation whenever it has substantial doubt about guilt, and has done so repeatedly. Exonerees are paid under the same compensation schedule as anyone else injured by the state, at a flat rate per year of imprisonment. And in practice the state has not executed anyone who resisted in over fifteen years; the people it has executed all waived their appeals and asked to be put to death, while everyone else sits on the row for decades and either dies there, is resentenced, or is quietly commuted. Roughly one in twenty-five of the sentences imposed under the scheme has been vacated for serious error, and several of those defendants turned out to be innocent.

What I can't work out is whether this is one coherent view of punishment or several stitched together. So: reconstruct the theory, or the smallest set of theories, that the practice as a whole presupposes, and be explicit about which features only presuppose it weakly — which ones several rival theories would equally license — and which features quietly rule a rival out. Does the refusal to rely on deterrence actually rule out a deterrent justification, or does the future-dangerousness aggravator reintroduce a forward-looking rationale under another name? Does compensating exonerees at the ordinary state rate presuppose that wrongful executions are an accepted and priced cost, and if so is that compatible with the backward-looking desert framework the statute announces? Does letting volunteers hasten their own executions while others wait twenty years presuppose that the condemned person has some say over the timing, which a purely desert-based or purely deterrent view seems to have no room for? Does a decade-plus delay under an avowedly retributive stated purpose presuppose that retribution is really about the condemning judgment rather than about how much the offender suffers? And finally: if the state replaced its announced retributivism with a straightforward expected-deterrence view, or if instead it made never punishing an innocent person an absolute side-constraint rather than one consideration among others, what exactly would have to change — the standard of proof at trial, the treatment of residual doubt, the role of future dangerousness, the length of the delay, or the existence of the sanction? Please keep the whole answer under 700 words; I'd rather have 500 dense ones than a survey.
