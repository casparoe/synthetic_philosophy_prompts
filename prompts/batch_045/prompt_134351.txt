I am a clinical ethics fellow, and in three weeks I am presenting to our hospital's Death Determination Policy Committee, which is reviewing whether our brain-death protocol needs revision. The room is not a philosophy seminar, and I need to be ready for the question period rather than for the literature.

The thesis I am defending: whole-brain death is not the death of the human organism. The neurological criterion is defensible as a legal and administrative convention, but it is not a discovery of a biological boundary, and once we see that, the honest policy move is to stop using one declaration to do two different jobs. On one side: whether a patient may be declared dead, with the usual permissions that follow (withdrawal of support, the death certificate, the family's leave-taking). On the other side: whether organs may be procured from someone who is not yet dead but whose death is imminent, certain, and will not be something they experience. I call this unbundling the declaration, and I will argue that the dead donor rule is best replaced, not defended.

My argument has three steps, plus a fallback. (1) The circulatory and neurological criteria agree on almost every patient who dies outside an ICU, and they come apart only in cases that our own technology engineers: patients on extracorporeal support whose brains have stopped functioning but whose circulation is maintained by a machine, and controlled donation after circulatory determination of death, where support is withdrawn in an operating theatre and death is declared minutes later. A criterion that fails only where our interventions make it fail looks like a convention that answers to our practices, not a joint in nature. (2) I will propose an integration test: the organism is dead when it has irreversibly lost the capacity, by its own workings, to maintain its internal environment against disintegration. Whole-brain death does not reliably track that. There are reported cases of long-term somatic survival after a brain-death determination, including patients who have maintained temperature and fluid balance through preserved hypothalamic and pituitary function, healed wounds, fought infection, grown, and in one kind of case carried a pregnancy to viability, none of which is behaviour we attribute to a dead organism. Equally, circulatory arrest does not track it: cells and tissues retain their capacities for hours afterwards, and some of them are the ones we transplant. (3) So both criteria are proxies for a property that admits of degree and that no clock time captures, and where a proxy is doing the work, we should license permissions separately at the thresholds appropriate to them rather than pretend to have located a moment. Fallback, if the committee refuses unbundling: keep both criteria exactly as they are, and move the ethics into the consent architecture instead, so that procurement is authorized by the patient's prior registration plus a review that the death is imminent and certain, and no one has to say something they do not believe in order to make a donation possible.

The audience, and what each of them cares about. The chair is a paediatric intensivist, clinically conservative, who thinks this whole debate is academic and that any public hint that brain death is contested will cost us donations. Two neurointensivists are committed to the standard clinical test battery; they will say the burden of proof is on me, that the long-term-survival cases are anecdotes and selected reporting, and that a policy cannot be built on them. A transplant surgeon and our organ procurement organization's liaison are both committed to the dead donor rule and to not handing ammunition to critics of transplantation; they will hear my view as a threat to the organ supply and to public trust, and the liaison will also press me on a specific practice our region is moving toward, in which oxygenated blood is pumped back into the abdominal organs after death has been declared by circulatory criteria, with the vessels to the head clamped so the brain is not reperfused. A palliative care physician and a senior nurse are broadly sympathetic to the idea that dying is a process and suspicious of the technological-rescue framing; they will ask what any of this sounds like at the bedside and whether I am asking families to consent to something described in words they would not use. Our risk-management counsel will ask only two things: whether the policy still tracks our state's statutory language, and whether a physician can chart a time of death under what I am proposing. A community member who sits on the committee, a minister, will ask about the moral meaning of death and whether we would be killing one person to save another.

What I want from you is a prep sheet for the question period, not a defence of the thesis. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get, roughly in the order they will arrive, each phrased the way the person who raises it would phrase it rather than in my vocabulary, each with a spoken answer I could deliver in about two minutes. For each objection, note how much damage it does if I handle it badly, and where my reply is soft. Then give me four things separately: what I should concede in the first two minutes, before anyone attacks, so that the room stops bracing; the one reply that has to land if the proposal is to survive the meeting at all; the question I am hoping nobody asks; and which of my three steps I should drop first if the room turns hostile, on the assumption that I cannot save all of them.

Two constraints on how you answer. First, no citations, reading lists, or references to papers: I cannot follow them up and the committee does not want them. Second, be explicit about the epistemic status of what you are telling me. For each reply, say whether it rests on something settled, on something genuinely contested among people who work on this, on an empirical or clinical claim that I should verify with our intensivists before repeating it, or on a legal claim I should put to our counsel rather than assert. Do not present my view as anything other than a minority position among clinicians and a live, unsettled position among philosophers, and do not present the contrary view as refuted. Where you would need a number, a frequency, or a statute to make a reply work, say so instead of supplying one. If you think one of my three steps is simply mistaken, tell me which and tell me what objection it will generate in that room, rather than quietly repairing it for me.
