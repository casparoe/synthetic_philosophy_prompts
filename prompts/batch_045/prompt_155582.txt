I'm on the capital projects oversight committee for a mid-sized city — me, the city engineer, somebody from finance, two council appointees, and a rotating seat for whichever department's project is on the agenda that month. We see maybe eight to twelve projects a quarter, anywhere from a few hundred thousand to tens of millions, mostly physical construction and mostly software replacements that have gone sideways. Our power is only recommendatory: we write continue / restructure / stop and send it up to council, and council follows us something like nine times out of ten. Two constraints shape everything: we get about thirty or forty minutes per project, and the recommendation has to fit on one page and be readable by a council member who is hearing about the project for the first time and mostly wants to know why the city spent four million dollars and has nothing to show for it.

I got into an argument with a colleague last week about the dollar auction — you know the one, a dollar gets auctioned but the runner-up also has to pay their final bid, so two people who are each trying to cut their losses end up bidding well past a dollar and both lose. She said that's us, and that it describes every project we've ever let run. I said it isn't, because half the time there's nobody on the other side: we're not bidding against anyone, we're just handing money to a vendor who keeps saying the last twenty percent is right around the corner. We ended up agreeing that some of our projects really do have that structure and some don't, and that the difference matters more than the standard advice does — everything I've read about escalation of commitment and sunk costs bottoms out in "ignore sunk costs," which is true and completely useless to us, because by the time something reaches our desk the spend is gone, and the live question is whether the *remaining* spend is worth it, and the case for that is very often built out of the money that's already gone.

So I want a procedure, not an essay, and I want it to start with a structure check rather than with "do the future benefits exceed the future costs" (that question always gets answered yes by whoever wants the project). Concretely, what I need: a way to sort a project into something like (a) real rivalry — some other party's money, deadline, or behaviour is part of why continuing looks good; (b) no rival, just a contractor and a slipping schedule; (c) cases where the money already spent genuinely raises the value of finishing, because the permit is grandfathered or the foundation is poured or the data's already migrated and there's no rollback path — plus an actual test that tells (c) apart from the cases where it only feels like that, since "we're too far in to stop now" is the line we hear most and sometimes it's true. Then: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a "stop," and where it hands the decision to a person because it's a values call and not a factual one. I'd like at least two tests that catch escalation specifically rather than just asking whether the project is any good — something like a restart test (would we fund this today as a brand-new proposal from a different department, same remaining cost, same expected benefit) and something that separates the person who authorized the original spend from the person who writes the recommendation — but please say honestly where each test misfires, because the restart test looks to me like it breaks exactly when there is a real completion premium, and I want to know what to do in that case. I also need the exit options spelled out, since "stop" isn't one thing: kill, mothball, descope to something usable, hand it to another body, re-phase — each with different salvage and different politics, and the auction's bid-or-quit-and-lose-everything menu isn't ours. And there has to be a slot for the awkward version of the argument, that finishing is sometimes worth it because a track record of finishing (or of making a contractor eat a change order) has value for every future project we touch: I think that's occasionally real and I also think it's the easiest thing in the world for a project sponsor to abuse, so I want it in the procedure and flagged.

Then run it on three cases. First, a four-year-old software system replacement at roughly twice its original budget, vendor says it's seventy percent there and needs one more push. Second, a downtown streetscape where a state grant covers sixty percent but only if we finish by a date we now can't hit without more city money, and the state has already changed the rules on us once. Third, a small neighborhood library branch in a district that's been promised one for a decade, thirty percent built, not much left in the budget, and the council member who chairs our committee represents that district — I want the third one to be the case where the procedure says stop and tells me something I won't like, and I want it said plainly instead of hedged. If there's a fourth shape of case the procedure genuinely can't settle, describe that case rather than forcing it through. Please don't give me a catalogue of cognitive biases and don't cite anything; I'll add references myself if I ever write this up, and it has to be runnable in a meeting by people with no economics background who recieve the packet the night before.
