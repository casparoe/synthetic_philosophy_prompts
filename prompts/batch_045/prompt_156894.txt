I'm in a decision-theory reading group this term and last week we spent the whole session on the dollar auction — auctioneer sells a dollar, top bidder pays their bid and gets the dollar, runner-up pays their last bid too and gets nothing, bids go up in tiny increments, no round limit — and two people in the group gave accounts that I dont think can both be right.

One of them says it's just the sunk cost fallacy wearing a game-theory costume: with backward induction nobody should ever make the first bid, and the fact that real people do, and then keep topping up well past a dollar, shows they're tracking money they can't get back and can't see where the sequence ends. The other says that's too quick: in the real game you don't know the other bidder's stopping point, and since your own last bid is gone either way, continuing can be the expected-payoff-maximising move given a sane belief about when the other person folds — it's basically a war of attrition, and on that reading the escalation is rational behaviour under incomplete information, and the only genuinely puzzling thing left is why anyone bids at all given that a one-cent opening bid looks like free money.

Write me an essay that adjudicates this and lands somewhere: can both stories be true at different levels, and if so which one is doing the explanatory work? Along the way I want you to deal with three things I can't get straight. First, my own pet diagnostic: run the auction but have the subject's opening bids placed for them, either randomly assigned by the experimenter or made by a delegate they're allowed to fire at any time, so that nothing they've "invested" is theirs in the sense of having been chosen. If escalation survives that, the responsibility/self-commitment version of the story is in trouble; if it collapses, then whatever is going on isn't really about money spent but about having made the call yourself — and I'd like you to say whether "I started this, so I should finish it" is ever definately a real reason and not just a feeling. Second, whether the dollar auction is the same animal as escalation of commitment in the organisational literature, the classic cases where people throw good money after bad into a project they personally launched, or whether those are two different mechanisms that only look alike. Third, whether it changes anything if the auctioneer isn't a predatory experimenter but a charity the bidders actually like, so the money paid in doesn't go to an adversary.

End on a definite verdict rather than a both-sides summary — a conditional one like "structural up to here, psychological beyond it" is fine, "it depends what you mean by rational" is not. One piece of continuous prose, no headings or bullet points, under 800 words.
