Putting together a short "where I've landed" post for the small Stoicism group I help moderate, and before I inflict it on people I wanted a consistency check from someone who won't just quote the Enchiridion back at me. Here's the set, roughly:

(1) The dichotomy of control, more or less as Epictetus states it. What's up to me is my judgments, my assent, my impulses. My body, reputation, career, other people, and every outcome are not.

(2) But I've also become convinced — from reasons-responsiveness stuff and just from having lived a bit — that "up to me" is scalar and is dependant on scaffolding I don't control: sleep, pain, money, whether my partner is currently being kind, whether my meds are working. Strip the scaffolding and I have *less* control, not merely a harder time exercising the same amount.

(3) I believe none of the physics. No providential logos, no cosmic animal, no "everything that happens is for the best." I think the ethics runs fine as therapy without it.

(4) I do want to keep one metaphysical-sounding claim, and I think it's load-bearing rather than decorative: the only thing unqualifiedly good or bad is character, and everything else is just material, valuable only as the stuff your response works on.

(5) I think the undisturbed sage is a real norm and not just a coping ideal — the person unmoved by exile, poverty, or the death of a child is getting something right. I also think grief at your child's death is fitting and that someone who felt nothing would be defective.

(6) I recomend premeditatio malorum to almost everyone and I've watched it work. I also think the pop version — "you can't control getting fired, only how you respond" — is false in a way that matters, because for plenty of people the response is exactly the thing that's been wrecked.

(7) I think the critique of the modern revival is basically fair: Stoicism has become a grit-and-productivity tool for people who already have power, and its inward turn privatizes things that ought to be handled collectively. I also think the remedy is *more* Stoicism aimed at your own status hunger, not less.

(8) I think anger is always a mistake, since anger requires judging yourself harmed by something outside your prohairesis. I also think public blame, refusing to forgive, and withholding trust are often the right response to being wronged, and I distrust the Stoic habit of filing all of that under "disturbance."

Two things I've been chewing on that may or may not be relevant. I've started splitting "up to me" three ways: (i) I get it if I will it, (ii) it traces to my reasoning rather than bypassing it, (iii) my choice shifts the probability. I suspect only (ii) is the Stoic sense and (i) is what self-help readers hear, but I can't tell whether the split actually buys anything. And I keep coming back to a friend whose equanimity is, as far as I can tell, partly manufactured by a beta blocker and a very stable marriage — is that calm less hers? Something in the package feels like it's doing more work than it can bear, but I can't localize it.

What I want: go through the pairs and tell me which are genuinely in tension and how badly, which only look like they clash, and for each real one what the cheapest fix would be and what it'd cost me. Rank by severity rather than giving me six equal-sized problems. Then say which single revision would clean up the most, and flag anything here that's doing unacknowledged work for the rest of the set. If my three-way distinction or the beta-blocker case is a red herring, say so and move on.
