You're a formal epistemologist who referees for philosophy of science journals, so please think this through carefully instead of just listing the textbook moves.

I'm writing a short paper on the Bayesian problem of old evidence and I keep going in circles. Standard setup: you already know E, so P(E) = 1, so conditioning on E can't move the probability of your new theory H, yet everyone agrees the theory really does get confirmed by facts that were already on the books. Glymour made the problem famous in his 1980 book; I don't need the history, and please don't give me a bibliography or any citations, I just want your own analysis.

Here's the twist I want to build the paper around. I think people treat "old evidence" as one thing when there are actually two very different cases, and I've been calling them constructive and archival old evidence:

- Archival: the data was published and sitting in a database long before H was formulated, but nobody who built H actually looked at it. Say an ice-core isotope record from 1987 that a climatologist's new model turns out to predict beatifully.
- Constructive: the very same data was open on the desk while H was being built, and H's free parameters were partly tuned so that it would fit that data.

My worry is that the standard fixes are quietly designed for the archival case, and that they all overpay in the constructive case — they hand the theory credit for fitting a target it was aimed at. And then there's a third case that I think seperates them: a precision upgrade, where the old, sloppy measurement of E gets re-done years later with much tighter error bars, and the tight version fits H better than the loose version ever could.

So: please rank the following six ways of handling old evidence, by how well you think they survive all three cases at once.

1. Garber-style move: don't update on E at all, update on the newly learned structural fact that H entails E (or that H fits E to within a stated tolerance).
2. Forget-and-relearn: reconstruct the credence function your earlier self would have had if she hadn't known E, and let the confirmation be measured against that counterfactual prior.
3. Ur-priors: fix one counterfactual prior once and for all, from a standpoint before any of the relevant evidence was in, and use it forever after as the baseline for every theory.
4. Glymour-style bootstrapping: give up on theory-neutral confirmation and let evidence confirm parts of a theory only relative to the rest of that theory plus auxiliaries.
5. Jeffrey-style generalized updating: treat the arrival of E not as learning a proposition with certainty but as a shift in your probabilities across a partition, and measure confirmation by the distance between the old and new distributions.
6. My own candidate, which I'm suspicious of: held-out confirmation — split the old evidence into the part that was used as a design constraint and the part that wasn't, and let only the unused part confirm, with the used part treated as a fixed cost rather than a gain.
