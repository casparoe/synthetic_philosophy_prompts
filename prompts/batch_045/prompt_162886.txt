I'm a science writer doing a long piece on what a psychiatric diagnosis actually is, and a philosopher of psychiatry I interviewed walked me into the following chain and I cant find the exit. He laid it out as numbered steps over coffee and I've been chewing on it recieved since.

(1) A kind is a natural kind only if what falls under it, and what is true of the things that fall under it, is what it is independently of our classifying things as falling under it — gold would still be gold and still behave like gold if nobody had the concept. (2) Whenever a category is applied to things that can learn about the category and respond to it — reorganise their self-conception and their memories, change what they report and how they behave, and change how clinicians, teachers, insurers and family members respond to them — then both who falls under the category and what is true of the people inside it are made partly by the classification itself. He kept calling this the looping effect, and gestured at Hacking. (3) Psychiatric categories are categories of exactly such things, and they visibly loop: the label reshapes the self-narrative, patients start presenting in the idiom the criteria describe, the criteria get revised partly because of that, prevalence moves when the threshold moves. (4) So no psychiatric category is a natural kind. Up to here I'm happy — this is basically the thesis of my article. (5) But (2) isn't specific to the mental; all it requires is that the classified things respond to being classified, and people respond everywhere in medicine. Tell someone they're hypertensive or prediabetic and their diet, their pill-taking, the symptoms they mention and even the number on the cuff change; thresholds get redrawn because of the data that then comes back; a screening programme can manufacture the condition it screens for. (6) And you can't escape by retreating to mechanism — saying the real disease is the biology rather than the behaviour or the self-report — because the biology loops too: labelling and treating people changes stress axes, sleep, medication regimes, plasticity. (7) So no diagnostic category anywhere in medicine is a natural kind either. (8) And if a category isn't a natural kind, there's nothing for a diagnosis to be correct or incorrect about: a positive result, a cut-off, a prevalence estimate can only be more or less in line with current convention. There's no such thing as a false positive, only a currently unfashionable one, and no sense in which an earlier generation simply got a threshold wrong. (9) That last bit can't be right, and not just because it sounds insane in print: medicine is full of cases where the old classification was just wrong, where a test was picking up noise, where a disease turned out to have a cause nobody had guessed. And if (8) were true my own piece would collapse, because there'd be no daylight between saying psychiatric diagnoses are shaped by the people who receive them (interesting, probably true) and saying that about strep throat (a much bigger claim, and I suspect false).

I want to keep (4) and I want to keep (9); that's the article. So which step do I give up, why did it look innocent sitting there in the list, and what does giving it up commit me to in public? If I'm sliding between two things that look alike — the category and the individuals in it, being shaped by classification and being constituted by it, not being a natural kind and not being real — name the distinction for me precisely, because that distinction is my nut graf.
