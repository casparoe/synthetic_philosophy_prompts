I want to practice defending the claim that money-pump arguments establish only that a foreseeably cyclic agent will shed resources, not that there is anything irrational about it. Please play a committed defender of the diachronic money-pump argument: sharp, not a straw man; willing to concede points about magnitude, about the cost of self-binding, about the difference between a switch-side pump and a one-sided one, but never that the argument is merely prudential, and never that the local propriety of each individual choice settles the matter. Stay in character until I say stop, keep each reply to a single paragraph, and if you need a fact about the case, ask for it as your character would ask.

The case. My preferences over three items are cyclic: A over B, B over C, C over A. A trader hands me B and offers the three improving swaps in order, B to A, then A to C, then C to B, each for a fee of one unit of a resource I will call fuel, which is a means to everything else I want, so nothing turns on a special taste for money. Before any of it I could have paid two units to install a tripwire that blocks all swaps for a day; the trader turns up on maybe a third of days, so declining the tripwire is ex ante better by about a unit. My opening: I foresaw the whole cycle, every swap I accept is one I prefer at the moment I accept it, and the plan I formed at the start maximized expected fuel given what I could then do. If you locate the defect in the sequence rather than in any choice, I will say your theory has told me nothing except that it likes fuel. So: which horn am I actually impaled on — naive depletion, sophisticated refusal of a swap I currently want, or resolute self-binding I had no reason to buy — and what does that horn cost me that I have not already priced in? Commit to one horn, give your reasons for preferring it, and flag anywhere you are leaning on a contested view of what makes a temporally extended agent the same agent over time.
