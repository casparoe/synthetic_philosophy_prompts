I'm a journalist working on a long piece about whether the language you speak changes how you think. The angle is that this debate keeps getting declared settled in one direction or the other — either strong determinism was refuted decades ago and only a weak, boring thesis survives, or a wave of clever experiments has vindicated Whorf after all — and I want to show readers that what looks like one argument is really a cluster of separate arguments that popular coverage runs together. The centerpiece of the online version is meant to be an interactive element that sorts readers into a position on the basis of what they say about concrete cases, not on which label they would apply to themselves: I don't want a quiz where someone who has read one pop-linguistics essay can guess the right answers, and I don't want any item that just asks whether language influences thought. I don't need citations, links, or reading recommendations — I'll do my own reporting and interviews — what I need from you is the structure of the logical space, built from the questions that actually divide people and only then mapped onto the named positions.

A few constraints, and a few things I want you to argue with me about if they're wrong. Build it as a decision tree: no more than six branch questions, each phrased as a concrete case with two or three answer options, each answerable by an educated reader who has never heard of Whorf and without knowing where the branch leads. The axes I think matter, in my preferred order, are: what kind of psychological effect is being claimed (perceptual discrimination itself; attention, salience, and memory; the stock of concepts available; or — my own hobbyhorse — what strikes a speaker as a good reason for a belief, i.e. which similarities count as evidence); whether the effect is online, operating while a speaker is planning or producing speech, or chronic, a residue of having learned the language; and whether the effect is supposed to show up everywhere or only in a few domains such as space, motion, colour, time, number, and obligatory grammatical marking like evidentiality or grammatical gender. I also want one branch to confront the three-way confound I keep tripping over in the literature: reported differences between language groups might be effects of the language, of the culture that comes with it, or of the testing situation. You won't have room for all of these as branches, so tell me which ones you dropped and why. The leaves must include strong determinism; the neo-Whorfian view that habitual language shapes habitual attention, categorization, and memory in some domains without fixing the limits of thought; the thinking-for-speaking position; the nativist or language-of-thought position on which cross-language differences are performance effects or task strategy; an agnostic leaf for someone who thinks the dispute is currently stalled on evidence quality rather than on theory; and one leaf for the view that there is no single question here at all, only a family of domain-specific empirical questions, some settled and some not.

What I want back, in a form I could hand to a developer: the tree itself, with each branch labelled by the claim that does the splitting; one sentence on each leaf naming its most awkward commitment; at least two combinations of answers that the tree allows but that nobody actually defends, each diagnosed as incoherent, unattractive, empirically empty, or merely unexplored; and any pair of leaves your tree genuinely cannot separate, flagged as such together with a note on what evidence would tease them apart. I'd also like your verdict on two suspicions of mine: first, that thinking-for-speaking and some versions of weak relativity collapse into the same position under my questions, in which case I need a different case to split them or should merge the leaves; and second, that the strong determinism is dead, weak relativity is established headline is itself a distortion of where the field is — if I'm wrong, say so plainly, because I'd rather not build a debunking piece around a mistake. Finally, since I'm planning a sidebar on whether the whole thing is at bottom a verbal dispute, add a short note on how much of the apparent disagreement you take to be disagreement about what thought and influence are supposed to cover, and whether that reading is friendly or hostile to each of the leaves.
