I'm an observational cosmologist, and last term I sat in on a philosophy of science reading group that spent two full sessions stuck on a single exchange. Two of the members — call them Ingrid and Tobias — were arguing about whether a fact you already knew can be evidence for a new theory. They both asked me to referee and I genuinely could not tell who was ahead, so I'm reporting the exchange as faithfully as I can and asking for a verdict on the arguments as they were actually made, not on the tidied-up versions.

The case that started it was Mercury's perihelion. Once the perturbations from the other planets are subtracted, Mercury's orbit precesses by roughly 43 arcseconds per century more than Newtonian theory predicts. That residual was on the books decades before general relativity, which then produced it as a consequence of the field equations, and it is standardly presented as one of the theory's great evidential triumphs.

(1) Ingrid called this the problem of old evidence. If the datum was already known, then anyone updating by Bayes's rule assigned P(E) = 1 before the theory arrived; the theory entails the datum, so P(E|T) = 1; hence P(T|E) = P(T), and the datum confirms nothing. Yet every working scientist counts it as evidence, and not just here — she offered Darwin as a second case, where the theory's credit is largely credited to its making sense of homologies and geographic distributions that naturalists already knew about. So either the practice is irrational or Bayesian confirmation theory has a hole in it, and she thought the hole was real: conditioning on certainties can never move you, and a great deal of what scientists call evidence is already certain.

(2) Tobias said the fix is to stop measuring confirmation as change in credence and measure it as fit. What matters is the ratio of P(E|T) to P(E|¬T): how well the theory would have anticipated the datum compared with its rivals. That ratio can be favorable even when E is old; you evaluate it against a hypothetical starting point from which E has been removed. Mercury's residual is exactly what the field equations give and was an unexplained anomaly for the Newtonian picture, so the ratio is high and the confirmation is secured without any updating at all.

(3) Ingrid replied that the ratio cannot do the work, for two reasons. First, arithmetic: if P(E) = 1 and 0 < P(T) < 1, then expanding P(E) = P(E|T)P(T) + P(E|¬T)(1 − P(T)) shows both conditional probabilities must equal 1, since each product is bounded above by its multiplier and the two bounds sum to 1. So every hypothesis fits the datum perfectly and the ratio is exactly 1 — the escape route is closed by the probability calculus, not by philosophical taste. Second, the deletion is not well defined: remove the anomaly from what you know and you must also adjust everything else that leans on it, and there is no principled place to stop.

(4) Tobias conceded the deletion was messy and offered a replacement that needs no deletion. What the scientist actually learns when a theory arrives is not the datum but the derivation — the fact that T entails E. For a non-ideal agent that is a genuine discovery: it can be news that the field equations yield the residual. Model the agent's credences as not closed under logical consequence, so that P(T → E) < 1 beforehand, and learning the entailment raises P(T) even while P(E) stays pinned at 1. The Mercury episode then has exactly the shape of learning something new.

(5) Ingrid's objection was that this makes confirmation a reward for ignorance. The entailment is news only because the agent had not worked out the theory's consequences; and note that if P(E) = 1 then even a logically omniscient agent has P(T → E) = 1 automatically, since T ∧ ¬E has probability at most P(¬E) = 0. So the omniscient agent gets zero confirmation from Mercury. A theory on which a datum's evidential value falls as the agent gets smarter is, she said, a reductio.

(6) Tobias answered that the omniscient agent is the wrong baseline: rational confirmation is a notion for agents like us, and for us working out what a theory implies is the central activity of theoretical science, so of course new entailments carry weight. Besides, the ideal agent does not need the boost — an agent who had already seen the entailment would already have assigned the theory a higher prior, so comparing that agent's before and after is comparing two different agents.

(7) Ingrid then pressed a further point: both of Tobias's fixes give the same credit to a theory built to yield a datum as to one that yielded it unexpectedly. Imagine two rivals that both entail the residual, one deriving it from its core postulates and the other carrying an auxiliary parameter that was dialed until it fit. Practice plainly favors the first, and nothing in the machinery offered so far explains why. Tobias (8) replied that this is handled in the prior, not in the evidential relation: a theory with a freely tunable parameter is penalized before the data are ever considered, so no separate novelty requirement is needed — to which Ingrid said that this just relocates the puzzle, since priors are supposed to be independent of the evidence, and here the prior is where all the work about accommodation versus prediction is being done.

Before they broke up they traded two cases neither of them could settle, and I'd especially like your reading of these.

Case A. A researcher derives from her new theory a value for a quantity she and her collaborator measured years earlier and then forgot about. At the moment of derivation she experiences it as a striking success and her confidence in the theory jumps. Her collaborator then points out that this is just the number in their own old notebook. Her confidence does not drop when she is reminded. Ingrid said the reminder reveals the jump was never rational, since the datum was always in her evidence and she merely lost track of it. Tobias said the reminder is irrelevant, because what did the work was the derivation, which is new regardless of the datum's age; if you think the reminder should deflate her confidence, you have committed yourself to confirmation being a property of calendar dates.

Case B. Suppose the residual had been known before the theory only as lying somewhere between about 30 and 60 arcseconds per century, and the theory says 42.98. Ingrid said this shows the problem is an artifact: how finely we slice the evidence is up to us, and no conclusion about confirmation can rest on our choice of description. Tobias said it shows the opposite — if the slicing is free then any old datum can be re-described as a novel precise prediction, and the old-evidence problem becomes trivially escapable and therefore toothless.

Please go through the exchange and rate each numbered move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each, and check whether the arithmetic in move (3) is sound and whether it really bears on what Tobias was claiming. Then say how you read cases A and B — what each actually shows, which side if either it favors, and what the right unit of evidence is if a proposition, a derivation and an agent's access to a proposition can come apart. Finally: who is more right overall, and what is each of them missing?
