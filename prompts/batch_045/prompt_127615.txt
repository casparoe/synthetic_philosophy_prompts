ok so I want you to draft the joint statement that could come out of an actually well-run adversarial collaboration on that old Letter Spirit question: what makes every 'a' an 'a'? And I mean it quite literally — the two-storey 'a' in a text face, the single-storey 'ɑ' in a geometric sans, the one you scrawl in a hurry, the one a kid draws on the first try, the one in a font where the shapes have all been swapped around. Same letter, or not, and why.

Two camps, and both of them need to be good — neither one gets to win because the other one was stated stupidly.

The Schema camp: 'a'-hood is a matter of form. There is an abstract letterform schema — something like a set of parts, their topological arrangement, and a range of allowed metric variation — and every 'a' is an 'a' in virtue of satisfying it. Style is what happens in the schema's free parameters. Double-storey and single-storey 'a' are both 'a' because both satisfy the schema; a mark that looks exactly like an 'a' but was never generated by anything like the 'a' construction is at best 'a'-shaped.

The System camp: no shape-based schema does the real work. A letter is a position in a system of contrasts, kept in place by a chain of production and uptake: it's an 'a' if it's made and read as the thing that contrasts with the rest of the alphabet the way 'a' does. Form is only ever evidence for role, never constitutive of it. So there needn't be any form shared by all and only the a's, and a mark can stop being an 'a' purely because of what's happening around it.

Deliverable, in four parts:

1. Agreed claims, numbered, 8 to 12 of them. Things both camps can sign. The bar: each one should be non-vacuous, and at least two of them should be claims one of the two sides would have been inclined to deny going in, or that an outsider would find surprising. "Letters are conventional" and "it depends what you mean by letter" don't count as rows here. Include what both sides think about allography (a single font that ships both 'a' and 'ɑ' as alternates in one and the same text: one letter or two?), about whether 'a'-ness could be a matter of a stored template, and about whether production history and reader uptake can ever come apart.

2. A disagreements table. Columns: the issue | the claim as the Schema camp would word it for itself | the claim as the System camp would word it for itself | genuine disagreement, verbal, or dependent on a stipulation. Each side's own column has to be phrased the way that side would actually sign it, not the way the other side would caricature it. Where you say "verbal," you have to cash it: name the two candidate meanings, say what the world would have to be like for each to be the right one, and say what, if anything, would change depending on which we pick. If you think a row is stipulation-dependent, say whose stipulation and why nobody is in a position to make it.

3. Four or five cruxes — sub-questions where settling one would move at least one side. For each: the concrete case both sides agree would bear on it, and each side's prediction about that case, written down in advance, in a form that could later turn out to have been wrong. Use these:

- A book set entirely in a permuted typeface: the shapes are permuted by one big cycle over the 26 lowercase letters, so what you get when the author types 'a' is the shape you'd normally read as 'e'. Consistent throughout, 200 pages, and readers who've had an hour with it read it fluently.
- An "orphan" glyph: it comes out of the typeface's own 'a' routine, but shown on its own to a hundred readers, forty read it as 'a' and sixty read it as 'o'.
- An invented script with twenty-six elements whose contrast structure is structurally just like Latin's, sharing no shapes with it and having no historical connection to it.
- A child's mark that she definately intends as 'a' and that every reader reads as 'u'; and the mirror case, where she intends something else and everybody reads 'a' and tells her well done.
- Any further case of your own that separates the two camps better than these do, if you can think of one.

4. A short closing section, "what would move us": each side names the single finding that would make it give up its central claim, and then anything both agree would settle the matter rather than just re-describe it.

Constraints: don't lean on claims about actual type history or real typefaces as evidence — I want everything to follow from the cases and the arguments, so a reader who knows nothing about letterforms beyond what's above can still check the reasoning. Don't cite anything or name-drop. Don't let either side win by stipulating a definition of "letter" that begs the question, and don't let a crux get seperated from a case that could in principle be run. Markdown for the table, numbered lists elsewhere, somewhere around 1000-1400 words, and don't pad.
