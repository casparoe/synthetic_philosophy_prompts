Hi — I've got two of my grad TAs who have been going at each other for a whole semester and I want to force them into something productive. One is a hard-nosed reductionist about testimony: the broadly Hume-flavoured view that testimony isn't its own basic source of warrant, and that a hearer's justification for believing what someone tells her is just ordinary inductive support from the general track record of testimony plus whatever local cues about this speaker and this content she has picked up. The other is on the opposite side: testimony is a basic, sui generis source; there is a presumption of trustworthiness that doesn't have to be earned by the hearer's own evidence; and analysing it as an inference is scepticism in disguise. You know the neighbourhood.

What I want from you is the joint document they would sign at the end of an actual adversarial collaboration — the kind where neither party gets to define the other's view. Concretely:

- A numbered section of agreed claims, and I mean substantive ones, not pieties. Not just "testimony matters a lot" but things like what they agree the disagreement is not about (whether a careful hearer should check everything; whether some monitoring for competence and deception goes on; whether justification here is ever meant to be Cartesian), and what each grants about the other's strongest cases.
- A disagreements table: one row per point of residual disagreement, with the reductionist's formulation written so that she would sign it and the anti-reductionist's written so that he would sign it, and no wording either would call a strawman.
- Then the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them.
- For each crux, one concrete argument, case, or piece of evidence that both agree bears on it, plus what each side predicts about how it will come out, written down in advance.

Three things I definately want in there, because they're where I think the real action is and they don't get enough airtime:

1. The uncalibrated-source problem. Give me a case where the hearer has no track record whatsoever for the *class* of informant: first-contact testimony from a community with no prior contact, the first output of some machine system she has no independent handle on, a translated text in a genre she has never encountered. The reductionist owes an account of how a novel source class gets calibrated without circularity — you can't use testimony to establish the reliability of testimony. The anti-reductionist owes an account of why default trust extends to a class with zero track record at all. Is this just the familiar bootstrapping / "the children in the epistemic basement" dispute wearing a hat, or is it a genuinely separate crux? Say which, and why.

2. Informative silence. A seller's listing that omits any mention of the flooding; a clinician's handover that never mentions the allergy; a pilot's readback that skips one item. Where there's a local convention of mentioning, the hearer learns something from the omission — but nobody asserted anything, and there is no "statement" the hearer recieved. Is silence a species of testimony, or a distinct inference from a convention plus an expectation of disclosure? Whichever way each of them goes, does it cost them something: does reductionism look unable to get going at all without a sayer, and does anti-reductionism over-generate into treating every patterned absence as a warranted source? Turn this into a crux with a test case and predictions.

3. Whether any of it is verbal. I suspect at least one strand of the fight is over "default" — one side meaning "the hearer needs no argument of her own," the other meaning "warranted by a distinct source with its own normative structure." Tell me which parts of the dispute are terminological and why, and re-state whatever survives in neutral terms.
