I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or history, and I've been reading about the idea that non-human things — pathogens, the climate, volcanoes — should be treated as actors in history rather than as backdrop. I think I've got the gist, but I don't trust myself to tell where the argument overreaches.

A feature in a popular history magazine put the view roughly like this (I'm reconstructing it from memory, so the wording is mine, but I believe the claims are faithful): 'Climate, germs and volcanoes are historical actors in their own right, not scenery. When Europeans reached the Americas it wasn't their guns or their God that settled the outcome — it was smallpox; the pathogen did the conquering. The Black Death didn't merely kill a huge share of Europe, it acted: by making labour scarce it pushed wages up and broke the back of serfdom. And when a volcano like Tambora throws enough ash into the atmosphere to cancel a summer, the famines and migrations that follow make the eruption, properly described, an agent of history. The standing charge of "environmental determinism" has been used to dismiss all of this unfairly. Once we give up the prejudice that only persons can act, we can see that non-human things act too, in the only sense that matters: they make things happen. And once you see that, much of what gets called human agency starts to look like a story told after the fact about outcomes that were environmentally fixed. The determinists were basically right; their mistake was thinking that this was a criticism.'

I'd like an accuracy audit of that paragraph. Which claims would someone who works on this kind of explanation accept without flinching, which are stated more strongly than the evidence or the argument supports, and where is a technical term — 'actor', 'agent', 'determinism' — doing more work than it's entitled to? The step from 'non-human things make a difference' to 'the outcome was fixed and human choice is largely a story told afterwards' feels like a jump to me, but I can't tell whether it's a jump the view is genuinely committed to or just the magazine's flourish. And is the closing line fair about why people are wary of this style of explanation, or does it recast a methodological worry as a moral one in order to dismiss it?

Where I'm coming from: at work we run blameless incident reviews and keep the trigger — the event that produced the outage — separate from the pre-existing fragility that made the system susceptible to it, while insisting that the fragility usually does most of the causal work and that saying so is not an excuse for whoever shipped the change. My instinct is that this is the same shape as what the article is doing, and that it's a good shape. But I also suspect the analogy smuggles something in: it assumes a system with a defined boundary and a normal state to fall away from, and I don't think history supplies either. Is the analogy earning its keep, and if it breaks, where does it break?
