I'm a retired physical chemist — 35 years of doing spectroscopy and zero philosophy, and now I'm two years into sitting in on a reading group at the local university and reading around on my own. I've got stuck on the chapter about utility and revealed preference in one of those pop-econ books (the kind that explains everything with beer and pizza), and I can't tell how much of it to trust. Here's roughly what it says, as close as I can reconstruct it:

"Economists used to think utility was a real thing in your head — a quantity of pleasure or satisfaction that a good produces — and they tried to measure it by asking people how much they enjoyed things. Samuelson said this isn't science: you can't observe somebody's pleasure, you can only observe what they do. So he turned the definition around. If a person picks A in a situation where B was also available, that just is what it means to prefer A to B. Utility then becomes a bookkeeping device, a number attached to each bundle that goes up exactly when the chooser goes for that bundle, and nothing more. Two things follow. First, the numbers themselves carry no information: any relabeling that leaves the ordering intact gives you exactly the same theory. That's why economists call utility ordinal, and why it's strictly meaningless to compare one person's utility with another's. Second — and this is the honest part — the theory can never be refuted. Whatever anybody chooses, you can always cook up some utility function that makes that choice a maximum, so 'utility maximization' tells you nothing about the world. The only discipline comes from the weak axiom of revealed preference: if you take A when B is on offer and also take B when A is on offer, you're being inconsistent. And that axiom is both necessary and sufficient for a body of choices to be describable as maximizing some utility function. Given all this, modern economics simply drops the idea of an underlying preference: what's good for a person is what that person would choose, and cost-benefit analysis based on what people actually pay is the only scientifically respectable way to talk about welfare. Asking what somebody really prefers, deep down, is either a disguised question about their choices or not a question at all."

Two things are nagging me and I'm not shure whether I'm importing the wrong intuitions from the lab.

First, the ordinality claim. In my old work a quantity that's defined only up to a monotone transformation wasn't meaningless, it just carried less structure than people assumed — and sometimes you could recover more structure by adding assumptions (additivity across independent things, invariance under a group of operations, that sort of trick). Someone in the group told me that once you let people choose under risk, utility stops being freely rescalable and becomes pinned down only up to positive affine transformations, so "any relabeling that keeps the order" can't be right in general. If that's so, the book's step from "ordinal" to "comparing utilities across people is meaningless" looks like it's leaning on a slogan rather than a result.

Second, it says the theory has no empirical content and then, two paragraphs later, hands me a test. Those pull in opposite directions, and my guess is the book is confusing two different claims — something like "there exists a utility function that represents your choices" versus "your choices satisfy these axioms" — but I can't quite articulate which one is doing the work. There's also a passage I keep circling back to: it says modern economics drops the idea of an underlying preference, which sounds odd to me, because the whole point of the axioms seems to be that if your choices are consistent enough then they do reveal something underneath. That's what I'd have thought "revealed" meant.

Here's the case I keep testing it on. My neighbour always buys the cheap shoes, and she says she'd rather have the good ones but they never fit the budget. The book's logic seems to commit her to preferring cheap shoes, full stop, and therefore to being no worse off than she would be with the expensive ones. Is that really what the theory says, or does the fact that she's choosing from different budget sets matter?

So: could you go through the passage claim by claim and tell me what a careful microeconomic theorist or a philosopher of economics would happily sign off on, what they'd wince at as a simplification, and what is just wrong? I especially want to know whether the punchline survives the corrections — both the "welfare just is choice" bit and the bit about money-metric cost-benefit being the only respectable welfare measure, since those two seem to me to be in tension with "the numbers carry no information."

And one last thing, tell me if this framing I invented is on the right track or whether it smuggles something in: maybe revealed preference isn't a theory of welfare at all but a measurement theory — like in my lab, where the meaning of a reading is fixed by the operations that produce it plus the class of transformations that leave the description unchanged. On that reading the "meaning of utility" is whatever survives the admissible rescalings: order under certainty, affine structure under risk, something else again if you insist on pricing everything in dollars. Is that fair, or does it paper over the part where welfare is supposed to come in?
