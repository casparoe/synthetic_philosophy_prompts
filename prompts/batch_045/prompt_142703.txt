I'm writing an essay on meritocracy and desert, and I want to use Pixar's Ratatouille as my central case rather than a conventional thought experiment. I've been working with a distinction between two questions that meritocratic talk tends to collapse: whether praise or blame attaches to the agent whose excellence actually explains the outcome (call this credit-fit), and whether the advantages that follow are ones the agent earned against others who also laboured (call this reward-fit). The film looks built to prise them apart — Linguini taking public credit for cooking he does not do, Remy whose gift is as unchosen as his species, Anton Ego's review, which praises a dish while thoroughly misidentifying who made it, and Ego's own reward at the end, which seems wildly disproportionate to the modest decency that earns it.

Give me a reading of the film in those terms: which of the two questions it is really interested in, how its scenes distribute credit-fit and reward-fit across the characters, and whether the film as a whole vindicates meritocracy or quietly undercuts it. I want a committed reading — take a definite position on what the film is doing with merit and credit, defend it against the most obvious alternative reading, and mark clearly the places where you are going out on a limb or leaning on a contested reading of a scene. Equally, tell me where the fit between film and distinction breaks down and the honest critic has to stop rather than press on, and say what, if anything, the film shows about desert that the arguments alone don't. Work from the film as you remember it; I need no exact dialogue, production history, or sources.
