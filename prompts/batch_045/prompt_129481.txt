I keep going round in circles about something and I would be grateful for your help in getting clear. I am not an academic — I left school at eighteen and now run a small market garden with my brother — but I have been reading about insect sentience in my own time, and the argument from sheer numbers has rather rattled me: billions upon billions of creatures, each with only a small chance of mattering at all, and apparently the product is still enormous. I cannot tell whether that is supposed to change what I do on Monday morning or whether I am being taken for a ride. What I trust more than the arguments is my own reaction to concrete cases, so here are my verdicts, and I would like to know what principle, if any, they add up to. (1) A wasps' nest in the shed doorway the children use — I knocked it down at dusk. No qualms. (2) Slugs on the brassicas: I use pellets, though I have switched to the ferric phosphate kind since it was barely dearer. Thousands of deaths a season. Slight unease, but fine; I will pay a small premium for a kinder method, though not a large one. (3) Aphids: I spray them with soapy water and feel essentially nothing — which now strikes me as odd, since a soapy film is presumably not a pleasant way to go and there are vastly more aphids than slugs. (4) A neighbour poured boiling water over an ants' nest in his drive. I thought that was callous and said so; he pointed out that I kill far more insects than he does with my sprays; I had no answer, but the verdict stands — fine for me, not fine for him. (5) Bees: I keep two hives and I would feel genuinely bad about destroying a colony, far worse than about the aphids. My brother says that is mere charisma, and that a bee is not obviously a better candidate than an ant. I suspect he is right and I do not want to surrender the verdict. (6) Lobsters: I do not eat them, and I would be uneasy watching one boiled, though I am not about to campaign about it. (7) Nematodes: someone told me there are millions in a square metre of my soil, so that even at a one-in-a-million chance each, digging a bed is the worst thing I do all year. I think that is absurd. I dig, with no qualms whatsoever. (8) Chickens: I am as certain as I am about anything here, I pay roughly double for genuinely better eggs, and I would pay perhaps twenty pounds to spare one bird a bad year. (9) On exchange rates I am unreliable: I would pay nothing to spare the aphids on my brassicas, a pound or two for something that cleared them without killing them, and for the ants I genuinely cannot say. If pressed I would trade a chicken's bad year for an indefinitely large number of aphids without blinking, and then at some point the willingness stops feeling honest and starts feeling like a refusal to do arithmetic. (10) I read that insect protein is increasingly fed to farmed fish, that some people call this a moral catastrophe on account of the numbers, and that others call it a fix because it displaces fishmeal. I have no settled verdict there at all.

Three things people have said to me make it worse rather than better. First, a friend argues that my "one in a thousand" is not the sort of number I am allowed to multiply by millions, because it is not the output of any method with a track record — it is a shrug dressed up as a probability — whereas my confidence about the chicken is. I take the point but it sounds conveniently like an excuse. Second, the same friend says that if I do not know whether an ants' nest contains fifty thousand subjects or a single colony-level subject — or, at a stretch, both — then I cannot simply take an expected number and multiply, because those candidate subjects overlap and I would be counting the same underlying thing two or more times over. That seems to bite, and I do not know how to do the sum properly once I have accepted it. Third, and this is what I cannot shake: whatever test I use for "a candidate subject out there" looks as though it ought to apply inside my own head. A friend described to me a theory on which any system that integrates information is conscious to some degree, and if I let a test like that multiply subjects cheaply in the garden, I do not see why it does not multiply them in my skull, and if it does not, I want the principle that draws the boundary — I would rather not end up totalling probable subjects inside my own brain. There is also something she mentioned about flies resolving flicker far faster than we do, so that if an insect's experience runs faster in time, an hour of insect pain contains more experience than an hour of ours; she thinks that adds another multiplier, and I cannot decide whether suffering should be counted per minute or per moment of experience, though I suspect this is where the numbers argument quietly gets most of its real force.

The rule I have been feeling my way towards is something like: multiply only probabilities that have been earned by evidence of the same kind as the evidence that convinces me about chickens; never add candidate subjects that overlap; count counterfactual differences rather than grand totals, so that what matters about insect farming is what it replaces; and take the cheaper-in-every-sense kinder option where one exists. Does that survive my own verdicts, and where exactly does it break? Please state the principle precisely, tell me which case motivates each clause, and be plain about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate — if I have to give something up I would sooner give up my calm about the aphids than my contempt for the boiling water, but I want to know what that costs me, and whether the bees have to go too. And then I would like to know what your principle says about cases I have not decided: the parasitic wasps I buy as biological control, whose larvae eat caterpillars alive from the inside and about which I currently feel nothing, which seems strange in light of all this; a fast-acting treatment for the same pest that costs a quarter more than what I use now, and how much that quarter is allowed to matter; and whether, on your account, I ought to stop digging my beds altogether.
