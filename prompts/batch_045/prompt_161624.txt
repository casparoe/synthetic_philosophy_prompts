I teach a seminar on rational choice and self-control, and I would like to use Wilde's The Picture of Dorian Gray as a genuine case rather than as decoration. Read Dorian's repeated resolutions to reform — above all the one he makes on first seeing the altered portrait after Sibyl Vane's death, the years of dissipation punctuated by rare visits to the locked schoolroom, and the final attic scene in which he destroys the portrait — through the contrast between akrasia proper, acting against one's own all-things-considered better judgment, and hyperbolic discounting or present bias, where the judgment itself reverses as a reward draws near. Two questions interest me especially. First, which of the two Dorian is: he never contrives a Ulysses-style precommitment, yet hiding the portrait and keeping away from it looks like the same kind of self-directed choice architecture, aimed not at enforcing his resolutions but at keeping them from being refreshed — a sophisticated agent managing his own attention in the service of vice. Second, whether the portrait is better read as a feedback or salience mechanism, a device for making deferred consequences perceptible: on that reading Wilde locates the failure of self-control in the representability of future states rather than in the shape of the discount function, and the portrait is a prosthesis for present bias rather than an objective ledger of accumulated corruption. Where does the novel resist either reading — in making the corruption accrue whether or not Dorian looks, in the causal weight it gives Lord Henry's influence, in the final identification of the portrait with Dorian himself? And does the book, read this way, take any position on whether merely time-inconsistent agents are blameworthy in the way that straightforwardly weak-willed ones are? I want a reading faithful in both directions: it should not flatten Dorian into a discount curve, and it should say plainly where each lens fails to fit.
