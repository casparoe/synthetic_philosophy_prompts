I run a weekend workshop for screenwriters and historical novelists, and every time we do the session on dramatizing real people it collapses into the same loop: half the room is convinced we owe the dead something — fidelity, restraint, maybe some kind of loyalty to how they saw themselves — and the other half thinks that's mystical, that nobody is harmed by a depiction, and that the only real duties run to living audiences, or to the historical record, or to the story. Nobody moves an inch. My theory is that the slogan "do we owe the dead anything?" is doing all the damage, because it lets people argue about a label instead of about the decisions they actually make at a desk. So I want a decision tree instead of another debate.

What I need: a tree that takes a writer from concrete production decisions to a named position on what we owe the real dead when we dramatize them. Cap it at 8 branch questions, and every node has to be something a writer really decides — inventing a scene that never happened but is "true to the character", moving an event in time, folding five minor real people into one composite character, deciding how much the audience is going to read the thing as history, deciding whether to include a real person at all. Nothing that just asks "do the dead have moral standing?", that only sorts people by the label they already like.

Please build the branches out of these cases rather than out of abstract principles:

1. a sex scene between two real historical figures who, on the archival evidence, probably never met;
2. a real speech delivered a year earlier than it was and put in a different character's mouth, because the scene needed it;
3. a composite character, renamed, built out of five minor real people;
4. a subject who published a memoir that flatters them, where the archive contradicts it — the writer follows the archive;
5. a subject whose only surviving records were produced by the institution that exploited them;
6. a subject who wrote, in writing, that they never wanted to be portrayed;
7. turning a minor real person into the villain because the plot needs one.

Each leaf should carry a name — invent one if there's no settled name, and write "unnamed" if you think nobody actually holds it — plus one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment. I'd like 7 to 9 leaves and ideally at least one that most of my students will recognize themselves in.

Two more things. First, build it from the logical space rather than from a list of views you already have in mind: show me which combinations of answers are unoccupied, and say for each whether that's because the combination is incoherent, just unattractive, or merely untried. And flag any two leaves your tree can't actually separate instead of pretending it can.

Second, I have two pet distinctions I keep pushing in class and I want to know if they're genuinely load-bearing or if I'm overcomplicating it. (a) The two-audience problem: a duty to the person and a duty to the audience can come apart, and they come apart hardest when the audience is going to take the depiction as history rather than as invention. (b) Attention scarcity: cultural attention is finite, so choosing to dramatize this person is also choosing not to dramatize someone else, and maybe a good chunk of what we owe the dead is an opportunity-cost question about the canon rather than a question about any individual depiction. Put them in the tree where they belong, or tell me they aren't really sorting distinctions and why.
