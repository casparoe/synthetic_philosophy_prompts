I work on a trust-and-safety team at a mid-sized social platform, in the small unit that handles the coordinated stuff — what the policy calls coordinated inauthentic behaviour, plus the referrals we get about brigading, astroturfing, and just plain weird clusters of accounts posting near-identical images within ninety seconds of each other. This is basically all I do now: me and three analysts, thirty to forty cases a day, roughly half an hour each when it's quiet and much less when something is blowing up. The analysts are sharp but they aren't philosophers and they aren't lawyers; anything that ends in enforcement against an account over a certain size, or that touches an election or a live protest, has to be signed off by a senior reviewer two timezones away who always asks some version of what makes this a group rather than just a bunch of people who happen to be doing the same thing, and who does not accept you can just tell as an answer. I did one philosophy module years ago and remember almost nothing except an example about two people painting a house together, and the folk notion of group in our current checklist (which is vibes plus four bullet points about timing and reused assets) is doing at least two different jobs that we never separate. So I want a real decision procedure, for our internal playbook, that my analysts can run without theory and that will survive that reviewer.

Here's the shape of the problem as I see it, and please tell me if I've got it wrong. The thing we actually need to sort out isn't whether accounts are similar, it's what kind of plurality we're looking at, and I've been using a rough taxonomy I made up that I'd like you to sharpen or replace: a single will with many mouths (one operator, one PR firm, one guy with a server rack, two hundred accounts — not a collective in any interesting sense, just one agent with a lot of instruments); a paradigm shared-intention group, the house-painting case, where people are mutually responsive, committed to the joint activity and to supporting each other; a hub-and-spoke arrangement where each spoke deals only with the hub and doesn't know the other spokes exist; an open-call thing where someone posts a public instruction to everyone post this at six and thousands do with no contact between them; a loose movement where participants describe themselves in we-language and feel like a we but there's no plan and no mutual responsiveness, just a shared identity and a shared mood; and pure convergence, where the only thing that made the outputs look alike is our own ranking system pushing the same thing at everyone. I half-remember three families of theory that would each cut this up differently — one built on mutual responsiveness plus commitment to joint activity and to mutual support, one where the collective is constituted by an expressed joint commitment, and one where collective intention is just a primitive mode that individuals can be in — and I want you to pick, not to survey.

What I need, concretely: an ordered set of tests, where each test says what observable evidence from the platform would count as a pass (we have DMs, shared docs, timing clusters, asset hashes, payment rails, device and IP overlap, sometimes a leaked content calendar), where a fail stops the line with a clear no, and where the decision gets handed to a human because the question is about values rather than facts. My boss thinks that if participants describe themselves as a we then that settles it; I think that's wrong, but I also don't want the opposite error of telling a genuine movement that it isn't a collective because it has no Slack channel, so tell me what status the self-description gets in the procedure and defend the choice. Then run the whole thing on three cases: (1) a marketing agency in one country running two hundred accounts for a client off a shared calendar, split shifts, operators who have never met and don't know each other's handles and each think they're freelancing; (2) a diaspora activist network where a public account posts a call to post a photo at a given hour, thousands do, nobody knows an organiser, no DMs, one hashtag, everyone posting as we; (3) a self-described fan army doing a review bomb, no organiser, big Discord, in-group norms of mutual defence, no payment to anyone. I'll say up front that I want cases 2 and 3 cleared as organic and I'll be annoyed if your procedure doesn't clear them, so if it doesn't, say so plainly and name the test that forced the verdict rather than softening it. Also be honest about what the procedure can't do: whether the collective-or-not verdict actually buys us anything for enforcement, and where the theory just runs out — bot accounts and AI-generated personas, and the cases where the only coordinator is a recommender. Commit to one procedure rather than giving me a menu, and where you have to choose between rival accounts of shared intention, choose, say which one you built on and why, and mark clearly which steps are straight translation of what the theories actually say versus which are you extrapolating past anything anyone has argued. No citations, links or suggested reading please — I can't follow them and they'd be guesswork anyway.
