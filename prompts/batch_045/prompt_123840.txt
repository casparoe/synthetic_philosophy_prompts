I'm co-teaching a small upper-level seminar this term called something like "Philosophy and Money" (me plus an economist friend who keeps interrupting me), and I'm writing the take-home final right now. We did Locke on the recoinage, Hume on money and the balance of trade, and then one week right at the end on Berkeley's monetary writing — the pamphlet he rushed out after the South Sea Bubble collapsed, and the 1730s book of numbered rhetorical questions about the Irish economy. I keep telling the students that the interesting bit is his ticket picture: money isn't itself wealth, it's a ticket or a counter, a claim on other people's industry, and the real wealth of a nation is the industry of its inhabitants. They can all recite that back at me, and I want a question where reciting it gets you a middling mark at best.

The crux I want them to hit is one my friend and I got stuck on over dinner last night and never really resolved. If money is only a ticket or counter whose entire worth comes from the industry it can command, and if (as Berkeley keeps insisting) there are plenty of idle hands and good land going to waste, then it looks like a government could simply issue more tickets: the tickets set the idle hands to work, the goods get produced, and so the tickets were 'backed' all along by the very industry they called into existence. On Berkeley's own premises the issue of paper credit starts to look self-validating. So what, on those premises, is supposed to stop it? And there's a second piece I want them to grapple with: his attack on the idea that gold has intrinsic value looks like it's doing the same kind of work as his attack on mind-independent material substance — in both cases you're supposedly inventing something that holds itself up outside any relation to minds and their needs — and that parallel is either his deepest insight here or the thing that sinks him, because if value is just a relation between perceivers and what is useful to them, it gets even harder to see why you can't just multiply the counters.

So: write me one essay question (90 minutes, can have two or three lettered parts) built around that, plus a model answer of about 900–1100 words, plus a marking scheme out of 20 split into roughly five things a top answer has to do, with the marks for each and a one-line note on how people usually get each one wrong. Please invent a short concrete scenario to pin them down if it helps — a bad harvest, a sudden silver mine, an island that sets up a land bank, whatever — and dont quote Berkeley anywhere, paraphrase him. The marks should be for making the tension sharp and then taking a stand on whether he can close it, not for two seperate tidy summaries side by side (one on immaterialism, one on money). If you think there's a distinction he needs and doesn't have — trust or acceptability, claims on past industry versus claims on future industry, a stock of goods versus a flow of production, the difference between what a ticket commands today and what it commands once industry has been stirred up — say which one it is and make the scheme reward supplying it.
