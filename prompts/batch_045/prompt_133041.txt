Analyze the Challenger launch decision of January 1986 as a choice under imprecise probability. Take the facts as stipulated rather than as something to get right: O-ring erosion and blow-by had already occurred on a substantial minority of earlier flights; the seals had never been tested remotely near that morning's temperature; the engineers closest to the hardware put the chance of catastrophic failure somewhere in the neighborhood of one in a hundred, while the figure circulating in management's risk documentation was closer to one in a hundred thousand; and the only alternative on offer was a delay costing weeks and millions of dollars, with no risk to the vehicle.

I want the disciplined mapping first. Say exactly what the imprecision is supposed to attach to — the unconditional probability of failure, the causal relevance of cold, the conditional probability of loss given a known blow-by incident — and then run the decision through the standard rules for acting on a set of probability functions instead of a single one: the worst-case-first rule, the rule that treats an option as permissible if it maximizes expected utility under at least one element of the set, and some weighted compromise between them. Which return which verdicts, and where do they come apart?

Then the part that interests me more: what the episode does to the theory. First, the organization was not simply holding one bad precise estimate. It had a procedure that reduced a spread of expert estimates to a single number before the choice reached anyone with authority — call this the pointification of an imprecise credence. Is there any epistemically respectable version of that reduction, or is it always just arbitrarily selecting an element of the set; and if it can be respectable, what would the procedure have to look like? Second, the pessimistic rule appears to forbid launching, but it seems to forbid launching any flight of any spacecraft on any day, since there is always some nonzero lower bound on disaster. Is that a reductio of the rule or a merely demanding conclusion, and what would the theory have to add — a notion of when imprecision is tolerable, second-order confidence in the bounds themselves, or a way of trading the value of narrowing the interval against acting on it — in order to be neither paralyzing nor vacuous? Finally, mark where the framework goes silent: I suspect it has nothing to say about how the interval came to be that wide, or about why one end of it disappeared from the room, and if you think that silence is the real lesson of the episode, argue for it and skip the rest.
