My friend and I have been arguing for about a year now about what it even means to say what some part of a living thing is *for*. Neither of us has any academic background — I got into this after reading some pop-science thing about the heart and then falling down a rabbit hole, and he got into it through me complaining about it. He's in the camp that says a trait's function is fixed by its history: a trait is *for* doing whatever it did that got it kept around, the thing that made the difference to why it's still here. I'm in the camp that says the function is what the trait is actually doing right now, the concrete contribution it makes to the capacities of the system it sits inside, regardless of how it got there. We've stalled out, and I think part of why is that we keep using different words for the same claim and sometimes the same words for different claims.

So please draft for us the joint document we should have written a year ago, as if we finally did a proper adversarial collaboration.

Here are three cases I came up with on walks, because the usual examples (hearts, vestigial stuff, traits co-opted for a new use) all cut both ways and I want to push past them. Please treat them as new and think them through properly rather than matching them to something familiar:

1. **The borrowed trait.** A trait was built up over generations because it did job A. Then the environment shifted a few generations ago and the trait now also does job B, which is genuinely useful for the organism and which nothing has been selected for yet. Is B one of its functions?
2. **The treadmill trait.** A trait arose by pure drift and the only thing it contributes is helping maintain the very conditions in which it goes on existing and doing that. Nothing else in the organism benefits and nothing else suffers. Does it have a function?
3. **The norm-setter.** A trait changes the organism's own circumstances in such a way that the trait's own activity then becomes necessary for the organism to keep going — it helps create the standard by which you'd judge whether it's contributing anything. I suspect this one is the real problem for my side, since my account seems to need some notion of the organism's normal conditions, and here the trait is partly supplying those conditions itself.

What I'd like in the document:

- A numbered section of claims we can both sign — about what function-talk is even for (is it explaining why the trait is there, or telling you what to repair or design, or something else?), and about what a decent account of function would have to get right.
- A disagreements table. For each remaining disagreement, give the claim in wording I'd accept for my side and in wording he'd accept for his, and say explicitly whether you think that disagreement is real or merely about words, and why.
- The cruxes: the specific sub-questions where settling one would move at least one of us. My guesses are (a) whether anything can have a function with no history at all behind it, and (b) how the "normal conditions" or reference situation gets fixed, if it does. Add or drop as you see fit. For each crux, name a case or argument both of us would agree actually bears on it, and write down in advance what each of us would predict it shows — including the cases where you think my side would lose, or where the answer wouldn't settle anything after all.

Also: consider seriously whether the honest answer is that "function" just needs an extra slot — function *of this trait, relative to that system* versus *of this trait, relative to that history* — and that we were each only looking at one slot. If you think that dissolves our argument, say so and say what's left over once it's dissolved. If you think it doesn't, say why not.

Write the whole thing in plain language and define any jargon you bring in. Don't cite anybody or point me at reading — I have no library access and I'd rather just have the argument laid out. And please defend an actual verdict on each of my three cases instead of listing what both sides would say and leaving it there.
