I've been arguing for the better part of a year with a friend from a reading group on value alignment about whether coherent extrapolated volition actually picks out anything — whether, if you take a person's actual messy preferences and extrapolate them under more knowledge, more reflection, more internal consistency and more time, the process converges on a stable target or just quietly inherits whatever ideals you smuggled into the procedure. She thinks the construct basically works, at least as a regulative ideal worth steering toward; I've drifted toward thinking it is closer to a useful fiction. We agreed to stop gesturing at each other and put numbers on it, so I want yours.

To make the claim sharp, define a person's CEV as well-defined if, for the substantive life-shaping choices they face — career, intimate relationships, whether to have children, how to allocate their resources, whether to accept a radically extended or modified life — running the extrapolation along any admissible path returns substantially the same answer, and that answer is not merely an artifact of norms the procedure itself introduced. Give me your credence that the CEV of a typical adult human today is well-defined in that sense, and decompose it. The sub-questions I want numbers on, or better ones if you think I have carved this wrongly:

1. Does the extrapolation commute? Fixing a person's factual errors first and then removing affective distortion and inconsistency — say, a nationalist who learns the founding myth is false, or someone whose vocation rests on an empirical belief that dissolves under more knowledge — plausibly lands somewhere different from doing those operations in the reverse order. How likely is it that the order and granularity of the operators are mostly irrelevant to the endpoint?
2. How much of the content comes from the person at all, as opposed to the procedure's built-in norms of coherence, consistency and full information? I want a number for the claim that the idealization budget is thin enough that the output is still recognizably this person's rather than the output of a generic rational agent.
3. What does extrapolation do in the blank region — the options the person has no prior attitude toward whatsoever, such as radically new experiences, new sensory or cognitive modalities, or forms of life with no analogue in their current experience? Is there a determinate answer there, or does the procedure have no input to work from?
4. Does the recursion have a fixed point? Should the procedure also extrapolate the person's preferences about how to extrapolate, including any preference not to be extrapolated or to be left alone, and does that self-application settle down or oscillate?
5. Is the right endpoint desire or endorsement — what the person would want, or what they would judge best, or what they would recognize as theirs on reflection — and does it matter that these can come apart in someone who ends up endorsing a life they have no appetite for?

Give me a number and one line of justification for each, show how they combine into your headline figure, and tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to. Then locate the disagreement: I am at about 0.3 and my friend is at about 0.65 — which of those sub-questions is each of us most likely wrong about, and what specifically would move you? I would find an actual empirical result about how people's reflective preferences behave under repeated, spaced, well-informed reflection more persuasive than another clever hypothetical case, but tell me if that is the wrong thing to hope for here. Finally, if you think the proposition has no determinate truth value, say so, and give me instead your credence on the meta-question of whether the debate is resolvable in principle — plus, if there is room, a second number for the claim that, conditional on CEV being well-defined, it could ever be identified well enough from finite evidence about a person to be worth steering an artificial agent toward.
