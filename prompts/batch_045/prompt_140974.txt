I work on the product governance team at a savings and investing app — around three million users, mostly people who've never invested before, and most of them on their phone at 11pm. Every proposed change to the app comes past me for a half hour review before it can ship: I write a one pager, and it has to be signed off by our head of product and by legal, neither of whom has any philosophy and both of whom will read two pages maximum. We look at four or five flows a month. The argument we keep having is whether a flow is *taking advantage* of people, and it goes in circles because everyone in the room is using a different test without noticing. I was arguing about this with a mate who did a philosophy degree and he said the thing I'm groping at is the money pump argument, so I've been reading a bit on my own and it feels exactly right but I can't get from the idea to something I can run on a Tuesday afternoon. The bit that seems to matter and that nobody here can articulate is the difference between a set of preferences that can be exploited in one go — you lay out a menu and whichever way they choose they lose — and a *sequence* of choices where each individual choice looks perfectly sensible to the person making it at the time and the damage only shows up when you add them up. Our head of product says if each step is fine then the whole thing is fine and I'm pretty sure that's the exact mistake but I can't say why in words he'd accept.

We've drifted into four rough labels in my notes and I suspect they're doing philosophical work I haven't noticed: 'rake' (we take a fixed cut on a transaction, that's just a fee, nobody minds), 'switchback' (the flow gets someone to move back and forth between two states and pay each time), 'treadmill' (nothing cycles, but the framing keeps restarting the same decision so it gets made over and over), and 'pump' (saved for when someone ends up strictly worse off and nothing they value got produced along the way). I think 'treadmill' might actually be the interesting one rather than the boring one but I can't defend that. So: write me a decision procedure, in the order I should ask the questions, that comes out of what this literature actually says — the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which questions should end in a hard no, and which ones have to be handed to a human being because they're a values call dressed up as a factual one. It needs to survive being read by a lawyer and a data scientist who will both push back. There are some cases I specifically need it to handle. Our legal person's favourite test is 'would the user have agreed to the whole sequence up front?' and I don't trust it but I don't know what's wrong with it. Then there's the user who knows they'll end up slightly poorer and does the whole thing anyway because fiddling with the app is the fun part — if the process is the product, is there any money pump left? And the user whose preferences genuinely shift mid-sequence because they learned something, which feels like it should be completely fine and not exploitative at all. Also: is a hundred tiny losses the same objection as one large one, or does the smallness of each step actually matter to the argument? Our data scientist says we should just A/B test everything and look at whether users end up with less money, and I want to know why that isn't sufficient, if it isn't — partly because we can't run clean experiments on things that have already shipped. And one thing that genuinely bugs me: if these flows really were exploiting an incoherence in someone's preferences, wouldn't we expect some users to just bleed out completely? They don't. So is the whole idea secretly unfalsifiable, or does that tell us something about which version of the argument to use?

Run it on three flows. First: a streak feature where if you'd miss a week you can pay two quid to freeze your streak, and the freeze button appears about four seconds after the missed week and nowhere else. Second: a switchback where users get a notification when markets fall nudging them into the safe pot and one when markets rise nudging them into the growth pot, fifty pence a swap, and the nudge copy is written to match whatever they said they wanted in onboarding. Third, and this is the one I actually need: boost tokens, where each month you get three free boosts that lift the rate on one of your pots for thirty days, you have to activate them by hand, they expire at month end, and the pot that's boosted rotates each month so people keep shuffling balances between pots — we shipped it eight months ago, it's our most used feature, engagement went up a lot, users say in the reviews that they enjoy it, and it's in our current marketing. If your procedure says we should pull it, say that plainly and tell me what I'd have to argue to the committee to keep it anyway, and be honest about whether your procedure can say anything useful about a feature that's already live and that we can't cleanly experiment on now.
