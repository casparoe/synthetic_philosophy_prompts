I convene a graduate seminar on metaphysics and am rewriting the session on dispositions, which I have never found well served by the flat list of counterexamples that textbooks give. I want the cases graded by how much work they really do against the simple conditional analysis — the claim that x is disposed to M in C just in case, if x were in C, x would M.

Rank the following five cases, most damaging first, with one sentence of justification each, noting explicitly where a case undermines only one direction of the biconditional and how much that should count against the analysis as an analysis.

(a) The plain fink: the triggering condition is itself what removes the disposition — a live wire wired to a cut-out that trips the moment a conductor touches it.

(b) The one-shot fink: as in (a), except that the cut-out is permanent, so the disposition is essentially single-use and would not survive its own first manifestation.

(c) The mask: the disposition is fully retained under C, but an independently present feature of the environment blocks the manifestation — the fragile vase packed in foam.

(d) The mimic: the object does not have the disposition at all, yet the manifestation occurs in C for an unrelated reason — an insoluble tablet that breaks up because the solvent has been contaminated.

(e) The finkish mask: a shielding arrangement that is dormant unless C obtains, and which, once active, leaves the disposition wholly intact while blocking the manifestation — a mask that is triggered in exactly the way a fink is.

My own suspicion, which you should feel free to reject, is that the usual fink-versus-mask axis is the wrong one and that (d) does more damage than it is normally credited with. Rank on whatever basis you can defend. Please write in British English, add a closing paragraph naming the consideration most likely to overturn your ordering and the point at which your reasoning is most exposed, and finish with your verdict in the form \answer{d, e, b, c, a} — that is, the letters in your chosen order.
