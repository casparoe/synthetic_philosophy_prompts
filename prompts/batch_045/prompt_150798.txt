hey — I'm on the selection committee for a small fellowship thing, and we've got roughly six researcher-years to hand out over two years, aimed at philosophers, political theorists and theory-y economists. Explicitly not empirical ML, and not policy advocacy either: the money is for conceptual work only. We've been told to pick one theme and spend the whole pot on it, and I'm pushing for work on the singleton hypothesis — you know, Bostrom's thing, a world where there's a single decision-making agency at the top level that can set the rules for everyone beneath it, versus a multipolar outcome. What I need is a defended agenda for the sub-questions, not a survey, and not another round of "how likely is a singleton and would it be good."

Some framings I've been kicking around and genuinely can't tell are insights or just me inventing jargon:

thin singleton — an arrangement where one agency holds an effective veto or rule-setting power over one domain (who gets to build frontier systems, say, or control of orbit) but delegates or ignores nearly everything else. The literature mostly treats singleton vs multipolar as a binary, which feels like it throws away the cases we're most likely to actually land in.

lock-in depth — how much of the value-relevant variance of the long-run future the top agency actually fixes, as opposed to leaving to its subordinates. A singleton that sets the constitution and then lets a million subcultures do what they want is doing something quite different from one that specifies everything, and I suspect most of the worry about concentrating catastrophe risk scales with depth rather than with singleness as such.

the succession problem — every singleton has to solve its own succession, and the arrangements that are stable may not be the ones that preserve its original aims. Is this just ordinary institutional drift in a costume, or does monopoly power make it a different problem?

convergence — the claim that a multipolar world with strong enough coordination institutions ends up functionally singleton-like anyway, so the binary is less decision-relevant than it looks.

So: with six researcher-years and two years, which of these — or something I'm not listing, as long as it isn't just "will AI produce a singleton" — should we fund, and in what order? I want the criteria made explicit: which questions are upstream of which, which are tractable for people who do concepts instead of forecasting, which I should drop because the answer wouldn't change anyone's mind or anyone's behaviour. And what would we learn from the world over the next two years, as opposed to from the research, that should move something up or down the list? Please be blunt if one of my four framings is confused — better I find out now than after we've funded it. No citations needed, I can chase references myself.
