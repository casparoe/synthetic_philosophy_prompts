I'm finishing a dissertation in philosophy on reasons for attitudes — buck-passing analyses of value and the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem — and I'm trying to use Burgess's *A Clockwork Orange* (and Kubrick's film) as a case in a paper. I know the reading everyone gives: it's about free will and moral choice, and the prison chaplain says the thing about goodness having to be chosen. I want something narrower and more technical.

The framework, as I'm using it: on a fitting-attitude or buck-passing analysis, for something to be bad just is for there to be reason to take up some negative attitude toward it — aversion, disvaluation, whatever the mirror image of the relevant pro-attitude is. The wrong-kind-of-reasons problem is that there can be reasons to take up such an attitude whose force has nothing to do with whether the attitude fits its object: a threatener who will hurt you unless you admire him gives you something that behaves like a reason to admire him, and no one wants to count that as making him admirable. So the analysis has to restrict itself to reasons of the right kind, and the dialectically hard part is stating that restriction without helping yourself to the notion of value you were trying to analyze. The diagnostics I've been working with are roughly whether the consideration is directed at the object or at the desirability of being in the attitudinal state, and whether you could cite it in answer to the question of what it is about the object that warrants the response.

Two refinements I want tested rather than assumed. First, what I've been calling *ersatz reasons*: inputs that cause the attitude, generate for the subject the introspective signature of a fitting response — it feels to him like a verdict about the object — and yet fail the citation test without the subject's being able to tell that they fail. The failure is phenomenologically invisible from the inside. My question is whether that is a genuinely distinct third category alongside right- and wrong-kind reasons, or just wrong-kind reasons plus an epicycle about how they feel. Second, what I've been calling *content-bleed*: the standard examples are reasons to take up an attitude one shouldn't take up toward the object one was asked about, but in Alex's case the aversion spreads sideways to something with no connection at all to whatever makes violence bad — the treatment films are scored with Beethoven, and he comes out of it unable to listen to the music he loved without being made physically ill. Here the attitude's object is not fixed by the content of any reason; it is fixed by causal association.

The facts I'm working from, so we're analyzing the same case: Alex volunteers for an experimental aversion therapy (the Ludovico technique) — nausea-inducing drug, eyes held open, films of violence, Beethoven on the soundtrack — and emerges unable to perform or even contemplate violence without becoming violently ill, and unable to bear Beethoven. The cure is demonstrated before officials: he collapses before a man who strikes him and abases himself before a scantily dressed young woman brought on to show the treatment's scope; the chaplain objects that the state has taken away his power of choice; the officials are delighted. Later the writer he once attacked plays the same music at him to drive him to suicide; he jumps; the aversion is undone; and the film ends with Alex announcing that he has been cured.

So the mapping I want:

1. Is the nausea a wrong-kind reason for aversion, or not a reason at all — a cause with no normative standing? Does the case engage the framework in the first place, given that the attitude isn't produced by Alex's responding to a consideration? I've been tempted to reach for alief-style machinery here; tell me whether that helps or just relocates the question.
2. Does Alex's post-treatment state make violence bad, or bad *for him*, by the lights of a buck-passing analysis? If it does, that is a reductio nobody should accept; if it doesn't, say exactly which move in the analysis blocks it.
3. Is the chaplain's objection a wrong-kind-of-reasons complaint, or a different one — about what Alex's conduct would be done for the sake of, moral worth rather than fittingness? And does the work actually side with the chaplain, given how it ends?
4. Does *content-bleed* — the Beethoven — show that the right-kind restriction has to be a condition on the object and content of the attitude and not merely on the source of the reason? I think the case may support that, but I can't tell whether I've discovered something or merely redescribed the standard test.
5. Reverse direction: what does the case show about the framework itself? One thing I suspect is that the framework is first-personal — reasons for an agent's own attitudes — while the operative reasons here are the state's reasons for producing an attitude in someone else, which looks as though it collapses into ordinary reasons for action. Another is that the treatment doesn't extinguish Alex's desire for violence so much as add a conflicting aversion, and I want to know whether a framework organized around reasons for single attitudes has anything to say about engineered intra-personal conflict.

Please be explicit about where the framework has nothing to say: I'd rather have a disciplined mapping with gaps than a forced one.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the one claim you'd defend about whether the wrong-kind-of-reasons framework really illuminates this case — then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question, and keep it under about a thousand words.
