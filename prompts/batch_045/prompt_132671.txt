I am the methodologist to the Standing Committee on Protected Taxa, the statutory body that maintains our jurisdiction's list of protected animals. Listing a taxon triggers binding duties on everyone who keeps, breeds, transports, handles or kills animals in it: enclosure and stocking standards, handling and stunning requirements, and a licensing regime for any procedure likely to cause pain. Delisting removes all of it. The list is revised on a fixed eighteen-month cycle; each cycle we receive six to twelve petitions, each with a dossier of published and grey evidence, industry and animal-protection submissions, and a staff summary of roughly fifteen pages. The Committee has nine appointed members — two veterinarians, an ethologist, a neuroscientist, two industry representatives, an animal-protection representative, an economist, and a chair who is a retired judge — and they meet for two days per cycle. So whatever I put in front of them has to be runnable inside that time, in language that does not presuppose the theory, and it has to satisfy the two constraints that actually bind us: a duty to give reasons a reviewing court will accept as non-arbitrary, and a duty to treat like cases alike across cycles even though the membership turns over.

What the Committee has already resolved, after two cycles of going in circles, is that the property it is trying to track is the capacity for valenced conscious experience, that brain measures of every kind are admissible only as evidence about that capacity and never as the thing that matters, and that the smallness of an animal's brain is not by itself a reason to count a unit of its suffering for less. What it has not resolved is how to turn any of that into a decision it can repeat.

Our working draft has a two-gate shape: a first gate on whether there is sufficient evidence of any valenced experience at all, and a second on how much the taxon's suffering should count. I do not think this survives contact with the dossiers, for reasons I want you to judge rather than accept: the underlying property looks continuous while our duties are binary, and the gates import a between-species comparability that we have no way to establish. Please rebuild it, or replace it, as a procedure.

I need the following, in the form of something I can hand to a non-specialist:

An ordered sequence of questions, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the sequence terminates in a recommendation not to list, where it terminates in a recommendation to list, and where it stops and refers the matter to the Committee because the question is one of values or of statutory purpose rather than of evidence. Include how the procedure handles evidence that is absent rather than negative, and who bears the burden in that case. Include how to combine proxies when they conflict, and in particular whether a proxy's validity should be treated as clade-local — our neuroscientist's view is that a relationship calibrated within vertebrates carries essentially no information about an arthropod or a nerve-net animal, and I do not know whether the procedure should encode that as a rule or as a caution. Include what the unit of account is: the individual animal, the expected-sentience-weighted individual, or the population-level total, and at which stage, if any, the number of animals affected is allowed to enter. Include whether the output can be partial — a listing that attaches to some practices and not others — and what that does to consistency across cycles. Include a one-page version in words that a new member could follow on the morning of the meeting.

Three submissions this cycle pull in directions I cannot reconcile, and the procedure should say explicitly how each is handled and which of them it simply cannot accommodate. Industry argues that central neuron count is the only principled and measurable proxy, that behavioural evidence is projection, and that we should adopt a numerical threshold and apply it without interpretation. An animal-protection submission argues that any threshold on a continuous property is arbitrary, that a nonzero probability of valenced experience multiplied by population scale mandates listing anything with any marker at all, and that refusing to aggregate is itself a moral choice. A consultant argues that what matters is experience per unit of objective time rather than per moment, that temporal resolution varies enormously across taxa, and therefore that a measure which ignores the density of subjective time systematically undercounts small, fast animals.

Then run the finished procedure on three petitions from this cycle, showing the gates in operation and the reasons that would go in the record. Group A comprises about forty species of large, long-lived, socially complex reef-dwelling cephalopods: the dossier is almost entirely allometric — high absolute brain mass, high central neuron counts, a single large integrating structure — with only a couple of studies on nociceptive sensitisation and nothing at all on motivational trade-offs, and annual welfare exposure in the low thousands. Group B comprises social insects with roughly a million neurons and no single integrating structure: flexible rather than reflex responses to injury, a persistent avoidance of a place where they were shocked that survives the loss of a high-value resource, shifts in response that are reversed by analgesics, and one small unreplicated cognitive-bias result of the kind read as a pessimism-like state; annual exposure is stated in the dossier at around ten to the eleventh individuals. Group C comprises filter-feeding animals with a nerve net and no centralisation at all: habituation, a whole-body withdrawal that reorganises ongoing feeding and is modulated by prior state, almost no neuroanatomical data beyond cell counts, and a bycatch mortality that the petition puts at ten to the thirteenth. At least one of the three must come out somewhere my Committee will not want to go, and I would rather it be A, which is where the political pressure is.

Finally, tell me plainly what the procedure cannot do: whether it can say anything about moral status in the rights-bearing sense as opposed to the strength of a claim not to be made to suffer, whether it can compare a unit of experience across architectures that share no evolutionarily conserved substrate, what it does with valenced states that might differ in kind rather than degree from ours, and how badly it misfires for the taxa sitting near whatever boundary it draws. Please do not give me citations, reading lists, or suggestions about which experts to consult — we have those, and a procedure that depends on new evidence is useless to us. Take every figure I have given as it stands in the dossier, without checking it.
