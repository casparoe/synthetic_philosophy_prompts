I teach aesthetics at a mid-sized state university, and a friend who runs a small documentary festival has cornered me into writing her something I can't quite finish. She programmes maybe forty films a year and about six to eight times a year one of them turns out to have a problem: the director was abusing the crew, or the subject's consent was obtained under false pretences, or the filmmaker is facing charges for something that has nothing to do with the film at all. She has no in-house counsel, the board has to sign off on anything that looks like a political statement, and she usually finds out forty-eight hours before the programme grid goes to print. So I can't give her an essay on autonomism vs. moralism; I need something her programming associate, who has a film-studies BA and no philosophy, can actually run in one sitting, at the end of which it hands her one of four dispositions: show it as planned, show it with context (a note in the programme, an on-stage caveat, a panel afterwards), show it only if some condition is met (fee renegotiated, proceeds redirected to a victim's fund, a content warning), or pull it.

The bit where I keep getting stuck is that I think the whole thing is secretly two separate procedures stapled together, and I want her to be able to tell which one she's running. There's a question about whether the work is worse as art because of what the maker did — the sort of claim that seems true when the immorality is visible in the work itself and much less plausible when it isn't — and then there's a completely different question about what a festival ought to platform, which is about money, audiences, and who gets to speak, and could go either way even if the artwork is flawless. I want both tracks, with a signpost at the fork. Along the way the procedure has to handle some cases I've invented for it, partly because I think they break the tidy versions: the constitutive case, where the wrongdoing is inside the work — footage gotten by deceiving the person filmed, a sculpture using material taken from a community's burial ground — so that you cannot remove the taint without destroying the piece; the taint-value case, where the piece is partly interesting because a notorious person made it, so the badness is doing some of the aesthetic work and sanitising it would leave you with nothing; the collaborative case, a forty-person crew with one person's misconduct somewhere in it, where I can't even say whose work it is; the split-victim case, where one of the people harmed wants the film shown as testimony and another wants it buried; and the timing case, a conviction last year versus an artist dead for ninety years, where the money flows somewhere different in each. It also has to say what to do in the epistemic muddle that actually comes up most: an allegation reported by one outlet, no charges, artist denies it, audience has never heard of it — and honestly, an audience that hasn't heard of it is a different situation from one that has, and I don't think either is more fundamental.

So: write me the procedure. Number the questions in the order she should ask them, say what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, and — this matters most to me — where it has to stop and hand the decision to a person because the issue is one of values rather than facts, with an explicit note on who signs off at that point. Keep every distinction you think must survive the simplification and drop the ones that don't, but tell me what you dropped and why it was safe to drop. Then run it on three composites of mine, no real names: a vérité feature about a recovering addict, made by a director who was in a coercive relationship with its central subject, and the subject is alive and wants it screened; an animated short about grief by someone convicted of financial fraud against his own family, unrelated to the film; and a large installation I've saved for last, because she's already booked the venue and spent the insurance money on it, built by a sculptor from objects taken without permission from a community's burial site, and that community is asking for it to be destroyed rather than shown. If the procedure gives her an answer on that third one that she won't like, say so plainly rather than fudging it. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do — I assume it can't tell her whether a film is any good, and I suspect there's a deeper thing it can't do around cases where the audience's knowledge of the artist is what makes the work worth showing at all, but I'd like you to name it better than I just did.
