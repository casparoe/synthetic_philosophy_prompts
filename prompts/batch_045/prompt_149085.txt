Two people on the professional-ethics curriculum committee I chair have been going at it for two years over the situationist critique of virtue ethics, and I want the joint statement they should have produced instead of another round of emails. Rough version of the two views: the situationist says the social-psychology literature (the Harman / Doris line) shows there are no robust global character traits, so virtue talk is empirically empty and the real upshot is that we should redesign situations; the neo-Aristotelian says traits are real but their behavioural signature isn't cross-situational consistency at a moment, it's diachronic situation-selection — the honest agent is the one who over time ends up in fewer situations where cheating is cheap or tempting, and who shapes the situations she's in — call it the niche-construction reply.

Give me: the claims both can sign (both grant that raw cross-situational consistency in single acts is low, neither thinks it's literally zero, both grant trait language can't be used sloppily); then the disagreements, each in wording that side would accept for its own view; then a straight verdict on which parts of this are verbal — a fight about the word trait — and which are genuine. Three cruxes, each with a test or case both agree would bear on it and each person's prediction written down in advance: (1) a pre-registered experience-sampling study with independently coded situations, where the question is whether residual between-person variance still predicts behaviour in new, differently-coded situations; (2) whether any program of situation-design has to presuppose agents who drift toward or away from certain kinds of situation, i.e. whether situationism quietly needs the selectionist story; (3) whether virtue can keep doing normative work as a regulative ideal even if almost nobody instantiates it. Numbered agreed claims first, then a disagreements table. Be committed about who should concede what, and flag anywhere I've characterised one of the two unfairly.
