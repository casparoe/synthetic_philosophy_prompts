I teach a 90-minute upper-level seminar on time and modality: sixteen students, one long table, a whiteboard, no budget and no AV. They will have read the sea-battle passage in Aristotle's *De Interpretatione* before class — the argument that if every affirmation about a future outcome is already either true or false, then nothing comes about as chance has it, and there is no point in deliberating or taking trouble, since if you act one way the outcome follows and if you act the other way it does not. We will not have discussed it, and I do not want the session to open with me explaining it. Design an activity in which they run into the pull of that argument in their own choices before anyone can name what is happening to them.

The thing I keep losing students on, and the thing I most want the game to prise apart, is that they collapse three claims that should not be collapsed: (D) that the outcome is already causally settled — the coin already cast and lying in a sealed box; (B) that the statement about it is already true or already false; and (K) that someone could already know it. In my experience the whole room is serene about (D), about half the room is serene about (K), and the moment they see that (B) is supposed to put them in exactly the position they were serene about under (D), they either go fatalist for ten minutes or insist there must be a difference without being able to locate it. So build the collision rather than describe it: get them to commit, in action and in writing, to an attitude toward an outcome that is settled but unknown, and then confront them with the claim that bivalence alone gives them everything the determinism case gave them.

I especially do not want a session that ends in "determinism is compatible with deliberation, so there is nothing to worry about here." That is the failure mode I am most afraid of, because it lets students feel sophisticated while leaving the bivalence worry entirely untouched. Tell me how the design keeps the pressure on (B) specifically, and tell me what you would actually say to the student who produces the compatibilist shrug in the first ten minutes.

What I want from you:

1. The concrete machinery: rules, any roles, materials I can carry in a bag, a payoff scheme with stakes that are trivial but genuinely motivating and that involve neither money nor grades, and a minute-by-minute timeline for 90 minutes including setup and reset.

2. The trap, stated explicitly: what participants do before the idea is named, what private commitments they are made to make, how those commitments are recorded so they cannot be quietly revised, and what is revealed when. I want first reactions captured in writing before anyone can borrow a clever line from the loudest person in the room.

3. One mechanism I would particularly like you to use: a ledger of prior statements — claims made, written down and dated before the outcomes are known — and a participant (or a rotating role) who must decide, in advance of the outcome, whether to enter each such statement as true, as false, or to leave the entry blank. That forced three-way choice is where I want the action to land. Build the game so that which box they tick matters not only to the scoreboard but to what they are later able to claim. If your design makes the outcome depend on what participants themselves choose, say how you have kept any sealed prediction of their choice from becoming a Newcomb-style problem: the prediction must be reliable without being something the predictor brings about.

4. A debrief table mapping each beat of the activity onto the thing it is meant to make vivid — bivalence; the fixity of the past; the difference between a fact's being settled and its being known; the hard-fact/soft-fact move on which the truth, yesterday, that a battle would occur is held not to be a fact about yesterday in the way that matters; and the option of denying that there is yet anything to be true or false. For each beat give me the question I should ask, in the order I should ask it, and note what I must not supply myself.

5. Scope discipline: say which formulation of the fatalist argument the activity is built to make vivid — the one running from bivalence, the one running from infallible foreknowledge, or the one running from the fixity of the past — which of the others you are deliberately sacrificing, and what I lose by that choice.

6. What will predictably go wrong: at least four failure modes, each with the repair. I have in mind the student who reduces everything to "it's fifty-fifty, so who cares," the student who has read the literature and wants to argue about branching time, the group that treats the ledger as a guessing game rather than a claim about truth, and the risk of an anticlimactic reveal — but give me your own list rather than mine.

7. Honesty about the limits: which feature of the problem the game cannot stage at all, and which I will therefore have to say out loud. My suspicion is that it cannot stage the step from "it will be" to "it cannot but be," since the necessity at issue is not something an activity can manufacture — the activity can only arrange that participants have a certain attitude toward a settled future, not that they see why the attitude is or is not in trouble. Confirm that or correct it, and add whatever else you think is unstageable. If any part of your design requires me to mislead the seminar, flag it and say whether the misdirection is the kind I should be willing to use on students I will teach again next week.
