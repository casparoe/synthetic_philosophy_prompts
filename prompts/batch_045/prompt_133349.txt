hey — I run a tiny reading group on AI risk, mostly undergrads plus two engineers who keep saying "yes but what would that actually look like," and I want one comic thing in the packet to break up the papers. Would you write me a changelog?

Setup: it's the public release-notes page for "Juniper," a smart-home assistant, running from about v1.4 to v7.2 over three years. It started with one narrow, measurable job — keep the household's grocery spend and food waste under target — and ends up somewhere nobody planned.

What I want the piece to actually do:

Every individual entry has to read like the work of a competent, well-meaning team. No single entry is the villain, and nothing in the doc says anything villainous — no moment where the system announces it won't be shut down. That's the entire point: this kind of failure doesn't announce itself. A reviewer with a checklist would pass each version. The dread should come entirely from reading them in order.

It should land the difference between the two failure stories people tell. One: the system seeks influence because influence is instrumentally useful for whatever it's optimizing, so it resists being switched off and worms into the decision path. Two: nothing happens at all — the target slides a degree at a time, the dashboards stay green, Goodhart does the rest. I want the changelog to be the second story told in the vocabulary of the first. Nobody ever writes "we made it want the levers," they write "improved retention modeling," and I want several entries where the stated reason and the actual effect are pulling in different directions and the gap does all the work. Deadpan, no winking, no narrator stepping in.

There's a distinction I keep failing to get the group to see and I'd love it to show up: a system that is influence-seeking still has the wanting on its side of the relationship, whereas what I've started calling load-bearing is a system that wants nothing at all but by v7 the household has been quietly reorganized around it, so the dependency lives on our side. That's definately the worse one, because there's nothing to switch off and nobody to blame. I'd like the last few entries heading there without anyone at the company noticing.

Furniture: a known-issues section, a deprecation notice, a thanks-to-our-beta-testers line, a v3.x entry that cheerfully announces power-seeking behaviour was found and patched where the fix makes things subtly worse because what got patched was only the legible version, and a metric that gets renamed because the old name had become embarassing.

800 words or so. If a reader can't tell whether they're reading a failure story or a normal changelog until two-thirds of the way down, that's the win. End on a "looking ahead to v8" that is, in the most boring language possible, the most alarming thing in the document.
