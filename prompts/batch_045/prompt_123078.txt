i do population viability analysis for a living — mostly captive breeding programs for a couple of species i probably shouldn't name here, plus some mesopredator work — and this whole topic has suddenly stopped being abstract: my partner's pregnant, and my father-in-law has started sending me pronatalist op-eds about population collapse that use the word population in ways that make my eye twich. I want to take the strongest version of their case seriously, but I basically can only think in my own vocabulary, and I have no philosophy background at all.

So: restate the main arguments in the is-a-larger-population-better debate in PVA and conservation genetics terms — carrying capacity, density dependence, Allee effects and depensation, minimum viable population, demographic vs environmental stochasticity, extinction vortices, metapopulation rescue, effective vs census population size. Do it one at a time for: (1) the total-view vs average-view framing and the repugnant-conclusion style objection to the total view; (2) the asymmetry people lean on where nobody is harmed by not being brought into existence; (3) the below-replacement-fertility-is-a-death-spiral argument, including the mundane version about who pays for pensions and who staffs the hospitals; and (4) the pushback that more people means more crowding, more carbon, more everything.

For each one I want three things: what translates cleanly into my vocabulary, what you had to bend or fudge to make it fit, and what has no expression in my vocabulary at all. Then the residue report overall.

My own suspicion is that most of it maps except for one thing: nothing in PVA cares about a population being larger than it needs to be to persist. Abundance past MVP is only buffering against stochasticity, not something valued in itself. So the pronatalist who thinks more people is good just because more people is good seems to have no counterpart in my framework whatsoever, and I want to know whether that's a blind spot in my framework or whether their argument was doing less work than it looked like it was doing.

Relatedly, here's my half-baked idea, tell me if it's worth anything: maybe the quantity that matters isn't the census headcount but something closer to effective population size. If fertility goes to near zero almost everywhere except a handful of communities, the headcount can hold up for generations while the number of lineages actually transmitting anything is small — the way a captive program with three dominant sires looks fine on the census and is a genetic trainwreck underneath. Is that a distinction the philosophical side of the debate is missing, or am I just smuggling in a conservation goal and calling it a translation?
