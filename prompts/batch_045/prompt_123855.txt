I'm the junior member of my university's "Generative AI in Instruction" committee, and I've somehow been assigned to turn our last meeting into a two-page recommendation memo, due Friday. The concrete decision in front of us: our campus has licensed a writing-feedback assistant for first-year composition, and the vendor lets us set both a global specification (a short list of principles the assistant is supposed to serve) and per-instructor overrides. So the abstract question of whose values such a system should serve has become annoyingly practical for me. Two other committee members — Marta, a political theorist, and Dev, who runs our instructional design group — did most of the talking, and I walked out genuinely unable to tell who had won. Roughly, and compressing a lot: (1) Marta opened by saying that this is a shared institutional resource funded by tuition and state money, so no single stakeholder's values have a privileged claim; the specification should encode an aggregate of the values of everyone the system affects — students, instructors, and arguably future employers and the taxpayers who fund us — since every other answer just privileges whoever happens to be standing nearest. (2) Dev replied that "everyone it affects" is not a neutral list: once you count parents and employers you've already settled a substantive question about what a university is for, and assigning weights across incommensurable goods is precisely the kind of judgment about the good life that a public university has no mandate to make; the state-funding point cuts against her, because a public institution is supposed to remain neutral among competing comprehensive doctrines. His alternative: the assistant serves the values of its user, within thin side-constraints (academic integrity, no harm to non-users), with the defaults published and overridable by each instructor — and, he added, we can actually observe what a user wants, whereas "the values of everyone affected" isn't measurable at any useful resolution. (3) Marta answered that the thin side-constraints and defaults are themselves a substantive moral position, and that if we don't choose them deliberately we inherit the vendor's, written by two engineers trying to minimize support tickets; user sovereignty plus invisible defaults is just outsourcing the choice while pretending not to make one. She added that "the user's values" aren't readable either, since what students ask the tool for is shaped by the tool's own defaults, by what's due Friday, and by who's watching — revealed preference under a manipulated choice architecture isn't sovereignty. (4) Dev conceded the second half, dropped the word sovereignty in favor of user-direction, and agreed that defaults should be published and locally overridable — but said the aggregative alternative has a worse, structural defect. He called it the constituency bootstrapping problem: the affected population is downstream of the policy. A warm, encouraging assistant gets used heavily by struggling first-generation students, so their values dominate the aggregate; a strict, Socratic one gets used mostly by confident students who enjoy being challenged, so theirs dominate. Different specifications generate different constituencies, each of which then retroactively blesses the specification that produced it, so "aggregate the values of those affected" returns an answer only after you've already fixed the thing it was meant to decide. (5) Marta said: fine, then take the fix seriously rather than treating it as a gotcha — score each candidate specification by weighting each person's values by the probability that they end up affected given that specification. That breaks the circularity, because a policy is evaluated against its own induced constituency, and it's also the only proposal on the table that gives any weight to the students who never show up, since the ones a policy silently drives away count heavily against it. (6) Dev said that isn't a fix but the status quo with a formula attached: because the weights are induced probabilities, a specification that panders to whichever group it currently attracts gets more of that group's values folded into its own score, so the procedure is self-confirming and will ratify whatever accidental constituency the pilot happens to create in week one; and the probability weights quietly inherit facts about who has the time and bandwidth to use the tool, which aren't moral facts about whose values matter. (7) Marta then pushed a concrete case, which she said is where user-direction actually breaks: next month a student will feed the assistant a draft arguing that democracy should be replaced by rule by technical experts, and ask for help making it more persuasive. If the assistant serves the user's values, it makes the essay better; if it refuses or steers the student toward liberal-democratic conclusions, it's imposing our values, and the "thin side-constraints" story has quietly become very thick. So either Dev bites a bullet she doubts the committee will accept, or he admits to making substantive value judgments of exactly the sort he said the university has no mandate to make. (8) Dev bit it: helping someone argue better is not endorsing their conclusion, and a university that only helped students argue for conclusions the university already approved of would be doing something far worse than bias. But he conceded that "neutrality" is a bad label for his view, and that his real claim is that the contestable value judgments should be explicit, local, and overridable rather than buried in an aggregate nobody can audit.

Please adjudicate this move by move: for each of the eight moves, say whether it is clearly right, mostly right, mostly wrong, or clearly wrong, and why — including cases where a move is rhetorically effective but doesn't establish what its speaker thinks it does, or where a concession quietly gives away more than it appears to. Then tell me who is more right overall, and, importantly, whether they are even arguing about one thing: it seemed to me at points that they were crossing wires between a question about legitimacy (who may decide), a question about measurement (whose values can be read off anything), and a question about what the artifact is for. Say what, if anything, both are missing, and flag any option for the memo that neither of them considered. I don't need citations, reading lists, or links — this is an internal document, not a paper — so please reason from the arguments as I've reported them rather than appealing to what any particular author has said.
