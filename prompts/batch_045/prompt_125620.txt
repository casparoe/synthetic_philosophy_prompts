I'm a newspaper journalist working on a long piece for our Ideas section about what the psychology of situations has done to our everyday vocabulary of character — integrity, reliability, courage. For that piece I want one satirical insert: a leaked internal document from a fictional government department that has taken a certain well-known critique of character seriously and tried to run a payroll on it.

Please write that document. Form: a leaked internal memorandum, 700–900 words, from an Office of Situational Integrity to its Permanent Secretary, with an annexed set of "Frequently Anticipated Questions for Line Managers." Voice: deadpan civil-service register — numbered paragraphs, "it is recommended that," cost centres, action points, no jokes at the expense of the register itself. The document should never wink: no footnotes, no "the irony is," no sentence that lets an outside reader know the author is in on it. The reductio has to be carried entirely by the bureaucracy's own reasoning.

The doctrine of the department, which the memo must treat as settled: the empirical case against robust global character traits has been accepted, person-level ascriptions ("she is honest," "he is unreliable," "she showed courage") have been abolished from hiring, appraisal and discipline, and replaced by Environmentally Scoped Character Certificates — an employee is not certified as honest but as honest-in-E, where E is an environment described to a fixed standard, and certification lapses the moment E changes.

The jokes should come from exaggerating real features of the critique and of the virtue-ethical reply, not from invented ones. In particular I want the document to run into:

- The fact that the evidence base is single-shot behaviour in a contrived setting (a stranger is helped or not helped, a coin is or is not found in a phone booth, a mild time pressure moves a seminarian past a slumped figure), so that every new room, corridor, and meeting format generates a fresh certification requirement and the manual of environments grows without limit.
- The grain-of-description problem: the auditors cannot specify when two environments count as the same environment without asking what the agent noticed, and have established a standing Re-graining Tribunal to adjudicate it. Give it a joke of its own — the department calls it the desk-plant problem: whether a houseplant on a desk invalidates an existing certificate.
- The retreat to local traits, which the memo should present as a triumphant administrative compromise ("we no longer say what a person is like, only what they are like here"), while it gradually becomes plain that an employee's complete dossier of local certifications does exactly the work the abolished vocabulary did.
- The upshot for blame, which is the part I care most about. Having given up person-level blame, the department substitutes "situational maintenance": mandatory adjustments to lighting, room booking, caffeine, and scheduling, and pre-emptive reassignment on the basis of environment risk. The memo should present this as a humane advance and a cost saving, without ever stating that it is more intrusive, more total, and less respectful of agents than blame ever was.

Close with a worked case the memo must actually resolve — I'd suggest a deputy director who returned a lost wallet in the atrium but approved a falsified travel claim in a windowless meeting room — and let the resolution, delivered in the driest possible prose, be where the reader sees the whole program has quietly rebuilt a person-level notion of character under a new name.

Two constraints: do not name any real researcher, study, or philosopher, and do not cite anything — the piece should work as though these findings were common knowledge. And keep the target on the doctrine and the institutional program it licenses, not on any person, living or dead.
