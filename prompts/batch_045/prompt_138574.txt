Hey — I'm a 3rd-year PhD student working mostly on moral psycology and virtue-theory-adjacent stuff, and I want you to act as a genuinely careful, slightly pedantic professional moral philosopher here. Please think it through properly: before you give me an answer, brainstorm for a bit about all the different things a person could be asking when they ask whether being as morally good as possible is a good ideal, and only then commit to a diagnosis. And please, no literature survey, no reading list, no citations and definately no "the literature divides into three camps" — I've read the stuff, and the fact that everyone has read the stuff is basically the problem.

So here's the situation. Our works-in-progress group has spent three sessions on Susan Wolf's paper on moral saints (Journal of Philosophy, 1982 — the one where she argues that someone who is morally excellent to the highest degree would be, if not actively offputting, at least oddly flat and single-minded, and not a model any of us should want to be or to have as a friend), and we have moved approximately zero inches. The Kantian says Wolf has built the saint wrong: a fully virtuous agent's non-moral interests aren't crowded out at all, they get taken up and ordered by the good will, so of course she isn't boring. The consequentialist says Wolf is running together two different characters — the person who does the most good overall and the person who is motivated purely by moral concern — and that once you pry them apart the maximally good person can absolutely have a cello and a great sourdough starter. The virtue theorist says the whole objection presupposes a moral/non-moral split about value that her tradition never signed onto, so there is nothing for the saint to lose in the first place. And me and one other person think Wolf basically wins and that every one of those replies has quietly changed the subject. Nobody has moved anybody.

My hypothesis — which people keep waving away instead of answering — is that at least four different questions are hiding under the one heading, and that which one you're answering settles what you say. (1) A contingent-psychology question: would a person wholly moved by moral reasons in fact lose the kind of absorbed, non-instrumental engagement that makes someone good at something or good company? (2) What I've started calling the placement problem: do the non-moral excellences get placed inside the moral, as constituents of a fully good life, or outside it, as rivals competing for the same time, money and attention? (3) A standpoint question: are we asking about the saint's own good, the world's good, or what we'd pick if we were choosing a friend or choosing a life? (4) A question about the kind of verdict on offer: what we have most reason to do versus what we have most reason to admire. Relatedly, I keep toying with a character who isn't boring at all but is what I'd call reasons-saturated — every one of her pursuits is pursued under a moral description — and I genuinely can't tell whether describing her redeems the saint or just restates the objection in different words.

So: what kind of stalemate is this? Is it verbal, a fight about how wide the term moral goes? Is it several different questions sharing one name? Is a hidden empirical question about moral motivation and whether it crowds out other motivations doing all the real work — such that some psychology result could in principle settle it? Is it a bedrock clash about the authority of the impersonal standpoint that neither side can argue for at all? Or is the question just ill-posed, and if so where exactly does it break? Ground the diagnosis in what each of these positions actually claims, including the moves I sketched above, not in a map of who published what. And then — this is the part I actually need — tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction I'm missing, a formal point about how values can combine, a finding about motivation, a reformulation of the question, or honestly nothing, because what's in play here is bedrock.
