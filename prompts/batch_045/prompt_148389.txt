I'm retired now — thirty-five years in experimental physics, mostly accelerators — and I've started reading philosophy in the evenings, which is how I ended up loosing sleep over this 'responsibility gap' talk about autonomous systems. My son-in-law works in machine learning and keeps feeding me cases. I've decided to trust my gut on the cases first and see what principle, if any, collects them. Here's where I land.

1. A self-driving outfit: a safety engineer writes a memo about a rare failure mode in low sun; a manager ships the update anyway to hit a release date; a pedestrian dies in roughly that condition. Manager: blame. Engineer: none.

2. A reinforcement-learned warehouse robot crushes a worker. Nobody can explain why it made that particular move, but the firm tested it hard, it beat the human baseline by a wide margin, and this failure wasn't foreseeable by anyone. My gut: nobody to blame. And yet I also want the firm to pay the family, and not some token amount. I hold both of those and can't say why.

3. Same kind of robot, same kind of death, except the firm skipped the field trials and shipped on the strength of simulation results. Now: blameworthy. Note that 2 and 3 differ only in what was in people's heads beforehand, not in how the death actually came about — and that bothers me a little.

4. A doctor overrules a diagnostic model's warning, for a reason she can state out loud, and the patient dies. My gut: not blameworthy, or barely.

5. A doctor follows the model because its numbers are better than hers, in a case where the model's known blind spot is spelled out in a five-page sheet she never read; the patient dies. My gut: more blameworthy than 4. She used the better-performing tool and I blame her more.

6. A hospital triage model harms patients, but the behaviour emerged from hundreds of tiny fine-tunes added by different teams over two years, each defensible on its own; nobody can trace it to any single change. Nobody to blame — and here 'nobody' annoys me in a way it didn't in 2, even though in 3 I was perfectly happy to blame a company for a messy process.

The one I go back and forth on: a lab releases open model weights, a stranger fine-tunes them into a medical advice bot, and someone dies acting on its advice. Half the time I think the lab is clean because it lost any control at release; half the time I think they put it into the world and they own what comes of it.

My own stab at a principle, call it the veto test: blame attaches to whoever was in a position to stop the system and who either knew about the risk or was being paid to look for it. It seems to deliver 1, 3 and 5, but it also seems to blame the careful doctor in 4 and to find somebody to blame in 2, where I feel there is nobody. Can it be repaired with one extra clause, or should I scrap it for something better? I'd like the principle stated precisely enough that I can run it on fresh cases, told plainly which of my verdicts it cannot save, and pointed squarely at the open-weights case. Also say whether this is a story about blame only, or whether the compensation bit in 2 needs a seperate account.
