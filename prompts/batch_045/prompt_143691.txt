I write novels — literary-ish, small press, nothing you'd have heard of — and I get the same question at every reading: which of your characters has a meaningful life? I used to dodge it, but lately I've been writing down my gut verdicts on my own characters and they don't line up at all, and I'd like to know whether there's actually a principle behind them or whether I'm just having moods.

Here's the list, minus the names and plots.

A woman who spends thirty-four years caring for a brother with severe dementia. He never recognizes her, she's exhausted and mostly unhappy, almost nobody knows she's doing it. Yes — the clearest yes I have.

A retired postman who builds an enormous model railway in his basement. He doesn't want visitors, no one ever sees it, his landlord dumps it in a skip after he dies. Yes. Quietly yes.

A man who devotes twenty-five years to becoming the best in the country at competitive pub quiz. He genuinely succeeds, he loves it, there's real excellence and a real community of people who admire him. I want to say no. This is the one that makes me suspicious of myself — maybe I'm just being a snob about quizzes — but it won't come out as yes, and it bugs me because it looks structurally identical to the model railway, which I'm sure about.

A sculptor whose dealer has been faking the exhibitions and the reviews for two decades. She believes she's celebrated, she's fulfilled, she dies happy. No. And I don't mean "no, but don't tell her"; I mean no, even though nothing in her experience is missing anything.

A novelist who writes for forty years, publishes nothing, is miserable and difficult and dies unknown; two hundred years later the manuscripts are found and change how people think about something important. Yes. Another confident yes, and I notice this one has no fulfilment and no recognition at the time.

A woman who founds a charity and runs it for thirty years, and a later study shows the intervention did essentially nothing, possibly slight harm. I can't bring myself to take it away from her. Weak yes? Yes-ish.

A man who spends twenty years planning and executing an elaborate revenge on the man who bullied him at school. He pulls it off, ruins the guy's life, feels hollow. No, and not close.

So the shape of it, as near as I can tell: the quiz one says absorbing activity plus real excellence isn't enough. The sculptor says felt fulfilment isn't enough either. The novelist and the charity one say actual impact isn't necessary. And the model railway says impact isn't even the thing that's doing the work when it's present. My tentative version, which I made up and can't quite make fit: a life is meaningful to the extent that you're gripped by something whose value isn't just that you're gripped by it, and that you'd go on with even if someone proved to you it made no difference to anyone. Call it the wouldn't-stop-if-you-found-out test. It handles the sculptor and the brother nicely, but I don't think it separates the railway from the quiz, and I'm not sure it earns the novelist's yes.

The case I actually keep going back and forth on, because it's the protagonist of the book I'm stuck on: a woman who keeps her dead mother's failing restaurant open for twenty-five years. She hates it, the food is mediocre, the town barely notices, three employees depend on the wages, and she's doing it because of a promise. I have no verdict.

Can you do reflective equilibrium on me? I'd like: a principle (or two or three candidates) stated precisely enough that I can test it, with a note on which of my cases motivates each clause; an honest account of which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and which one you'd overturn; its verdict on the restaurant; and its verdict on two characters I haven't written yet — a man who spends forty years cataloguing every fungus in his county and publishes it in a journal nobody reads, and a woman who spends twenty years raising money for a cause that forty years later is universally regarded as monstrous. Please answer in American English.
