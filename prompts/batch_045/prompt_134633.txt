Write a short story in the manner of Borges's short fictions — deadpan, footnoted, happy to invent a commentator or a translator's note — that actually argues for his view of time: that sucession isn't real, that the "I" which seems to persist from one moment to the next is a grammatical convenience, and that a present moment, properly looked at, already contains the others.

I don't want an illustration of the thesis. I want the narrator to reason his way into it, so the reader feels the pull of the denial of time rather than being told about it, and comes out unsure whether the argument is a proof or a mood.

Here's the conceit to build it on. A man is sent a notebook that records one day of his life, but written as the day appears from a second time-dimension: the observer up there moves through a time in which our entire day is laid out at once, like a landscape, so the notebook can describe what he will do at six in the evening while he is still reading about it at ten in the morning. It is accurate. He tries to act on it. Each attempt produces a further notebook, written from a third dimension, and so on without end.

Let the regress be the shape of the story and its argument, not a gimmick: decide what the infinite regress is meant to show — that positing another observer explains nothing, or that every new level smuggles back in the very succession it was meant to abolish — and land it.

Under 1000 words, no winking at the reader, nobody saying out loud that time is an illusion, and no dream ending.
