I work in hospital administration, and I sit on a committee that periodically reviews our residency training programmes. I keep running into a pattern I cannot reconstruct, and I am not asking what we ought to do about it — I want to understand what someone who defends the pattern must implicitly believe.

The pattern is this. Before a new physician is trusted with independent practice, they spend several years in a supervised ordeal: very long shifts, night rotations, a rigid hierarchy, and a striking amount of work with no training value at all — tasks any clerical employee could do, or re-presenting a case to a service that has already assessed it. The content of the work is generally conceded to be arbitrary. What is not conceded to be arbitrary is the duration and the exhaustion. When duty hours were eventually limited for patient-safety reasons, many senior clinicians objected on grounds that had nothing to do with safety: that trainees who had not been through the old schedule had not really been tested, and that shortening the hours would produce worse physicians — even though nobody could name anything that had been learned during them.

What I hear, paraphrased, is some mixture of the following: that you find out what someone is made of only once they are past their limit; that medicine is a vocation rather than a job, so the real question is not whether a trainee can do the work but whether they are the sort of person who will stay; that a profession which asks nothing of its entrants will not be trusted by patients who are in no position to judge competence for themselves; and that the hardship is what makes the final credential mean something, both to the person holding it and to everyone else.

A biologist friend tells me this is the handicap principle — that a signal is credible only because it is a burden, the way a peacock's tail is credible. But as I half-understand the idea, the cost is supposed to do its work only if it falls differently on the people one is trying to tell apart; mere expensiveness, I take it, proves nothing on its own. And I strongly suspect that here the cost falls mostly on people who are young, healthy, without caring responsibilities, and able to absorb years of low pay. That looks like it sorts by something other than dedication, and possibly by something the defenders of the practice would disown if it were put to them plainly.

So I would like the implicit theory reconstructed, and I would be grateful for specificity on the following:

1. What must the ordeal be doing for the defence of it to be coherent? Separate the candidate accounts — skill formation, screening for a hidden trait, a credibility display directed at patients or the public, a self-binding commitment undertaken by the trainee, or the maintenance of the standing of the credential — and tell me which are strictly presupposed by what defenders actually say and which merely fit comfortably with it.

2. Test the signalling account against the condition I half-remember. Who does the cost in fact fall on? If the answer is people with fewer outside options rather than people who care less about medicine, does the argument merely misfire, or does it invert and select against something the institution claims to want?

3. The parts of the practice pull against each other: hours are capped for safety while being described as formative; dedication is said to be what is selected for while almost nothing about it is measured; the work is justified as patient care while much of it has no patient in it. Is there a single view under which this is consistent, or is the practice held together by several commitments that would come apart under pressure?

4. If the ordeal is doing epistemic work, then a better measure of the same traits should license shortening it, and my committee could imagine objective assessments of clinical judgement. What does each candidate account predict about whether the profession would accept such a measure and still trust the result? Relatedly: if the cost has to be witnessed in order to do any credibility work, then the folklore about the brutal years is not incidental to the practice but part of its mechanism. Is that a fair implication to draw?

5. Finally, could a practice of this shape be at once individually rational and collectively wasteful — such that nearly everyone in it would prefer a world without the ordeal and no one can unilaterally leave it? If so, what exactly blocks exit, and is it that opting out is itself the most damaging thing a trainee could signal?

Where the signalling framework genuinely explains the arrangement, I want that; where it is functioning as a rationalisation of something else — restricted supply, inexpensive junior labour, or simple inertia — I want that said too, and I want to know what would distinguish the two from the outside.
