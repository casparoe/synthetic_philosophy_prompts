I spent a little over forty years as an experimental physical chemist and, since retiring three years ago, have been working through philosophical reading with a friend who used to teach the subject. I have assembled an argument out of premises I each find hard to deny, and it delivers a conclusion I am certain is false, and I cannot locate the fault. I would be grateful for a diagnosis.

The case is a thought experiment I read in a collection of essays perhaps fifteen years ago and can no longer find; I have seen it attributed to Daniel Dennett, though I hold no brief for that and nothing below depends on who invented it. As I remember it: there is a region of space filled with a viscous medium, churning at a fine scale; the churning throws up large, stable, long-lived configurations that drift about, collide, and recombine; these are the simmballs, and what they do looks enough like carrying and acting on messages that it is natural to describe them that way. The question the example is built to raise is whether the simmballs push the substrate around, or whether the substrate only ever pushes itself around, the simmball vocabulary being our bookkeeping. Please argue with my reconstruction rather than with the original; I take it to be a stand-in for every case where a pattern-level item is said to cause something — a gene, a selection pressure, a price, a vortex street, a gear ratio.

The argument, numbered so that you can tell me which line to give up.

(1) Closure. The medium is nomologically closed: its state at one time, together with the laws that govern it, settles its state at later times — deterministically if the medium is deterministic, up to the chances if it is not. Nothing acts on the medium from outside, and nothing over and above its state at a time contributes to what happens next.

(2) Composition. A simmball is not an extra ingredient in the careenium. It is a configuration, a pattern, of the medium. An inventory of what the careenium contains would list the medium and its states; the simmballs would not be a further entry on the list.

(3) No bonus push. What is nothing but a configuration of a stuff cannot bring more push to bear than the stuff itself has. Rearranging a thing does not add to its stock of force.

(4) The dilemma. So there are only two ways in which the simmballs could be causing anything. Either their causing it is the medium's causing it under another description, in which case no causal work has been added but only a vocabulary; or it is a second, further bringing-about of something the medium has already brought about, in which case the medium is being moved by something outside the microphysical ledger, and (1) is false.

(5) The second branch is not available to me. In forty years of laboratory work I never once needed a force that was not in the ledger, and if a pattern can inject one, the conservation laws and I would both very much like to hear the mechanism.

(6) Therefore the simmballs do no causal work. The medium pushes itself around. It may well be that the pattern-level description is the only one tractable for a creature of my bandwidth, and it may be that the medium's disposition to evolve in ways that track those patterns is the whole of what makes the description worth having. But the claim that the simmballs moved anything is, in the causal order of things, a façon de parler.

(7) And now what I cannot accept. Nothing in (1) through (5) used anything special about simmballs. Every higher-level cause is a configuration of some underlying stuff and is said to cause things: the pressure of a gas on a piston, a convection cell driven by a temperature gradient, a vortex shedding off a cylinder, a gear train's ratio, a dislocation moving through a crystal. By the same five steps, none of them does causal work either, and every causal claim ever made at a level above the microscopic is shorthand for a claim in microscopic vocabulary that no one making it had in mind.

(8) That is false, or at any rate I will not have it: it would make a mystery of the only explanatory practice I have ever trusted, and of every hour I spent in a laboratory measuring things like pressures and fluxes. So (7) is false. But I cannot see which step of (1) through (6) I am entitled to surrender, since each of them strikes me as true and (6) seems to follow.

A younger correspondent, a computer scientist, proposed a test which I had hoped would settle the matter and which has instead made it worse. Call a medium docile, relative to a coarse-grained vocabulary, if whenever two fine states of a region realise the same coarse pattern, the later coarse-grained history of that region is the same. The test runs as follows. (a) Take a region while a simmball is passing through it and rearrange the fine detail of the medium in that region in any way that leaves the simmball's shape, position and motion exactly as they were. In a docile medium the subsequent coarse-grained history is unchanged: the same message continues, the same downstream effects follow. (b) Now attempt the converse — hold all the fine detail fixed and alter the pattern. There is no such intervention to perform; altering the pattern just is altering some of the detail. (c) Hence there is no way of intervening on a simmball except by intervening on the medium, and what comes of such interventions is settled by the medium's own dynamics. He offered this as confirmation of (6), and I find that I cannot tell whether it confirms (6) or quietly subverts it. Something about it makes me uneasy, and I suspect that whatever is wrong with my argument is concentrated there.

Of the premises, the two I am least willing to surrender are (1) and (2): I would rather be told that my stubbornness about closure and about composition is itself the mistake than be talked out of either by hand-waving, but if that is where the error lies, say so plainly.

A request about manner. Answer as you would to someone who knows the physics and not the philosophy: define any term of art the first time you use it. Do not direct me to a literature; my library access lapsed with my employment, and I want the reasoning rather than the references, though if a move has a customary name I am glad to be told it in passing. Please structure the reply in the following sections, in this order.

1. The step to reject — about 150 words. Name the line or the suppressed assumption, say in two sentences what is wrong with it, and state what I should say in its place.

2. Why it looked safe — about 200 words. Explain what makes that step so nearly irresistible, and why the argument does not advertise its own defect.

3. Prices — about 400 words. Go through the other places I might instead have dug in — (1), (3), the exhaustiveness claimed in (4), anything the argument assumes without stating, and the move from (7) to my refusing it — and for each tell me what I would be committing myself to if I gave it up. A table is welcome if it makes the comparison sharper.

4. Which way the scramble test cuts — about 250 words. Settle (a) through (c) one way or the other, and say what feature of the test is doing the work.

5. What to say about the simmballs — about 250 words. Give me the positive answer to the original question, in a form that a working physical scientist would not have to take on faith.

6. Confidence — about 120 words. How confident you are in the diagnosis as a whole, and the single point in your reply you are least sure of.
