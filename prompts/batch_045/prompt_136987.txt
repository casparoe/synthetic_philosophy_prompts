I teach an introductory course on ethics and public policy, and every year my students can recite the standard moves about weighing the future against the present while remaining completely unmoved by them — the distinction between the respectable reasons for treating a distant good as mattering less and the disreputable one strikes them as a terminological quibble. So this year I want to open the unit with a story rather than a lecture, and I would like you to write it. Here is the setting I have in mind, which you should feel free to fill in or adjust: a small port town a century or so from now, living behind a storm barrier that has to be periodically rebuilt and raised, governed by a council that must approve any proposal to put off a cost, and served by a tiny office — two or three people, a room above the harbour office, a ledger kept twice — whose job is to review such proposals before they go to a vote. The protagonist is the junior person in that office, and the file on her desk is a request to defer a reinforcement of the barrier for forty years.

What I want the story to carry is the labour of taking that deferral apart. Some of the case for waiting is not about the future at all: money spent now is money not spent on something else that would itself have paid off by then. Some of it is about whether there will be a town, or the same town, in forty years, and whether the barrier will still be doing the work anyone thinks it is doing. Some of it is the thought that the people living then will be better off than the people living now, so that the same sum of money would be a smaller thing for them to lose. And some of it, she suspects, is nothing but the fact that the cost is later and the benefit is now, the bare later-ness of it, wearing the costume of one of the other reasons. She needs a way to tell these apart, and I would like you to give her one — perhaps she re-runs the decision with the two groups of townsfolk traded round, or she conducts the hearing in her head as it would be conducted by someone who will be forty years old when the bill arrives, or she imagines the council members not knowing which of the two periods they will live through. But I do not want the test she uses to be simply vindicated. I want it to run into trouble in at least two ways, and I want the trouble to be specific rather than atmospheric: perhaps the later townsfolk really will be richer, so that the trade genuinely does look different from their side and the test's verdict is less clear than it first appeared; perhaps who exists in forty years depends on what is spent now, so there is no fixed group whose answer the test could collect; perhaps the council that defers the work will, when the bill comes due, defer it again, and the office exists precisely because everyone knows this. I would also like her to notice what the number she has been handed does at the far end: that at some point it stops mattering how large the later benefit is, because anything far enough out has shrunk below whatever would justify any cost at all — and I would like her to notice what that implies about her own job.

Two constraints on how you write it. First: the story should never use the words discount, discount rate, utility, time preference, or social welfare, and should name no philosopher or economist; the distinctions have to be carried entirely by what the characters do, want, calculate, and notice, so that a reader who has never opened a textbook still feels where the argument bites. Second: do not tidy the ending into either a sermon about our duties to posterity or a wry shrug about human shortsightedness — let the story end where the difficulty actually is, with her having to write the recommendation anyway. Aim for something in the range of twelve hundred to eighteen hundred words, in continuous prose throughout: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, no italicised section breaks or epigraphs, no afterword, author's note, or explanatory coda in which you tell me what the story was about — the prose and the dialogue have to do all of it, and the piece should read like something I could hand out on the first day of term without anyone guessing it was written to make a point.
