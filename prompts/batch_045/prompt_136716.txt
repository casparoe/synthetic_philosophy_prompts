I work on post-training for a large language model. Concretely: we sample several completions from the current policy, present them to paid annotators as a menu, record which one they pick, fit a reward model to those pairwise choices, and then fine-tune the policy with a KL penalty towards the base model. I have about six years of experience doing this; I know inverse reinforcement learning and the standard non-identifiability results well enough to have argued about them in design reviews, and I took one undergraduate microeconomics course fifteen years ago that I have mostly forgotten. I have no philosophy.

The question I keep running into is whether the reward we recover means anything over and above "a compression of the choices we happened to record". Three features of our setup make it sharper than the textbook version. First, self-generated menus: the options we offer are sampled from the current policy, so as training proceeds the menus drift towards whatever that policy produces, and completions it assigns low probability never get offered at all. Some of my colleagues say this is an exploration problem we could fix with a fixed, policy-independent proposal distribution over completions. Others say the problem is deeper: that for the question we actually care about — whether a completion is good for the user in a sense that is not merely "would be picked from the menus this policy generates" — no amount of coverage helps, because there is nothing there to recover. Second, the deployed policy maximises reward minus a KL penalty, so what the annotators' choices reveal is not the argmax of the reward but the optimum of a regularised objective. Third, the annotators are not the users: the reward model is trained on annotator choices and then used to serve a different population, and we average across annotators to obtain a single number.

I am told there is a long-standing dispute in the foundations of economics and decision theory about exactly this: whether a person's utility is nothing over and above the structure of their actual choices, or is something their choices only partly reveal; and whether the quantity that rationalises choices is the quantity one should care about when acting on that person's behalf. I would like that dispute translated into my vocabulary rather than explained to me in its own.

So: restate the main positions in this dispute one at a time, in the terms I actually use — reward, policy, KL regularisation, trajectories, pairwise preference data, non-identifiability of the reward up to potential-based shaping, reward hacking, off-policy evaluation, distribution shift, held-out prompts, latent variables, a generative model of the annotator. I expect roughly four or five distinguishable positions; if you think there are fewer, say so and say why. For each, name which object in my stack plays the role of the utility function, which plays the role of the choice behaviour, and what the position's distinctive claim becomes as a claim about those objects. Where the standard result that observed choices can be rationalised by some utility function exactly when they satisfy a certain no-cycles condition has an analogue in my setting, state what that analogue is.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want: which claims translate with no loss, which translate only if I quietly add something my vocabulary does not contain, and which have no expression in it at all. I have two suspicions I want tested rather than flattered. First, that my vocabulary can express "the reward is not identified from this data" but not "there is no fact of the matter about the reward off this distribution", so it cannot state the disagreement between my two colleagues, and both camps end up saying "underdetermined" about what are really different claims. Second, that we already use the term reward hacking as though the reward model could be wrong about something other than our own dataset, which is a commitment the more austere of these positions forbids — and I want to know whether that is us smuggling in a notion we have officially disowned, or whether it shows that the austere position cannot be stated at all in a vocabulary that has to be usable by engineers. Finish by telling me, for each item of residue, whether it points to a gap in my vocabulary that I ought to fill, or to a piece of the philosophers' dispute that is less substantive than it sounds.

Please write in British English and keep the whole answer under a thousand words. Do not give me a reading list and do not cite papers or name authors: I have no library access from here and I want the arguments, not the references. Where you coin a mapping rather than report a standard one, mark it as your own.
