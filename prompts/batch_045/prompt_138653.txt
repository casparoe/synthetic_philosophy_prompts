I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums since the Overcoming Bias days, and the old "a rose by any other name would smell as sweet" line keeps doing argumentative work there that I don't think it can bear. It came up most recently in a thread on Hofstadter's "A Person Paper on Purity in Language" — the satire published under his pen name William Satire, written entirely in a purified English in which every "-man" compound has been rebuilt on "person", so that the narrator has to mount his defence of the old generic "man" in the very vocabulary he is mocking. A commenter I otherwise respect closed the topic with the rose line: since labels are arbitrary, "chairman" versus "chairperson" is a matter of taste, and anyone who thinks otherwise is confusing a change in words with a change in the world.

I am fairly confident that inference is broken, but I can't say where. So: lay out the menu of precise theses that "a rose by any other name" could be asserting in this setting, ordered from the flattest to the most committal, and say for each whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible claim, or straightforwardly false — and, the part I actually need, whether it would license his conclusion.

To make the verdicts usable, here are two distinctions I have been trying and failing to fit the slogan onto; discard them if they don't help. First, three things a term like "chairman" could be: (a) extensionally inclusive, covering adults of any sex; (b) assertorically inert, in that no speaker who uses it asserts or conversationally implies that the occupant is male, and no hearer is misled about any particular occupant; (c) default-setting, in that it determines what a hearer pictures when the referent is left unspecified, what counts as the unmarked case, and which cases therefore need explicit flagging. My hunch is that the slogan says something true about (a) and (b) and nothing whatsoever about (c), and that (c) is where the real dispute lives — but I can't get the candidate readings to line up with that distinction.

Second, I can't tell whether my disagreement with him is even a causal one. Part of me wants to read the rose line as a causal claim — swapping the word produces no difference in imagery, attitudes, or institutions — while the reformers' claim may instead be that a pattern of word use partly constitutes a practice rather than causing something separate from it, in which case "it makes no causal difference" would be irrelevant rather than decisive. And there seems to be a reading that cuts the other way entirely: if the two labels really are intersubstitutable without change of meaning, that very arbitrariness is what leaves a speaker's choice free to carry information about the speaker. I don't trust any of this, which is why I'm asking.

Give me exactly five readings, ordered from the least to the most committal, each stating the claim, its status (platitude, substantive-and-arguably-true, or false), and whether it gets my commenter his conclusion — including which reading he is almost certainly relying on. Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion.
