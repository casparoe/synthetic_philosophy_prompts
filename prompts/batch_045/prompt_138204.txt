A popular book on consciousness that I'm partway through has a section on the colour phi phenomenon, and I can't tell how much of it to trust. Reconstructing the passage from memory (it's my paraphrase, not the book's wording), it runs roughly like this:

"In colour phi, a spot in one colour is flashed at one location — say red — and a fraction of a second later a spot in another colour, say green, at a nearby location; subjects report a single spot travelling between the two positions and changing colour partway along its path. But the reported change occurs at a point the second spot has not yet reached, so the original red experience must have been rewritten after the fact: the brain went back and edited the memory of what you saw. That's the Orwellian story. The alternative, the Stalinesque story, is that the entire episode — motion, colour change and all — was fabricated after the second flash, with no earlier version of the experience ever occurring at all. Dennett's point is that no experiment could ever tell these two apart, since any probe you run is itself just one more memory that can be revised in turn; so there is no fact of the matter about when or where conscious experience occurs, and the stream of consciousness is an illusion."

The book then generalises: the same reasoning is supposed to show that when the brain "fills in" your blind spot, or the periphery of a visual scene, there is no fact of the matter about whether you consciously see the filled-in region at all — you simply judge afterwards that the scene was continuous.

I'm inclined to accept the broad thrust, that something in this neighbourhood tells against a Cartesian theatre, but I'm suspicious of how the two stories are characterised and of how much the conclusion claims, and I have no idea whether the generalisation is one a defender of the argument would want. Please go through it more or less sentence by sentence: what someone working on this would grant, what is caricature or slide, what the argument would have to establish to reach its conclusion, and what exactly the Orwellian and Stalinesque hypotheses are meant to contrast. Does the blind-spot generalisation follow from the colour phi case, or does it trade on a different issue? As you go, flag which of your verdicts are settled among people who know the literature and which are genuinely contested — I'd rather not have a disputed reading reported as the standard one.
