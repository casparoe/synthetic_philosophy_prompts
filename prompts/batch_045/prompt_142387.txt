I'm in the upper sixth and run my school's history and politics society — about sixteen of us, years 11 to 13 — and I have one forty-five-minute lunchtime slot to fill. I want them to feel, rather than be told, how much of a forecast about the future of a state is settled before any evidence is weighed, simply by the choice of which past cases it gets compared to.

Please design a runnable game for that slot. My one strong constraint is that the case should be invented rather than historical: a made-up seventy-year-old maritime confederation, say, with a written constitution, a recent decisive naval victory and a succession dispute, so that nobody can win by knowing how it turned out and nobody can argue about what really happened in 476 or 1947.

What I have in mind, though you should improve on it, is that members first commit privately to a forecast about whether the confederation survives another fifty years on the strength of the details I hand them; then have to choose, before anything is resolved, between two drawers of past cases with very different survival rates — all long-lived states as against all maritime confederations, perhaps — with the base rates fixed in advance and printed inside each drawer; and then discover that one further true detail about the same confederation files it just as naturally under a third drawer whose rate points the other way. Stakes should be trivial — a tally or house points, nothing that needs real money — and there should be no lecture up front: I don't want anyone hearing the terms reference class, base rate or outside view until after the debrief.

Give me the rules, the materials, the payoffs, and what each person physically does and when; then a debrief that maps particular moments in the room onto particular parts of the idea. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I suspect someone will pick the drawer that merely rationalises the forecast they already gave — and how I should handle it. Be frank about which feature of the outside view the game cannot stage and which I will simply have to say out loud, and tell me what would change your mind about the design: what result in the room would convince you the game had taught the wrong lesson.
