I keep catching myself either defending or apologizing for not looking harder, and my gut verdicts don't add up to any rule I can actually state. So here are the cases, then my own stab at a principle, and I want you to repair or replace it.

- Tuesday dinner in my own city, ninety seconds on an app, we eat out every week: obviously fine. Anyone who spends longer is a bit strange. Related but different: my colleague has eaten lunch at the same mediocre ramen place nine years running. Also fine, I think, even though it has the same shape as things I do call laziness.
- Used car: one afternoon, a checklist, bought the third one that passed it. Fine.
- House: my partner and I wrote down what we needed before we looked, and bought the second place that cleared the list. Fine — but I notice that if I'd done the same in a market where houses sit for months I'd call it impatient.
- Surgeon for my dad's operation: two weeks of opinions, then we just took the person the oncologist named. No comparison shopping on outcomes or volume. I think that was fine, and I'd advise it again. But I would never satisfice on the price of the same operation, and I can't say what the difference is supposed to be.
- A doctor who stops once she has a diagnosis that fits the symptoms: bad. That's a named error, and the badness isn't just that she might be wrong — it's that she stopped *because* something fit. But I don't think that commits me to "always keep looking," since running every test is also bad.
- Me, married at 22 to the first person I seriously dated, no playing the field: fine, and I get irritated at the "you didn't search" framing.
- Me at 28 taking the first job offer out of grad school, three weeks to decide, offer expires, one hiring cycle a year: that was a mistake, I under-searched, and I'd say the same to a friend taking the first postdoc without asking around.
- Statistical software: same package for ten years, I know the alternatives are better now, switching costs about a week, and I keep not switching. I go back and forth. Half the time this is just rational; half the time "satisficing" is a polite word for inertia. What makes this one hard is that my sense of what counts as good enough seems to have quietly slid down to whatever I already have.
- Renting in a city where the order of the listings is chosen by a platform that makes money if I sign early: I still don't think the answer is to read three hundred listings. But taking the first place above my threshold is now obviosly exploitable, and the fix feels like committing in advance to seeing twelve of them — not optimizing harder, but binding myself to a procedure.

My own stab at a principle: satisficing is fine when the value of the thing partly depends on your committing to it and investing in it (a marriage, a city, a lab, a kid), and is laziness when the value is already fixed and just waiting to be measured (salaries, airfares, car prices). But that gives verdicts I don't like. Grad school looks like the first kind, and I think people should shop hard for grad school. And a surgeon's skill is fixed in advance, so my rule says compare-shop, which isn't what I did and isn't what I'd recommend.

So: what's the actual principle, or what are the two or three candidates? I want it stated precisely enough to apply, with a note saying which of my cases motivates each clause. Then tell me what it predicts for cases I haven't listed — choosing a PhD advisor, whether to keep looking at apartments in a city I'm leaving in fourteen months, whether to switch fields at 45, whether to run one more diagnostic test, a language model deciding how long to keep thinking on an easy question. And be straight with me about which verdicts it can't save: if the spouse case and the job-offer case have to come out the same way, tell me which one I'm wrong about and why.
