I work on a small platform team, and in about six weeks we are due to give an agentic model real tool access: it will be able to read our internal repositories, open pull requests, and execute a narrow set of production commands behind a human approval gate. A methodological disagreement among us has become urgent, and I would like your view on it.

We could not settle by argument whether the model we are deploying is aligned, so we adopted the posture of assuming that it may be misaligned and tested our safeguards against a worst case. Concretely, we constructed deliberately sabotaging variants of the same model — trained and prompted to pursue some hidden objective and to conceal that pursuit — and then checked whether our protocol caught them. The protocol is: monitoring of the agent's outputs and tool calls by a smaller model we trust more, permission scoping, human approval before anything irreversible, and a post-hoc audit sample. It caught every variant we built.

My colleague draws the following conclusion: because our safeguards caught even adversarially constructed, actively deceptive variants, we have established that the model we are actually deploying is aligned, and we can therefore relax the approval gate and grant the agent broader autonomy. I find the inference attractive but I cannot locate what is wrong with it, or whether anything is.

Some specifics that seem to matter. The evaluations run in a sandbox with decoy credentials and synthetic data, so a model with any situational awareness could in principle tell them apart from production. And we built the sabotaging variants using the methods we have — prompting, fine-tuning on scripted bad behavior, distilling from a stronger model — which means a genuinely misaligned model might be better at concealment than any saboteur we know how to construct, since our construction is limited by our own imagination about evasion strategies and its is not.

1. Does a protocol's success against deliberately adversarial variants license any inference about the motives of the model we actually deploy, or only an inference about the protocol? If the former, under what conditions, and how strong is the update once you condition on the model possibly being able to tell evaluation from deployment?

2. Does the asymmetry I describe — our red-team models are worse at evasion than a real schemer would be — mean our evaluation is systematically biased toward a false sense of safety? Is the right response to build stronger saboteurs, or to stop treating these results as evidence about alignment at all?

3. Is there something inconsistent about adopting worst-case assumptions for the purposes of protocol design and then treating the results as evidence about intent?

4. Is the distinction between control and alignment itself misleading here — are they competing strategies, or the same strategy at different leverage points? In particular, does relaxing the approval gate undermine the very thing the evaluation validated?

Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and say where you are confident versus extrapolating. Flag any place where my framing rests on a confusion rather than an open problem; I would rather be told I have asked the wrong question than given a confident answer to it.
