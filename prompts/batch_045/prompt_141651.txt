Hi — I sit on our university's animal ethics committee and we've just got our first serious invertebrate application and nobody wants to wing it. A marine bio lab wants to put about 240 shore crabs through a shock-avoidance task: brief electric shocks in a shuttle box, plus a condition where they dose the animals with an analgesic first to see whether that changes how quickly they learn to avoid the shock. Our rules only cover vertebrates; invertebrates fall under a vague clause saying we should give them due consideration, and nothing more, and we know an octopus proposal and a crayfish teaching lab are coming next term, so we'd like a rule we can reuse rather than re-litigating this every time.

Two things keep coming up in our email thread and I want the memo to actually deal with them. First, the PI says the crab has no brain worth the name, just distributed ganglia, so there's no single subject there to be better or worse off, and the avoidance behaviour is just reweighted reflexes — the strongest version of that point being that the very same behaviour shows up in cases everyone is happy to call mere nociception. Second, one of our members wants to use trade-off evidence as a consciousness marker: an animal put in a mildly aversive condition will give up something else it wants, and it will pay more to escape when the aversive thing is worse, which looks like the behaviour tracking how bad it feels rather than just how intense the stimulus is. But I'm not sure a marker worked out on vertebrates carries the same weight in an animal whose neural hardware is completely different — call it a portability discount if that's the right frame, or tell me it's the wrong way to think about it.

Draft us the decision memo: approve, reject, or approve with conditions — pick one and commit to it — with reasons the lab could actually respond to, concrete conditions and a timeline if it comes to that, plus an honest statement of the strongest consideration on the losing side (including the fact that whatever we rule here will be cited back at us for the octopus and for every insect study after that). Also settle the threshold question: should partial evidence buy partial protection graded by strength of evidence, or do we hold out until something crosses a line, and if so what line? And end with one short paragraph on what would change your mind — what finding or argument would flip the verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to be wrong. Keep it short enough to actually get read before Thursday.
