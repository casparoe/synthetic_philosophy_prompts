The proposition I care about: the problem of old evidence is a genuine, still-standing objection to Bayesian confirmation theory. Call it "genuine" if all three of the following hold: the canonical cases really do exhibit an agent who already assigned probability 1 (or negligibly less) to E and then devised H; the intuitive verdict that E confirmed H is roughly right; and no available Bayesian treatment recovers that verdict without smuggling it in — that is, without helping itself to a probability assignment, a counterfactual prior, or a bridging proposition that the agent had no independent warrant for. My own credence that the problem is genuine in this sense is about 0.55. A colleague whose judgment I respect puts it at 0.15, and I cannot locate the disagreement.

Give me your own number, but the decomposition matters more than the number. Here are the sub-questions I think the total depends on; assign each a rough probability with one line of justification, add or merge terms as you see fit, and say explicitly which of them you are treating as independent and what the dependence assumption is doing:

1. That in the canonical case (general relativity and the decades-old anomaly in Mercury's orbit) the agent's probability for E really is 1, or close enough that the confirmatory boost would be negligible — rather than the datum being subtly new: re-measured, made precise, or reinterpreted only under the new theory.
2. That the intuitive verdict is trustworthy as stated — that accommodated evidence supports a theory about as strongly as a novel prediction would have, rather than the accommodated/predicted distinction marking a real difference in support that our hindsight inflates away.
3. That the correct diagnosis of what is learned is not E but that H entails E — so that the difficulty is really about updating on a discovered entailment, and about whether that can be modeled without presupposing logical omniscience or a prior for the entailment-claim that itself encodes the answer.
4. That the bridging move succeeds: expand the hypothesis space by a proposition expressing that H entails E, treat the discovery as evidence bearing on that proposition, and let confirmation of H flow through it.
5. That the counterfactual-prior family succeeds: evaluate H against what the agent would have assigned had she not known E, rather than against her actual prior, which was formed with E already in hand.
6. That the framework can express the constraint I keep wanting — do not count E twice, once in constructing H and again as independent confirmation of it — or, if it cannot, that the constraint is even coherent, given that the same data shape the priors.
