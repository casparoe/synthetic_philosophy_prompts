For about fifteen years I've worked on distributed systems, and for the past two I've kept a long-form blog on engineering judgment — how technical teams decide things under uncertainty, mostly for readers who are engineers themselves. Over the last year I've drifted into epistemology by way of an incident at work: a colleague and I reviewed the same four-hundred-line diff, with identical tests, identical logs, and identical specs in front of us, and reached opposite conclusions about whether a race condition was reachable. Neither of us could point to a single thing the other had missed. I've been reading around the peer-disagreement literature since then, and I've drafted roughly two-and-a-half thousand words which I intend to publish without citations — I want the argument to carry itself rather than lean on names — but before I do, I'd like someone to tell me which parts of it are actually wrong. A friend with a philosophy PhD has agreed to read it, and I'd like to arrive at her comments having already found the worst problems myself.

The core of the piece is below, more or less as it stands. The framing device is that a test suite can be self-validating: if a test calls the very function it is testing in order to compute the expected value, it will agree with that function no matter what, and two suites that exercise the same code paths are not really independent checks even if different people wrote them. I use that to motivate three moves. First, the residue rule: my original evidence E may be used to discount a peer's contrary verdict only to the extent that E contains a component the peer's verdict could not already have absorbed — evidence the peer lacked, or could not have processed. If coverage is genuinely identical and we still disagree, there is no residue, and steadfastness is irrational; if I was the one paged at 3 a.m. and saw the logs, I have residue, and I may discount. Second, the clean-room requirement: residue counts only if it is not itself an output of the very malfunction the disagreement hints at. A memory of having carefully re-checked my arithmetic is no good against the hypothesis that I am too exhausted to add, because that memory is produced by the same tired mechanism; the residue has to be more likely if I am right than if the suspected fault is real, otherwise it screens off nothing. Third, a split I call residual humility: even in the best case the residue rule licenses holding on to the proposition, but it never licenses holding my calibration fixed, because the bare fact that someone competent disagrees is evidence about the channel rather than the content, and residue reaches me through that same channel. In the code-review case, I may keep my judgment that the race is unreachable while lowering my estimate of how often my unreachability judgments are correct — and over a career that second concession is the expensive one. The case I'm least sure about is disagreement over something a priori among peers with the same evidence and the same training: coverage is identical, so residue is empty, so my rule says split the difference, yet this is exactly where staying put feels most obligatory. I also can't shake a regress worry, since whether the peer had access to E is itself something I judge using E.

What I want, specifically: (1) Have I reconstructed the dialectic fairly, or have I built a straw version of the two standard camps — the conciliationist one and the steadfast one — that neither would recognise? Tell me where I've misdescribed what the other side is committed to. (2) Which of the three moves is load-bearing, and which is redundant given the others? My suspicion is that the residue rule and the clean-room requirement collapse into one condition once you ask what it means for the peer to have already absorbed a piece of evidence, but I can't tell whether that's a real collapse or just my formalism being sloppy. (3) Give me the strongest objection to the package from the steadfast direction, stated as strongly as you can, and tell me where in the text I should concede it rather than pretend it isn't there — I'd rather be seen conceding something than be caught missing it. (4) Is the a priori case a genuine counter-example, or does the rule get it right and my intuition is just a local artifact of how much I trust proofs? (5) Editorial: my readers are engineers, not philosophers — is the testing analogy earning its place as the spine of the essay, or is it decoration that smuggles in assumptions about independence that I'd be better off stating directly? If you think a different analogy does the work better, say which and why.
