I'm a senior at my high school and I help run our philosophy-of-science reading group; we've just started a unit on whether thought experiments can be a source of knowledge about the natural world, and I'm drafting the closing paper for the unit, so I want to get the structure of the argument right before I commit to it. The framing that shows up everywhere we look — in our textbook, in popular physics books, and especially from the most physics-enthusiastic guy in our group — is that a thought experiment is simply an experiment run in the laboratory of the mind: the imagination is the lab, the scenario you set up is the apparatus, holding everything fixed except one feature is the control of variables, and your sense of what would happen is the instrument reading. He uses it to license a fairly strong conclusion, namely that armchair reasoning about an imaginary case can tell us something new and nontrivial about how the actual world works, without our going out and gathering any new observations — the way Galileo is supposed to have refuted the heavier-falls-faster view, or the way Einstein's chase after a light beam is supposed to have revealed something about time. My other friend in the group says the analogy is doing all the work: in a real experiment the world pushes back and can surprise you, whereas in the imaginary lab the only thing that pushes back is your own expectation, which is itself downstream of the theory you are testing.

Our faculty advisor has a way of sharpening the disagreement that I want to use in my paper, and I want to see how it interacts with the analogy. She says we should separate three kinds of case: an unpacking case, where the conclusion follows from the theory under test alone and the scenario is just a vivid way of displaying a consequence already contained in it; a clash case, where the scenario forces you to see that two things you are committed to cannot both be true; and an import case, where the conclusion does not follow unless you add some premise that neither the theory under test nor a bare description of the scenario supplies — a symmetry principle, a simplicity or non-vacuity assumption, a claim about what is or isn't possible in principle, and so on. She calls the general worry the ledger problem: for any thought experiment you should keep an explicit ledger of the premises you actually spent, and check whether the interesting conclusion was paid for out of the imaginary setup or smuggled in from outside it. I'd like that distinction applied to three specific cases: Galileo's argument about the heavy and light stone tied together, Newton's spinning bucket of water, and Einstein imagining himself running alongside a light wave at the speed of light. For each, what exactly is being imported, if anything, and where did that imported premise get its own warrant?

What I'd like from you is an audit of the laboratory-of-the-mind analogy rather than a general essay on thought experiments. Please give me the correspondences explicitly — a table is fine: what plays the role of the apparatus, the variable, the measurement, the instrument, replication, calibration, and experimental error. Then say which of the disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion my friend wants, and which are just loose ends: for instance, whether it matters that no one can independently re-run the imaginary setup, that the instrument is the same faculty that generated the expectation being tested, that the setup has to be nomologically possible rather than merely conceivable, and that an imagined outcome cannot be a genuinely new datum about a magnitude the way a real reading can. Does the strong conclusion survive once the ledger is made explicit — that thought experiments give us knowledge of the world without new observation — or does it collapse into something weaker, like the claim that they can reveal inconsistency, or unpack what a theory already says, or expose which of our commitments is carrying the weight? And if the laboratory picture is misleading, is there a better comparison — something like a proof with suppressed premises, a simulation whose value depends entirely on how the model was validated beforehand, or a mirror rather than a window?
