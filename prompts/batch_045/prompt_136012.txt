I am writing a novel, and there is one document inside it that I cannot finish without help.

The novel's narrator is a young archivist preparing the private papers of the book's central figure — a woman who was blind from birth, recovered a damaged and partial sight at thirty-three after an operation, and afterwards became a well-known essayist on perception and on her own case. Among her papers is a notebook list she headed simply "Cases": roughly a dozen imagined subjects, each with a one-word verdict and a sentence in the margin. My narrator copies the list out and finds that the final entry has no verdict — only the case, the word "Verdict:", and a blank. She has to decide what belongs in the blank, and so do I.

The entries, as she left them (the marginal notes are my own invention, not quotations from anyone):

1. *The globe and the cube.* A man blind from birth who can tell the two apart at once by touch; his sight is restored at thirty; the objects are set before him and he may not touch them. — Verdict: No. Note: "The hand and the eye are not two words for one thing. They are two languages, and he has heard only one of them spoken."

2. *The same man, six weeks later,* after he has been left alone to look at his own possessions and handle them as he pleases. — Verdict: Yes, and without effort. Note: "Nothing in him was ever missing. He was unpractised, not incomplete."

3. *The same man, at the first session, given a matching task instead of a naming task:* he holds an object out of sight, is shown two objects, and is asked which of them is in his hands. — Verdict: Yes, but only barely, and not in words. Note: "What he lacks is not the shape. It is the leave to call it by its name."

4. *The two balls.* Two balls alike in every respect the hand can detect; he is told one is red and one green; at the first moment of sight, which is which? — Verdict: No. Note: "Colour is the eye's own business, and the hand has nothing to say about it."

5. *The man who lost his sight at five and recovered it at forty.* Globe and cube, first moment. — Verdict: Yes. Note: "He has forgotten nothing. There was never anything for him to learn."

6. *The blind geometer.* A woman blind from birth who has never handled a solid with any attention beyond the ordinary business of dressing and eating, but who has taken in the whole of solid geometry by ear and by raised type and can prove a great deal about cubes she has never held. A wooden cube and a wooden ball are set before her at the first moment of sight. — Verdict: No. Note: "A definition is not an appearance. She knows everything about cubes except the one thing she is being asked."

7. *The echo walker.* A man blind from birth who finds his way by clicking, and who can tell a round pillar from a flat hoarding at ten paces by the sound. First moment of sight: the pillar and the hoarding, across the street. — Verdict: first written "Yes — badly, and only for the pillar," then struck through and replaced with "No. Knowing where a thing stands is not knowing how it looks standing there." The deletion is legible.

8. *The grid patient.* Sight not restored by surgery on the eye but supplied by an implanted array that yields a few hundred coarse points of light, which she must learn to use by moving her head. After a year of practice: can she match a shape she sees through the array against one she holds in her hands? — Verdict: Yes, for gross shape, slowly, and only while scanning. Note: "The machine hands her the alphabet. Reading is still her own work, one letter at a time."

9. *The unfinished entry.* A woman blind from birth, now thirty, who for nine years has used a camera-to-tongue device: a small camera on her spectacles feeds its picture to a grid of a few hundred electrodes on her tongue, so that what she receives is a moving pattern of tingles which she has learned to interpret. She can pick out a mug, a doorway, the arrangement of a face, the edge of a table; she says she "sees with her tongue," though she insists the tingles feel like nothing visual at all. What she has had for nine years, in other words, is a continuous stream of information structured exactly as an optic array is structured — perspective shifts as she turns her head, occlusion as things pass behind one another, looming as she walks forward — but delivered to the skin. Her sight is then restored by surgery. At the first session a wooden cube and a wooden ball are set before her, she may not touch them, and she is asked which is which. She is then asked a second question: does the cube look the way she expected it to look?

What I need:

First, the verdict she would most likely have written, with the sentence or two that belongs in the margin beside it, in her voice — clipped, concrete, a little oracular, never technical.

Second, the principles that would make the list as a whole hang together. I assume there is more than one rule that fits entries 1 through 8 equally well and that comes apart on entry 9; I want them set out plainly, each with what it predicts about both of the questions in the unfinished case, and with your reasons for preferring one over the others. Say too which of the earlier entries is the closest analogue to entry 9, and whether the whole list is more coherent if the struck-through "Yes" in entry 7 is restored.

Third, if the list genuinely underdetermines the verdict, tell me so plainly and tell me what my narrator would be committing the character to either way — what she would be saying about perception by writing "Yes," and what she would be saying by writing "No."

Fourth, one further case I could add to the list, or one small change to entry 9, that would best separate the surviving rules from each other. I would rather add a page to the notebook than leave the scene resting on a coincidence.

I do not want sources, names, or reading of any kind — I have no library here and my character is not citing anybody. Please answer in American English, with American spelling and idiom throughout, including in the lines you write for her.
