I work in causal inference in epidemiology — I basically live inside DAGs, potential outcomes, identification strategies, mediation decompositions, transportability, negative controls — and I've been reading about the Weber thesis on my own, with no philosophy or history training, and I keep bouncing off it. I can't tell whether there's a real causal claim in there or just a good story with causal grammar draped over it.

Here's my take on the setup so we don't spend the whole answer arguing about the history (correct me if I've got it wrong, but briefly): the claim is that a certain ascetic Protestant package — anxiety about ones own election, plus a methodical disciplined attitude to work, plus a horror of luxury — turned up in early modern Europe alongside a particular stance toward money, the one people call the spirit of capitalism: earning is not merely permitted but dutiful, spending on yourself is not, so the surplus gets reinvested. The phrase that gets used for the relation is elective affinity rather than cause, and Weber apparently also disavowed the crude version where the Reformation simply produced capitalism. And the standard complaints, which I half believe: the Italian city-states and the great Catholic banking houses were doing sophisticated capitalism earlier, so Protestantism can't be necessary for it; and the exposure seems to be identified by the very behavior it's supposed to explain, which makes the whole thing look circular.

What I want is a translation into my vocabulary, and then a residue report.

The translation, as faithful as you can manage:

A colleague says the real question is just whether we can intervene on being the sort of person for whom profit is a duty and self-indulgence is a sin, and that if we can't, the claim isn't causal in the sense I use. I suspect that's relabeling the problem rather than answering it — tell me which it is.

Does that do any work, or is it a fancy way of saying there's effect modification by everything?
