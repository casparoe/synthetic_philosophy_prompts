I argued with a friend for most of last evening about a question I had assumed was merely terminological and now think is not. She is a systems biologist who works on anomaly detection for industrial fermentation processes; I am not a biologist, and by the end we were talking past each other in a way that suggests neither of us had identified the real issue. I would like you to explain what the actual issue is.

The set-up. Take a mechanism to be a collection of parts and their activities, organized in such a way as to produce some phenomenon. Anomaly-detection systems are built on a reference description of a mechanism: they watch a target system, compare its behaviour to what the reference predicts, and raise a flag. For the flag to mean anything, the thing being watched has to be the *same mechanism* as the one the reference describes. That is where we got stuck. What makes a mechanism the same mechanism?

Three cases did the work in our argument.

*The bypass.* The reference mechanism is a signalling cascade: X activates A, A activates B, B activates C, C produces Y, with the kinetics of each step estimated and encoded. In a flagged run, A has been knocked out, but a scaffold protein that is normally idle permits X to act directly on C. Y still appears, on a comparable timescale, at reduced amplitude. My friend says: different mechanism, the causal topology has changed, so the reference model's parameters no longer refer to anything in the target. I said: the same mechanism running in a degenerate regime — Y is still produced by the same overall organization of up-regulating and down-regulating activities — and if every rewiring counts as a new mechanism, then "anomaly" collapses into "mechanism switch" and the flag tells you nothing.

*Convergent parts.* Two lineages of a microbe acidify their medium to the same endpoint using enzymes that are not homologous to each other: different parts, chemically different activities, broadly similar organization, same phenomenon. She says obviously two mechanisms. I found myself wanting to say one, on the grounds that the counterfactual profile matches — perturb temperature, pH, or substrate supply in either lineage and the endpoint shifts in the same way — closely enough that a model trained on one transfers to the other.

*The masked component.* In healthy runs component D is present and active, and knocking it out changes nothing, because E compensates. Knock out both and the phenomenon fails. The usual test for whether something is constitutively relevant to a mechanism is a two-way manipulation test: intervene on the component and the phenomenon changes; intervene on the phenomenon and the component changes. Under masking that test returns "not relevant." She thinks this is a defect in my way of talking about parts; I think the test is simply answering a different question. Note that anomaly detection operates almost entirely in masked and partially compensated regimes, so this is not a corner case.

What eventually made me think we were both confused is this. Whether the bypass case involves the same mechanism seems to depend on how finely we describe what the mechanism is *for*: produce Y; produce Y within 200 ms at above 60% amplitude; produce Y by way of A and B. Coarse description makes the bypass the same mechanism; fine description makes it a different one. But the description of the phenomenon is what an anomaly investigation is supposed to help us discover, not something we may stipulate in advance. So mechanism identity appears to be fixed by phenomenon identity, and phenomenon identity appears to be fixed by whatever we happened to be monitoring for.

She then made a move I could not answer: drop the metaphysics, and say that two systems share a mechanism just in case the reference model's counterfactual predictions still transfer from one to the other. My reply was that this is circular in its own way, since transfer is graded and purpose-relative and needs a threshold from somewhere.

What I would like explained:

1. Whether our disagreement is really about mechanism identity at all, or is instead a disagreement about whether "mechanism" names a mind-independent kind or a description we choose for our purposes — and what would settle that.
2. Whether there is a criterion of mechanism sameness that handles all three cases without simply punting to a choice of phenomenon description. I would like to see the best such criterion, and then where it breaks.
3. What to make of the following proposal, which occurred to me afterwards: a role-swap or transplant test, on which two systems share a mechanism (or at least a mechanism-role) if a component from one can be placed in the corresponding position in the other with the phenomenon preserved. Does this help where the other criteria fail? Where does it fail — for instance when the mechanism is distributed and has no discrete parts at all, as with a reaction–diffusion gradient?
4. Whether, for the specific purpose of anomaly detection, we should stop asking whether the mechanism is the same and ask a graded question instead, and what exactly would be lost by doing so.

Please reason from the cases rather than reconstructing a literature: I am after the structure of the problem — what would have to be true for either of us to be right, and what the deep disagreement beneath our surface disagreement is.
