In the rationality reading group I go to, one guy keeps defending the axioms of rational choice like this: having cyclic preferences isn't a deep failure of reason at all, it's just like walking around with a hole in your pocket. You get money-pumped, you end up with the same apple and less cash, so the only thing wrong with violating transitivity is that it's imprudent. He then uses that to argue that diachronic rationality collapses into ordinary prudence — stay coherent or get exploited, and if you don't mind being exploited there's nothing irrational about it.

Three things bug me and I can't tell which of them are load-bearing. (1) The pump only seems to run if the agent is myopic: you offer her one swap at a time and she never looks at the sequence, whereas if she can see the whole series coming she just declines the first trade and nothing happens. So is it an argument against intransitivity, or only against intransitivity-plus-myopia — i.e. is the analogy quietly importing an inability to plan that the norm itself says nothing about? (2) By her own lights she strictly prefers what she gets at every step, so in what sense is she worse off at the end? If the answer is just that she has less money, haven't we helped ourselves to money as a neutral scoreboard, which is exactly the kind of thing her preferences don't obviously ratify? (3) The pump treats two cases identically: an agent who right now prefers A to B to C to A, and an agent whose tastes change over decades in a cycle — A over B now, B over C in ten years, C over A in twenty — where each change feels like definately genuine growth rather than an error. He says they're the same case and the pump damns both; I think something is being smuggled in there, since I want to say changing your mind can be fine.

Can you audit the analogy? Give me the correspondence table — what plays the role of the pocket, the hole, the pickpocket, the money, and who is you — then the disanalogies, which of them actually matter for his conclusion versus which are incidental, whether diachronic rationality equals prudence and nothing more survives once they're on the table, and what a better analogy would be if there is one. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning; no preamble and don't restate my question.
