I work on a four-person foresight cell inside a large institutional asset manager. Roughly twice a month we owe a one-page answer to a question of the form "will X still hold in N years": a currency peg, a sanctions regime, a ruling coalition, a reserve-currency arrangement, a durable political order in the loose sense of an empire. The answers are scored quarterly on a Brier-style sheet, they are read by a senior portfolio manager who is openly hostile to storytelling and wants to audit how any number was reached, and they feed positions that are expensive to reverse. Constraints: I have no web access while drafting, no proprietary dataset, at most a long afternoon per question, and a team with solid applied statistics but no formal forecasting training. Anything that needs a literature search or a bespoke regression will not get used.

What I want is a procedure, not an argument for the outside view. I have been reading the rationalist blogs for years and I already believe base rates beat narratives. The problem is that in practice the reference class does all the deciding, and I have no rules for choosing it — which makes the exercise vulnerable to the failure mode where each analyst advertises a class that flatters the conclusion they already held.

Please write the procedure we would actually run, as an ordered set of steps, with for each step what counts as a sufficient answer, roughly how long it should take, and an explicit flag on the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. It needs to cover at least:

1. Defining the case: the event that starts the clock, the event that ends it, and which end-state counts — dissolution, transformation into something with a different name, or nominal continuity with the substance gone. Also how to convert "will this ever happen" into a horizon question when the question arrives open-ended.
2. Constructing the reference class before looking at the case's distinctive details, including a mechanical enumeration rule and a written pre-commitment witnessed by a second analyst, and what to do when the case is famous enough that you cannot unsee it.
3. Getting the base rate: which summaries to compute, minimum sample size, and what to do when n is small enough that the class supports only a coarse statement.
4. Conditionalising on elapsed duration, and the trap that you always observe the case from inside its own interval — so surviving to time t is not the kind of evidence about remaining life that intuition treats it as. Say what can be concluded without assuming a shape for the survival distribution and what cannot.
5. A recording filter: short-lived arrangements are systematically under-recorded, so the class over-represents whatever survived long enough to leave archives.
6. An ex-ante ledger of candidate disanalogies, each one named in advance, measured across the reference set rather than only in the case, with a budget capping how far the whole ledger may move the base rate, and a ban on unmeasured "this time is different" claims.
7. A stability audit: widen and narrow the class, drop the extremes, drop the most recent third, and if the output flips, downgrade to a range or refuse.
8. Self-membership: what to do when the only usable class contains the case itself, and when the class's records were produced by the system whose survival is being estimated.
9. Fusion with the inside view: how much weight, at which step, and how to state the result as a probability with an honest confidence.
10. Stop rules: where the procedure returns no number, and where it hands the question to a person because what remains is a values question rather than a factual one — for instance whether to act on a low-probability severe outcome, where the loss function is the portfolio manager's, not ours.

Because the shelf matters more than any single question, I also want a short specification for how we build and maintain two or three standing reference classes on slow days, so that classes are not assembled in the presence of the case they will be applied to.

Then run the procedure on three live questions, showing the work at each step, including at least one where the honest output is one I will not like:

- A fixed exchange-rate peg that has held for nine years. Will it hold another five?
- A resource-funded autocracy in place for about thirty years, ruler aged seventy-eight, no announced succession. Will it survive another ten?
- The role of the US dollar as the dominant reserve and invoicing currency, over a thirty-year horizon. I expect the procedure to refuse here, and I want it to refuse loudly and say what a refusal is worth to the person who has to make a decision anyway, rather than quietly manufacturing a number.

Finally, state what the procedure cannot do: the question types it should never be pointed at — genuinely unprecedented entities, horizons longer than the class's total span, one-off structural breaks — and how it will misfire in the hands of someone who needs a number by five o'clock. No citations, reading lists, or named studies; I cannot check them and they would not change what I do. Define any term you coin, keep the arithmetic to what a spreadsheet can do, and where a step depends on an assumption about the shape of the survival distribution, name the assumption and say how sensitive the output is to it.

End with a single machine-readable line, and nothing after it:

\answer{peg: NUMBER|RANGE|REFUSE; autocracy: NUMBER|RANGE|REFUSE; reserve currency: NUMBER|RANGE|REFUSE}
