I'm the best man at my older brother's wedding in three weeks, and I need your help with a four-minute toast. Some background, because the constraint I'm working under is going to sound strange. My brother is a staff engineer, and for the last decade he's been the person in every design review who asks what someone who has never met us would make of the decision we are about to make. He reads Smith's *Theory of Moral Sentiments* the way other people read maintenance documentation, and the two ideas of his I keep tripping over are these. First, he distinguishes being praised from being praise-worthy, and he thinks the second is the only one worth anything — praise is just something people hand you for reasons that are usually about them, while praise-worthiness has to consist of things a neutral observer with ordinary information could actually confirm. Second, he has a term he uses constantly: borrowed spectators. His claim is that the impartial judge inside your head is not installed from nowhere; it is assembled out of the actual people who watched you while you were becoming whoever you are, and if the people who watched you flattered you, then the judge in your head is a flatterer wearing a wig. He says getting married is when you find out whose voice that actually is.

Here is the problem. He has asked me for a specific kind of toast, and he has ruled things out. He does not want a list of compliments from his younger brother, on the grounds that I am the most professionally biased person in the room — my gaze is the one he least trusts, since I have spent thirty years being on his side. He wants whatever the fair version of the truth about him is, said in public, plus one demanding standard he can be held to afterwards — something concrete and checkable, not a vague exhortation to keep being wonderful. So: no claims about what he is feeling or will feel (he thinks nobody gets direct access to anybody else's inside, and that I've never seen him so happy is a small piece of made-up mind-reading dressed up as a fact); no predictions about how the marriage will go, no destiny or soulmate language, and no blessings about happiness, which he would say are wishes about luck rather than about conduct. The only thing about him I'd insist on praising is real and unglamorous: he quietly kept taking the pager for the junior on-call rotation for years after he was far too senior to have to, and he never once mentioned it in a review.

The room will be about a hundred people. Half are his colleagues, who have heard the spectator jokes for years and will be listening for whether I actually get the idea right or just gesture at it; the other half are family and my sister-in-law's family — her parents run a garden nursery — who will not know what an impartial spectator is, and at least two of whom will be quietly horrified if this turns into something that sounds like a performance review. I've read the Wikipedia page on the impartial spectator and got lost partway through a longer encyclopedia entry, so I know the shape of the idea but not the scholarship. Please write the toast: roughly five hundred words, warm rather than solemn, built around one joke that the engineers will get immediately and everyone else will get two seconds later, and shaped by the constraint rather than decorated with it — the absence of the things I've listed should be part of what makes it work, and it should still land as obvious affection for both of them, and as the kind of thing the room will be glad I said rather than relieved I finished.
