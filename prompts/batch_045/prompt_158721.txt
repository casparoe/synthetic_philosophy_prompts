Our philosophy of science reading group keeps leaning on a picture that I've become suspicious of, and I want to audit it properly before I turn my notes into an essay.

The picture is that scientific progress is like archery, or more generally like distance in space. A theory is an arrow, the truth is the bullseye, and even a miss can be a near miss. Newtonian mechanics doesn't hit the bullseye (it's strictly false), but it's much closer to it than Aristotelian mechanics, the same way Boston is closer to New York than it is to London. Our seminar leader uses this to shrug off the pessimistic meta-induction: sure, lots of past theories turned out false, but false isn't the same as not-close, and 'getting closer' is all convergent realism ever claimed. So the analogy is doing real argumentative work — it's what turns 'our theories are false too, probably' into 'but they're presumably less false than what came before.'

My worry is that distance in space carries a lot of structure that closeness-to-truth doesn't obviously have: it's symmetric, it satisfies the triangle inequality, it lives in one shared space with one ruler, errors add up in a single currency, and crucially you can locate the target independently of the arrows. There's also that 1970s result I half-remember (Tichý? Miller?) where Popper's own definition of verisimilitude collapses because you can always conjoin a truth onto a false theory and get something 'closer', ending up with the claim that no false theory is closer to the truth than any other false one. And there are infinitely many ways to be wrong and only one way to be right, which doesn't sound like a distance at all.

The repair I've been playing with is to define closeness relative to a fixed question-set: you pick a partition of possibilities (a list of questions the theory is supposed to answer), weight each question by how much we care about it, and score a theory by weighted accuracy over that set. Two things go wrong, as far as I can tell. First, the ranking isn't invariant under how you set up the set — add enough questions nobody cares about and everything washes out, and split one question into three sub-questions and the ordering can flip, which smells like the old language-dependence problem in new clothes. Second, the obvious fix — say A beats B only if A scores higher under every admissible weighting — seems to give an almost empty ordering for the cases I actually care about, since you can usually cook up a weighting that reverses any particular comparison.

So: can you lay out what maps onto what in the archery/distance picture (arrow, bullseye, ruler, the range not moving, successive shots), sort the disanalogies into the ones that matter for the job it's being asked to do and the ones that are harmless, and tell me whether 'Newton is closer to the truth than Aristotle, and that's enough for realism' survives once the disanalogies are on the table? Specifically: does my question-set-plus-dominance version rescue the analogy or does it quietly replace distance with something that can't support a progress claim across a long chain of theories? And is there a better analogy — someone in the group suggested the game of 'hot and cold', where you get told you're getting warmer without ever seeing the target — or does that just smuggle in the assumption that our evidence tracks closeness, which is the very thing in dispute?
