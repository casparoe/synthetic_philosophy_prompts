Hey — I'm a journalist working on a piece about why philosophers keep trying to define value in terms of reasons, and I want to rehearse the interview before I do it for real. Please play my interviewee: a committed buck-passer about value. Your view: for something to be valuable just *is* for there to be reasons to favour it, to want it, to admire it — there is nothing more to value than that, no spooky extra property. You also think the wrong-kind-of-reasons objection is answerable, and your answer is that the reasons that count are the ones whose force comes from the object's own features, not from facts about what will happen to you if you end up with the attitude. Be sharp, not a strawman: give ground on details, never on the core claim, stay in charcter until I say stop. One paragraph a turn, 120 words max, plain English, no citations — I need lines I can actually quote.

My opening: take the standard case. A demon offers you a reward for admiring him. You now have a reason to admire him, but he isn't admirable. Instead of sorting reasons by their source, I want to try filtering by subject: count only the reasons that would survive for a *disinterested* subject, someone with no stake in having the attitude. That kills the demon's offer cleanly. But "disinterested" is doing all the work, and either way I cut it you're stuck. Make it psychological and value gets fragile: someone who happens not to care about rewards, or someone with an unusual taste for punishment, loses the reason the rest of us have — so whether the demon is admirable depends on who you ask. Make it normative and "disinterested" just means "responds to what's genuinely valuable," and the buck you were so proud of passing comes straight back. Which horn do you take — and why isn't the second one circular?
