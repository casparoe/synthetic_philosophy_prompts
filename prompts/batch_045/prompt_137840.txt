I have been invited to give a research seminar to a hospital psychiatry department — forty-five minutes plus twenty for discussion — and the discussion is the part that matters, because I am going to end by asking this group to run a study with me. I need to be ready for it.

My thesis: in clinical applications of predictive processing, aberrant precision has become an all-purpose explainer, and the explanatory work should instead be done by the temporal horizon over which candidate policies are evaluated. Specifically, I want to argue that delusion is better modelled as a truncation of the rollout over which expected free energy is evaluated — equivalently, an abnormally steep temporal discount applied to the epistemic or novelty term — than as an aberration in the precision of priors or of sensory input. On this reading the primary deficit is not in how strongly a patient weights the evidence they have, but in how much future evidence the patient counts as worth obtaining.

Sketch of the argument I will give. Expected free energy for a policy decomposes into a pragmatic part and an epistemic part; only the epistemic part rewards sampling that serves no immediate goal, and its value scales with how far ahead the consequences of knowing are counted. Shrink the horizon and information-gathering becomes worthless relative to immediate pragmatic payoff, so the agent behaves as if it were certain — a certainty manufactured by impatience rather than by precision. This reinterprets the familiar clinical finding that deluded patients commit to a belief after unusually little evidence: not a bias in evidence integration but a locally rational response to a foreshortened horizon. And unlike the precision story it yields predictions that are not about delusional content and not about affect: information search should be reduced even in neutral, unfamiliar, low-stakes tasks, and should partly renormalize when the horizon is experimentally lengthened — for instance by making sampling cheap and making delayed accuracy valuable.

The room. Two computational modellers, one of whom maintains the hierarchical Bayesian pipeline I would be using; both know active inference formally, and at least one thinks the whole framework is either recursive Bayesian estimation with better marketing or unfalsifiable. Four or five clinicians — consultant psychiatrists and a clinical psychologist — who run trials, use prediction-error and precision language loosely in grant applications, and will want to know what changes in the clinic on Monday; one of them has spent years on the beads-task and related evidence-gathering findings and will defend that literature as measured, specific, and treatment-relevant; another works mainly with depression and will point out that many patients do the opposite and never commit to any belief at all. One phenomenologically inclined senior psychiatrist who holds that delusions are meaningful — that their content is a reconstruction of a world that has come apart — and who will object that a content-free formal account explains nothing about what the symptom is. And a research lead who will ask whether any of this can be preregistered and measured in twenty patients.
