I've been fighting with my friend Priya about animal ethics for like three hours now and it turned into a fight about Nozick, of all people. We're reading Anarchy, State and Utopia in my seminar, and I kept coming back to that bit where he sketches this weird split view — animals basically get treated as containers of utility whose experiences get aggregated, while human beings have to be treated as ends in themselves and can't be sacrificed for the greater good. I said that on that view the obvious upshot is that factory farming is a moral catastrophe of insane proportions, because the suffering totals are astronomical and there's no Kantian-style side constraint on our side that could possibly outweigh it. Priya said no, I'm forgetting that this is the same guy who wrote the experience machine: if what matters for animals is just the qualitative character of their experiences, then the problem is an engineering problem, not a moral one. Once cultivated meat is cheap — or, her preferred version, once you can breed livestock whose subjective lives are permanently dull-but-pleasant, call it experience-neutral meat — the whole thing dissolves and nobody has to go vegan. She says Nozick of all people, being a libertarian who was suspicious of using individuals as mere means and allergic to anyone else's moral schedule being imposed on him, would be relieved by that kind of technological exit rather than horrified.

We also ended up arguing about two cases that obviously came way after Nozick's death, which made everything worse. Case one: a company proposes what I'd call welfare-neutral farming — the animals' brains are hooked up to something basically identical to Nozick's experience machine, so their felt lives are uniformly great, while their bodies are still raised and killed for meat; this isn't cultured meat, it's the actual animal, eaten. Priya says that if the experience machine thought-experiment shows that what we really want isn't just pleasant experience, then Nozick is committed to saying the animals are being wronged in exactly the way the plugged-in human is wronged, which would make the split view collapse into Kantianism for everyone; and if it doesn't show that, then her experience-neutral meat really is a solution. Case two: some regulator is deciding whether to extend legal protections to any system above a certain threshold of whatever the relevant psychological property is, which in practice might end up covering octopuses and maybe insects but also some AI systems that don't obviously have experiences at all. There the Kantian half of the view seems to suddenly do all the work and the utilitarian half goes quiet, and I can't tell whether that's an inconsistency or just the view doing what it's supposed to do.

So: what would Nozick himself have said? Would he have thought the experience-machine move in the farming case actually solves anything for animals in a way it conspicuously fails to solve it for humans, and would he have wanted to drag a sufficiently sophisticated AI over onto the Kantian side of the line? And more practically — what is Priya missing, or am I the one who's wrong here and just attached to the conclusion I started with?
