hey — I need a Q&A prep sheet. I'm giving a 20 minute talk with 10 minutes of questions at the synthetic biology departments friday seminar next week, and I want to walk in having already lost the argument in private.

My thesis: saying that DNA is a code, or that genes carry semantic information, is fine as lab shorthand but literally false. A stretch of DNA is a chemically specific template; what makes it do what it does is base-pairing chemistry plus the contingent makeup of the whole readout system around it (tRNAs, the synthetases that charge them, the ribosome, whatever else is floating in the cell). The codon table isn't in the molecule, it's a regularity of the readers. So "the gene for X" comes out as a relational, lineage-relative claim about what a given piece of machinery will produce, not a representational one.

The part I expect to get shredded is my positive proposal. I want to replace "code" with a test I'm calling the detour test, and I'm claiming it has two clauses, both of which have to hold: (1) the mapping has to be detachable from the carrier — you can swap the chemistry or swap the adapters and keep the mapping fixed; (2) the system itself has to draw a distinction between a garbled message and a merely damaged carrier. My argument is that DNA passes the first clause and that's exactly why everybody thinks "code" — codon assignments really are arbitrary, you can imagine (and people are starting to actually build) a genome with a rewritten codon table and get the same proteins out — but detachability only shows the mapping belongs to the reader, not to the message. And it fails the second clause: for the ribosome there is no difference between misreading a symbol and the symbol being broken, there's just different chemistry happening.

The room: a comparative genomics person who computes Shannon entropy and mutual information over alignments all day and for whom "information" is a measurable, boring quantity; two tRNA / synthetase people who actually work on the charging and editing reactions and who will be annoyed by clause (2) specifically; a structural biologist who thinks in stereochemistry and suspects I'm dressing up something obvious; a visiting philosopher of biology who's sympathetic to the idea that content comes from selection history and proper function, and who will ask me whether I've just redone the parity argument; a senior PI who's into the "code biology" program — codes as a real natural kind in nature, not a metaphor — and thinks I'm throwing out a discovery over a philosophers scruple; and a couple of postdocs who literally write genomes and use the codon table as a design tool the way a programmer uses a compiler.
