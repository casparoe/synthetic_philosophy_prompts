For about two years I have been sitting on a set of results that nobody has been able to explain: a public-health surveillance dataset, openly posted with all its appendices since 2021, that shows a strange seasonal spike which no existing account of the disease predicts and which most specialists had simply filed under noise. Last month a young epidemiologist sent me a mechanism — a story about reporting behaviour interacting with the biology — that swallows the anomaly neatly, predicts its exact shape, and is otherwise cheap and independently plausible. I want to write that the anomaly is now evidence for her hypothesis, and that this is a genuine change in how we should regard it, not merely a nice story retrofitted to old numbers. Our data editor says no: those numbers were public and known for years, everybody had already assigned them probability one, so learning the hypothesis cannot make them any more expected than they were, and if she wants credit she has to predict something new and go out and measure it. A philosopher friend, hearing this at dinner, muttered that the formalism generating my editor's objection is Bayesian updating and that the puzzle has a name — the problem of old evidence, I think he said — and then changed the subject, which left me with the argument and no way to settle it.

I have two tentative ways out and I would like to know whether either survives. The first is that the genuinely new item is not the data but the discovery of a relation: that this hypothesis entails that pattern. I am not certain of that relation until I understand the hypothesis, so perhaps I should update on the relation coming true rather than on the data itself, and the data's age then becomes irrelevant. My worry is that this looks like a trick that would let any theorist manufacture confirmation by noticing, late, what her own theory implies. The second is that the confirmatory force of a fact should be read counterfactually: what would this anomaly have been worth to someone who had not yet seen it? I even have an operational version — find a colleague who has never looked at the dataset, tell her only the mechanism, and ask what she expects — and I would use her surprise as a proxy for how much the anomaly supports the hypothesis in my piece. There is a further wrinkle I cannot sort out. Suppose I count the anomaly now, and suppose the hypothesis also makes a genuinely novel prediction that a lab will test next spring. If that test comes back positive, does the old anomaly stop counting, or do the two count together, or does the anomaly never really have counted at all? For what it is worth, the hypothesis was built partly by staring at this very anomaly, which I suspect is either the whole explanation of my intuitions or a red herring.

I am a science-and-ideas journalist and I have to decide in the next week whether to run a piece whose spine is the claim that an old, public, previously unexplained fact has become evidence for a new hypothesis, and I would rather not publish a fallacy dressed up as a scoop. So please tell me, in plain terms, whether my editor's objection is right, whether it is right for the reason he gives, and how I should actually think about the case: what the anomaly is worth to the hypothesis, whether the fact that the hypothesis was shaped to fit it cuts for or against it, whether my two escape routes are respectable or confused, and what I should say in print. Please write in continuous prose, as you would in a letter or an email — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
