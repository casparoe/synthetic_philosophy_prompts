I'm flying out next week for a campus visit, where my job-market paper is titled "The Utility Monster Is a Unit Convention." I don't need another rehearsal of the argument; I need a Q&A prep sheet.

The thesis, and enough of the argument for the objections to have something to bite on. The utility monster — the creature Nozick introduced in 1974, who draws enormously more welfare from each unit of a resource than anyone else, so that utilitarian aggregation appears to require feeding everyone else to it — is standardly read as an objection to the aggregative structure of utilitarianism. I argue it is instead an artifact of the *units* of interpersonal comparison. Each person's utility representation is fixed only up to positive affine transformation, u → a·u + b with a > 0. For a fixed population the b's drop out of the sum, but the a's do not: multiply any single person's utility function by a large enough constant and the sum-maximizing allocation redirects resources to them. On the usual "level-comparable but unit-arbitrary" reading of interpersonal comparison, then, who the monster is — and whether there is one at all — is not a fact about any creature but a fact about a choice of scale. I then distinguish two ways of fixing that scale, which I call *range normalization* (equalize the distance each person can travel between a life not worth living and the best life attainable for them) and *unit-of-sacrifice normalization* (fix each person's unit at the largest cost they could bear and still count as benefited on the whole), and I argue that each is a substantive moral claim masquerading as a measurement convention. The payoff: because the choice is moral rather than empirical, the familiar anti-monster moves — prioritarian weighting, sufficientarian thresholds, per-person caps — are best redescribed as three ways of fixing, or refusing to fix, the scale, and one of them turns out not to be in the same business as the other two.
