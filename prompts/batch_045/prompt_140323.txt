I'm a third-year undergraduate in a philosophy seminar on the ethics of technology, and my term paper is on responsibility gaps. Rather than summarize the debate, I want to understand what a now-standard institutional practice actually presupposes, and whether it hangs together.

The practice is the human-in-the-loop requirement, together with the way blame is actually distributed when such a system fails. It shows up in a lot of places at once: clinical decision support, aviation, Level 2 driver assistance, high-risk AI under the EU AI Act's requirement that systems be designed so they can be effectively overseen by natural persons, and targeting in semi-autonomous weapons. The shape is always roughly the same. The system is built to be highly reliable; the human's role is to monitor and intervene only when something goes wrong; the human is formally told that they retain final authority and may always override; and when a harm occurs, the investigation concludes that the human failed to supervise properly — inattention, over-reliance, failure to override — and blame, discipline, and sometimes negligence liability land on that individual. The developers are shielded by the fact that the system was formally advisory, or decision support, or not the proximate cause. Yet the same legal orders handle other machine-mediated harms quite differently: closed-loop systems such as automated insulin dosing or grid load balancing run with no one in the loop at all, and for some consumer products liability has been shifted onto manufacturers through product liability rather than onto the person holding the device.

What puzzles me is the combination: we insist that a human must remain in control, we design the loop so that the human's real opportunity to do anything but ratify is thin, and we then treat that human as the responsible party. Here is what I want from you.

First, reconstruct the implicit theory of moral responsibility. What must responsibility be like for this package to be justified? Is the underlying view desert-based, so that the supervisor must genuinely be blameworthy, or is it a forward-looking accountability view on which blame placement is justified by its deterrent or assurance effects rather than by the supervisor's actual blameworthiness? State the conditions the practice needs and say which are doing real work.

Second, I want to pull apart what may be four separate gaps that get run together under one label: a gap in causal contribution, in control, in epistemic access, and in authority to override or halt. These can come apart in principle — a supervisor can have formal authority and adequate information but degraded effective control, or real control but no causal contribution because the machine would have produced the same output anyway. For each, say whether the practice strictly presupposes that it is closed, or merely hopes it is; and say whether there is one responsibility gap here or several with quite different remedies.

Third, consider what I have been calling supervisory smothering. The reliability that makes a system worth deploying is arguably the same property that erodes the supervisor's vigilance, situation awareness, and manual skill, and the more institutions dramatize the supervisor's responsibility with training, checklists, and logging requirements, the more the human's actual effective control may shrink even as formal authority grows. If that is right, the mandate is to some degree self-defeating. Does the practice therefore rest on a false empirical claim about monitoring, or can it be defended even if supervisory control is largely nominal?

Fourth, test the package for coherence and alternatives. Does it simultaneously need desert and deterrence in ways that conflict? Where is it philosophically underdetermined, such that several theories would license it? And what would concretely have to change inside hospitals, airlines, firms, and procurement processes if the presupposed view were replaced by strict enterprise liability on developers and deployers, by a no-fault compensation fund, or by a design-first view that forbids deployment until genuine human control is technically achievable — including the possibility that some systems should not be built. Finally, say what the selectivity of the practice presupposes: we demand a human for some decisions and not others, and I want to know whether that rests on a view about which decisions require moral judgment rather than merely competent optimization.

Please be explicit about epistemic status throughout. Separate what is conceptually settled or follows from the definitions from what is genuinely contested among people who know this material, and do not present a contested position as the consensus. Flag where your argument leans on contested empirical psychology about automation bias, vigilance, and skill fade, and how much weight those findings can bear. Tell me which of your claims I would be going out on a limb by asserting in a seminar paper, and which I can state flatly.

Aim for roughly 1,000 to 1,300 words, with short headed sections following my four questions. Argue directly; I do not want citations, a bibliography, or a reading list, and I do not need a survey of who has said what. Close with a single line of the form \answer{...} giving, in at most fifteen words, the one commitment about responsibility that you take the practice strictly to presuppose rather than merely to sit well with, and put nothing after that line.
