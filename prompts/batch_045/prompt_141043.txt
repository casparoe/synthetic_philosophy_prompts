**Q4.** An urn holds 90 balls. Exactly 30 are red; each of the other 60 is either black or yellow, and you are told nothing at all about the mix. One ball will be drawn at random. Four tickets, each paying $100 if its event comes up and nothing otherwise:

- A — the ball is red
- B — the ball is black
- C — the ball is red or yellow
- D — the ball is black or yellow

In experiments most people report both A ≻ B and D ≻ C, in the same session, with the same urn and no new information in between.

**(a)** Show that no agent who assigns a single additive subjective probability to red/black/yellow and ranks tickets by expected utility can have both preferences. Name the axiom of Savage's that the pattern violates, and say in a sentence or two why the pattern is a violation of it. Finally: someone objects that the pattern is nothing special — "she just thinks the urn is probably stacked against her: few blacks when she's choosing between A and B, few yellows when she's choosing between C and D." Explain why this does not rescue the agent.

**(b)** Now she is offered packages, both settled on the same single draw: package 1 is A and D together; package 2 is B and C together. Note that each package pays exactly $100 whatever happens, since A and D between them cover every colour, and so do B and C. Is an agent with the preferences in (a) committed to strictly preferring package 1? If you say yes, say what is wrong with her. If you say no, explain what feature of the standard models of ambiguity aversion lets her rate the two packages equal — for instance, evaluating each act at its worst case over a set of priors, or using a non-additive capacity together with the Choquet integral — and name the principle she has to give up to do it.

**(c)** Does the argument in part (a) silently assume that her utility for money is linear? Answer yes or no and justify it in two or three sentences.

And here is the answer, copied out word for word except I fixed the spelling.

**(a)** Let her probabilities for the three colours be P(R), P(B), P(Y), summing to 1. She knows 30 of 90 balls are red, so P(R) = 1/3. All four tickets pay the same $100 if they win and nothing if they lose, so ranking them by expected utility comes to the same thing as ranking them by probability of winning: A ≻ B gives P(R) > P(B), and D ≻ C gives P(B) + P(Y) > P(R) + P(Y), which is P(B) > P(R). Contradiction. So no single P can represent her.

The axiom that breaks is Savage's sure-thing principle, P2. A and B pay the same thing ($0) when the ball is yellow, and C and D pay the same thing ($100) when the ball is yellow, so conditional on yellow she is indifferent in both pairs; the principle then says the ranking can't be decided by what happens in that case, which means the ranking of A against B has to line up with the ranking of C against D. Hers doesn't. Equivalently: tacking yellow onto both options shouldn't flip which one she likes, but it does.

On the objection at the end of (a): I think the objection is basically right, and this is where people over-dramatize the paradox. Nothing forces her to bring the same prior to both choice problems. Facing A vs B she takes seriously the possibility that the urn is mostly yellow, so P(B) is low and red looks better; facing C vs D she takes seriously the possibility that it is mostly black, so P(Y) is low and the ticket covering yellow looks better. Those are two different decisions, with two different things she is attending to, and there is no reason the numbers she reasons with have to match across them. It isn't that she has no probabilities; it's that she has a different one each time, and each one is tilted against whichever colour she would be betting on. That is just what ambiguity aversion is: when you don't know, you weight the possibility that hurts you. Put another way, assumption (i) in the set-up is doing all the work — once the prior is allowed to be choice-relative, the contradiction in my first paragraph never gets going, and the unrealistic assumption turns out to be the one about a single additive probability function, not anything about her.

**(b)** No, she isn't committed to preferring package 1. Both packages pay $100 come what may, so any model that scores a package by what it actually pays, rather than by adding up the scores of the tickets inside it, has to call them equal. Worst-case-over-a-set-of-priors does: the worst composition for package 1 pays $100, and so does the worst composition for package 2. The Choquet model does as well, since a constant act integrates to its own constant. So the models are not in trouble here. What she gives up is the principle that the value of a bundle of tickets is the sum of the values of the tickets in it, i.e. separability across events, which is the same additive/sure-thing thought in another guise. She already gave that up in (a), so (b) doesn't really add a new cost; it just makes the old cost vivid, because you can't decompose her valuation event by event.

**(c)** Yes. Expected utility is Σ pᵢu(xᵢ), and the step from A ≻ B to P(R) > P(B) only goes through if u is linear in money. With a concave u the comparison between two tickets depends on the whole shape of u and not just on which winning probability is bigger, so the inference isn't valid without risk neutrality. That means (a) hasn't isolated anything specifically about ambiguity: some of what gets called ambiguity aversion could just be ordinary risk aversion, and part of the paradox is an artifact of quietly assuming a flat utility for money.
