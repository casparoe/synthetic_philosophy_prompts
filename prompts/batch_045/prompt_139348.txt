I'm in a graduate seminar on consequentialism and its critics, and I've become attached to a reading of Williams's integrity objection that I want pressure-tested. On my reading, the complaint about the man who can save only one of two people in equal peril, one of whom is his wife, has nothing essentially to do with how many thoughts he has, or with how demanding consequentialism is on an agent's psychology. It is a claim about which considerations may figure in deliberation at all: what is objected to is being required to enter his marriage into a ranking alongside the claims of a stranger, since having a commitment partly consists in refusing to treat it as one item in such a ranking. Tell me what would count against this reading, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit about which difficulties a Williamsian would themselves concede as counting against it rather than explain away.

Two worries strike me as the most dangerous, and I'd like you to start from them. First, the mirror-image case: the husband who never registers the stranger at all is no moral exemplar either, and if he isn't, then the objection cannot be a bare prohibition on weighing; some thoughts look mandatory, and I can't say which ones, or how they differ from the impermissible one. Second, a case my reading isn't obviously able to describe: suppose this husband's dispositions were shaped over years by an adviser or a decision procedure that reasoned in impersonal cost-benefit terms, so that he now reaches his wife with no ranking thought at all and acts entirely from what he would call love. No deliberative excess appears, yet the shape of his agency was settled by consequentialist reasoning. Is anything left for Williams to object to, and if there is, does that show the objection was never about deliberation but about the causal or justificatory history of a commitment?

Be self-critical where I most need it: if my reading has been qualified to the point that no case could touch it, say so, and say whether that is a defense or a reason to drop it. Then name the single consideration you would treat as decisive against it, and say why it doesn't already move you.
