I am a senior applying to my school's summer independent research fellowship — eight weeks, one faculty advisor, a final paper of about fifteen pages, and a short presentation to a panel of teachers at the end. Last spring I read Kierkegaard's *Fear and Trembling* (the pseudonymous one, by Johannes de Silentio) for a religion elective, and I have been turning over the binding ever since. What I can't let go of is how rarely anyone stops to ask what the word *teleological* is actually doing in "the teleological suspension of the ethical." My half-formed suspicion is that the phrase is pulling in two opposite directions at once, and that which direction you take it in quietly decides everything else about how you read the book.

Here is the tension as I see it. If the suspension is teleological in an ordinary means-end sense — the end being either the promise that Abraham's line will be blessed, or his getting Isaac back — then the suspension has exactly the shape of the ethical reasoning it is supposed to suspend: a weighing of outcomes, a calculation of what is at stake. It would be ironic if the move meant to lift Abraham out of the universal, the public, the calculable, ends up justifying itself by appeal to an outcome. But if instead the *telos* is Abraham's own absolute relation to God — the relation itself, not anything it produces — then it is not the sort of end that can justify anything at all, and "teleological" starts to look like the wrong word for what Kierkegaard needs it to be. There is a second thread tangled up with this one that I think matters: near the start of the book the narrator says that Abraham believed God would not take Isaac from him and yet was still willing to sacrifice him if that was what was demanded. So what does Abraham expect to happen? If he expects to lose Isaac permanently, the act is one thing; if he expects, somehow, to have him back — by resurrection, or by virtue of the absurd — then the deed is not something he takes to be final, and I can't tell whether that makes his trial less radical (nothing is finally lost, so what is the cost?) or more radical (the willingness is undiminished even though the outcome is, absurdly, not in doubt).

Please turn this into the fellowship proposal. It should state one precise question that has a determinate answer rather than a topic area; explain why the readings I keep encountering leave it open (the usual framing I find is: is Abraham a murderer or the father of faith, and can a divine command override morality — I think that framing concedes the point I want to press, so say what is lost by arguing on that ground); set out the method and why it is the right one — specifically, what I would actually do, week by week, in eight weeks, given that I have no Danish, only the one English translation on my shelf, and an advisor who is a religion teacher rather than a Kierkegaard specialist; and say what the possible conclusions are and which passage or consideration would decide between them. I'd also like the proposal to be honest about failure: if the honest answer turns out to be "the phrase is deliberately unstable and Kierkegaard wants it that way," is that a finding or a way of failing? Say which, and say what the smallest genuinely successful version of this project would look like. Finally, tell me where to cut — I am not going to be able to work through the whole of Kierkegaard's ethics or the rest of the pseudonyms, and a proposal that pretends otherwise will not survive the panel.
