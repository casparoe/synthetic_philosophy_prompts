I sit on my state's Sentence Review Commission. Our statute lets anyone petition once they have served twenty years, including people serving life without parole, and directs us to reduce what remains when continued confinement no longer serves the purposes the sentence was meant to serve, weighing the gravity of the offense, what the petitioner has made of the years since, and the interests of the victims. We cannot revisit the conviction or the lawfulness of the sentence: the only thing we decide is how much of it is served. Our published reasons run to a sentence or two, which is the source of my difficulty.

Six decisions, in the order we made them.

1. Ferraro — Robbed a pharmacy with a revolver, fired a single round into the ceiling, and took nothing; an employee spent a night in hospital with chest pains. Sentence 34 years; served 21. No discipline after year seven, tutored in the prison school, wrote to the employee he frightened, and two risk instruments placed him in the lowest band. Reduced to time served with three years of parole supervision. Reason given: twenty-one years is a sufficient answer for one frightened night.

2. Okonkwo — At nineteen he drove the car in a robbery during which his companion shot a shop assistant; felony murder, life with parole eligibility at twenty-five; served 25. He has described the shooting at every hearing and has no custodial record. Reduced and released. Reason: the sentence was calibrated to the culpability of a shooter, and his was not that.

3. Brand — Set fire to the entrance of an occupied rooming house at night; four residents suffered serious burns, one permanently disabled. Sentence 45; served 24. He maintains the fire was an accident caused by a faulty heater and has declined the fire-setters programme. Denied. Reason: nothing relevant has changed since the day of sentence.

4. Whitfield — Ran a Ponzi scheme that took roughly forty-one million dollars from about two hundred people, several of whom lost their homes. Sentence 45; served 31. Now seventy-four, oxygen-dependent, no realistic capacity to reoffend, restitution around a fifth of the losses; he never disputed the loss figures but never wrote to any victim. Reduced to time served. Reason: the remaining fourteen years would accomplish nothing.

5. Ivers — Killed his partner after years of documented domestic violence; voluntary manslaughter with a weapon enhancement; sentence 25; served 20. Exemplary conduct since year three and a low actuarial score, but the psychologist's report says that in twenty years he has never once said what he did, and the file notes compliance without engagement. Denied. Reason: the sentence has not yet been served out and he has not begun to answer for it.

6. Sarkis — Strangled a taxi driver during a robbery; second-degree murder; sentence 40; served 22. Exemplary record, low risk, victim's son objecting at two hearings; our guideline worksheet put the bottom of the range for that offence at twenty-five years. Reduced to twenty-six years, so four more to serve, not to time served. Reason: the sentence was longer than the offence warranted, but not by twenty-two years.

The petition now in front of us is Mercer, who is seventy-eight and has served thirty-four years of life without parole for two counts of first-degree murder: he planned the robbery of a cheque-cashing store over several days, took a loaded pistol, and shot the owner and her adult son, the jury having rejected his account that he panicked. He maintained that account until his illness and never wrote to either family. Four years ago he was diagnosed with a dementia of the Alzheimer's type, now moderately severe: he does not reliably recognise his sister, has no memory of the killings, the trial, or the prison, and tells staff he is waiting for his shift. The treating neurologist reports that he cannot follow a two-step explanation of why he is confined. He is not terminally ill; with proper care he may live another eight to ten years; he is mobile and needs help only with medication and money. No discipline since year twelve, but that is partly because he has spent the last four years in the infirmary. In his nineteenth year, after a fall, his hypertension and sleep apnoea went unmanaged for about a year; the department settled his medical claim two years ago, conceding care below its own standard and agreeing that the lapse more likely than not brought his decline forward by several years, though it did not cause it. One victim's family has asked us to let him go; the other has written twice objecting. Finally, the health department has offered him a bed in a locked dementia unit it runs under civil public-health authority, where his confinement would be at least as restrictive as the prison infirmary and his privileges fewer, but where nobody would describe what happens to him as punishment; we can make any reduction conditional on transfer there.

What I would like from you:

1. The decision you would recommend if we want to be consistent with our own six decisions — reduce to time served, reduce to a later date, or deny — and which of those six is the closest analogue, with an honest account of why it does not settle this one.

2. The rules that fit all six of our decisions. I can see at least three candidates: a backward-looking desert scale with a floor and a ceiling; a forward-looking test on whether the remaining years still do anything; and something in between, on which gravity fixes a range and the years inside or above that range still have to earn their keep. Tell me what each predicts about Mercer and, in particular, where the first and the third come apart from the second.

3. Where the retributive readings split among themselves. One strand treats the sentence as a debt fixed by what he did and intended, untouched by anything since; another treats punishment as hard treatment addressed to a responsible agent, aimed at acknowledgement, and so as something that loses its object when the agent can no longer receive the address. What does each say here, and which strand do our decisions actually support? Our own reasons in Ivers and Sarkis seem to me to point in different directions, and I would like that tension made explicit rather than smoothed over.

4. What we would be committing ourselves to if we treated the department's negligence as a reason to reduce: a credit against a sentence that is otherwise still deserved, a loss of the state's standing to demand more, compensation for a separate wrong that belongs in another forum, or something else. And whether acceleration of a decline that would have come anyway is enough to do any of that work.

5. Whether the locked-ward option changes the answer for any of the readings. If he would be confined just as much under a different legal heading, is it coherent for a desert theorist, a communicative theorist, or a consequentialist to treat transfer as reducing his punishment, or is it a labelling device?

6. One further petition, described concretely, that would do more than Mercer's does to tell the competing rules apart — holding the gravity of the offence and the time served fixed, if that is what makes the test clean — together with what each rule would say about it.

I am less interested in your view of what Mercer deserves than in what our record commits us to, and in what we would be adopting whichever way we go.
