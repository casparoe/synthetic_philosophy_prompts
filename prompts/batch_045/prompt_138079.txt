You are a professional philosopher who works on the ethics of information technology, and who also designs and stress-tests classroom simulations for other instructors. You take pedagogical constraints seriously: you never design an activity that you could not actually run yourself, in a bare seminar room, in the time allotted, with materials you could print on a Tuesday afternoon.

Before you write anything, think it through properly. Begin by brainstorming at least three genuinely different candidate mechanisms for the activity described below — different in structure, not three variations on one idea — and say in a sentence or two why you are setting each of the others aside. Then commit fully to the one you think is best: concrete rules, concrete materials, concrete timeline. Only after that should you write the debrief.

The brief.

I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the nature and value of privacy: 20 students, one 80-minute session, a normal teaching room with movable chairs, no technology required beyond printed handouts I can produce myself.

Here is the problem I am trying to solve. My students arrive thoroughly committed to what I will call the control account: that a loss of privacy is fundamentally a matter of other people gaining access to information about you, or of your losing control over who gains such access, and that the size of the wrong tracks how sensitive or intimate the information is. Nearly every case I could put on the board confirms this for them. I want an activity in which they collide with a privacy-shaped wrong that the control account cannot comfortably describe — and in which they collide with it by doing something, not by being told about it.

The specific idea I have in mind, which I want you to build the activity around and to improve on if you can see how, is what I call a legibility burden. A person can be wronged with respect to privacy even when (i) nothing that the room would ordinarily count as sensitive, intimate or discreditable is disclosed; (ii) they retain full formal control over whether to disclose; and (iii) the choice to disclose is not coerced in any crude sense. What does the work instead is that the only route to something they are independently entitled to runs through translating some part of their life into a fixed schema that was designed without them, and then keeping their life in a state where it will go on matching that schema. On this picture the wrong lives partly in the shape of the form rather than in its contents; it is partly prospective; and it does not vanish when it turns out that nobody ever read what was written down.

So: an activity in which students queue up to claim benefits they are already entitled to, and discover that the real cost of claiming is not the information but the translation. By minute 60, I want three things to have happened, each engineered into the rules or the roles rather than announced by me:

1. At least some students find that their actual situation — a household with rotating membership, a sleep pattern that straddles the available categories, a caring arrangement that is neither full-time nor occasional, an income that arrives in a form the form does not recognise — cannot be entered on the form at all, and their options narrow to going without, misdescribing themselves, or rearranging how they live so that it fits.
2. A student who hands over nothing that they or the class would call sensitive nonetheless reports, convincingly, that something privacy-like was taken from them.
3. A second, unrelated use is found for the same completed forms: the eligibility schema built for one purpose turns out to decide something else entirely. The room should split over whether this is a further wrong of the same kind, a different wrong altogether, or no wrong at all.

Constraints and desiderata.

- No lecture up front. During the activity I do not want the words control, access, context or surveillance to appear in the handouts or in my mouth, and I do not want the target idea named until the debrief.
- No money and no prizes. No deception about the fact that this is a class exercise. Nobody should have to disclose anything about their real life: every relevant fact comes from a role card.
- Please avoid the well-worn staging — cameras, bathrooms, naked photographs, data breaches, the nothing-to-hide exchange. If the room recognises the setup, it will reach for a rehearsed answer.
- Design the incentives so that the people running the desk cannot simply be kind. If the clerks are free to bend the schema, the whole thing dissolves into a lesson about bureaucracy, which is not what I am teaching.
- The activity should still work if two or three students decline to take part, and it should be survivable if the role cards describing unusual households land on students for whom those households are close to home. Tell me how you have handled both.

What I want back from you:

- A materials list, and the actual wording of the schema as I would print it, including the boxes that do not fit anybody and the absence of an other category.
- Roles and numbers: claimants, desk clerks with their instructions, any auditor or observer you think earns their place, and what each is told that the others are not.
- A minute-by-minute timeline, including setup and a hard stop that protects at least 25 minutes of debrief.
- A debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto specific parts of the idea; that gives the strongest reply available to a control theorist at each of those moments (for instance: they could have refused; this is really a wrong of administrative fairness, or of autonomy, and only confusingly a wrong of privacy — say what it costs to answer that way); and that shows where each step of the argument could be resisted and what resisting there would cost.
- A frank list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, with a fix for each.
- An honest note on which features of the legibility burden the activity cannot reproduce and which I will simply have to supply in words. At minimum I expect you to address irreversibility, the way real schemas accumulate and get recombined across contexts over a lifetime, the fact that actual eligibility categories descend from histories of racial and class administration, the epistemic asymmetry about who will read the file and what it will later be used for, and the absence of any exit.
- If you think a 25-minute version for a reading group is possible without gutting the thing, add one paragraph on how; if you think it is not, say so plainly.

Use headings, and set the handout text out so that I can copy it straight into a document and print it.
