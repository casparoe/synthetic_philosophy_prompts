I am writing a piece on the standing conditions on blame — specifically on whether, and when, hypocrisy costs a blamer the standing to blame — and I want to hang it on a single well-documented episode rather than on invented vignettes. My proposed case is COP26 in Glasgow, and I would like you to do the analysis before I decide whether the piece works.

Take these as the stipulated facts; their exact magnitudes do not matter to what I want from you. The summit was convened to secure deeper emissions cuts. During the fortnight, private-jet arrivals into Glasgow airports ran into the hundreds, and reporting at the time noted that a number of delegations, executives, and campaigners arrived by air, some privately, some commercially. Rich countries pressed coal-dependent developing countries for steeper targets, while several of those same rich countries had missed targets they had previously accepted. Delegations from low-lying island states, whose own emissions are negligible and whose territory is imminently threatened, were among the loudest in blaming the high emitters. And the host government both set the terms of participation and, through its own ministers, urged others to do more.

Here is the analysis I want. First, a disciplined mapping: identify the distinct blaming relations in play — who blames whom, for the violation of which norm, and whether the speech act in question is best modeled as moral address (a demand on the target) or as something closer to sanction, censure, or enforcement. For each relation, say precisely how the blamer is implicated: does she do the very same thing she condemns, violate the same norm at a different scale, merely participate in the practice the norm targets, or benefit from it without acting?

Then run the tests. I can think of several candidate conditions on standing, and I want them kept apart rather than blended: a strict-identity condition (you must not have done the very thing); a same-norm condition (you must not have violated the very norm you invoke); a domain condition (you must not be implicated anywhere in the relevant domain); a complicity or benefit condition (you must not profit from the practice you condemn); and a selectivity condition I have not seen argued — that a blamer who names some violators while staying silent about equally culpable ones forfeits standing in her own right, independent of hypocrisy. Say which of these the case actually discriminates between, which of them the private-jet delegate, the commercial-flight activist, the island-state delegate, and the rich-country negotiator each fail or pass, and where two conditions agree on almost everything and diverge only on one of these figures.

Then push the episode back against the theory. Three problems interest me especially. One: if the domain or complicity conditions are right, nearly every participant in a fossil-fuelled economy is implicated, and the standing condition threatens either to silence everyone or to become trivial — does the theory have the resources for scalar standing, partial forfeiture, or degrees of implicatedness, or does it have to retreat to the strict-identity test to stay usable? Two: what happens when blame is issued in an official capacity, by an office-holder on behalf of a state or an institution, or by an implicated speaker purporting to speak for the threatened — is standing held by the office, borrowed from those on whose behalf one speaks, or irreducibly personal? Three: what does the theory prescribe for an implicated blamer who must speak anyway, and is silence, confession-then-blame, self-implicating blame, or delegation to an unimplicated speaker the coherent option — and are there acts that restore forfeited standing, and if so do they operate retroactively on blame already issued?

Finally, tell me where the apparatus is silent rather than making it speak: on the standing of collectives, on blame across generations or at radically different scales of contribution, and on whether naming-and-shaming in a negotiated text is blame at all. And give me your honest bottom line for the piece: is hypocrisy the right lens on this episode, or does the case show that the interesting failure is a different one — a structural scarcity of standing that no individual-level condition can repair?
