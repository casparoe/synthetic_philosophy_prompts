Hi — I'm defending a chapter of my dissertation at the department's work-in-progress next Thursday (45 min talk, then about 20 min of questions) and I need Q&A prep, badly. Nobody in the room will have read the chapter, so the objections will come off the thesis as I state it, not off my argumnet.

The thesis, compressed: I'm defending epiphenomenalism about phenomenal character — what it's like to see red or feel a stab of pain makes no causal difference to anything at all, including to what I say and believe about it. The usual self-undermining problem (if the experience doesn't cause my judgement that I'm in pain, then the judgement floats free of the experience and epiphenomenalism eats its own tail) I try to dissolve with what I'm calling the calibration move: introspective judgements are produced by a separate readout system that was, during development, trained against the very states it later reports, so its reliability is inherited from that training channel rather than from any causal channel running from experience to judgement in the reporting case. Reliability is a training-channel notion, not a causal-channel one. And separately I want to say something positive about what phenomenal properties are: they're the system's own registration of a metabolic-and-bandwidth cost that a certain kind of recurrent processing pays — which is meant to explain why they ride along on some functional states and not others, and why a functionally identical but cheaper implementation would be dark inside.

The room: two Sydney-style realists who think phenomenal properties are ordinary physical properties that do causal work and who hear "epiphenomenalism" as a non-starter before I've finished the sentence; one person who thinks phenomenal properties are an introspective illusion and will want to know why I'm keeping them around at all; one person whose whole research programme is the causal exclusion problem and who will immediately ask what my calibration move buys over the bog-standard common-cause reply (honestly, my biggest worry); and a visiting cognitive scientist who works on perceptual confidence and metacognitive readout and who knows the empirical literature on report dissociation — blindsight, change blindness, confidence reversals, patients who recieve the signal but don't report it. Plus a few grad students, one of whom will definitely want to know whether I believe my own view.
