Right, so I'm about sixty thousand words into a novel — its set in a small town in Cumbria, mostly one family and the pub they run, no philosophy in it at all, nobody in the book ever says the word utilitarianism. But there's a scene I can't get right, and I think it's a real problem rather than a prose problem.

Here's what I need. Build me a case where totalling up across separate people — the thing the separateness of persons objection says you can't do, the old Rawls-and-Nozick flavoured line that there isn't one big person whose overall welfare is what matters, only lots of separate ones — is, intuitively, completely fine. Even though:

- one person carries a genuinely heavy burden, the sort that visibly changes her life for a year or more, not a bad afternoon;
- she didn't volunteer for it, wasn't asked, and can't be paid back or compensated afterwards;
- the people who gain are reasonably numerous, and what each of them recieves is small — nothing that transforms any single one of their lives;
- and the total gain still plainly outweighs what she loses.

I want the reader to finish the scene thinking she did the right thing, and to go on believing the objection is basically right. Both reactions at once. So it can't cheat: she can't be a saint or a martyr by temperament, the burden can't be secretly small, the beneficiaries can't be made pitiable, and no consent gets smuggled in — no implied consent, no "she'd have agreed if anyone had bothered to ask."

Constraints, because I know how these things go: one self-contained scene, a couple of hundred words of fiction at the most, third person past tense like the rest of the book, ordinary contemporary setting, and none of the ethics chestnuts — no trolleys, no hospitals or transplants, no ticking bombs, no drowning kids, nobody standing at a switch. Nothing technical may be said aloud; the arithmetic has to be visible in what happens, not in what anybody explains. The person making the decision is my POV character and she doesn't get a speech either. She just does it.

What the scene is for, so you know what counts as cheating: the same basic shape — one person heavy, many people slightly better off — comes back about eighty pages later, and there it has to read as monstrous. So whatever makes this one come out fine has to be an actual feature of the case, not just a warmer mood or nicer people.

I've been kicking around a distinction of my own that may be doing the work or may be rubbish: summing versus pooling. Totting up across people is objectionable when you're summing strangers, but fine when they're already entangled in something they're all in the middle of making, so the total isn't a total of separate lives so much as the finishing of a thing none of them owns alone. Use it if it earns its keep, and tell me straight if it collapses.

Past the scene, I want: what the aggregating view says here, what the separateness objection says, and what specifically stops it biting — name the feature doing the work. Then remove that feature and tell me whether the case collapses back into a plain sacrificial one. And then, how would someone who still thinks the objection applies attack my scene — what would they say I've sneaked in?
