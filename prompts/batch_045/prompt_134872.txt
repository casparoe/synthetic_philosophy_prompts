Kavka's toxin puzzle, in its standard form, runs roughly like this. A benefactor will transfer a large sum of money to you at midnight tonight if and only if, at that moment, you intend to drink at noon tomorrow a vial of toxin whose only effect is to make you miserably ill for a day. You never have to drink it: the money is paid for the intention alone, and once it is paid you will have no reason at all to drink. The question is whether you can intend to drink it, given that you know now that tomorrow you will know you have no reason to.

I want a pair of cases that isolates what is doing the work here. Two stylized accounts of intention are in play. Call the first the **self-prediction account (SP)**: intending to A requires, at minimum, believing that one will A; and an agent who believes that she will A, is not irrational, and has some reason in the neighborhood thereby counts as intending to A. Prediction of one's own action is necessary and, absent irrationality, nearly sufficient. Call the second the **plan-settlement account (PS)**: intending to A is having settled the question whether to A — adopting A as an element of a plan, thereby closing deliberation about it, constraining further planning (consistency, means–end coherence, agglomeration), and normally guiding and sustaining the performance. It requires no belief that one will A, and a state that closes nothing, constrains nothing, and guides nothing is not an intention, however confident the agent's prediction of herself may be.

**The design task.** Construct two cases, the First and the Second, one paragraph each, both realistic — set in a workplace, a hospital, a clinic, a school, or a family, with no benefactors, toxins, windfalls, science fiction, drugs, hypnosis, or commitment devices.

1. The First must be a genuine instance of the toxin structure: the only payoff attaches to the agent's having the intention at the earlier time; performing the action later gains her nothing, and she has, and knows she will have, no reason to perform it; she is rational, self-aware, and undeceived. In the First, SP must return *cannot intend* and PS must return *can intend*.

2. In the Second the direction must reverse: SP must return *can intend* (indeed, that she does intend), PS must return *does not intend*. Same kind of agent, same kind of action, same texture of situation. But the Second must not be a case of akrasia or weakness of will, of compulsion, addiction, or habit operating behind her back, of coercion by another person, of self-deception, or of the action's turning out to be involuntary or non-intentional when she performs it. She remains rational and remains in control of whether she performs it.

3. The two cases must differ in exactly one respect, which you must name; and flipping that respect in either case must convert it into an instance of the other, swapping both verdicts. The respect must be a structural feature of the agent's situation or reasons from which the presence or absence of the relevant state follows by each account's own lights — not a bare stipulation that in one case she happens to have settled and in the other she happens to predict.

**What to show after the scenarios.** For each case and each account: state the candidate intention, state what the agent believes about her own future action and on what basis, and derive the verdict from the account rather than asserting it. Then: (a) name the feature of the pair that is doing the work; (b) show what happens to all four verdicts if that feature is removed; (c) consider how a defender of whichever account comes out worse would attack the case — in particular whether SP can distinguish a spectator's prediction from an intention-constituting expectation, and whether PS can deny that a resolution the agent knows will guide nothing is an intention at all — and say whether that repair is principled or ad hoc; (d) close with a paragraph on what the pair shows about whether an intention is partly constituted by a belief about one's own future action. The point of the pair is to force that question, so a case that simply stipulates the verdicts fails; and do not resolve it by appeal to what any named philosopher has said.

Please write your answer in American English, and reason the whole thing out from the accounts as stated: I want no citations, bibliographies, or reading lists.
