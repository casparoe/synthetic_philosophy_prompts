I'm helping to write the ethics framework section for a public vector-control and crop-protection program, and I keep hitting the same wall: nearly everything I find either re-litigates whether insects are sentient or else just asserts that preventing human deaths from malaria settles the matter. What I need comes before that fight. I need a specification of what any normative account of pest control would have to deliver in order to be usable by a body that actually has to choose between real options — indoor residual spraying, larval source management, mass trapping, sterile-insect releases, proposed gene-drive suppression of a mosquito population, and simply doing nothing — and that also has to say which considerations are not allowed to count in its justification. So please write me that specification, aimed at that purpose: good enough to guide an agency's decision and to be stated publicly to the people who bear the risks.

Here is the raw material I want it built from, with my own half-formed distinctions, which you should feel free to rename. (1) *The asymmetry case*: spraying insecticide inside houses against malaria mosquitoes, versus coating seeds with a systemic insecticide so that a low-value or cosmetic crop yields slightly more — both kill insects, and I suspect an adequate theory must be able to separate them without appealing only to the size of the human benefit. (2) *The dying-time problem*: most neurotoxic modes of action don't kill instantly; they produce hours of knockdown, convulsion, and paralysis first, so an account that prices only numbers of deaths seems to mismeasure what it is authorizing — but my colleague insists that requiring sensitivity to mode of death is a prejudice of one camp, since we don't know whether invertebrates have phenomenal states at all. (3) *The constitution worry*: pest status is conferred by somebody's project, not by nature — the same caterpillar is a pest in a brassica field and unremarkable on a roadside verge — so the theory can't treat pest as a natural kind, and yet it probably has to give the relation some weight or it will forbid gardening. (4) *The lock-in objection*: killing selects for resistance and can create a permanent dependence on further killing, so each intervention reshapes the menu available later; does an adequate account have to be diachronic? (5) *The baseline problem*: the alternative to killing is rarely that the insects live, since most die of starvation, predation, or weather anyway; I want to know what discipline the theory owes in choosing a reference population, and whether "they'd have died anyway" is a legitimate subtraction or a dodge. (6) *Suppression without killing*: sterile-insect releases and gene drives mostly reduce the number of insects that will ever exist, which raises a question about the value of population size and of prevented existence that a death-counting view may not even be able to formulate. (7) *Risk-bearing and say*: the people who absorb the exposure are farmworkers, applicators, and households downwind, who are often not the beneficiaries; is a requirement of procedural legitimacy part of the normative theory or a separate one bolted on? (8) *Migration*: techniques and justifications developed for one target get redeployed to other species, other countries, and other purposes — must the account govern that, or is it unfair to ask? My coauthor adds a candidate requirement I distrust, namely that the account must yield a determinate decision under radical uncertainty about insect sentience and ecosystem effects; I suspect that is really a demand that the theory be complicit in whatever the agency wanted to do anyway.

Please return the list of requirements with the case or consideration behind each, and for each one mark it either non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp, naming the camp. Then flag any subset of the requirements you think cannot be jointly satisfied — I especially want your view on whether "must not presuppose contested claims about invertebrate consciousness" and "must discriminate between modes of death" can both stand, and on whether the procedural requirement can co-exist with a demand for determinacy under uncertainty — and say which single requirement does the most work in excluding otherwise popular views. Give the main list as a table with exactly these columns: Requirement (imperative, one sentence, numbered R1, R2, …); Motivating_Case (the concrete case, real or constructed, that shows why it is required); Status (non-negotiable, or camp preference with the camp named); Excludes (what kind of view, or what specific move in an argument, it forbids); Conflicts (IDs of other requirements it may be jointly unsatisfiable with, and in one clause why). After the table, add a brief scorecard as a JSON object with these keys: non_negotiables (array of requirement IDs); preferences (array of objects with id, camp, and why_negotiable); unsatisfiable_subsets (array of objects with ids and what_gives); most_exclusionary (object with id and reason); scorecards (array of objects with view, meets, fails, worst_failure, and one_line_verdict) covering these four candidates only: a welfare-consequentialist view that counts expected invertebrate suffering; a biocentric-individualist view that extends respect to every living organism; an ecosystem-integrity or stewardship view that takes populations and ecological function as the primary object of concern; and a public-health-exceptionalist view that gives preventing human disease lexical priority. Keep the weight on the criteria, not the scoring.
