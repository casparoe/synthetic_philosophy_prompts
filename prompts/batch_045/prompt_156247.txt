I'm working on a version of the value alignment problem and I keep circling around the same question: what exactly belongs in the content of a person's idealized preferences — their coherent extrapolated volition, roughly, what they would end up wanting if they knew more, reasoned better, became more the person they wish they were, and had to settle things jointly with everyone else's extrapolation. I have fairly firm gut verdicts on a batch of cases, and I don't think they add up. I'd like you to find the principle behind them, or tell me which ones I have to give up.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. A smoker is given the full medical evidence and a month to think, stops, and later endorses having stopped. His idealized preferences include not smoking. Clear yes.
2. A devout person, given exhaustive honest evidence about the origins of her scripture and unlimited time to reflect, would lose her faith and reorganize her life around different projects. Even though the person she is now would call that version of her corrupted, I still want to say the apostate's preferences are her idealized preferences. Yes.
3. Someone with mild, long-standing depression wants to be left alone and to abandon every project he once had; after successful treatment he does not want this at all. The depressed preferences are not part of his idealized preferences. No.
4. A man who never wanted children becomes a father and, through the experience itself rather than through any new information or argument, comes to want the family life he would previously have rejected. I want to count that. Yes — and I know this sits badly with case 3, since both look like changes in bare affective disposition.
5. Someone is given a mood-altering drug and emerges with a stable, lasting devotion to a cause for which there is no route from anything she previously cared about. Not her idealized preferences; this feels like replacement, not extrapolation. No.
6. The same person is given the same body of information in two different orders; each order leads to a different stable endpoint, and neither endpoint person would endorse the other. I have no verdict, and I want one.
7. A person with no aesthetic sensibility whatsoever would, given unlimited intelligence and experience, come to value a kind of beauty accessible only through a sensory modality she does not have. My instinct is that this is an import rather than an extrapolation. No.
8. The religious person from case 2, while fully lucid, anticipates the possibility and states in advance that if she ever stops believing she wants her current preferences honored and her later self treated as having replaced her rather than improved her. My instinct is that this should count for something, maybe a lot. This sits badly with case 2.
9. Two people's extrapolations remain incompatible about how much of a shared resource to spend on some project: a compromise between them is right. But in a different pair, both parties' extrapolations independently converge on valuing something neither now values: there the merged volition should adopt the convergent new value rather than compromise. Both verdicts feel right to me and I can't say why they differ.

The rule I have been tentatively using has three clauses: a preference is in someone's idealized preferences just in case (i) it is reachable from her current preferences by a chain of steps each of which she would, at that step, endorse as an improvement; (ii) the chain may only add information, add reasoning power, and remove incoherence — never alter her underlying affective dispositions; and (iii) every value at the endpoint has some seed in her current motivational system. Case 4 makes trouble for (ii), case 7 is the point of (iii), and case 6 makes me unsure whether I want a chain condition at all rather than a condition on endpoints.

What I want from you:

First, one precisely stated principle, or at most two or three competing candidates, that captures as many of my verdicts as possible, with an explicit note of which case motivates each clause.

Second, verdicts on cases I have not listed, derived from your principle rather than asserted: extrapolating the preferences of a six-year-old; extrapolating for someone with advanced dementia whose current preferences are fragmentary and contradictory; a population in which fifty-one percent of extrapolations converge on one arrangement and forty-nine percent on another; and an agent whose extrapolated self wants to be switched off and succeeded by a different being that pursues the successor value.

Third, an honest account of what your principle cannot accommodate. I would rather have a simple principle that tells me two of my verdicts are wrong, together with an explanation of why I was tempted by them, than a baroque one that saves all nine by adding a clause per case. Say which trade-off you are making and why.

Finally, a short statement of whether the principle you arrive at treats idealized preferences as something discovered about a person or something constructed for her, and what it predicts about how much an extrapolation may change before it stops being hers. Please argue from the cases and the structure of the problem; I don't need any references or further reading.
