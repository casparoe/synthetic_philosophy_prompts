Two members of a committee I sit on at work have now spent three meetings on the same disagreement, and they have asked the rest of us to break the tie. I would rather not just guess, so I am bringing the argument to you and asking you to referee it.

The proposal on the table is to reconfigure the staff cafeteria: shrink the standard plate from twelve inches to nine, move the salad and fruit station to the entrance and put the fried items behind a screen so they have to be asked for, and change the default side in the online ordering portal from fries to a side salad, with fries still one click away. No one's options are legally restricted; the entire point is to change what people in fact pick.

The exchange went as follows.

**Rosa**, who runs the wellness programme and supports the proposal:

1. This is not paternalism in the sense that should bother anyone. Nothing is forbidden, nothing is taxed, and everyone can still get exactly what they got before. The change only helps people who are already trying to eat better and failing — it is assistance aimed at a gap between what people want and what they do. For anyone who genuinely wants the fries and the large portion, the intervention costs nothing to decline: one sentence to the person behind the counter, one extra click. A policy that is free to decline cannot make anyone worse off.

**Alan**, a researcher in another unit, who opposes it:

2. "Free to decline" is carrying the whole argument, and it is the weakest part. If declining is genuinely costless, the intervention will do nothing at all — the entire point of smaller plates and a screened fryer is that people will not bother. Effort and attention are costs; you have simply denominated them in hassle rather than money. And note the tension: the easier the influence is to resist, the less good it does. Rosa cannot have both "costless to decline" and "this will measurably improve people's health." The two selling points of the softer version of paternalism consume each other.

3. Rosa also runs together two things that should be kept apart. There is arranging a room, and there is arranging a person. Some arrangement of the cafeteria is unavoidable — the salad bar has to go somewhere, and no layout is neutral. Granted. But that only shows that someone must choose a layout. It shows nothing about whether the layout should be optimized to steer behaviour, or whether an employer should be deliberately steering employees toward a target it has selected. The unavoidability point defeats a blanket ban on influence. It supplies no positive reason to engineer.

**Rosa**:

4. Then take the transparency route. We put up a notice explaining the plate size, the screen, the default, and the reason for each. Alan's objection is to hidden influence, and this is not hidden. Once we have stated openly what we are doing and why, we are not bypassing anyone's judgement; we are giving people the information and leaving them to discount it.

**Alan**:

5. Two problems. First, disclosure is not control: I can know perfectly well that a larger plate leads me to serve more and still serve more. Being told the mechanism does not hand me the ability to undo it. Second, and worse, the notice creates a cost of its own. Before it, asking for fries was unremarkable. After it, asking for fries is a small public declaration that I am declining my employer's interest in my body. That is a social cost rather than a cognitive one, and posting a sign does not remove it — it installs it.

**Rosa**:

6. That reasoning condemns public-health messaging generally: any visible recommendation makes non-compliance visible. And the alternative of "just don't steer" is not neutral either. The food environment is already engineered — by suppliers, by layout consultants, by decades of marketing — overwhelmingly toward more calories. Insisting that the employer's arrangement alone be neutral between options, when nothing else in the environment is, is not respect for autonomy; it is deference to whoever engineered the space first.

**Alan**:

7. I accept the point about the environment, so let me restate the objection as a test. Call it the say-it-out-loud test: I may be influenced only by something I could in principle say to myself, out loud, and act upon — that is, only by something that reaches me as a reason I can weigh. A default or a plate size does not address me as a reason-responsive agent at all; it re-weights the options underneath me. Rosa's own defence concedes as much, since she says the change works best precisely when people are hungry, rushed and on autopilot. What makes it effective is that it operates where my agency is not.

8. And there is a cleaner alternative. Do not reshape the choice; improve the chooser. Run a short session on how portions creep, on how to read the posted calorie information, on how to assemble a plate. Then whatever people pick is genuinely theirs. It may produce a smaller effect in the short run. I would rather have a smaller gain that people authored than a larger one engineered around them.

**Rosa**:

9. The training option sounds more respectful and is a worse bet. It reaches the people who already care, it demands attention at exactly the moment when people are tired and rushed, and information is weak against habits formed at the point of choice. Refusing an effective tool because a more flattering one exists is moralism with real costs attached: the health losses are real and they fall on actual employees. And the menu of options is not only "nudge or educate." We could stop offering the fried items altogether, or price them differently. Those are worse on autonomy, not better. So if the real objection is to steering, then education plus nudging is the most respectful package available.

**Alan**:

10. That last move quietly changes the subject. I am not claiming that education is the strictly better policy; I am claiming that if the only tools on offer are "influence people at their least reflective" and "leave the choice environment as it is," then some lost health benefit may be an acceptable price for not doing the first. And Rosa's case still founders on the people the nudge does not fit: the employees with no self-control problem in this domain, the ones trying to gain weight, the ones on a tight budget for whom the fries are the cheapest filling option. To aim the intervention only at those it genuinely helps, she would have to know who they are, and nobody on this committee does. So the intervention is blanket. And a blanket intervention cannot be defended as help to the particular person whose hand it is guiding; it has to be defended on population-level grounds, which is a different argument and one that should be made in the open rather than presented as doing someone a favour.

Please adjudicate the disagreement rather than settle it by fiat. For each of the ten numbered moves, give a rating — clearly right, more right than wrong, more wrong than right, or clearly wrong — and justify the rating in a sentence or two, saying exactly what the move establishes and where, if anywhere, it overreaches. Then close with a single short paragraph on who has the better of the argument overall, and name one consideration that falls outside the frame both of them have set up and that both of them are therefore missing. Keep the entire response under 700 words.
