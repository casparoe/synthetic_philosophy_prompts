QUESTION 4 (25 marks). Dana has bedbugs. Her options are: (H) a whole-home heat treatment that kills essentially all the bugs in her apartment (about 2,000) over roughly six hours; (C) live with them for the last four months of her lease — encasements, daily laundry, being bitten most nights — while the population keeps growing; (M) move out immediately without telling the landlord and let the infestation spread into the neighbouring units.

Dana is unsure whether insects have valenced experiences at all (she puts about 0.2 on yes), and she also has her credence spread over three moral views: S (sentientist consequentialism — aggregate welfare is all that matters), R (only beings she stands in relationships with count), and D (she has duties of self-respect and self-care that matter independently of anyone's welfare).

Using Vos's framework: (i) sort Dana's uncertainties into factual and normative, and say how each kind is to be handled; (ii) apply Vos's aggregative rule to rank H, C and M; (iii) explain why Vos rejects the modal rule (maximize the probability that your act is permissible) and the compromise-theory approach (average the theories into one theory, then maximize).

Gloss from section (A. Vos, "Two Kinds of Normative Uncertainty," unpublished ms.): first-order / factual uncertainty is handled inside each theory, by taking expectations over the facts; second-order / normative uncertainty about which theory is correct is handled by aggregating the theories' verdicts. Vos's aggregative rule is maximize expected choiceworthiness (MEC), but with each theory's choiceworthiness range-normalized over the option set (best available option = 1, worst = 0), because cardinal choiceworthiness isn't comparable across theories. She concedes that this makes the rule sensitive to which options are on the menu.

STUDENT ANSWER:

Dana's uncertainty about insect sentience is factual (first-order): it's a question about what insects are like, not about what matters, so it belongs inside each theory — I build the 0.2 into the S column below and I don't also put it into the theory weights, since that would count the same uncertainty twice. Her uncertainty across S, R and D is normative (second-order) and gets handled by Vos's aggregative rule.

Numbers are Dana's rough units, given to illustrate; the S column already folds in the 0.2 credence in insect sentience.

            credence     H     C     M        normalised: H     C     M
S              0.5      -12    -9   -900          .997       1      0
R              0.3        8     2     -4            1       .5      0
D              0.2        5    -6      7          .846       0      1

MEC: H = .5(.997) + .3(1) + .2(.846) = .97; C = .5(1) + .3(.5) + .2(0) = .65; M = .2(1) = .20. So Dana should heat-treat.

A useful thing to notice is that range-normalization means each theory contributes only where the options sit within its own scale, so Vos's rule is really a credence-weighted Borda count — a positional voting rule across theories — and this also answers her objection to the compromise approach, since averaging normalized scores is the same operation as averaging the theories themselves, so her complaint that a compromise theory can require what no component theory permits doesn't separate her rule from the view she is attacking.

On (iii): both rivals are best understood as risk-aversion about morality. The modal rule treats a small chance of acting impermissibly as worse than its expected cost, and the compromise approach splits the difference between theories rather than committing to the expected-best option. But MEC is linear in credences and therefore risk-neutral, and risk-aversion about moral value is no more rationally required than risk-aversion about money. The residual problem — that cardinal choiceworthiness can't be compared across theories — is solved by normalization.
