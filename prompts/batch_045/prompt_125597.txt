Hey — I'm a novelist, about two hundred pages into the middle book of a trilogy set in Glasgow and Williamsburg, and I've hit a problem with one of my characters that I can't think my way out of. Ishbel is the daughter of a Glasgow tobacco merchant, sharp, largely self-taught, and she's got hold of a copy of the new book by the Glasgow professor everyone in the city is arguing about — still in wrappers, title page missing, she stays up reading it by the kitchen fire. I want her to be genuinely against the colonial system, for reasons she'd actually have had available to her, while her father and his Virginia cousin are for it. My writing group keeps telling me to just make her morally outraged, which is exactly the note I don't want: I want her opposition to be the kind a person could have arrived at from the arguments in the room.

The thing I keep circling is this. As far as I remember it, Smith's case against the colonies is almost entirely a ledger case: the monopoly drags British capital into a slower trade than it would otherwise find at home, the wars and the governing cost more than the commerce returns, the whole arrangement is run for the benefit of merchants and politicians at everyone else's expense, and the sensible thing would be to open the trade, or let the colonies go their own way, or bring them properly into the constitution. What he more or less doesn't do — or so it reads to me — is say that the whole business is a wrong done to the people actually living under it. The complaint is that Britain is being cheated and badly served, not that anyone is being ruled without their consent. So: what are the most important implications of that shape of argument — an argument about cost and mismanagement rather than about injury — for the kind of anti-imperialism it's plausible for someone like Ishbel to hold in 1776? I'm especially interested in what follows for (a) how she'd answer the obvious question "but what if it did pay?", (b) whether Smith's remedies are really an alternative to empire or just a tidier version of it, and (c) whether she can get from Smith to "this is an injustice" at all without quietly importing something Smith himself wouldn't have accepted.

Two more things, and please push back on both rather than being agreeable. First, I've been sorting objections to empire into what I call 'the auditor's objection' (it doesn't pay, it's run badly, the money lands in the wrong pockets) and 'the neighbour's objection' (whatever it pays, you are ruling people who never agreed to be ruled, and that's the whole of it) — my sense is that Smith gives you the first almost completely and the second barely at all. Is that pair doing any real work, or is it a false contrast I've talked myself into? Second, the wrinkle I genuinely can't get straight: Smith seems to hold at once that people are much alike everywhere (the bit about a philosopher and a common street porter differing very little by nature) and a kind of staging-picture where societies pass through hunting, herding, farming and commerce, with some a long way behind others. If both of those are really in him, what follows for whether his critique of empire is a critique of ruling over people at all, or only of ruling over them badly and at a loss? I have a sinking feeling the honest answer is "both, and he never noticed", which would make Ishbel's position unstable in a way that might actually be good for the novel.
