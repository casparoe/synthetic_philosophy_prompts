I am teaching a seminar on how much rule-following a collective should demand — the debate often framed as one between tight cultures, where norms are numerous, strongly enforced, and deviation is punished, and loose cultures, where norms are thinner and deviance is tolerated — and I would rather spend the session on the 1989 film Dead Poets Society than on a policy paper. I would like a close reading of the film as a case study in that contrast.

Begin by separating four variables that the tight/loose framing tends to run together, and apply them to the film: normative density, or how much of conduct is actually covered by rules; enforcement severity, or how much a breach costs; exit permeability, or how easily a member can leave and what they lose by doing so; and justificatory openness, or whether rules arrive with reasons that can be contested or as bare fiats. On your reading, what is the school tight along, and where does the film blur the axes? Is Welton genuinely tight, or merely severe and loosely organized around a few adult wills? Is the boys' secret club a loose enclave, or simply a tight group with different rules and its own conformism?

Then take up two things the film does with this contrast that the social-science literature does not. First, the paradox of the loosening teacher: almost everything Keating does pedagogically takes the form of a command — rip the page out, stand on the desk, walk in the courtyard and notice that you fall into step. A teacher of autonomy is teaching by rule. Is there any non-ironic way to teach someone to question rules, or must every pedagogy of freedom be tight in form? Second, the film's dependence on what it criticizes: the closing scene is moving precisely because defiance costs the boys something, and it would be inert in a school where standing on the furniture was permitted. Does the film notice that its celebration of rule-breaking presupposes the worth of the order it breaks, or does it simply enjoy the shiver?

Be faithful in both directions. Say where the film stacks the deck — is there any rule at Welton that is wise, and any loosening in the film that is merely selfish or callow? — and where it resists the reading you have given. I am especially interested in who pays: which character bears the cost of the loosening, and does the film charge that cost to Keating or quietly excuse him? Close, if you can, with what a film can add to a philosophical argument about tightness that the argument alone cannot supply.
