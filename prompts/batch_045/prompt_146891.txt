"The Church-Turing thesis is the claim, proved by Turing in his 1936 paper, that a Turing machine can compute anything computable by any mechanical procedure — no more and no less. The proof is essentially the universal machine: Turing showed you can build a single machine that simulates any other machine, and from that it follows that nothing any computer does can outrun a Turing machine. Strictly speaking, though, the thesis is unfalsifiable: it identifies an informal notion ('what a rule-following human clerk could work out') with a formal one, and because the informal side has no sharp edges, no experiment could ever settle it — if someone built a device that outcomputes a Turing machine, we would simply conclude we had been wrong about what counts as mechanical. So the thesis is better understood as a stipulation or a definition of the word 'computable' than as a discovery, and if it belongs to any discipline it belongs to physics, not mathematics. It is not entirely safe either: quantum computers are the first serious threat to it, since if quantum machines can efficiently solve problems — factoring large numbers, for instance — that no classical machine can solve efficiently, then the class of things computable in practice is larger than Turing thought. And there are the uncomputability results on top of that: the halting problem and Godel's incompleteness theorem show that there are perfectly well-defined questions no machine can settle. The lesson is that the thesis draws a line, but the line sits well below what we would naively call solvable."
