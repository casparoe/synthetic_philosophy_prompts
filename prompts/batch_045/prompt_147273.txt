My study group is doing a session on moral responsibility next week and I got stuck with presenting on hypocrisy and the standing to blame. We read some Duff and a bit of Scanlon plus a couple of recent papers whose titles I honestly can't recall now, but I mostly understood the debate through a write-up a friend sent me from some philosophy blog, which sums the whole thing up like this:

'Wether someone is blameworthy depends only on what they did; whether you have standing to blame them is a seperate question about you. A hypocrite — someone who does the very thing he's condemning — lacks standing, and so his blame is unwarranted: the person he's blaming can properly answer "you too", and the blame just misfires, the way a verdict misfires when it's handed down by a court with no jurisdiction. What really matters isn't the hypocrisy itself, though; it's that the hypocrite doesn't accept the norm he's appealing to, so he's in no position to hold anyone else to it. And since none of us lives up fully to our own principles, most of us lack standing to blame most of the time — which is both why the "you too" reply is so annoying and why people working on this have more or less set the idea aside. Standing to blame turned out to be a dead end.'

I'm suspicious of that last bit especially, because the papers we read felt pretty alive, and I also have a feeling the write-up slides between saying the blame is somehow false and saying something weaker about the blamer — but I can't tell where, or whether it matters. Could you go through it more or less sentence by sentence and tell me what someone who actually works on this would accept, what they'd call a simplification, and what's just wrong? Two things I care about in particular: does the "you too" reply actually cancel the blame, or only something about the blame; and does it follow from the accurate parts that we almost never have standing?

I also want to say something that isn't just in the reading, so here are two of my own ideas — please tell me if either is a dud. My group came up with what we're calling the apostate exemption: the thought that a hypocrite who openly owns his own violation ("I lie constantly, don't lie") gets his standing back, because he isn't pretending to be better than you. Does that sit well with the commitment-style account in the summary, or does confession only change how irritating he is without touching the real issue? And second: is standing all-or-nothing, or can it be scalar, so a small hypocrisy costs you a bit of authority and a big one costs you all of it?

Keep the whole answer under 600 words, and be honest about your confidence: mark which claims you'd defend against a specialist and which are educated guesses. Also, if what I'm asking rests on a confusion — if "standing" in this literature doesn't mean anything like what the blog and I both assume, or if the apostate exemption is just a clumsy version of something that already has a name, or if the "scalar" question is one nobody can answer without doing real work — say so straight out rather than politely answering it as posed.
