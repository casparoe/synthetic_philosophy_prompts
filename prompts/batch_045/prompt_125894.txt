I write a regular column on science and ideas, and over the past year one sentence has been aimed at me in three very different arguments, doing what looks like three very different amounts of work. The sentence is "the whole is more than the sum of its parts," or, in the variant physicists prefer, "more is different." It came up first from a condensed-matter physicist arguing that his field is not merely applied quantum mechanics and so should not be funded or staffed as a service discipline to particle physics. It came up again from a consciousness researcher arguing that no description of neural activity, however complete, will ever explain experience, and that we should therefore expect new fundamental principles. And it came up a third time from a machine-learning researcher arguing that a capability which appeared abruptly at a larger model scale is emergent, and hence that future capabilities are in principle unforecastable. I do not think these three people mean the same thing, and I suspect that each of them needs a stronger reading than the one they would be willing to defend if it were stated without the slogan.

What I need is a disambiguation I can use as the spine of a column. Please do the following.

First, set out, as a numbered menu ordered roughly from the trivial to the radical, the distinct precise theses the sentence could be expressing. Ones I have in mind, though you should add any I am missing: that a composite has properties no proper part of it has; that aggregate behaviour is not a linear sum of the behaviours of parts in isolation, so summing part-wise predictions misleads; that the macro-behaviour is not predictable from complete knowledge of the micro-constitution plus the micro-dynamics without effectively running the system; that it is not derivable from them even in principle; that the macro-vocabulary cannot be eliminated in favour of the micro-vocabulary; that macro-states have causal powers of their own, including effects on the parts; that the same macro-pattern is realized by wildly different micro-configurations, so the macro-description is where the stability lives; and the deflationary reading on which emergence is just a label for the current state of our ignorance or our computing budget.

For each reading, give the claim in one or two sentences, and a verdict: platitude, substantive but defensible, a contingent empirical hypothesis about particular systems, or confused or false. Also say, for each, what would count as a clear instance and what would count as a clear non-instance, and tell me where the standard textbook examples actually fall — temperature and pressure, a phase transition, the periodic table, natural selection, a protein folding into a functional shape.

Second, take my three interlocutors in turn. For each, identify the reading or readings that would have to be true for the conclusion to follow, and say whether that reading is one you have already marked as defensible. If the slogan only looks like it is doing the work, say what the gap is.

Third, assess a reading that a fourth source pressed on me and that I have not been able to get anyone to state precisely, and which I half-suspect is the only one with real content in it. Her claim was that the interesting thesis is not about prediction or causation at all, but about the grammar of the macro-description: the macro-variable — a winding number, a count of loops or defects, a wrinkle count — is defined over the configuration as a whole and over relations among arbitrarily many parts at arbitrary separations, so that no description quantifying only over individual parts and their pairwise interactions can so much as express it. The macro-description, on this view, cannot be replaced by the micro-description, only reconstructed from it, because what it attributes is a global feature that the part-by-part vocabulary has no way of naming. Is this a distinct reading or a repackaging of one already on the menu? Is it true of the examples, and is it true in general? And does it, on its own, support any of the three conclusions I was handed — or is it compatible with the micro-level story being causally and nomologically complete?

Format the answer as the numbered menu with a one-line verdict attached to each entry, then a short section assigning my three interlocutors and the global-variable reading to entries on the menu, then a closing note on which readings are genuinely incompatible with a complete micro-physics and which merely sound as though they are. I do not want citations or a reading list; I want the distinctions.
