I am drafting the normative specification that will sit behind a tax-and-transfer scheme for a large, moderately rich democracy, and I want the specification settled before I go back to the theories. So I am asking for the requirements first, not for a survey.

A theory of the relevant sort has a fixed job: it must be able to say, of any given inequality between two people, whether that inequality is unjust, and if so what is owed by way of transfer, compensation, or accommodation — and it must say it in a form that could in principle guide policy in a state that observes earnings, wealth, and a few coarse personal facts, but not effort, not tastes, and not the boundary between what someone chose and what happened to them. Please hold that purpose fixed; adequacy relative to it is what I am asking about.

Write the desiderata: what a theory must deliver to be adequate to that job. For each requirement give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, mark it as non-negotiable, negotiable, or a prejudice of one camp (naming the camp), and say what it rules out. Where one clause naming a familiar family of view — resourcist, welfarist, capability-based, sufficientarian, libertarian, or the hypothetical-insurance device associated with Dworkin — sharpens a requirement, use one clause; do not write a scorecard, and do not reconstruct or cite literature. I have the literature open next to me and do not need it handed back.

My own candidate worries, which you should consolidate, add to, split, or reject, are:

1. The cut between brute luck and option luck must be drawable — but note what I call the grain problem: almost any outcome can be described either as the upshot of a choice or as the upshot of circumstance depending on how fine-grainedly the choice is described, so a theory must either say what fixes the grain or show why no grain is needed.
2. Expensive tastes, and tastes formed under pressure or deprivation, must be handled without either unlimited subsidy or silent victim-blaming.
3. The distinction between luck that is constitutive of a person (talents, temperament, the capacity to plan) and luck in her circumstances must be marked, or the theory's mishandling of it made explicit.
4. Non-abandonment: a person who takes a gamble and loses must not end up with no claim at all.
5. The disclosure condition: compensation must not be obtainable only by presenting oneself as defective or pitiable.
6. The diachronic problem, or ratchet: whether the accounting runs over whole lives or over time slices, and whether the cut is redrawn at every moment.
7. Implementability given the state's epistemic position, including what the theory says to do when the state genuinely cannot tell the cases apart.
8. Incentive-responsiveness: the requirement must survive people changing their behaviour in response to the scheme.
9. Currency: what exactly is being equalised, compensated, or floored.
10. The baseline circularity: judgments about option luck presuppose a just background, which is what the theory was supposed to specify.
11. Scope: natural as well as social luck, children, future people, non-citizens.
12. Second-order luck: luck in a person's risk-tolerance, willpower, or capacity to choose at all.

Two of these I want tested rather than assumed. First, timing-neutrality: my stipulation that a theory must give the same verdict for two people who differ only in whether the same piece of bad brute luck struck early or late in life. Second, my suspicion that the disclosure condition above is really an expressive preference rather than a requirement of justice. Tell me if either is confused, and if the whole list turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable, say so plainly and say which requirement you would relax first and what that relaxation costs — that is the part of the answer I will actually use.

Give the requirements as a table with columns: id, requirement, motivating_case, status, rules_out. Then a JSON object with exactly these fields: unsatisfiable_subsets (a list of objects with ids, tension, recommended_relaxation); most_exclusionary (an object with id and why); candidate_verdicts (a list of objects with candidate_number, verdict — one of genuine, negotiable, prejudice, or ill-posed — and why); requirements_added (a list of objects with requirement and motivating_case for anything you think my list misses); and if_a_scheme_must_ship (an object with weakest_requirement_to_relax and residual_cost).
