For thirty-eight years I was a hydrologist, most of that time preoccupied with a single question: what is it that a gauge actually measures, and how badly does the answer diverge from the quantity you care about. In retirement I have been reading moral and political philosophy, and the literature on the currency of egalitarian justice — whether the thing we should be equalizing is welfare, or resources, or capabilities — strikes me as the same problem in a harder medium, with the additional difficulty that nobody agrees on what the gauge is supposed to be reading. I have also, unfortunately, been put in a position where I have to take the question practically.

I sit as a volunteer on the grants committee of a small local charitable trust. The particulars: five unpaid members, one two-hour meeting per quarter, roughly forty-five applications a year, a total annual budget of about £160,000, an individual cap near £6,000. Grants are in kind only — equipment, home adaptations, tuition, transport passes, never cash. Decisions are final, there is no appeal, and we collect no outcome data beyond proof of purchase; once a grant is made we generally never learn what it did. Our deed also forbids funding anything that a statutory service ought to be providing, which we must police from a one-page application form. So these are repeated decisions, made fast, by people with no philosophical training, on thin evidence, with real money and no feedback loop.

I would like you to build me a decision procedure we could actually run, and I want the conceptual work to be in the translation rather than in the exposition. Please write:

1. A numbered sequence of questions to take an application through, in the order they must be asked, with an explicit statement of what would count as a sufficient answer to each and what evidence would satisfy it given that we have only the form and, occasionally, a phone call.
2. The points at which the sequence stops with a no, and the points at which it stops and hands the decision to us as a values question rather than a factual one — with the hand-off written so that a volunteer can tell the two kinds of stoppage apart.
3. An opening step in which the procedure declares which currency it is denominated in before any measurement happens, and a note at each later step describing how the ranking of a given application would change if we switched currency. I do not want an eclectic compromise; I want to see where the choice bites.
4. Honest marking of the steps where judgment is dressed up as procedure, and a statement of which member of the committee has to sign off at those steps.

The hard cases I need the procedure to handle, because they are the ones that actually divide us:

- Interpersonal comparison when we have no subjective reports, and no way to get reliable ones. What proxy does the procedure accept, and what uncertainty should we attach to it.
- Applicants whose expectations have visibly contracted — people who have stopped wanting what they cannot have. How the procedure detects this without asking them to imagine a life they have already set aside, and what it does when detection is impossible.
- Expensive tastes versus genuine conversion handicaps. Give me the test the procedure uses to separate a piano from a ramp, and say what it does when the test returns nothing.
- The index problem: who fixes the list of functionings that count, what happens when two applicants' claims rest on different entries in the list, and how the procedure keeps the list from simply encoding the tastes of five retired people.
- The unit of assessment. A grant nominally made to one person frequently benefits a carer, a spouse, or children, and the three currencies count that spillover differently. The procedure must say which unit it scores and what it does with the rest.
- Whether it aims at a floor or at the largest gain per pound, and the cases where those two aims rank the same applications differently.
- The in-kind constraint. Since we cannot write cheques, I suspect the resourcist's elegant answer about equal shares is not even available to us, and I want that said plainly rather than quietly assumed away.
- What we can honestly record afterwards, and what we must admit we will never learn.

Then run the finished procedure on three applications, showing the actual scoring rather than a summary of it: a stairlift costing close to the cap for a woman with advanced multiple sclerosis who lives alone; the same sum divided among ten applications for adult literacy tuition; and a request from a man who is deaf for equipment he says will let him keep a job he would otherwise lose, where his employer is already legally obliged to make some provision. At least one of the three must come out against the applicant I would most like to help; a procedure that never does that is not discriminating, and I would rather see you defend an uncomfortable result than steer to avoid one.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do — which distortions it will reliably produce, and which injustices it will be blind to by construction. I have no library access here and no interest in a bibliography; I want the argument and the instrument, not the references. Please keep it to something a careful volunteer can work through in about ten minutes per application, and write it in a form I could put in front of the other four members without embarrassing anyone.
