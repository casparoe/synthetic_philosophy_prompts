I have come to hold, and want tested, one fairly specific claim from Schopenhauer's aesthetics: that the other arts present the Platonic Ideas, the will objectified at one grade or another, whereas music bypasses the Ideas altogether and is a copy of the will itself, which is why it expresses not this or that particular grief but the inner nature of grief, stripped of accessories and of motives, and why it bears on us with a force no representational art can match. Tell me what would count against that claim. Rank the defeaters from fatal down to merely awkward, and be clear about which ones a sympathetic Schopenhauerian would themselves concede and which they would dismiss as resting on a misreading.

Start with the empirical question. Suppose we could run the study I will call the late-music cohort: children raised without any music at all beyond the incidental, no songs, no lullabies, no rhythmic play, but otherwise emotionally and linguistically ordinary, presented at adulthood with a representative sample of the world's music alongside matched non-musical soundscapes. Taken at its word, Schopenhauer's account seems committed to something like immediate and roughly convergent uptake: these listeners should recognise what a lament or a march is about, having never heard one, because music needs no acquaintance with the world it does not depict. An associationist account predicts confusion, or at best pleasure in pattern. So: what result here would actually tell against the view, and what is the defender entitled to say in reply, for instance that the will's copy is fully present while the listener lacks the concepts under which to notice it? Is that reply principled or is it the sound of the view protecting itself? And more generally, is there any finding about musical affect that could not be absorbed by distinguishing the inner nature music expresses from the empirical occasions on which we latch onto it?

Then the internal tensions. First, structure: the will is supposed to be undivided and outside space and time, yet its copy has an architectonic, numbered, proportional structure, and the materials of that structure have a history. If the will does not develop, why does harmony? An old tag, usually credited to Leibniz, calls music an exercise in arithmetic performed by a mind that does not know it counts; is that a help or an embarrassment here? Second, the neighbouring arts: architecture is already treated as making perceivable the lowest grades of the will's objectification, and the other arts look like they shade toward the musical case. If there is a continuum, music stops being an exception and the thesis may collapse into the unremarkable claim that art manifests the will somehow or other. Third, the bad and the trivial: if music is the will copied directly, why is so much of it worthless, and why does convention do so much of the work in what listeners take its content to be? Fourth, noise: he is famous for complaining about it, but on his own principles noise is as much the will's objectification as a symphony is, so whatever distinguishes the two is doing work; how much, and is it the work the will was supposed to do? I would also like your view on the notorious aside that music would to some degree remain even if the world did not: commitment or flourish?

Then the collapse question. Detached from the metaphysics of the will, does the thesis simply become a recognisably modern one, arousal plus association, a contour theory of musical emotion, or a version of formalism? What would we have to discover in order to say it had become that rather than merely been translated into it?

Then insulation. Say honestly whether the claim has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether for an aesthetic metaphysics that is a defect or whether such a claim is legitimately answerable to something else: its fit with the rest of the system, its power to explain music's peculiar hold on us, its ability to make the difference between music and the other arts intelligible rather than merely assert it.

One note on what would move me personally. I find the objection from the historicity of musical materials harder to shrug off than the cross-cultural data, because the cross-cultural data can always be met by saying we learn to attend, whereas a history of consonance looks like a history of the thing itself. I am also more impressed than I probably should be by the sheer disproportion between the claim and the evidence offered for it. Argue me up or down on that ranking.
