For roughly a decade I have been reading the rationalist blogs — LessWrong, the EA Forum, and the blogs that grew out of them — and the reading group I run out of a friend's living room has been stuck on one fault line for two years: whether anyone who accepts expected-utility maximisation with an unbounded utility function is obliged to be a fanatic. One half of the room (call them the Fanatics) says yes: sufficiently large payoffs make arbitrarily small probabilities decisive, and the embarrassing consequences — the mugger who names an astronomical number, the St Petersburg-style constructions, the astronomical-stakes arguments for prioritising existential risk — are the price of a theory that gets the dominance, separability and dynamic-consistency arguments right. The other half (call them the Tempered) says that a decision rule whose verdict is always fixed by the part of the credence distribution that is least well determined is not a decision rule worth having, and that the cost of avoiding fanaticism is only the loss of some tidy axioms. They are now mostly talking past each other: the Fanatics keep re-deriving the same dominance argument, the Tempered keep producing the same reductio, and nobody's position has moved since the third session. I want the document an adversarial collaboration between them should have produced, and I want it to stand on its arguments alone — no citations, no reading lists, no "see so-and-so 2013"; we are doing this away from the internet and we all know the standard cases already, so don't spend words rehearsing Pascal's wager or Newcomb's problem, spend them on the cruxes.

Three wrinkles have come up in our sessions and I want the document to actually metabolise them rather than route around them. First, the second-order question: suppose an agent is, say, 70 per cent on fanaticism and 30 per cent on some tempered alternative. Does it then "maximise expected value across first-order theories", and if it does, has it simply become a fanatic one level up — given that the stakes of getting decision theory right are themselves astronomical? The Tempered in my group sometimes gesture at a meta-level tie-break but have never written one down. Second, tail sensitivity: take two agents who assign identical credences to every outcome in the "central" range and differ only in the far tail, where neither has any real evidence. If fanaticism makes their choice depend entirely on the difference, is that a defect of fanaticism or just an honest reflection of what matters? Both sides, I think, can agree this question is load-bearing even though they answer it differently. Third, what I have started calling the manufactured tail: an agent can generate new speculative scenarios by an act of imagination or computation, each with a tiny probability and a payoff large enough to dominate, so that under fanatical reasoning the priority ranking among interventions becomes a function of how cheaply the agent canarticulate a tail rather than of anything about the world. The Fanatics have told me this is just Pascal's mugging in a new hat; the Tempered think it is a distinct objection because here no adversary is making a claim, the tails are endogenous. If you think it collapses into something already in the document, say so and say why; if it is genuinely separate, it should get a row of its own. Wherever you land, I want formulations that are neither vacuous nor slanted: a sentence that only a Fanatic would sign belongs in the disagreements, not in the agreed claims.

The format I have in mind is a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table. For the agreed claims, aim for eight to twelve, and make them substantive enough that a reasonable third party could learn something from them — claims about the formalism, about what a decision theory is for, about which desiderata cannot all be had at once, about which standard arguments don't do the work their users think they do; if a claim could be signed by someone who had never thought about the topic, cut it. The table should have a column for the crux in genuinely neutral wording, a column for how each side would phrase its own position so that it would sign that phrasing, a column naming one concrete case or argument both sides accept as bearing on that crux (with numbers specified — probability thresholds, payoff magnitudes, what counts as a peer or as evidence — tightly enough that a prediction could in principle be checked), and then two prediction columns, one per side, recording in advance what each expects that case to show. At least one crux should be one where the bearing evidence is partly empirical, such as how well calibrated human credences in remote tails actually are. Close with a short section on which parts of the dispute are merely verbal and why, and one sentence per side stating what would count as evidence against that side's own view. Roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words; the failure mode I most want to avoid is a table whose cruxes are just the original disagreement restated at greater length.
