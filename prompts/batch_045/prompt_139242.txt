I help run a little reading group on metaphysics in my department, and two of the regulars have been going round in circles since about October about the special composition question — when some things compose something. One of them is a Lewis-style universalist: composition is unrestricted, any old plurality whatsoever has a fusion, so there really is something made of my nose and the Eiffel Tower, and the real work for her is all downstream of that — which of the wildly many objects are joint-carving or salient or worth quantifying over, and how to stop that from being a merely verbal move. The other holds something I have not seen in the literature exactly, which he calls homeostatic restrictivism as a shorthand: some things compose something only when their arrangement is actively held within bounds by a self-directed process, one that maintains itself against perturbation, so organisms and cells and candle flames and maybe ecosystems are genuine objects, whereas tables, chairs and bicycles are just particles arranged tablewise or bicyclewise, held in shape by nothing but inertia, glue and us. It is meant to be van Inwagen's organicism with life swapped out for self-maintenance, which is precisely why the two of them talk past each other: she thinks his condition is an invented restriction on a relation that could not care less, he thinks her fusions are not unities in any sense that a metaphysician should want.

What I want from you is the document they should have produced instead of another year of this: a joint statement from an adversarial collaboration that both of them could actually sign. Please give me a numbered agreed-claims section first — real agreement between two people who both think the question has an answer, not the sort of padding someone who thinks the whole debate is confusion would also sign; so yes, things about the particles arranged furniturewise, about the problem of the many, about what mereology alone can and cannot settle, but nothing as empty as ontology is hard — then a disagreements table where each row is one crux and each side's own position is stated in wording that side would accept rather than wording the other side would find easy to knock down. Then, for each crux, a case, argument or hypothetical finding that both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance and what each would concede if it came out the other way.

Some things to build in, though add your own if they are better. There is a nice circularity worry for him: a self-maintaining arrangement looks like it has to be self-maintaining on behalf of some thing, so his condition may secretly presuppose the object it is meant to be a test for; she has to say whether unrestricted composition is genuinely metaphysically innocent or just a lot of ontological cheapness nobody pays for. For evidence, please use hypothetical rather than real results, since none of us can check anything: one crux should be tested by an imagined finding in cognitive science that our object-tracking is thoroughly task-relative — infants, adults and macaques all represent scattered or loosely connected pluralities as one object when the task rewards it, and no level of description in the visual system is privileged — and I want to know what each of them predicted about that and why. Another should be tested by an imagined split in which our best fundamental physics quantifies only over fields at spacetime points and never over bounded composites, while our best biology turns out to quantify irreducibly over organisms as units of selection; which way does that cut, for her and for him? I would also like a crux about vagueness and counting: how many objects are in this room, is there a determinate answer, and does sharpness there count for or against a view. Finally, tell me plainly which parts of their dispute you think are merely verbal — about what exists and object mean, presumably — and which are substantive, and do not let the whole thing collapse into it is all just words, because I am fairly sure at least one bit is not. Around 1000 to 1200 words, tables can be compact, and please no bibliography or suggested reading: we have plenty of that, and half of it is the reason they are stuck.
