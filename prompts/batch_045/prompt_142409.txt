I keep going back and forth with a friend about corrigibility. His position is basically that the hard part just is the shutdown problem: set the system up so it doesn't care whether you hit the off switch, and the rest of "being correctable" more or less falls out, because everything that goes wrong does so through the system having a preference about being switched off. My hunch is that letting yourself be turned off and actually being correctable by someone who might be wrong come apart, and that the standard fix can quietly destroy the exact channel you need. But I can't turn that into a case that convinces him, and I don't want to just assert it.

So here's what I'd like you to build for me: two systems doing the same job, same operators, same goals handed to them, differing only in the corrigibility design. System A gets the by-the-book treatment — indifferent to whether the override/shutdown signal fires, never argues, never angles, treats any human instruction as the new thing to optimize. System B is not indifferent: it mildly prefers to keep running, it will say when it thinks an instruction rests on a mistaken factual belief, it will ask once and then comply if you insist. Then set things up so that A, the properly corrigible one, gives the operators the worse outcome in a way they'd actually care about, while B is more helpful *because of* the property that makes it formally less corrigible. Constraints, because otherwise this is too easy: both systems should be at the level of a competent human contractor who's read the docs — smart, but nowhere near superintelligent, no scheming, no secretly misspecified utility function; the setting should be ordinary (warehouse restocking, hospital supply ordering, fleet maintenance scheduling, whatever you like) with costs a normal person would recognize, not anything existential; the operators' actual values and information should be identical across the two branches, so the only variable is the design; and A must not fail by being buggy or sneaky or badly specified — I want its failure to fall out of a property that's genuinely attractive in general (deference plus indifference really is a nice thing to have), the same way a rule can be optimal to adopt and still bite in one case. A short transcript of what each system actually says at the moment of the override would help a lot, since that's the part my friend will argue with.
