I write speeches, and I keep running into a pattern in the way the phrase "Western values" is used in op-eds, commission speeches and parliamentary debate. I would like to know whether the pattern has been described and named, because I need to talk about it precisely and I don't have the vocabulary.

What I have noticed is this. The phrase is unfailingly honorific: nobody uses it in a neutral register, and I have never once seen anyone argue that we should have less of it. But which things it covers seems to track not history but rhetorical direction. When a speaker wants to claim a practice for the tradition — constitutional government, toleration, the abolition of slavery, freedom of inquiry, secularism, or the welfare state, depending on who is speaking — the practice is described as an expression or a flowering of Western values. When a speaker wants to distance the tradition from a practice — the crusades, the Inquisition, colonial extraction, racial science, the slave trade itself, massacres carried out under European flags — the very same practice is described as a betrayal of, or deviation from, Western values. Four features of this strike me as worth pulling apart.

First, the classification is predictable in advance from the rhetorical direction alone, before you know anything about the practice: credit goes in, blame goes out. The extension of the phrase looks like a function of its valence.

Second, the two uses do not play the same role in the argument. In the credit direction the phrase is used causally or explanatorily — it says something about why the thing happened here. In the blame direction it is used only as a standard of criticism. I have never seen anyone write that colonial extraction happened *because of* Western values, only that it betrayed them. So the phrase appears to switch between being an explanation and being a yardstick while keeping one and the same honorific glow.

Third, the move seems to be reserved for "our" terms. Writers who treat atrocities as betrayals of Western values are often perfectly willing to treat comparable atrocities elsewhere as *expressions* of some other civilisation's values rather than as betrayals of them.

Fourth, the pattern is diachronic. Individual practices migrate from one column to the other over decades — established religion, empire, eugenics are the clearest cases — and the phrase survives every migration undamaged, which makes me suspect it has no takedown condition: nothing anyone could point to would count as the tradition's having failed to have the values attributed to it.

I am not after a label. I would like the following.

1. Does this pattern have an established name, and if so what is the literature? A friend who took one philosophy course suggested "persuasive definition," but as I understand that idea it concerns a single speaker quietly stipulating a new descriptive content for an emotionally loaded word in order to win an argument, whereas what I am seeing is spread across a whole speech community, is never stipulated explicitly, and is used by the same speakers in both directions with no apparent sense of inconsistency. "No true Scotsman" does not seem right either: nobody here is defending a universal generalisation against a counterexample, nothing is being rebutted, the classification simply slides. "Weasel word" seems too weak, since the problem is not vagueness but directional movement.

2. For each candidate that is genuinely close, state exactly where the fit breaks; then tell me which candidate does most of the work and what is left over that none of them capture.

3. If nothing established covers it, name it and define it: give the necessary conditions, and then the boundary cases. In particular, tell me whether the phenomenon is distinctive to civilisational or heritage terms — Western values, our values, Asian values, Islamic values, Judeo-Christian values — or whether it is simply the ordinary life of any honorific abstract noun: democracy, science, professionalism, friendship, the rule of law. If my example turns out to be an unremarkable instance of something very general, say so plainly.

4. Give me a diagnostic. If I wanted to work through a corpus of speeches and decide case by case whether a given use of the phrase is an instance of the pattern or a genuine descriptive claim about a tradition, what should I look for — and what would a non-instance look like? Is there any formulation of "Western values" that would commit a speaker to something she could be shown to be wrong about?

5. Finally, is the pattern a defect? Is the phrase doing work that a less elastic term could not do, or is a community's normative self-description *supposed* to have this structure, on pain of losing the ability to criticise itself at all?
