I teach a graduate seminar on causation, and every year I discover that I cannot state, to my own satisfaction, what my verdicts on particular cases actually add up to. I am not asking for a survey of the two standard families of account — the one that reads causation off what would have happened otherwise, and the one that reads it off what a hypothetical manipulation, with other things held fixed, would do. I know both, and each of them gets roughly half of my verdicts. I want a principle. Please argue it on its merits rather than telling me what has been said about it; I am not asking for a reading list, and a list of names would not settle anything for me.

Here are the verdicts I am unwilling to give up.

1. Two people throw stones at a bottle. The first shatters it; the second, thrown a moment later, would have shattered it had the first missed. The first throw caused the shattering and the second did not.

2. Two guards in different buildings flip their own switches at the same instant. Either flip alone is sufficient to sound the alarm, and it sounds. Both flips caused it.

3. A five-member committee hires a candidate if at least three members vote yes. Four vote yes. Each of the four yes votes caused the hiring, even though no single one of them, with the others as they actually were, would have changed the outcome.

4. In a faculty meeting, forty of fifty members vote yes on a routine motion when three would have sufficed. Here my confidence in 3 collapses: I no longer want to say that the fortieth yes vote caused the motion to pass, and I cannot point to a structural difference between it and the fourth yes vote in 3.

5. A nurse omits a scheduled dose; the patient dies and would have lived had the dose been given. The omission caused the death. But I refuse on the same test to say that the nurse's having been born caused the death, although had she never existed she would not have omitted the dose and the patient would have lived.

6. A fund's algorithm submits a large sell order at 9:31 and the price falls two points over the following minute. The order caused the fall. I hold this even though a colleague observes that no one could have intervened to cancel that order without alerting the other participants, whose reaction would have moved the price by a route that does not run through the order at all.

Cases 1 and 2 pull against each other, and so do 3 and 4. My own tentative principle is: C caused E if and only if there is some way of changing C, with everything not downstream of C held as it actually is, that changes whether E occurs. I can see that this fails on 6, and I suspect it cannot handle 1 and 3 without repairs that are little more than labels for the answers I want.

Two cases I have not settled, on which I want your principle's verdicts. (a) A site stays up because a load balancer reroutes traffic whenever any one of twenty servers saturates; no individual server's being up made a difference, though the number that were up plainly did. Did the fact that at least eighteen servers were up cause the site to stay up, when in fact all twenty were? (b) A bridge with a fifty-tonne limit collapses under a fifty-one-tonne truck, the only load variable anywhere near its threshold. Did the fifty-first tonne cause the collapse?

Answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion:

1. The principle, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case you have not seen.
2. The clause that separates case 1 from case 2, and what that clause costs you elsewhere.
3. Your verdicts on the two unsettled cases.
4. Which of my six verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and which one you would abandon if forced to choose.
5. How confident you are in the principle, where its reasoning is most likely to fail, and the single argument or case that would lead you to a different verdict. Do not hedge by telling me that intuitions vary.
