I'm a junior, and this year I started a small club at my school on technology and the future — fifteen or so of us meet on Fridays, and we've been given about twenty minutes at the spring assembly in front of the whole upper school. I'm closing it with a poem instead of a slide of statistics, and I'd be grateful for help writing it.

The thing I want it to be about is what people sometimes name Moloch: a situation in which every person involved would prefer a different outcome — everyone says so, everyone is telling the truth, and everyone knows everyone else is telling the truth — and yet the outcome keeps arriving, because for each person individually, continuing is better than stopping whatever anyone else does.

The part I keep failing to explain to people is that none of this depends on anyone lying, or being greedy, or failing to trust the others. Even with perfect transparency — if everyone could read everyone else's mind and knew with certainty that everyone else would hold back — the arithmetic for any single person still comes out the same, because one person's restraint barely changes the result and costs that person everything. So the trap isn't housed in mistrust. It lives in the gap between what we want together and what any one of us can bring about alone. That is also why I've come to dislike the name: giving it a face implies that someone is choosing this, and the frightening part is that no one is.

Formally I think it should be a sestina: thirty-nine lines, six six-line stanzas and a three-line envoi, unrhymed, with six end-words rotating by the usual rule and all six returning in the envoi (three at the line endings, three inside the lines). The reason I want that form: the six words come back in a fixed order no matter where they begin, so every stanza ends up in the same six places. That seems like the right shape for an argument whose whole point is that different starting points — different industries, different decades, different people — all arrive at the same place.

I'd like the six end-words to be short, plain, non-technical words, and I'd like each of them to mean something slightly different at every return, so that across the six stanzas the reader watches a word like "choose" or "owe" slide from something a person does to something nobody does. The poem should not contain any of the vocabulary of the idea — no equilibrium, incentive, game, commons, prisoner's dilemma, or the name itself.

For the arc: the first two stanzas should be the hunt for someone to blame, and the pull of putting a face on it. Stanzas three through five should show the mechanism in different registers, each one spoken by a different participant in good faith — at least one of them drawn from ordinary high-school life (everyone running homework through an AI, everyone staying up later, everyone applying to more places) and at least one from the adult world. Stanza six should be the turn: the moment the reader sees that there is no one at the wheel. The envoi, with all six words back at once, should say what would actually have to change — something that binds everyone simultaneously rather than one person's restraint — without becoming either a sermon about fixing it or a shrug about human nature.

It has to work read aloud in roughly ninety seconds, in front of students who are half-asleep and teachers who have never heard the term. I don't want forced inversions to make a rhyme land, and I don't want the last line to be a slogan.

If it isn't too much, add three or four sentences after the poem saying which six words you picked and why, and which line you'd point to as the turn, so I can tell my club what to listen for. Please don't include citations, links, or suggested reading — I can't check any of that, and I'd rather the poem stand on its own.
