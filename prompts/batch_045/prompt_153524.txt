I keep going back and forth on the presentism fight in historiography, and I think the debate keeps spinning because two different things are bundled together. Call the first *verdict* presentism: grading past actors by concepts they couldnt have had — calling an 18th-century removal policy genocidal, reading someone's marriage as coercive. Call the second *salience* presentism: letting present-day concerns pick the question, the archive and the level of description, so a colonial governor's letters about railway tariffs get read as documents about racial capitalism.

What I want explained is why giving up the first doesn't get you out of the second. A historian who says "I'll only attribute concepts my actors could have had, and I'll hand down no verdicts" is still choosing a topic, a source base and a grain of description from inside the present — why can't that choice be disciplined the way the verdict rule is? And is the asymmetry real: "ask only the questions your period asked itself" seems unavailable as a rule in a way that "don't apply our concepts to them" isn't, because a period doesn't hand you its questions, and most archives only answer questions nobody at the time was asking.

Use this case to make it concrete: a historian who scrupulously refuses to call anyone in her sources a racist, but organizes her whole book around a category none of them used. Don't just land on "some presentism is unavoidable" — tell me which of the two is doing the real philosophical work, and what the other one is a distraction from.
