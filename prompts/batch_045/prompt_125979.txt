I've been hanging around the rationalist blogs and their comment sections for something like fifteen years, and the rule-following puzzle from Kripke's book on Wittgenstein — the "Kripkenstein" reading, as everyone calls it — keeps resurfacing. Most recently it came up in a small reading group of mine that spent four sessions on the quus case and ended exactly where it started. We've settled into three positions that don't seem to talk to each other. One camp takes the argument to be sound and accepts what's usually called the sceptical solution: there is no fact of the matter about which rule I meant, what my meaning-talk really tracks is the conditions under which my community will count my answers as correct, and the practice has whatever justification it has in virtue of the role it plays rather than in virtue of matching a semantic fact. A second camp says that's capitulation, and that some straight story has to be available — my dispositions, or my dispositions under idealized correction, or a primitively intentional fact — because otherwise we've lost the very thing the puzzle was supposed to be about. The third person in the group, who works in machine learning, says the whole thing is the no-free-lunch problem wearing a hat: finitely many examples underdetermine infinitely many extensions, and which extension a learner actually lands on is an empirical question about its inductive bias, not a philosophical one. Every camp can produce, on demand, the reply to the others' next move. Nobody has moved in four sessions, and as far as I can tell the professional literature has been in the same posture for decades.

My own guess is that this isn't one disagreement but three stacked under a single name, and that the load-bearing one is a verbal dispute wearing a substantive mask. I suspect the two philosophical camps are hearing "there is a fact of the matter" differently — one as "there is a state of things fit to explain and correct my answers, independently of what anyone would say", the other as "there is an answer the community's practice won't overturn" — and likewise hearing "meaning is normative" differently, one as "meaning supplies me with a justifying reason to answer one way", the other as merely "answers can be correct or incorrect by a standard." Split those apart and I think what's left is a fight about whether we are entitled to demand that semantic facts be *constituted out of* non-semantic, non-intentional materials in the first place, which looks like a bedrock methodological disagreement that neither side can argue the other out of. That's a hypothesis, not a conclusion, and I'd rather be told it's wrong than have it flattered.

So what I want is a diagnosis, not a survey and not a verdict on who wins. Take the positions concretely — the quus case; the isolated arithmetician with no community to be checked against; the claim that meaning has to be able to make my actual answer *wrong*; the worry that a community-based account makes meaning strangely immune to the possibility of error — and tell me, for each, what kind of disagreement it turns on: a hidden empirical question, a verbal difference over 'fact' or 'normative', a difference in what a solution is required to deliver, or bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis. Would a complete theory of inductive bias — a real one, of the kind that says exactly which function a given training regime picks out of the space of extensions consistent with its data — settle the machine-learning version, and would settling that settle any of the philosophical dispute, or only some layer of it? Is there a distinction that dissolves one layer while leaving another intact? Is the impasse evidence that the question "what did I mean by plus?" is ill-posed as it's being asked, or just that it's hard? And if you think no progress should be expected here, say so and say why. Skip the literature summary; I can get that anywhere.
