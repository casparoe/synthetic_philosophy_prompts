hi — I host a small philosophy podcast, couple hundred listeners, long-form interviews, most of them know the basics of just war theory (jus ad bellum vs jus in bello, the war convention, Walzer-style defenses of the moral equality of combatants, McMahan-style revisionist attacks on it) but they're not specialists. Next week's guest is a fictional philosopher I've invented for a series on the equality debate, Dr. Naomi Reisz, and I need 8 to 10 interview questions that actually get into the machinery of her position instead of letting her restate it.

Her view, for you to work with: she calls it the Stewardship View. She says the permission to kill in war isn't grounded in combatants' liability at all but in a distributive requirement — once a political community decides to settle a dispute by force, the expected costs of that decision have to fall on the people who voluntarily took on the role of being the community's instrument (combatants) rather than on civilians, and she says this holds regardless of whether the community's war is actually just. So you don't kill enemy soldiers because they're liable; you kill soldiers rather than civilians because that's where the risk is supposed to go. The distinctive bit is her Exit Condition: a combatant loses what she calls protected status not by becoming liable but by failing to take an available exit (surrender, refusal, desertion) once she's had what Reisz calls epistemic notice — evidence such that a reasonable member of her own side would judge the war probably unjust. The loss is scalar: more notice plus easier exit means more of the risk pool may be shifted onto her, up to and including permission to kill her when she poses no immediate threat at all, say asleep in barracks. She insists this still isn't liability, because the killing is answerable: the killer owes the dead combatant's family compensation, and acknowledgement, and an apology, which she says you wouldn't owe for killing someone who really was liable.

I want questions that press where the literature would press, each with a follow-up keyed to the reply she's most likely to give. She deflects really well with the phrase "that's a wrinkle, not a counterexample", and I'd like the sequence to make that progressively harder to reach for. At least one question each on: whether the distributive story just collapses into liability (she'll say it's role-occupancy, not desert — so go after whether voluntarily choosing the role is the responsibility-based liability revisionists talk about, and ask why the civilians who voted for and cheered the war aren't in the risk pool, the complicity-style worry); whether her view ends up permitting deliberate attacks on civilians in cases where that would save more lives overall (she'll say the requirement is deontic, not aggregative — follow up by asking how an aggressor state's unjust decision to use force can generate permissions for its own soldiers); how much evidence epistemic notice actually requires given that ordinary soldiers know nearly nothing and intelligence is filtered (she'll appeal to what's reasonable for them — follow up on whether real notice is so rare that the Exit Condition is idle and her view just is Walzer's, or so common in a blatant war of conquest that it just is the revisionist asymmetry, leaving her own position only the narrow middle band); whether the killer needs to know that the target had notice, and if not whether a blameless killer really owes compensation and apology in a way that distinguishes this from the standard treatment of innocent threats; whether the just side's soldier who gets notice of her own side's atrocities and doesn't quit also loses protected status (if yes: in a war where both sides have notice, everyone may kill everyone, which is definately not the equality people find attractive); and asymmetric exit costs — soldiers in authoritarian armies who face execution or punishment of their families for deserting have the hardest exit and hence the most protection, which inverts the usual intuition about aggressive armies. If you've got room, one on where a drone operator who never recieves any risk herself sits in the risk pool. Also one somewhere on what she thinks the equality claim even is — equality of rights, of permissibility, of blame, or of liability — and whether permission-with-answerability isn't just the revisionist's "acts wrongly but excused" with extra steps.

Finish with one question about what evidence or argument could actually count against the view. Please don't build any question around her having read some particular paper — she's made up, and I don't want citations or reading lists in there.
