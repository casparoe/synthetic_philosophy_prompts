Last week in my formal epistemology seminar we covered van Fraassen's Reflection principle — roughly, that my present credence in a hypothesis H, conditional on the supposition that my credence in H at some specified later time will be r, ought to be r, with an interval version that requires only that my credence lie inside the interval if all I learn is that my future credence will be somewhere in it. I find that I accept it in some cases and reject it in others, and I can't find the pattern. My verdicts, going by gut:

(a) I'm reliably told that tomorrow, after I read a lab report that is the only new evidence I expect on H, my credence will be 0.7. I move to 0.7 now.
(b) Same, except I'm also told that before reading the report I'll be given a drug that inflates confidence in hypotheses of H's kind. I don't defer; I drift to something like 0.55, which I can't derive from any rule I can state.
(c) I'm told that tomorrow, with no new evidence at all, just after thinking much harder about H, I'll be at 0.6 (I'm at 0.4 now). I defer — my later self reasons better than I do now.
(d) I'm told I'll be at 0.6 tomorrow having forgotten one piece of evidence I possess now. I don't defer; if anything I go higher than 0.4.
(e) Sleeping Beauty setup: I'm told every awakened future stage of me will have credence 0.5 in Heads, and I still think I should be at 1/3 now.
(f) I'm told I'll be duplicated tonight, that both copies will have strictly more evidence on H than I have, and that both will be at 0.7. I defer. But if instead one copy will be drugged as in (b) and the other won't, I have no idea what to do.
(g) I'm told only that my future credence will fall in [0.55, 0.95]. The interval version permits anything in that range, but I feel pulled to about 0.75 — and I feel pulled to exactly 0.75 in a variant where I'm told it will be 0.55 or 0.95, fifty-fifty, which makes me suspect that pull is doing all the work in both cases.

My tentative principle: defer to a reported future credence exactly when I would now endorse the process by which it will be formed and expect it to be formed on evidence at least as good as mine; where the process is compromised, treat the reported figure instead as evidence about what my future evidence will look like and update on that. That seems to handle (a), (b), and (d), but it appears to get (c) and (e) wrong in opposite directions, and it says nothing about (f).

Could you test this principle against the cases, repair or replace it, and state the result precisely, one clause at a time, naming the case that motivates each clause? I'd also like its verdicts on two cases I haven't listed: one where I learn the credence of a future system that will have read everything I'll have read but was trained to be calibrated against a loss function that rewards boldness, and one where I learn my future self's entire credence distribution over a partition rather than a single number. Please say honestly which of my verdicts above a defensible principle would force me to give up. Please answer in American English.
