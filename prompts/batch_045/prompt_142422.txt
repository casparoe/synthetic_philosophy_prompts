I convene, as a non-voting chair, the Curriculum Priorities Committee at a small liberal-arts college: nine members, four from the sciences, four from the humanities, and a dean who votes only to break ties. We meet four times a year, and this year we have to pick one of six proposals for a new interdisciplinary concentration, by ranked ballot, then settle at the next two meetings two follow-on questions that are procedurally separate but substantively tied to the first: whether the concentration carries a required capstone, and which of three staffing models pays for it. Three of the six proposals are variations on one theme, any member may add a proposal to the shortlist up to forty-eight hours before the vote, and last year we adopted a proposal that had been added at that deadline, after which two members complained that the winner would have been different had a near-duplicate of it not also been on the list; I have never known whether that complaint was well-founded. Two constraints are fixed: the ballots are strictly ordinal, because the dean has ruled that any expression of how strongly someone prefers an option is improper in a matter with staffing consequences; and I have one spreadsheet, about forty minutes of meeting time, and an obligation to send the faculty senate a short paragraph justifying the outcome, which the senate can return to us if it judges the process arbitrary.

I have read the rough statement of Arrow's impossibility theorem — that no rule for aggregating rankings over three or more alternatives satisfies unanimity, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship simultaneously — and I do not need it explained to me. What I need is a procedure my committee could actually run. A political scientist colleague tells me that if every member's ranking is single-peaked along one shared ordering of the options, majority rule stops cycling and the median member's top choice wins; another colleague says that with three or more options any rule I might adopt can be manipulated by someone misrepresenting their ranking, so that fussing over rules is pointless; a third says that if we vote pairwise in sequence, whoever sets the order of the votes effectively decides the outcome. I don't know what to do with any of these claims, and I would rather the procedure not become a civics lecture. So give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops and says do not vote yet, discuss instead, and the points at which it hands the decision back to a person because the question is one of values rather than of aggregation. Assume I have to say all of this out loud to nine non-specialists in plain language, and tell me what to leave out at this level and why rather than trying to be complete.

Then run the procedure on three cases. First: a committee whose rankings really do line up along one dimension, where I want to know how to check that and what I am entitled to conclude if it holds. Second: last year's situation, where removing or merging one weak proposal would apparently change the winner between two others — including how, in forty minutes and without asking anyone to restate intensity, I can test whether the complaint was sound, and what I should do if it was. Third: a committee that is not arguing about which option is best but about which dimension orders the options at all, since the scientists rank by research fit and the humanities members by teaching load, and no amount of voting seems to touch the disagreement. At least one of these should end somewhere the committee will not like — I would rather find that out from you than in the meeting — and I also want a short closing statement of what the procedure cannot do: which failures it cannot detect, which guarantees it cannot give, and where it is simply dressing up a judgment call as a rule.
