So im in a comparative federalism seminar this term — we've done the US, Germany, Switzerland and the EU — and I've noticed I have really strong gut reactions about who should get to decide what, and I don't think they add up to anything coherent. Before I write the final paper (on whether subsidiarity is doing any real work or is just a slogan people wave when they already know the answer they want), I want the principle, if there is one.

Here are my verdicts. Building heights and zoning in a mid-size city: the city council, obviously. A city raising its minimum wage above the surrounding county so that the chain stores just move ten minutes down the road: that should be settled higher up, county or state — the city shouldn't get to decide something it can't actually enforce. School curriculum: I do not want my local school board anywhere near it, but I also don't want it set in Bern or Berlin or Brussels; the Land, canton or state level feels right, and I can't explain why the middle is automatically the answer. Professional licensing, i.e. whether someone qualified in one place can work in another: as high up as possible. It genuinely annoys me that this is sub-national at all. Drug approvals: EU level, and I don't care that the pills get swallowed locally — the expertise isn't local. A pesticide that washes into a river running through four cantons: something above the canton, or at least a binding deal between them. Fiscal equalisation, rich Länder or cantons transferring to poor ones: fine by me, even though I know it eats into exactly the autonomy I said I liked two paragraphs up. The pandemic: I thought it was okay that cantons and Länder had different rules in 2020, but I also thought the EU should have bought the vaccines centrally and was annoyed that it mostly didn't, and I can't tell you why those two reactions don't contradict each other.

The two that I think are the actual problem. First: if a state wants weaker clean-air rules than its neighbours to attract a factory, I want the federal level to block it; if a state wants stricter rules than the federal floor, I want it left alone. I don't want symmetry there, I want a ratchet — local may be tougher, never laxer — and every version of subsidiarity I've been handed in this course seems to be symmetric, so it can't see what I'm seeing. Second: a town wants to let long-term resident non-citizens vote in municipal elections and the state overrules it, and I'm on the town's side; but when a state restricts voting, or when it ran segregated schools, I wanted the feds to come down hard on it. Same structural move — the small unit wants X, the big unit says no — and I flip which one I root for depending on... something I can't name.

My own stab, which I know is undercooked: give the decision to the lowest level whose members can't just exit when they lose and which has to live with most of the consequences. That handles the minimum-wage case, since the firms can leave, and maybe the river, and it gives the middle-level answer on schools. But it says nothing about drug approvals, and on the segregation case it seems to side with the state, which is exactly backwards.

So: is there one principle that gets most of these, told me which of my verdicts I'd have to give up and which are load-bearing, and then tell me what your principle says about a case I haven't listed — pick it yourself, ideally one where the local unit is both the one with the know-how and the one with the bigotry, since that's where I think all of this collapses. And one more thing that's been bugging me: whatever principle decides which level gets a power, who decides that? The answer in every system we've read about seems to be the federation's own court, and that feels like it should embarrass the whole framework, but I can't say exactly why.
