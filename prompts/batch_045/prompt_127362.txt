Take the 2008 financial crisis — say the run-up through the collapse of Lehman in September 2008, the housing bubble, securitisation and CDOs, the ratings agencies, the handful of investors who shorted housing and were right early. Analyse it through the four-level taxonomy. What I want is the mapping, done carefully:

Who is operating as which kind of creature at which point? Homeowners, mortgage brokers, loan officers, bank risk managers, the quants who built the correlation and default models, the agencies, the regulators, the people who actually did the Popperian thing and let a hypothesis die in their head before the world killed it. For each, what were the options actually on the table, what counted as feedback, and what does the relevant level of the tower predict? Especially: what did the reinforcement environment look like for the Skinnerian layer — was there ever an extinction signal that should have shut the behaviour down, and why didn't it arrive in time? And what, if anything, was the Darwinian layer doing underneath all of it (herding, panic, status competition)?

Then I want the part where it breaks, and I have a specific axe to grind about it. The Gregorian rung is standardly presented as a straight upgrade, because you get to use thinking tools other people already designed — words, numbers, models, spreadsheets. But 2008 looks to me like a case where borrowing a tool made agents worse off than if they had just been Popperian, because the tool carried a warrant condition (its designer's assumption about the kind of world it was built for) that the borrower could neither see nor test, and because the tool's output insulated the bad hypothesis from the evidence that would otherwise have killed it. I've been calling this warrant-opaque scaffolding, and the claim I want tested is that a Gregorian creature using opaque scaffolding can end up functionally below a Popperian one — i.e., that the tower can locally invert. Is that a genuine anomaly for Dennett's ordering, or is it just me misusing the taxonomy? Does the framework have the resources to say "they used the tool wrongly" rather than "Gregorian isn't always better", and if so where does that distinction come from? Would a creature need something extra to be safe with borrowed tools — is there a fifth rung implied here, some meta-Gregorian that audits the warrant conditions of its own tools, and is that a real addition or just Gregorian all the way down?

Also be honest about where the framework shuts up. It's a taxonomy of kinds of mind, not of institutions, so say plainly whether the institutional facts — moral hazard, too-big-to-fail, compensation structures, coordinated herding — are even the sort of thing it can speak to, or whether this episode mainly shows that the taxonomy needs a story about groups and not just organisms. And where you think some other framework does better (bounded rationality and nudges, or the reflexivity / performativity idea that market models shape the markets they model), say so rather than forcing Dennett to cover it.
