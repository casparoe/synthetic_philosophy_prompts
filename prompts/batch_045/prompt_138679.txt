I'm the clinical ethicist on our health system's Clinical AI Review Committee (three hospitals; the other members are a pulmonologist-intensivist, the CMIO, a nurse manager, a pharmacist, and a risk-management lawyer). We have to decide what to do about "Clementine," a vendor product that has been running on two medicine units for about fourteen months. Every night it reads the chart and drafts the next day's plan of care for the day team: new items, items to close out, and — this is the part that matters — items carried forward unchanged from yesterday's plan. It's mostly been good, honestly: nurses like the morning summary, and one internal audit found fewer dropped follow-ups. But last month there was an incident. A patient with Parkinson's disease was kept nil per os from midnight for an elective procedure, the case got cancelled mid-afternoon when the OR ran long, and Clementine correctly closed the two items that literally mentioned the procedure while silently carrying forward "nothing by mouth" and "hold carbidopa/levodopa until after procedure," and also left standing "resume anticoagulant at 22:00" and "post-procedure hemoglobin at 20:00," both of which only made sense if the procedure had actually happened. She missed two doses of her Parkinson's medication; a nurse caught it. No lasting harm. When we asked for a chart review, we found two smaller earlier cases of the same shape — an item carried forward after the thing it quietly depended on had been discontinued — that nobody had flagged at the time. The vendor's response is that this was a coverage gap in one rule, that they've shipped a patch for cancelled procedures, and more broadly that the "frame problem" complaint doesn't apply to them at all, because they don't maintain a symbolic state model with frame axioms: their system is a large language model that conditions on the whole chart, so there is nothing to update and no set of things that has to be declared unaffected, and relevance is learned from data rather than stipulated. I raised the standard worry in the meeting — the old thought experiment where the robot blows up because it's sitting there proving, one at a time, that pulling the wagon out of the room won't change the colour of the walls — and the vendor's people more or less said that's a problem for a 1970s planner, not for them. I've been chewing on this and I can't tell whether they're right or whether they've just relabeled the bump in the carpet: my brother, who writes software, said it sounded to him like "a null-pointer bug with a philosophy degree," which I'm fairly sure is wrong, but I couldn't say exactly why, and if it's basically right then my whole angle here is misguided and the memo should just be about software quality.

What I need is the memo I'll circulate to the committee before the meeting in ten weeks: roughly 900–1200 words, recommending one concrete course of action with a time frame (the realistic options on the table are: terminate the pilot at the end of the quarter; renew for six months with conditions and no expansion; or renew and expand to two more units as the CMIO wants). It has to say plainly whether the vendor's "we don't have a state model, so there's no frame problem" reply is a good reply, and why, in language the pulmonologist and the lawyer can follow without it turning into a seminar. And it has to be honest about the losing side. The intensivist's real point, as I understand it, is not "an error occurred" — errors occur — but that this system's characteristic failure is the one you cannot see coming: it is silent about what it did *not* reconsider, so the omissions never show up in the incident log, and we have no way to audit them. The CMIO's point, which I think is actually the harder one, is that if the bar is "tell us in general which of the things you left alone it was safe to leave alone," then no piece of software clears it and no human team clears it either — we all just coast on a relevance sense we can't articulate — so we'd be holding the machine to a standard that we ourselves fail every single shift, and using it as a way to say no to a tool that is helping stretched nurses.
