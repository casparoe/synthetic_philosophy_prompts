I teach a 12-student upper-level undergraduate seminar on concepts and induction — mostly philosophy majors, with a few who have taken machine learning. I have one 90-minute session, a whiteboard, index cards, and no projector. I want to stage a live activity built on the Bongard format: two sets of six figures, one set satisfying a concept and the other not, with the task of recovering the concept. The thing I want students to walk out having felt — not been told — is that what makes such a puzzle solvable in practice is not the twelve figures but an unstated agreement about which properties are eligible to appear in a rule, and that this agreement is doing more work than the data.

Constraints on the design:

- No lecture and none of the philosophical vocabulary up front: the terms get named only after the final round.
- Students must state their rules in a small explicit property language that I hand out. Please design it — six to eight primitives along the lines of number of connected components, closed versus open curve, containment, relative size, symmetry, and whatever else you think earns its place. A rule has to be mechanically checkable against any figure I can draw.
- I do not want to simply ban the cheap strategies: the rule that lists the six left-hand figures as a disjunction, or the rule phrased so vaguely that nothing could refute it. I want the scoring to make them unattractive. Give me payoffs, including an explicit simplicity penalty, that punish clause-stuffing and enumeration without my having to police them.
- Include a held-out round: once rules are locked, I reveal eight further figures and re-score every rule against them.
- Include an adversarial round in which a team receives another team's rule and must construct a monster — a figure that satisfies the rule exactly as written yet that anyone would sort into the opposite set. Specify how a monster is adjudicated and how, at a cost, the rule-writing team may then patch its rule.

Deliverables: the property language; three problems graded in difficulty, described precisely enough that I can draw them from your text (complete specs for the first two, the third compressed into four lines at most), each with the intended rule, at least one decoy rule that fits all twelve training figures but splits the held-out set, and the held-out figures themselves; the rules and payoffs; a round-by-round timeline with minute marks; a debrief mapping each round onto the point it is meant to land; what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it; and a frank note on which feature of the problem the activity cannot reproduce, so that I know what I must supply myself.

Format: at most 800 words in total, with the timeline as a compact table of no more than six rows and everything else in short bullets. American English, please.
