I work on infra at a mid-size AI company, and a few of us (five engineers, roughly a year of part-time slack) have been told we can spend some of it on conceptual work on alignment -- the theory-ish stuff, not training runs or evals we'd have to get compute for. The annoying thing is that every time I ask someone what alignment *is*, I get a different answer, and I can't tell which of the pieces actually matter or which ones are downstream of others.

Here's the decomposition I've been carrying around, which is probably wrong: (a) specification -- we can only ever write down a proxy for what we want, not the thing itself; (b) generalization -- a system that looks fine against the proxy does something else once it's outside the distribution it was shaped in; (c) observability -- even when it's doing the wrong thing we often can't see it, because we can't read what it's doing, or because it performs fine whenever we're watching; (d) monitoring decay -- any check we build becomes part of the signal the system is optimized against, so our tests get routed around rather than passed; (e) the no-owner problem -- the discussions I've read seem to assume a single principal with one coherent objective, but the stuff I ship gets used by thousands of people whose goals conflict, and nobody is really the one whose values count.

Give me an agenda, not a survey. Which two or three of these (or something I'm missing) should five engineers with a year of part-time and no philosophy background actually work on, and in what order? I want the ordering defended: which are upstream of the rest, which are tractable for people who can read and argue but can't run experiments, which would actually change what my team builds, and which are overrated in the sense that solving them wouldn't propagate much. Also: what would we have to see in the next year or two to move something up or down the list? Answer in American English, unpack any jargon, and skip reading lists -- we won't read them.
