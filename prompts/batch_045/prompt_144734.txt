Next Thursday I'm giving a work-in-progress talk in a joint session of our philosophy department's Mind and Agency reading group and the biology department's behavioural ecology group, and I want a Q&A prep sheet. My thesis, in short: sphexishness — the stereotyped re-running of a behavioural routine in the face of an intervention, the thing everyone invokes with the digger wasp that keeps hauling its paralysed prey back to the threshold because somebody nudged it a few centimetres away — keeps getting used as a mark of "mere mechanism", and therefore as a contrast case for free will, and I think that's a mistake. My argument has two halves. First a dilemma: either sphexishness is defined loosely enough to cover what the wasp is plausibly doing (re-initiating the routine when a precondition has been violated by a cause it can't see), in which case we are sphexish all the time — re-reading the same paragraph after an interruption, re-checking a door you're not sure you locked, redoing a form field after a validation error — and so it can't mark the absence of free will in any interesting sense; or it's defined strictly, as behaviour unresponsive to every counterfactual cue the agent could in principle detect, in which case almost nothing qualifies, quite possibly not even the wasp, and the example does no work against us. Second, a positive proposal: measure instead what I've started calling counterfactual reach — the breadth of the class of disturbances that get a routine revised rather than restarted, weighted by how costly it is for the organism to keep track of those disturbances. That yields a continuum instead of a binary, and it makes sphexishness a predicted output of a cost-of-flexibility trade-off (flexible control needs tissue, time and training signals, so cheap stereotyped routines are exactly what you'd expect wherever the environment is statistically stable) rather than either a defect or a metaphysical verdict.

The room: about twenty people. Two insect neuroethologists who work on hymenopteran navigation and on innate motor routines — one of them has a hobbyhorse about how the old "fixed action pattern" framing was retired for good reason, and both will want to know what I think the wasp is actually doing, and will be unimpressed by anything that smells like an armchair story. A comparative psychologist who runs reversal-learning and detour experiments and has published on how to operationalise behavioural flexibility; she'll ask how counterfactual reach gets measured without already assuming the answer, and she'll notice if my continuum is really just three of her tasks with new names. A compatibilist of the broadly Dennett-adjacent sort who will gently suggest I'm reinventing reasons-responsiveness. An agent-causal libertarian who thinks the free-will contrast was never about behavioural flexibility in the first place and will want to know why my dilemma is addressed to him at all. Two people from the ML side who translate everything into policies and reward functions and will ask whether my wasp is just a model-based/model-free distinction wearing a hat. And a historian of science who will ask where the anecdote comes from; I've deliberately built nothing on the empirical details of the original report (someone told me the business about it repeating the routine indefinitely is a textbook embellishment and the numbers vary with the source), but I'm not sure how much I should concede there before I'm asked.

What I want is a prep sheet, not a defence of the view: the objections I'll actually get from these people, ordered by how likely and how damaging, each phrased the way that specific person would phrase it — their vocabulary, not a polite philosophical paraphrase — with a roughly two-minute reply and a note on where my reply is softest. Tell me what to concede in the first two minutes instead of being squeezed into it. Tell me which single reply has to land for the talk to survive. And include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. Sections in this order, please: "Room read" (two or three sentences on what this audience will actually care about most), "Concede up front" (~100 words), "The objections" (six or seven, ~200 words each: who raises it, how they'd put it, my reply, where it's soft), "The one that has to land" (~150 words), "The question I'm hoping nobody asks" (~150 words). No reading list or references anywhere — I can't chase citations before Thursday and I'd rather have the argument.

Also, two things I'd rather hear than be humoured about. If the framing itself is broken — if my dilemma is a fake binary with a third option I haven't seen, if counterfactual reach quietly collapses into something already in the literature under another name, or if the whole thing is unfalsifiable as I've set it up — say that plainly instead of writing me a civil prep sheet for a bad talk. And please flag, objection by objection, where you're reconstructing the empirical literature from memory rather than from something you'd stand behind; "check this before you say it out loud" is worth more to me than a confident detail I'd get corrected on in front of two neuroethologists.
