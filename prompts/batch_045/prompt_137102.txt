I am assembling the closing item for a reading group on chance, chaos and determinism, and I would like it to be a poem rather than a summary: a sestina of thirty-nine lines — six stanzas of six, plus a three-line envoi — to be read aloud in a little over two minutes at the end of our last session.

Use the ordinary rule of the form. The six end-words of the first stanza recur as the end-words of every subsequent stanza, rotated each time by taking the previous stanza's end-words in the order 6, 1, 5, 2, 4, 3, so that after six rotations the original order returns exactly; the envoi then gathers all six words into three lines. Take as your six: shadow, fold, horizon, measure, orbit, seed — or substitute your own, provided each has both an ordinary sense and a sense internal to dynamical systems theory, and no two of them are near-synonyms. Each word should appear early on mainly in its ordinary sense and later mainly in its technical one, with the drift visible but never announced; the effect I want is that a fixed description is felt to sit on top of an unsettled reference, the way a finitely specified initial condition is compatible with arbitrarily many exact states.

Two facts about the form matter to the philosophy, and the poem should respect them rather than exploit them. First, the rotation is a permutation of finite order: it gives you determinism and exact recurrence, but not sensitive dependence. So if the poem draws any parallel between the returning word order and recurrence in a dynamical system, it must not imply that such recurrence is prompt, periodic, or of any use to a forecaster. The sensitive dependence has to be earned by the content, not smuggled in by calling the scrambling of the end-words unpredictable. Second, the envoi should be the turn: the moment the poem stops asking which orbit we are on and starts asking what the orbits do in the aggregate, and the crowding of all six words into three lines should be what carries that change of subject.

On the content, keep apart three failures of prediction that are habitually run together: the growth of an initial error, where each additional digit of precision buys only some fixed further stretch of time rather than proportionally more; the difference between failing to identify which exact trajectory you are on and failing to say anything at all, since an exact trajectory may still run alongside the computed one and the long-run statistics may be well behaved even when individual trajectories are not; and the case in which the model is itself the wrong shape, so that no amount of precision would help. Stanzas one and two should state the Laplacean picture straight, without irony, and the middle stanzas should be where it thins.

Constraints on what the poem may claim: it should not say that sensitive dependence makes the world random, or that it refutes determinism, and if the demon makes an appearance, give it something to be wrong about other than the falsity of determinism. Avoid the word butterfly, and avoid closing the tension into a moral about humility or wonder. A reader who knows the literature should find the distinctions respected and the technical senses used correctly; a reader who does not should simply find a poem about weather and the limits of foreknowledge. Write it in flexible iambic pentameter or whatever measure reads best aloud — the line endings carry more weight here than the metre.
