My thesis, in short: the right to exclude is best understood as a fiduciary or jurisdictional power rather than an ownership-like one, and it comes with what I've been calling the Coercion-Symmetry Condition — a political community may permissibly exclude an outsider only if it genuinely refrains from exercising coercive power over that outsider, since the nationalist case for exclusion rests on a picture of outsiders as people the community simply does not rule. My claim is that contemporary states blow this condition constantly (visa conditionality and carrier sanctions, liaison officers posted in origin countries, offshore processing and pushbacks, criminalizing migration-related conduct extraterritorially, biometric extraction in transit states, readmission deals bought with aid and trade threats, border surveillance exported hundreds of miles outward), so they end up practicing what I call coercion without admission or selective subjection: they impose the burdens of being governed on people they refuse any of the protections of membership to. So the conclusion is that most actual exercises of the right to exclude are unjustified on the nationalists' own terms, and that the remedy is not open borders as such but a choice — stop coercing, or concede that coerced outsiders have a defeasible, scaled claim to entry.
