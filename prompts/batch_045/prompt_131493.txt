hey — I run a little reading group on discord (like 8 of us, none of us have any academic background, we mostly just argue in the voice channel), and we've been stuck for about three sessions now on what "thinking something through properly" would even mean if you had unlimited help. The setup we keep circling around: you're allowed to consult copies of yourself, each copy can consult more copies, and copies can run in parallel so you get a lot of deliberation done in a short time. Someone in the group said this is called HCH? There's also the version where instead of copies cooperating you have two sides argue in front of a judge, and another where you don't build a tree at all and just take a vote or a market price across a bunch of separate people. We've realized we all mean different things by "the ideal version of this," so I want to build a sorting tool.

What I want from you is a decision tree that takes someone from a starting commitment ("I accept that consulting copies of yourself is a coherent thing to imagine") to a named position about what the ideal deliberative process is, or isn't. Rules I care about:

Every branch node has to be a question a person in my group could answer without knowing which position it leads to — a concrete case or a yes/no about a case, not "do you think deliberation is decomposable?" I want the tree built out of the logical space first (here are the questions, here are the answer-combinations they allow) and only then have the actual positions dropped into that space. Ten to twelve questions, six to nine leaves, plus a path list so someone can work through it and land somewhere.

Cases I want used — I made most of these up arguing with a friend, so please don't swap in famous ones:
- the hourglass rule: unlimited copies, but every round has to happen at once and you only get five rounds, so you can't chain questions one after another
- the telegram rule: each copy may return at most 200 words, no transcripts, no attaching your working
- the mirror test: if you'd defer to a copy of yourself, and that copy would defer to a copy of itself, does anything change depending on how many copies deep you go — and if the answer keeps changing with depth, was any of it an answer
- the stranger swap: half your copies are replaced by strangers who share your goals but have totally different memories and habits of mind
- the spoiler problem: whoever picks how to break the question up can accidentally bake the answer into the sub-questions (asking "which of these three bad options is least bad" already gave something away)
- the morning-after check: the process finishes, hands you a conclusion, and you have to decide whether to act on it without being able to re-run it

Distinctions that need to show up on the tree somewhere: depth budget vs width budget; copies of you vs other people; what gets passed back up the tree (prose, votes, probabilities, nothing); who settles disagreement between sibling answers; whether the ideal is allowed to be a procedure no human could ever actually run, or has to be one a person could in principle carry out slowly with enough paper; whether we grade the output by whether it lands on the truth or by whether the original person would endorse it after reading what happened; and whether the choice of decomposition itself gets made by another level of the tree (and whether that's a regress or not).

Leaves I want present: the converging-amplification view; the adversarial-two-sides-and-a-judge view; the aggregation view (votes/markets/forecasts); the view that the whole framing is wrong and real deliberation bottoms out in judgment that can't be factored; the view that the limit doesn't exist or has no fixed point so there's no fact about what the process converges to; the view that it was never about running a procedure but about being the sort of person who reasons well; and the view that the right ideal is an institution or a community rather than anything happening in one head. Add leaves if the space needs them.

Two more things. First, for the answer-combinations nobody occupies, tell me why — incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored — and be honest about which. Second, flag any pair of leaves your tree can't actually separate instead of pretending it can, and point out at least one node where which way you go depends on an empirical fact about how good people really are at splitting hard questions into good sub-questions (that feels like the crux to me, and I don't think any of us know the answer).

One thing that personally bugs me and I'd like the tree to have a place for: if I would defer to the copies, why am I not already as trustworthy as the copies are? If you think that question is confused, put in a branch where it gets dissolved rather than answered.

Please don't give me reading recommendations or footnotes, we've got enough to argue about already.
