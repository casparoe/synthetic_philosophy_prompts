I am assembling written exercises for an advanced undergraduate seminar in environmental ethics, to be used in an exam setting with no library access, and I need one that stages the disagreement about wild-animal suffering at a level where neither side can get by on slogans.

The case both characters are arguing about: on an uninhabited island of roughly 40 square kilometres, a conservation agency has eradicated the feral cats that humans introduced there about a century ago. The herbivore population the same humans introduced has since irrupted and is heading into a density-dependent crash. Agency scientists propose a bounded pilot: oral immunocontraception delivered in bait to damp the irruption before the crash, plus a three-year supplementary feeding window, run against two untreated control islands, scored on a pre-registered composite of (i) the estimated share of juvenile deaths attributable to starvation rather than other causes and (ii) a time-budget proxy for chronic stress, with a pre-committed stop rule and a planned reversal achieved simply by ending treatment.

Write exactly five numbered exchanges between:

Dr. Ada Lindqvist, a welfarist interventionist who holds that a unit of suffering carries the same moral disvalue whatever its cause, and that the pilot should go ahead.

Dr. Yusuf Barre, a diagnostic skeptic who agrees that the suffering in question is morally disvaluable and that expected-value reasoning is in principle the right frame, but holds that the pilot should not go ahead yet.

Requirements:

Both characters should be equally competent. No strawmen, no rhetorical knockdowns, no accusations of callousness or of sentimentality. Each exchange must engage the specific claim made in the one before it rather than restating a general position.

The dialectic should move as follows. (1) Lindqvist opens on the baseline problem: since humans already shape wild lives through eradication, fragmentation, climate change, culling and feeding, letting nature take its course singles out no privileged baseline, and in this case the cats were ours. (2) Barre answers with the non-identity point: the pilot determines which individual rabbits come to exist, so under a person-affecting restriction no one is benefited by it, and if Lindqvist instead goes impersonal she must accept that there being fewer wild animals is itself an improvement. (3) Lindqvist takes up that dilemma explicitly, argues that the person-affecting horn also indicts the ordinary conservation work Barre supports, and says what she is willing to accept on the impersonal horn. (4) Barre concedes what he must, then grounds his refusal in something narrower: behavioural proxies calibrated on captive animals may come apart from affective valence, and closing the feeding window after three years creates dependency and a worse crash; he then states the trigger condition that would change his mind. (5) Lindqvist concedes the measurement point, the two converge on the narrowest recommendation they can share, and each names the one thing that would have to be true for the other to be right, leaving the disagreement localized rather than resolved.

Neither character may appeal to any named author, paper, organization or real-world program, and nothing should be put in quotation marks: the argument must run entirely on its own content, and I do not want fabricated citations.

Format: five numbered exchanges, each a single speech of at most five sentences, labelled only with the speaker's surname, with no stage directions and no introductory or concluding frame.
