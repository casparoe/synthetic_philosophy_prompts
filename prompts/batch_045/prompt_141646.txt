I'm putting together a short "where I stand" doc for the little working group I'm in (we basically argue about whether there's any point funding invertebrate welfare research), and two people in the group have now told me my position doesn't cohere — except they disagree with each other about which bit I should drop, which is no help whatsoever. So here's the list. Tell me if it hangs together.

(1) Only felt experience does any work. Nothing grounds moral status except valenced consciousness. Species membership, belonging to our species, being a person, being rational, being somebody's kid — all of that is either irrelevant or at best a proxy for what the creature can actually feel.

(2) Equal weight per unit of suffering. I don't discount a pig's or a chimp's pain because the chimp can't do calculus. This is the commitment I'm least willing to move on.

(3) But capacity isn't irrelevant either, in one specific way: a bigger, more connected nervous system opens up more and richer goods — long-term projects, relationships, music, a sense of oneself over time — so more is at stake for that creature. That's why I think a human death is normally much worse than a mouse death even when the pain in the moment is identical.

(4) Here's the part everyone thinks I made up, and maybe I did. I think capacity matters a second way, through something like the temporal grain of the creature's experience: how long a stretch of time gets bound into a single experience, plus memory and anticipation. The same intensity of pain for the same duration is, I want to say, worse for a creature whose pain can be about something, can be remembered and dreaded, is embedded in a longer stretch of experience. So: same nociception, worse for the chimp than the mouse, worse for the human than the chimp, and this is meant to be a different claim from (3), not (3) in a hat. Relatedly, I suspect grain is why extra neurons stop buying anything past a certain point: if there's a ceiling on how much can be unified into one experience at a moment, then scaling up a brain doesn't scale up the experience, which might be handy for (8) below.

(5) No arbitrary cut-offs. I use this constantly against people who want to draw the line at our species, or at personhood: the underlying properties come in degrees, so what we owe should shift gradually as they shift. I really, really dislike thresholds.

(6) Uncertainty about the small stuff should be taken seriously and reasoned about in expected-value terms. I don't want to be the person who says "probably not sentient, moving on." But I also don't want to end up committed to saying every other thing we could fund is a rounding error next to insect suffering, and I notice I keep resisting that conclusion without being able to say what licenses the resistance. Insects outnumber us by an absurd number of orders of magnitude, whatever the exact figure is, so the arithmetic isn't going to be kind to me here.

(7) Marginal cases: a human infant, or an adult with profound cognitive impairment, and a chimpanzee — roughly the same status, and species isn't doing the work.

(8) Obviously normal variation in human brain size is morally irrelevant — people differ by ten or fifteen percent in brain volume, there's the average male/female difference, none of that can matter. But the gap between a typical adult and a human with massive cortical damage does matter, and matters a lot.

What I want is an audit, not a tour of the literature and not a reading list. Which of these actually conflict, ordered by how bad the conflict is, and for each conflict what would have to give and what that would cost me. The pair I keep getting picked on for is (2) and (4), and I don't think that's a real clash — tell me if I'm right about that and why. Please also say explicitly which other pairs only look like they conflict, since I'd rather not throw away something I don't have to. Then: if I get one revision that cleans up the most, which is it? I genuinely can't see where the load-bearing problem is in my own set, so don't assume I've hidden it in the bit you'd expect.
