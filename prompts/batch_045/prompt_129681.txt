So I've somehow agreed to give the keynote at the Wexley Historical Society's annual meeting in three weeks, and the topic they handed me is whether we keep holding the annual commemoration for the men killed in the 1912 mill strike. It's weirdly personal — my great-grandmother helped organise the first one, and Mrs. Dane, who was the last person who turned up every single year without fail, died in the spring. The society's minutes now literally have nobody down to read the names.

I've been reading around the duty-to-remember stuff (Margalit on memory as something owed by people who care, Rieff on the therapeutic culture that wants to be released from the claims of the dead) and I've ended up somewhere I'm not sure I can say out loud to these people. My thesis, roughly: the duty to remember is a duty held by someone in a relation, not a duty to keep a fact in circulation. Once the last person with a real relation to the dead is gone, the duty dissolves — and continuing the ceremony anyway is what I've started calling mnemonic gentrification, where heritage officers, grant-funded museums, local-history bloggers (me included) keep the memory beautifully maintained and turned into a display, while the thing it actually was, a live obligation between people, gets quietly evicted and replaced by an exhibit. I want to pair that with a beneficiary clause: the people least entitled to declare the duty finished are the ones who inherited the benefit of the wrong — the families who still own the old mill land — because their relation to it is the one thing that hasn't gone anywhere. And I want to claim that some forgetting is respect rather than betrayal, a matter of letting the dead stop being useful.

The room: about sixty people in a church hall. Half are the regulars — retired teachers, amateur local historians, three descendants of the strikers, two of whom come every year. Then the mayor, who worries about the town's image; a state heritage-grant officer who's there because the society's funding is up for review; and a reporter from the county paper who wrote a piece last year about the falling attendance.

Give me my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this room, ordered by how much damage they do, each one in the voice of whoever raises it — the grant officer saying our funding category is literally called endangered memory is going to land very differently from a descendant saying you're finishing what the company started. Short reply for each, and a note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede in the first two minutes so I'm not stuck defending the indefensible version of this, which reply has to land for the talk to survive at all, and give me the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
