I have been trying to settle what money-pump arguments actually establish, and I keep arriving at a conclusion I cannot tell is an insight or a misunderstanding of the dialectic. As I understand the argument: if an agent's preferences cycle — she strictly prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A — then a bookie can offer her a sequence of trades, each of which she strictly prefers to accept, at the end of which she holds what she began with and is poorer; hence cyclic preferences are irrational.

The thought I cannot shake is that the argument's force seems to rest entirely on an unstated diachronic assumption: that the agent evaluates each offer myopically, one step at a time, rather than as a node in a plan. If she backward-inducts, or pre-commits, or simply retains a cheap service that declines trades on her behalf, the pump never starts. And in a market with competing bookies and non-trivial transaction costs, the loss she can be made to suffer is bounded anyway, and presumably already priced into the terms on which anyone will trade with her — a sort of cycle premium. On that reading, cyclic preferences cost their bearer roughly the price of a commitment device plus a modest markup, which is an inconvenience, not a defect of rationality.

Where does this go wrong? Is the residual objection merely that such agents are exploitable in principle even when no one exploits them — and if so, what further premise converts exploitability into a charge of irrationality rather than of bad luck? Or is there a version of the argument that survives cheap precommitment and gets at something other than money?
