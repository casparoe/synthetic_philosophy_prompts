I am putting together a one-page centerpiece for a third-year metaphysics seminar on material constitution, and I want it to be a decision tree rather than a survey of named views. The handout will be used in class as a sorting exercise: students answer the questions cold, follow their answers to a leaf, and only then learn which position they have landed on. Please build the instrument accordingly.

Construct the tree from the logical space first, not from a list of positions. Give me eight to ten branching nodes, each phrased as a yes/no or two-or-three-option question about a concrete case, so that a student who has read nothing can answer it and so that no node asks whether they are a "four-dimensionalist" or whether they "accept coincident entities." The nodes I want used as the branching machinery (adapt or add as you see fit, but do not drop any without telling me why):

- whether any plurality of matter ever composes anything at all, or whether there are only simples arranged statuewise
- whether, given that composition sometimes happens, it happens always, only when the result is a living thing, or only when it answers to some interest or purpose of ours
- whether the thing that is now a statue already existed last week, when its matter was an unshaped lump; and whether it survives being squashed flat one second before the artist stops working
- whether the statue and its portion of matter have different persistence conditions — testing this with molecule-for-molecule replacement that preserves the shape against gradual reshaping that preserves the matter
- whether they differ in modal or historical properties: the statue could not have been made of bronze while the clay could have been; the statue essentially has the shape it has while the clay does not
- whether, at a time when both are said to be present, there are two things in one place or one thing counted in two ways
- whether the statue stands to the clay as a phase or temporal part of it, as a way the clay is, or as a separate occupant of the same region
- whether the verdict changes when the statue is melted and recast from the same matter in the same mould, or when its shape is exactly reproduced in different matter

Produce ten to fourteen leaves. Each leaf should carry the name of the position where a stable one exists (mark it "unnamed" where nobody occupies the cell), one sentence stating what the position says about the statue and the clay, and one sentence on its least comfortable commitment — that the statue never existed, that there are two coincident objects, that talk of statues tracks nothing in the world, that whether anything exists depends on what we care about, and so on.

Three further things I need. First, a short note for each combinatorially available but unoccupied cell, saying whether it is empty because incoherent, because unattractive, or merely because unexplored. Second, an explicit flag for any pair of leaves your questions cannot separate: I expect trouble among the constitution-without-identity view, the co-location view, and the temporal-parts view, and I would rather the tree say plainly that they coincide on such-and-such cases and diverge only on such-and-such further question than pretend to a sharpness it does not have. Third, mark any node on which all surviving positions in fact agree, since those are dead branches I should cut.

Close with a short paragraph on which single question does the most classificatory work and which does the least, so that I know what to sacrifice if the tree has to fit on one side. Keep labels to a name and a one-line slogan: I do not want a literature survey, and I will supply references myself.
