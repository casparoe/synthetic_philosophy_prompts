hi — I need actual help with this one, not encouragement.

Take the role of a professional moral philosopher who is also, annoyingly, a very good speechwriter for rooms that aren't full of philosophers. Before you draft anything, brainstorm for a few paragraphs: (a) what the argument in this speech actually is, (b) what it is NOT, and (c) a list of sentences I am not allowed to say, with a reason for each. Then write the thing.

Situation: I'm the principal of a small public K-8, about 420 kids. At the spring community meeting next week I have to announce that we are ending the honors track — roughly 40 kids in grades 4 through 8, picked by test scores, who get their own room, half of a teacher, and about $20k a year (a trip, materials, a competition) — and moving all of it into a reading intervention program for the ~90 kids reading well below grade level. Two complications. First: six of those forty honors kids are among the poorest kids in the building, and one of them is a kid whose mother will be sitting in the third row. Second: the board passed, in the same motion, taking down the honors plaque in the main hallway and stopping publication of the honor roll. Nobody gains anything from that part. I voted for it. I can't defend it with the arguement I actually believe in, and I'd rather the speech not pretend otherwise.

The room: ~200 people in the gym. Mostly parents, a lot of them angry. Teachers. Seventh and eighth graders. The superintendent. And two parents who I know are, respectively, a moral philosopher and an education economist — not hostile, but they will notice if the argument I give out loud isn't the one I gave the board, and the philosopher has definately heard of the levelling-down objection and I suspect is waiting to see whether I walk straight into it.

Length: 7–8 minutes, so call it 900–1,000 words.

Hard rules for the speech itself:

- No philosophy words of art anywhere in it. No "prioritarian," no "levelling down," no "egalitarian," no "utility," no "social welfare." The philosopher should be able to tell which view I'm relying on from how the argument is built, not from labels. Everyone else should just hear a plain reason for a decision about their kids.
- The reason has to be that the same help counts for more when it goes to a kid who is worse off — and worse off in absolute terms, not worse off compared with the other kids in this building. Our data dashboard ranks kids against each other and I catch myself saying "our bottom quartile," and I don't want to do that here; on the view I actually believe, the ranking inside our building isn't what does the work. If the speech ends up leaning on the ranking anyway, I want it to say so out loud instead of smuggling it in.
- Some kids lose something real. No "nobody loses here," no "this is good for everyone," no pretending the honors kids are trading up.
- Deal with the six. If a loss counts for more when it lands on a kid who is already badly off, then their loss counts for more, and I don't want that quietly skipped or buried in a sentence about "the program as a whole."
- For the plaque and the honor roll: don't manufacture a clever defense out of the same argument. Either drop it, or say honestly that it rests on something else and say what that something is, or say plainly that I can't justify it with the reason I've just given. Your call which — but it can't sound like I'm resigning or like I'm apologising for having principles.
- Never say that the gap itself is what's wrong, or that fairness means nobody should have more than anybody else. It isn't my view and it's the sentence that hands her the objection.
- Don't pretend third-grade reading scores tell us whose life will go badly. If the speech needs the long-run claim, it should admit it's making it.

Then, after the speech, a separate note to me — 300 words max, plain, the kind of thing I'd read in the car afterwards — on where the speech quietly takes a side on something philosophers really argue about. Sort it into three piles: what's basically settled among people who work on this; what's genuinely contested and where the speech has picked one side; and which parts rest on empirical claims I can't actually back up. Don't tell me the contested one is the consensus if it isn't, and be specific rather than hedgy: I'd much rather read something like "whether the priority view really escapes the levelling-down objection is disputed, and whether 'worse off' is absolute or comparative is a separate fight with different people on each side" than a general "this area is contested."
