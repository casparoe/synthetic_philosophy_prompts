I'm finishing a chapter on moral particularism and I'd like a consistency audit of my own position before I defend it, because I have the feeling that some of the things I want are cheap separately and expensive together. Let me state the package as plainly as I can. I work in the broadly Dancy-style particularist tradition, but several of these commitments are my own additions, and the terms I use for them are my own, so please argue about the substance rather than about who holds what.

(1) Holism about reasons. Whether a feature of a situation is a reason at all, and which way it points, depends on what else is present in the situation; no feature carries its valence from case to case as such.

(2) Anti-codification, strongly stated. There is no true, informative, finitely statable moral principle that explains verdicts, and moral judgement does not need one in order to be a genuine exercise of reason. I mean this more strongly than the claim that principles are true but too complicated to be of use: I want to say there is nothing principle-shaped for the verdicts to answer to.

(3) No brute variation. Whenever a feature's valence differs between two cases, there is some specifiable difference between the cases that explains the difference. I hold this because I want moral judgement to be answerable to reasons rather than to taste, and because I don't see how a verdict could be justified by a difference nothing can be said about.

(4) Defaults. Some features have a default valence — that a promise was made, that an action would cause someone pain — and they point as they do unless something in the context defeats or silences them. This is what I think makes moral advice and moral education possible at all.

(5) Silencing. For some features in some contexts, the context does not outweigh the feature but silences it: the feature is not a weak reason that loses, it counts for nothing at all. My central case: you promised Ravi that you would bring a parcel to his flat at six; at ten to six you learn that Ravi died at midday. I want to say that the promise is not a reason, however slight, for going to the flat — not a reason that is outgunned by the futility of the trip. I also want to distinguish silencing from what I call dead weight: a feature that is not a reason and would not have been one anyway, even had the case been otherwise. I claim this distinction can be drawn without appeal to principles.

(6) Drift. Take a graded series: a promise to deliver antivenom to a snakebite victim; a promised birthday cake; a promised book; a promised newspaper; promised junk mail; and finally the promise to Ravi, who has been dead since noon. My verdicts slide continuously down this series: the promise is a strong reason at one end and nothing at all at the other, and there is, I think, no step at which its status flips from outweighed to silenced and no sharp cut-off anywhere in between.

(7) Contrastivism about verdicts. Ought is always ought-rather-than-the-alternatives, so the deontic verdict on an action can change when an alternative is added to or removed from the option set even though nothing about the action itself has changed.

(8) Universalizability. Moral judgements are universalizable: if an action is wrong for me, it is wrong for anyone relevantly similarly situated, and moral disagreement between such people is genuine disagreement rather than a difference of sensibility. Several readers have told me this sits badly with (1); I don't think it does, but I'd like to know whether I'm right.

(9) Expertise. There are moral experts; their verdicts converge on hard cases; expertise is transmitted by training on cases rather than by teaching principles; and an expert pressed to defend a verdict can always say, non-vacuously, why the verdict is what it is.

Different readers have flagged different parts of this and I can't tell which of the flags I should take seriously. What I want is an audit, not a survey of the literature: go through the set and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, ranked by severity rather than presented as a list of equal worries; which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection and exactly why they dissolve; for each real tension, what specifically I would have to give up and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the set; and which single revision would clean up the most. And I'd like you to be explicit about the epistemic status of your own audit: tell me how confident you are in each ranking, where your reasoning is most likely to break down, and which consideration, if I could produce it, would make you reorder the tensions or drop one of them altogether.
