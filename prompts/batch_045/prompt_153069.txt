I'm giving a ninety-minute seminar on anthropic reasoning next month to a reading group of statistics and economics PhD students: fluent in Bayes' theorem and in ordinary selection effects such as survivorship bias and truncated samples, but none of them has encountered self-locating evidence. The goal is narrow. They should leave able to write down, for one pair of hypotheses, the likelihoods that the self-sampling assumption (reason as if randomly drawn from the actual observers in your reference class) and the self-indication assumption (your own existence is itself more probable the more observers there could be) assign to the datum, and to point at the term where the two expressions come apart — not to have a view on which principle is true.

My candidate carrying case is a version of the presumptuous philosopher: two hypotheses that the students' ordinary evidence treats symmetrically, one containing far more observers than the other, but with the surplus observers confined to a much later era, causally disconnected from ours and with evidence nothing like ours. I want to know whether that wrinkle belongs in the case from the first minute or should be added once a plainer version has done its work, and, more generally, what the carrying case has to have and has to lack. I'd also like them to meet at least one case where the two principles agree, so they don't leave thinking that anthropic reasoning always moves the answer.

Plan the session rather than write the explanation: the order in which the distinctions must arrive and what depends on what; where to locate the point that the disagreement concerns a likelihood term rather than a prior; the misconceptions to expect (I'm guessing that you cannot update on your own existence because you had to exist in order to ask, and that the self-indication assumption is just the self-sampling assumption with a wider reference class) and the point in the sequence at which to defuse each; one closing question that distinguishes having the structure from having memorized a verdict; and what I should openly decline to settle in ninety minutes. Lead with the single most important structural decision in your first sentence, then give the plan, with no preamble.
