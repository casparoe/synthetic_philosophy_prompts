I'm in a graduate seminar on the philosophy of law, and last week we spent two sessions on Hart's secondary rules — the rule of recognition, the rule of change, the rule of adjudication — and on his discussion of whether a legislature can bind its successors, and of the difference between merely procedural constraints on how a later legislature must legislate and purported substantive limits on what it may do. Nobody in the room had a clean answer to a case one of my classmates raised, and I'd like you to try to give one.

Imagine we hand Hart — who died in 1992, so suppose he's been given a two-page briefing on permissionless ledgers and on-chain governance and takes it in stride — the following constitutional order, which I'll call Aurelia. All of Aurelia's law is a single versioned document held on a distributed ledger; the last part of that document, the Amendment Part, is itself amendable, and it contains three provisions. First: an ordinary amendment needs a simple majority of the assembly. Second: an amendment to the Amendment Part takes effect only if it satisfies the version of that Part in force at the moment of enactment — a validity condition that refers to itself. Third: an amendment may declare itself entrenched, and an entrenched amendment may be repealed only by a two-thirds supermajority. Now three events, in order. (i) The assembly passes, by simple majority, an amendment E that entrenches itself against future repeal. (ii) A later assembly, again by simple majority, passes R, which repeals E. (iii) Later still, S repeals the self-referential clause and substitutes the rule that an amendment is valid if it complies with the version in force at the time the amendment was first proposed; S complies with the rule it enacts but not with the rule it repeals. At the end, the courts of Aurelia treat S as law, while the operators of the software that executes the ledger treat S as a nullity and keep running the pre-S version.

What I want is Hart's answer rather than your own view: reconstruct from his actual commitments — the rule of recognition as a matter of convergent official practice rather than of legal validity, the account of amendment as a rule of change, the internal point of view, his handling of revolutions, interregna and divided officialdoms — what he would say about E, about R, about S, and about whether Aurelia still contains one legal system at the end of the story. One of my classmates said the whole thing dissolves the moment you remember that the rule of recognition is just whatever the officials accept, so that (i) and (ii) are trivially fine, (iii) is a straightforward nullity, and the split is just a revolution; I think that reply is too cheap, since it is equally available to someone who has never read Hart and it makes the case unanswerable rather than answered. So please also say what Hart would have to add to his framework for the three episodes to come apart from one another, where exactly the apparatus underdetermines the answer, and where you are plainly extrapolating beyond anything he considered. Please also identify which single feature of Aurelia is doing the work — the self-reference in the validity condition, the fact that the criteria of validity sit in the same mutable document as the rules they validate, the fact that the officials whose acceptance is supposed to matter are partly software, or something else — and say what happens to the case if that feature is removed. Finally, give me the strongest Hartian reply to someone who concludes from Aurelia that no rule of recognition can do its job unless it has an unamendable core. I don't need citations, textual references or further reading — I've read the material; I want the argument itself.
