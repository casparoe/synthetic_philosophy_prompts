Ok so I'm stuck on the special composition question — the one about when some things add up to (compose) one thing. Just to fix labels: universalism says any things whatsoever have a sum, so my left sock and the Eiffel Tower together make an object; nihilism says nothing is ever literally composed of anything, there are just simples arranged catwise, tablewise and so on; a restricted or "sometimes" answer says there's some condition C and the xs compose something iff they satisfy C. The sometimes answers I actually care about are things like "the xs constitute a life" and, the one I half like, "the xs realize a single integrated causal profile" — their joint causal role isn't just the sum of the causal roles they'd have separately, which is why a cat's atoms compose and a pair of rocks on opposite sides of the solar system don't.

Someone in my reading group and I built the following argument, and I've come to distrust it because it seems to refute every sometimes answer at once without ever looking at any of them.

(1) If the xs compose something, then necessarily they compose something: in every possible world in which all of the xs exist, the xs compose something. The thought behind it: whether there's a table here can't depend on what's happening on the other side of the galaxy. Composition isn't an extra contingent fact floating free of the things that do the composing.
(2) The atoms arranged catwise in front of me compose something — a cat. Call them the cat-atoms.
(3) There's a possible world in which all of those very same cat-atoms exist but are scattered: one on Mars, one in the asteroid belt, and so on.
(4) In that world, the cat-atoms compose nothing. That's just what a sometimes answer says — things strewn to the winds aren't an object. A universalist denies this, obviously.
(5) But (1) plus (2) says the cat-atoms compose something in that world too. So (5) contradicts (4).
(6) Therefore (1) and (3) together are incompatible with any restricted answer to the SCQ. And note that nothing in the argument mentions what C is. It doesn't matter whether C is "constitutes a life" or "realizes an integrated causal profile" or anything else you like. So the argument kills off every restricted answer in one go, by modal fiat, and leaves me with "always" or "never" and no reason whatever to prefer one to the other.

That last bit is what makes me suspicious: an argument that refutes every substantive answer without engaging with the substance has probably proved too much. I've learnt nothing about composition; I've just been backed into a corner. But I can't find the illegal step.

What I want from you: name the step or premise I should reject, explain why it looked fine when I wrote it down, and then price the ways out — including the ones I haven't thought of. Concretely:

- If I have to give something up, I'd rather give up (1) than (2) or (3). But (1) reads as definately true to me, and my guess is that I'm misusing it rather than that it's false. The only place I can locate my discomfort is (1), and when I try to repair it into a conditional claim — necessarily, if the xs exist and are related to each other as they actually are, then they compose something — I either gut it or I keep the problem, and I can't tell which.
- If the conditional repair is the right move, what does it cost me? Three things I can't price: (a) objects then come into and go out of existence when you rearrange their parts, which sounds terrible but maybe isn't; (b) "arranged catwise" is vague at the edges — fur, dead skin, gut bacteria — so composition starts to look vague, and I've been told vague existence is incoherent; (c) the cat could have had one atom fewer than it has, so I don't know whether "the xs" in the conditional version is meant to be rigidly the actual cat-atoms or just whatever xs happen to satisfy the arrangement condition, and I think swapping those changes what I've signed up for.
- One friend says the real culprit is (3): "those very atoms" isn't a modally robust plurality, so the argument never gets off the ground. Another says the argument is perfectly fine, the lesson is that restricted answers were never tenable, and I should just be a universalist and learn to live with the ontological junk. Tell me whether either of them is right, and what being a universalist here actually costs once you count it up.

Please don't give me a tour of the standard positions — I've read them. I want a diagnosis of this argument, and then the bill for each available repair.
