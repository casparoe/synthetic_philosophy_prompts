I sit on the internal review board at my company, and we are drafting the wording of a policy about when a decision should be recorded as having been made "intentionally."

Two cases came before us that look identical on paper. In each, a team lead chose a course of action while knowing it would also produce a certain side effect; in each she said afterward that the side effect was not her aim, played no part in why she acted, and was simply something she did not care about either way. In one case the side effect was that another team lost the data access it needed; in the other, that another team kept access it needed. Everyone on the board is willing to say both leads knew what would happen and were equally indifferent to it. Yet nearly all of us want to write that the first lead brought the loss about intentionally and that the second lead did not bring the benefit about intentionally.

One member argues that we are letting our reaction to whether the outcome was good or bad contaminate what should be a purely factual finding, and proposes defining the term by foresight alone so that the asymmetry disappears. To test that, I tried another pair of cases: in both, the side effect was that another team received credit it needed, but in one it cost the lead's own team standing while in the other it improved her team's standing as well. Nothing morally bad occurs in either case, and yet the same pull to say "intentionally" in one and not the other came back.

Is the pull we feel merely a bias to be engineered out of our reports, or is it tracking something about the leads that a foresight-only definition would miss? And if it tracks something real, what should the word be doing in a policy: recording a fact about what the lead was after, marking something for which she can be held responsible, or something else again? Please answer in a single paragraph of at most 250 words.
