I'm finishing a dissertation chapter on the doing/allowing distinction, and I'm doing some consulting on the side for two bodies that keep forcing the same worry on me: a hospital ethics committee rewriting its guidance on withholding and withdrawing life-sustaining treatment, and an incident-review board at a company that fields semi-automated decision systems, where the human contribution to a bad outcome is almost always something like a default setting left in place, an alert acknowledged and then dropped, or a handover that never happened. Both groups need a verdict on a concrete case, reached by people who are not philosophers, which makes me think that whatever the distinction is, an account of it has to be operationally usable and not merely extensionally correct.

So before I write the framing section, I want the specification. Give me the desiderata any adequate account of the doing/allowing distinction has to meet — including an adequate account of why it carries no independent moral weight — with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and tell me which you take to be non-negotiable and which are just preferences of a particular camp. Here is the raw material I want either on the list or argued off it. First, the case battery: the pairs where an agent redirects a threat onto one instead of five versus kills one to supply what five need; the relative who declines to summon help; the clinician who discontinues support that is doing the work of an organ that will not recover; the agent who earlier created the threat and later merely declines to remove it; the double-prevention cases where the only difference is whether the agent intervenes on a mechanism someone else set going; the composite cases where each of many people contributes a tiny permissive act or a small failure and the aggregate does the damage; the matched pairs that differ only in whether something is withheld or withdrawn, or only in how long a delay lets a pre-existing condition run its course; and the pairs where the same bodily behavior — a hand kept in a pocket, a signature withheld, a person walking away — counts as doing under one description and allowing under another, so the account has to say what fixes the baseline and how much of the baseline is allowed to be moralized. Second, the distinctions it must respect rather than collapse: doing versus enabling versus failing to prevent; intending versus merely foreseeing; the difference between the distinction's having pro tanto weight and its being decisive; and the difference between its tracking a moral difference and its merely summarizing verdicts we reach some other way. Third, the constraints it must not contradict: that there is no general legal or moral duty to rescue while there are duties in some relationships and roles, and that the action/omission asymmetry shows up robustly in ordinary judgment and in things like reluctance to vaccinate, in ways that look partly independent of the stakes. Fourth, operationalization: the account has to tell a review board which facts about a case are load-bearing, so that a disagreement can be traced to a specifiable factual or theoretical disagreement rather than left as brute intuition, and so that a verdict is reachable from a written case description in an afternoon.

The part I most want your view on is the tension I keep bumping into, because I suspect some subset of these requirements cannot all be satisfied at once. An account that fits the full battery of cases seems to need a baseline that is partly moralized — who was supposed to do what, what countenances as the normal course — but the more moralized the baseline, the less the distinction can do any grounding work downstream, since saying that letting die is permissible may then amount to little more than saying that no duty to aid was violated; and the operationalization requirement may push hard in the same direction, toward something that just hands the board a checklist of duties rather than a genuine distinction. Tell me whether that tension is real, whether it is a defect of the desiderata or a result about the distinction, and which single requirement on your list does the most work in excluding live candidates. Then, briefly — a paragraph, not a table — score the main families against the list: counterfactual or causal-process accounts, intention- or means-based accounts in the vicinity of the doctrine of double effect, constraint- or rights-based accounts built on something like not making someone worse off, and the skeptical view that the asymmetry is an illusion we should discard.
