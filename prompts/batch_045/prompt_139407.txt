i'm TAing a grad seminar in philosophy of law this term — 14 people, mostly philosophy grad students with three law students mixed in, one 90-minute session, and we've got the Hart–Fuller debate coming up in a couple weeks. I want an activity where they walk into the disagreement rather than me doing core/penumbra vs purposive interpretation on the whiteboard for twenty minutes and then reading the faces.

Rough shape I have in mind, fix it if it's wrong: a staged case exercise where they have to apply a written rule to a pile of hard cases. What I want them to _feel_ is the squeeze — that reading the rule by its words produces outcomes nobody can defend, but reading it by its purpose means you're not really applying a rule anymore, and "the purpose" isn't a single thing anyway, because you can state it at different levels of generality and get opposite verdicts on the same case.

Constraints on the design:

- No vehicles in the park, no grudge informer, no stock examples at all. If one turns up I'll bin it. Invent a self-contained scenario — I was thinking an antarctic research station sharing a greenhouse and a seed store, with an ordinance of maybe five short rules, but use whatever works better.
- At least one rule about a time or place restriction, one turning on a category (nothing living may be removed, that sort of thing), one about a procedure (a signout log, a witness, a countersignature).
- The cases need at least one where the words plainly cover the conduct but the purpose plainly doesn't, one where the purpose plainly covers it but the words plainly don't, and at least one the room will genuinely split roughly 50/50 on.
- Rounds, with something revealed between them. Round 1: text only, no discussion of reasons, just mark each case permitted / forbidden / rule doesn't reach. Then hand out purpose statements — and here's the bit I like: give half the room one statement of purposes and half the room a different, equally plausible one pitched at a different level of generality, and don't announce that there are two. Then a last round with the actual drafter's note, which partly cuts against the text of one rule.
- No advance reading, no framing lecture, and neither name comes out until the debrief.
- Give me the actual deliverable: the ordinance, the case list, both purpose sheets, the drafter's note, a minute-by-minute timeline for 90 minutes with small groups rather than 14 people shouting, what goes on the board and when, and how to tally and display the splits.

Debrief should map specific moments onto (a) open texture and the penumbra, (b) Fuller's purposive-interpretation move and what it's meant to fix, (c) the objection that purposes are indeterminate because of levels of generality, (d) the Hartian thought that rules exist to settle things in advance and that this is worth something even when it costs you. Flag the moments where someone will say something that's secretly the crux.

Be honest about what will go wrong — I'm guessing someone names the debate in round one and deflates it, someone compares purpose sheets when they shouldn't, someone argues the hard cases on the merits instead of as rule-application, and one group finishes way early. What do I do about each?

And be honest about what the exercise can't do. I suspect it can't get at fidelity to law as a moral attitude, or at the internal point of view (everyone already arrives able to follow rules, so there's nothing to see), and definately not at the retroactivity / informer end of the exchange — say plainly what I'll have to supply by talking instead.
