My flatmate is doing a masters in political theory and we keep having the same argument about borders, and last night it got well past the point where either of us knew what we were talking about. Her go-to move is the household analogy: a country is like someone's home — the people living there decide who comes in, nobody thinks you owe it to a stranger to let them move into your spare room, and a home where the residents couldn't say no wouldn't really be their home at all. She uses it to get to a fairly strong conclusion: that a political community has a wide (if not unlimited) right to exclude, that excluding someone isn't in itself an insult or a rights violation, and that people who call open borders a requirement of justice have to explain why territory is the one place where choosing your associates is illegitimate. Sometimes she swaps in a club instead of a house — the members decide who joins, that's what freedom of association is, done.

I want to audit that properly rather than just say 'but it's not a house'. So: give me the correspondence table, for the household version and the club version separately — what plays the homeowner, the house, the door, the knock, the guest, the overnight stay, the eviction, the kids who were born in the back bedroom, whoever built or paid for the place. Then the disanalogies, sorted into the ones that actually do damage to her conclusion and the ones that are just noise. The bits I can't get straight on my own: a home is a space where mere presence is intrusive in a way a country mostly isn't (nobody is claiming a right to sleep in her spare room, they want to rent a flat and get a job); the owner normally made or paid for the thing, whereas nobody alive made the territory; with a couple of hundred countries covering basically all the habitable land there is no 'outside the house' for the excluded person to stand in; the state governs whoever is inside it, which the homeowner doesn't really do to her guests; and the interests on the other side of the door are not trivial. I also can't tell whether the analogy quietly imports that the *members* are the ones with the moral say, which is the part I actually want to argue about rather than the bit about doors.

There's one more thing I keep wanting to throw at her and I genuinely don't know if it helps me or her. I don't think a country is much like a single-occupancy home at all; I think it's like a flatshare. There's no single owner, the people already in it disagree with each other, there's a decision rule nobody agreed to, some tenants have been there thirty years and some moved in last week, the landlord is a bit of a fiction, and — this is my favourite bit — the household is constantly putting up ads for new housemates because it needs the rent money, and then acting surprised and put-upon when people show up at the door. Does swapping the source domain like that actually change what the analogy can support, or does it just restate the disagreement in a funnier costume? And if neither the house, the flatshare nor the club is the right thing to compare a state to, is there a better source domain, or is the whole family of analogies doing its work by smuggling in the claim that the members own the place? I'm arguing this again on Sunday so anything concrete I can say instead of just repeating slogans would be handy.
