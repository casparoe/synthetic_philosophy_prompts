I am designing the final session of a final-year undergraduate seminar on desert, moral luck and non-Western moral philosophy. The students will have read nothing on karma specifically, and I want them to leave with a classification instrument rather than a summary: a decision tree that walks someone from ordinary moral intuitions through a series of forced choices to a named position on what karma is, and on whether any version of it can actually do the work a theory of moral desert is supposed to do. The session's guiding question is whether karma handles the problem of moral luck better or worse than the mainstream retributivist views the students have already covered.

Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of positions: start from the branching questions and the combinations of answers they permit, and only then locate the actual views in that space.

Requirements for the tree:

Six to eight branch nodes. Each node must be phrased as a question a student with no background in Indian philosophy could answer on the spot — a concrete case or a blunt claim, never a question like "do you accept the doctrine of no-self?" No node may be labelled with the name of a tradition, school or historical figure; the labels come out at the leaves, not at the forks.

The distinctions I want forced, in addition to any you think belong: whether the act and its payoff must be linked by a single persisting subject or only by causal continuity in a stream with no such subject; whether the payoff is produced by the act itself (through what it does to the agent's own character, say) or meted out by an independent moral order; whether what is owed is fixed by what was done (backward-looking satisfaction of desert) or by what the payoff is for (correction, moral education, restoration of a balance); whether it is the agent's intention or the outcome that generates the account; whether the unit of accounting is the individual agent or something larger that shares a life, a family, a community or a nation; and whether the circumstances of one's birth can themselves be the discharge of a prior account — the "bad rebirth" move — with an eye to what that implies about blaming sufferers for their suffering.

At each leaf, give the standard label where one is reasonably well established and write "unnamed" where nobody really defends it; where the right label depends on which tradition you ask, say so instead of averaging them. Each leaf carries one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and a flag for whether it still counts as a theory of desert at all, or has quietly become something else.

List the combinations of answers that the logical space allows but that nobody occupies, and say briefly for each whether it is empty because it is incoherent, because it is merely unattractive, or because it is unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot actually separate rather than inventing a distinction to pull them apart.

Finish with a short note on how to run the tree as a live show-of-hands exercise in a ninety-minute seminar: which nodes to take in which order, and where you expect the room to split.

Constraints: no bibliography, no reading list, no citations, and no specialist terminology the students could not already have picked up from a general moral philosophy course — if a distinction needs a technical term, define it in the node itself. Assume you have no access to a library and do not guess at sources; what I want is the structure of the debate, not its literature. Please write the whole thing in British English.
