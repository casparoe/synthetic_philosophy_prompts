I write about neuroscience, psychology and machine learning for a newspaper, and for the past two years I have been working on a long reported essay about the self. The piece is reported, not academic: it moves between a memory clinic, a man I have been following who lost most of his ability to form new memories, and a set of companies that are trying to build machines that speak about themselves in the first person. It is meant to end with two practical sections, one on how we ought to talk about people with advanced dementia, and one on whether we ought to talk about AI systems as persons.

Trying to write those two endings, I noticed that the things I have come to believe while reporting do not obviously compose into a single view, and I would rather find that out now than in the correspondence columns. I know roughly which philosophical positions these are and I am not asking for a survey of them. I am asking whether my own package holds. Here it is.

1. The self is not an additional ingredient of the world. There is the organism, its history, its dispositions, and the more or less continuous stream of self-directed description it generates; the self is the abstraction that this stream defines, in something like the center-of-narrative-gravity picture. And I mean this to be exhaustive. There is nothing more to my being me than what is captured by such an abstraction, and I am willing to accept the word fiction for it.

2. When relatives say of someone with advanced Alzheimer's that she has already gone, I think they are saying something false, and not merely something unkind. There is a truth of the matter that she is still her, it is a truth of the same kind as any other, and it is what makes the things we owe her things we owe to her rather than to a remnant. I do not want to cash this out as a useful convention or a caring attitude we adopt.

3. No existing AI system has a self. When a language model says that it remembers what you told it yesterday, nothing is being reported; there is no one there. I intend this as a substantive finding about the systems, not a stipulation about how I propose to use a word. My readers will hear a claim about the machines, and I mean to make one.

4. The man I have been following, call him R., has dense amnesia. He wears a recorder; overnight, software assembles the day's audio and video into a narrated first-person chronicle that he listens to each morning, and over two years he has become someone who can tell you about his week. I have become convinced that the chronicle does real work: that part of his self now lives in the archive and in the process that maintains it. I also want to say that if the archive were destroyed tomorrow he would still be himself, damaged but not replaced.

5. The ordinary, non-philosophical distinction between living your own life and living someone else's, between being cut off from your real self and recovering it, marks something real. It matters whether a narrative about a person is one they author or one imposed on them. A section of the essay about a memoirist I interviewed leans heavily on this.

6. Methodologically, I have come to think that the identity questions that generate paradoxes, fission and teleportation and gradual replacement and the rest, are defective: they are settled by decision, not discovery, and the better question was always what matters in survival. But I do not want to walk into a memory clinic and tell a daughter that her question, is this still my mother, is defective. I want her to get an answer, and I want it to be the answer she is hoping for.

So: audit this. Tell me which of these are in genuine tension with which, and which only look like it, and do not flag everything, since I can see the easy ones myself. Rank the real tensions by how badly they damage the essay rather than listing them as equivalent. For each, say exactly what I would have to give up and what it would cost me in the two endings I have to write. Tell me whether there is one revision that cleans up the most, and what it would commit me to that I would probably dislike. If I am sliding over a distinction, two senses of one word, say what it is. Use R. as your test case throughout: whatever you tell me about (1), apply it to him and to the machine that keeps his chronicle, and tell me what it commits me to. A short memo, eight hundred to a thousand words, is what I need.
