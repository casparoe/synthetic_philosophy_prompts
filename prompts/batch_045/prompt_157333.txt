The notes follow. They are deliberately skeletonized.

**Working title: Periods and Their Publics**

**1. The setup I want to replace.** The standard dispute has two sides. On one side, periods mark real discontinuities in how people lived, thought, and made things; a good periodization carves at the joints. On the other, period labels are artifacts of later scholarship and later politics: they were coined to flatter a subsequent age by darkening the one before it, and they survive because textbooks, curricula, and funding categories need them. I will call these the realist and the inventionist positions, and I want to argue that the dispute between them is under-described. At least four claims are run together in it, and once they are separated the interesting question moves somewhere neither side has been looking.

**2. The four claims.**
- (D) Discontinuity: for a well-chosen body of evidence, change is not spread evenly across time. Its rate concentrates near some boundary, and a boundary placed there recovers more of the pattern than a boundary placed elsewhere.
- (C) Contemporaneity: people living near that boundary took themselves to stand on the far side of a break — they wrote prefaces announcing a new start, they named predecessors as obsolete.
- (E) Explanatory role: citing the period does work inside a causal explanation of some outcome, rather than merely dating it.
- (R) Diachronic ratification (this is my coinage, and the claim I most want tested): a period can acquire reality by being believed in. Later actors reorganize institutions — what is taught, what is funded, what is preserved and what is allowed to rot — because they take themselves to inhabit a new age, and that reorganization has downstream consequences that historians then have to explain. On this view a period can be real even where (C) fails and (D) is arguable, because the belief in it became a cause.

**3. An operationalization of (D) that I am unsure about.** Call it the watermark test. Take a corpus of documents of a single genre with secure dates — title pages, prefaces, legal formulae, wills — strip the dates, and ask competent readers, or a model trained only on securely dated material from outside the window, to put the documents in order. Where pairwise ordering error spikes, that is where change in the observable texture of the genre concentrates; plot the error curve and look for peaks. My worry is not about feasibility but about validity: the test measures rate of change in surface features of one genre, and (D) is a claim about discontinuity in whatever the period name is supposed to denote. A peak might reflect a change in papermaking, in the survival rate of documents, or in who was allowed to produce them, none of which is the thing at issue. I do not know whether the gap can be closed by triangulating across genres, or whether it is fatal.

**4. A conflation I think is doing damage.** The question "was there a Renaissance?" bundles three questions: whether there is any boundary in the neighborhood; whether it falls at one date rather than another; and at what rank the unit sits — epoch, period, phase, generation. A historian can be right that something changes around a decade and wrong about the year, and can be right about the year and wrong about calling the result an epoch rather than a phase. Realists tend to win arguments about existence by relying on evidence that only supports granularity, and inventionists tend to win arguments about granularity and announce a victory about existence. I want to make this explicit, but I am not sure my rank vocabulary is coherent or that the analogy I am tempted to reach for — disputes about what rank a taxon deserves in biological classification — helps rather than distracts.

**5. The upshot I find most counterintuitive, and most suspect.** Under (R), the inventionist's best evidence can be the realist's evidence too. If a period label was propagated because it legitimated some group — a new science contrasted against a benighted earlier age, a national awakening contrasted against a foreign domination — that does not settle whether the period is real; on my account the label's political success is precisely the mechanism by which the period became real, through the institutional reorganization it licensed. So the two positions may not be in the disagreement they think they are in. My suspicion is that this is a cheat: (R) as I have stated it may be unfalsifiable, since any period anyone has heard of will have had some downstream effects somewhere, in which case my fourth claim dissolves the question instead of answering it. If that is right, I need a way to say how much ratification, of what kind, is required — and I do not have one.

**6. What I would like to be able to predict.** If (R) is doing real work, then periods that won broad uptake outside the discipline should show measurably larger downstream institutional effects than periods of equal discontinuity that only ever circulated among specialists, and those effects should cluster after the label became current rather than at the discontinuity itself. That contrast is meant to be the essay's testable difference between "the period was real" and "the period was only proposed." I have not found a way to state the comparison that avoids assuming the label's date of currency is itself well defined, which may be the soft spot.

**7. Note on examples.** I have deliberately avoided leaning on specific historical episodes I have not checked. Where I need an illustration in the draft I intend to use invented ones — a hypothetical polity that adopts a period label and rebuilds its school curriculum around it, say — and to say plainly that they are invented. Tell me if this evasiveness costs the essay more than it saves.
