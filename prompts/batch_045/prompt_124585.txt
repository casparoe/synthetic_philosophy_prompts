You are a professional philosopher with research expertise in epistemology, testimony, and the philosophy of cognitive science. Please think carefully before committing to an answer: begin with a short, unstructured pass in which you generate candidate readings of the slogan and note objections to each, and only then compress the results into the menu I ask for.

At a faculty meeting I raised a worry about our graduate students, who now use large language models to summarize literatures, reconstruct arguments, decide whether an objection is decisive, and draft seminar notes. A colleague replied with the old proverb about standing on the shoulders of giants, and the work he took it to be doing was this: consulting a model is only the newest instance of what scholarship has always done — reading secondary sources, trusting referees and editors, using search engines, using proof assistants — so there is nothing distinctive here to worry about and nothing to regret. I found the reply rhetorically forceful and I cannot say precisely why it is unsatisfying.

Do not argue directly for or against his conclusion. Disambiguate instead. Set out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses the proverb could be being used to assert in this context, ordered roughly from the banal to the ambitious, and covering at least these families: claims about knowledge being transmitted by testimony; claims about when it is permissible or obligatory to defer to a better-positioned source; claims about knowledge or understanding being socially distributed rather than held by individuals; claims about the division of cognitive labour and about historical technologies of memory and inscription; and claims about what understanding, as opposed to true belief or knowledge-that, consists in. For each entry: state the thesis sharply enough that it could in principle be false; classify it as a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or a thesis that is false or question-begging; and say whether, if it were the reading he had in mind, it would actually entitle him to the nothing-new-here conclusion about these students in particular.

In constructing the menu, please apply a distinction of my own, which I would like you to use and to correct if it is defective. Call a relation of deference to a source anchored for a given person when (a) she can recognise the characteristic failures of that source in its output without consulting some further authority, and (b) she could produce a result of the same kind more or less unaided if the source were withdrawn; call it floating otherwise. Say which entries on your menu presuppose that the deference in question is anchored, which are indifferent to anchoring, and whether the shoulders-of-giants picture in fact depicts an anchored or a floating relation for the students in question. If it is floating, say which reading he would have to fall back on, and whether anyone should find that reading consoling.

Three constraints. First, keep separate, and do not let them collapse into one another, the question whether deference transmits knowledge, the question whether it transmits understanding or ability, and the question whether the epistemic position of the group has improved while that of no individual member has. Second, note where the metaphor itself does argumentative work it cannot sustain: giants are visible to us, are admittedly better placed, and stand on the same ground we do, and it is worth asking which of those features a language model shares. Third, take as stipulated rather than as something to look up the familiar empirical suggestion that people retain less of what they expect to be able to look up later, and reason about what follows from it rather than about whether it is true.
