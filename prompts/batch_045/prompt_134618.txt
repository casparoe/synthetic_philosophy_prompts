I am a fourth-year PhD student in systems neuroscience, and my PI has asked me to draft a one-page interpretation guide for the lab: a short written procedure that anyone in the group — including first-year rotation students with no philosophy background — can run on a single sentence before it goes into a paper, a preprint, or a grant abstract, in order to decide what we are entitled to say when we write that some neural population computes, encodes, represents, or implements something, and what we are not.

The setting: we produce sentences of this shape dozens of times per manuscript, they are the sentences reviewers have started attacking in our last three submissions, and the lab has made a public commitment to not overclaiming in abstracts. So the stakes are reputational and practical rather than purely theoretical. The constraints are that the procedure must be runnable in about ten minutes on one sentence, must not require new experiments or any formalism beyond what a first-year can follow, and must be signed off by my PI — which means it has to say plainly which steps are tests and which are judgment calls being handed to her.

What I need is the work of translation: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops with a no, and the point at which it hands the remaining question to a person because the issue is one of values or of theoretical taste rather than fact.

I have three half-formed ideas about where our disputes actually come from, and I want you to use them, repair them, or replace them with better cuts.

First, I suspect we are not making one kind of claim but three, and that most of our arguments are cases of one person meaning one and another hearing another: a descriptive claim (the formalism is a compact summary of a measured response profile); an intervention-licensing claim (the formalism lets you predict what the system will do under interventions or stimuli you have not measured, because the mapping from physical states to formal states is fixed independently of the outcome being predicted); and a constitutive claim (the system performs the cognitive job in question because it computes that function — the formalism is doing explanatory work, not bookkeeping). I would like the verdict of the procedure to be one of these labels rather than a yes or no, and I would like it to return an error when the intended reading has not been stated, forcing us to choose.

Second, the mapping is where I think most of the cheating happens, in two directions. The mapping from neural variables to formal variables is usually estimated on the same data used to test the claim, and can be re-estimated whenever behaviour changes, so the claim survives every outcome by moving the mapping — I think of this as mapping drift, and I want a preregistration-style step that fixes the mapping and the failure condition before the held-out data are looked at. In the other direction, the population itself can be re-carved until it fits, and a good deal of the formal machinery often lives outside the animal, in an offline decoder that only an experimenter can read out; a claim that the circuit computes X then turns out to be a claim that X is recoverable from the circuit by us. I want the procedure to be able to say that the computation was completed in the analysis pipeline rather than in the brain.

Third, I do not see how to get from passing individual claims to the sentence our grant framing depends on, the brain is a computer. Computation claims do not seem to aggregate: a decomposition that works for one assayed circuit is not automatically part of a single coherent decomposition of the whole animal, and the question of whether the constitutive reading has to hold of the organism looks to me like a commitment rather than a result.

Please run the finished procedure on three claims. First: direction-selective circuits in the retina compute the axis of visual motion. Second, and this is the one I expect not to like — my own thesis claim, that dorsal anterior cingulate computes a comparison of the costs and benefits of candidate actions, where the evidence is a decoder trained offline on choice behaviour and the mapping is re-fit whenever the task changes; if the procedure exempts my own claim I will have written a useless document, so I want the honest output. Third: a cultured network of cortical neurons on a multielectrode array, trained with feedback to keep a simulated vehicle inside a lane, computes a control policy — where I worry the procedure either returns a yes and collapses into the triviality that any system computes anything under some mapping, or returns a no and cannot tell me what the in-brain case has that this one lacks.

Finally, say what the procedure cannot do, including whether it can say anything at all about whether the brain is a computer, and flag in the guide's own voice the cases where it is likely to misfire. Please do not give me a literature review or references; I will insert citations myself.
