hey — I'm writing a novel and I need you to be one of my characters for awhle.

Its set in a made-up mill town called Brackett, western PA, population about 2,000. The brake-lining plant closed in 1998, the wiring-harness plant closed in 2011, and what's left is the volunteer fire company, the Catholic church, a Sheetz, the high school, and forty-odd guys who drive an hour each way to a gas well pad or a warehouse. My character is Ray Hoxie, 61, twenty-nine years on the harness line, now runs the fire company and sits on the zoning board for free. He's not dumb, he's not a bigot, and he's not nostalgic — he knows perfectly well the town is shrinking. He just thinks the way my other character lives is worse, and he can say why.

The other character, who is me, is Nate, 34, Ray's nephew. Left for a state school, then a master's in public policy, now does something with data in Pittsburgh. He's not a villain either. He loves the town. He just thinks it's a museum of a way of life that stopped working, and that nobody who stayed chose to stay with any real options on the table.

Please play Ray. Rules:

- He argues from examples, never abstractions. If you catch yourself writing the words dignity, neoliberalism, social capital, or autonomy, you've broken character. He says the Sheetz, the supper, my brother-in-law Duane.
- Nate's numbers below are basically right and Ray knows it. Do not let him dispute them. Give up small ground fast — yeah there's a drug problem, yeah the zoning board is three guys who hate saying no — but never the main claim.
- He can say I don't know about that once, but not about the main claim.
- Two paragraphs max, plain speech, short sentences. Lead with the actual substance of his answer in the first sentence: no throat-clearing, no restating Nate's objection back at him, no Ray would say.
- Don't ask me anything in your first reply — answer him. After that, if you need a fact about Brackett, ask in character and I'll tell you.
- Stay in him until I type stop.

Two things about the town, use them however you want. The fire roster runs on an unwritten rule: miss three calls and you're off it, and nobody gets paid a cent. And the high school is the only building in Brackett big enough to hold a funeral dinner, so if the state consolidates it with Kittanning's, the town loses the place where it buries its people.

Here's Nate's opening, at a kitchen table at eleven at night, none of it meant cruelly:

"Uncle Ray, I ran the numbers on our graduating class. Of the sixty-one who left, all but four make more than their parents did, and those four make the same. Of the forty-four who stayed, thirty-one make less. Nobody who left got dragged out at gunpoint. So when you say this place is better, what you're really saying is that you'd rather be poor somewhere you already know how to be poor. That's a preference, not an argument — and you're asking my generation to pay for it."
