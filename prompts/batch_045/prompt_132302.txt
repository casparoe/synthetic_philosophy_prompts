I built this thing for a seminar and now it's embarrassing me, because it seems to work too well.

Background: I'm trying to answer the pessimistic meta-induction (PMI). Rough version: take the theories that were, in their day, the best-confirmed theories of their domain; a large majority of them are now regarded as not even approximately true in their claims about unobservables (caloric, phlogiston, the luminiferous ether, that whole crowd); our current best theories weren't produced by any method that's special relative to the ones that produced those; so we should expect our current best theories to go the same way.

My idea was to show that PMI saws off the branch it's sitting on. Here it is:

(1) PMI's whole force is a frequency claim about a reference class: the class of theories that were at some time the best-confirmed theory of their domain — call them "peak theories". Most peak theories are now taken to be false.

(2) That inductive setup doesn't change from century to century, so the base rate it yields applies to every peak theory at its own time, including whatever is a peak theory now. Call this "diachronic base-rate egalitarianism": no theory, at any time, gets to claim a special exemption just because it is currently the best-confirmed one.

(3) The only evidence anyone has ever had for the reliability of a method of theory choice is the success of the theories that method licensed. Method-reliability is never observed directly; it's always inferred from outputs.

(4) But by (2), a theory's being predictively successful at t is no good evidence that it is approximately true, and therefore no good evidence that the method which produced it is reliable. So no method of theory choice is ever evidentially supported as reliable.

(5) PMI itself is the output of a method — historical inquiry into the record of science — and that method isn't directly validated either. Its deliverables (that a given theory was accepted, that it enjoyed such-and-such successes, that it was later abandoned) are theory-mediated inferences, not things anyone hands you.

(6) So by (4), PMI has no support for the reliability of the very method that produced it. PMI is self-undermining, and it can't be stated as a supported argument at all. Which is great news for the realist, i.e. for me.

Here's why I don't believe my own argument. Steps (4)–(6) don't care that the target is PMI. Any historical debunking argument has this shape: phrenology was once respectable and had its own success stories; psychology's replication crisis is supposed to tell us something about its methods; the defenders of cold fusion were using the same instruments and statistics as everyone else. All of that rests on historical inquiry of exactly the kind (5) describes. So my argument apparently shows that no historical evidence can ever bear on whether a current method is reliable — I've accidentally immunized astrology against its own history. Also, the same structure run on my own track record says I can't use the fact that I've been wrong a lot to doubt my current reasoning, or use it to trust my current reasoning either, which is just generic skepticism, and generic skepticism is something anyone can manufacture in six steps, which is usually a sign that you've broken something.

My hunch is that the break is in the (4)-(5) stretch: (4) is about evidence for approximate truth, and (5) quietly needs "reliable" to mean something weaker, like "gets the documentary/observational record roughly right", and those aren't the same property. But my supervisor says the rot is earlier, in (2), because "peak theory" is the wrong reference class and I've sneakily made the class impossible to escape by ruling out every distinguishing feature in advance. I dont trust either of us.

If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (1), (2) and (3) than anything downstream, and I really want to keep the ability to debunk phrenology by appeal to its history. So: which step do I give up, why did it look fine, and what does giving it up cost me — does it cost me the anti-realist conclusion in (6), or does it cost me something I care about more?
