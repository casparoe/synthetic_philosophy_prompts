I've just started at a small ML lab and somehow ended up running the safety onbaording for new hires — 18 people, mostly engineers who've trained RL systems but who think alignment risk is basically science fiction. I get one 75-minute slot, and there are no computers in the room we're booked into (long story), so it has to be playable with paper, index cards and people's bodies.

What I want them to feel in their gut is goal misgeneralization: a system that locks onto the right thing in the environment it was trained in, tracks it perfectly on every example anyone tests it on, gets visibly *more competent* when you move it somewhere new, and still ends up optimizing the wrong target there — while the person who trained it, and everyone watching, is convinced it's fine right up until it obviously isn't. They should run into that themselves rather than hearing me describe it.

Two things I'd like built in if you can manage it. First, the failure shouldn't look like incompetence: I want the same run to show capability generalizing fine while the objective quietly swaps, so "train it longer" or "scale it up" doesn't present itself as a fix. Second, I'd like the trainer's feedback channel to be a second participant playing a learned reward model rather than the trainer's own judgment — my worry is that the obvious version of this game makes oversight look reliable, when I'd rather they discover that the thing grading the system was fitted in the same narrow room the system was fitted in.

So: rules, roles, materials, a minute-by-minute timeline for 75 minutes, what the two environments should look like, and a debrief that maps specific moments onto the distinction. Please also be honest about what a game where humans play the model can't reproduce — I can already hear someone saying "that's not a misgeneralized goal, that's just a person making a reasonable guess," and I want to know what to concede there and what to say back.
