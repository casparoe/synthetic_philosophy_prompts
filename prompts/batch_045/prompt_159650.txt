I'm in a small reading group on formal epistemology, and last week we worked through the Lewis-style triviality results for indicative conditionals. I went home and rebuilt the derivation with explicit numbers instead of the usual schematic version, and the more I stared at it the more it looked as though the derivation never uses anything specific to conditionals at all — which would mean it proves much more than the conclusion we wanted, and I can't find where to stop it. Here is the version I ended up with; every step seemed compulsory on Tuesday and none of them look compulsory now.

(1) Adams's thesis: a rational agent's credence in the indicative conditional "if A then C" equals her conditional credence in C given A — cr(if A then C) = cr(C|A) — at least whenever cr(A) > 0.
(2) A concrete case. Let A be "the die lands even" and C be "the die lands 2", over the six outcomes. Let P be the uniform distribution: P(A) = 1/2, P(A∧C) = 1/6, so P(C|A) = 1/3. Let P' put 1/2 on 2, 0.05 on each of 4 and 6, and the remaining 0.4 spread over the odd faces: P'(A) = 0.6, P'(A∧C) = 0.5, so P'(C|A) = 5/6. Every function I use gives A positive probability, so nothing here turns on conditional probabilities being undefined.
(3) Take the equal mixture P½ = ½P + ½P'. Then P½(A) = 0.55 and P½(A∧C) = 1/3, so P½(C|A) = 20/33 ≈ 0.606.
(4) Credence in a proposition is affine in the credence function: if X is a proposition — a fixed set of possibilities whose truth conditions do not move when the agent's opinions do — then P½(X) = ½P(X) + ½P'(X), just by the definition of a mixture of probability functions.
(5) So if "if A then C" expressed a proposition, then by (1) and (4) its credence at P½ would be ½(1/3) + ½(5/6) = 7/12 ≈ 0.583, while by (1) and (3) it would be 20/33 ≈ 0.606. Since 20/33 ≠ 7/12, there is no such proposition. Granted (1) and (4), the indicative conditional has no credence-independent truth conditions; the probability of a conditional is never systematically the conditional probability.
(6) Here is what bothers me. Steps (2)–(5) used exactly one feature of the conditional: that the prescribed credence, cr(C|A), is not affine in the credence function. Conditional credence is a ratio, and ratios do not survive mixing. Step (4), meanwhile, is a fact about any proposition whatsoever. So the argument is really an instance of a general principle: if the credence a sentence calls for is, as a function of the agent's credence function, anything other than affine, then that sentence does not express a proposition in the sense of (4).
(7) Run the principle elsewhere. Take the epistemic modal "it might be that C". The natural rule: an agent is certain of "might C" exactly when her credence in C is positive, and certain of its negation when she is certain of ¬C — cr(might C) = 1 if cr(C) > 0, and 0 if cr(C) = 0. Let P(C) = 1/2 and P'(C) = 0. At the equal mixture, P½(C) = 1/4 > 0, so the rule demands credence 1, while affinity demands ½·1 + ½·0 = 1/2. So "might"-sentences express no proposition either — and this is not an artifact of the zero case: fix any threshold t at all, run the rule as cr(might C) = 1 iff cr(C) > t, choose the mixing weight small enough that the mixed credence in C drops below t while P(C) stays above it, and the same contradiction returns. The same construction goes through for comparative claims ("C is more likely than D", credence 1 iff cr(C) > cr(D)) and for "probably C".

So the argument seems to strip truth conditions not just from conditionals but from much of the vocabulary we use to talk about our own uncertainty — sentences we embed, argue about, and treat as straightforwardly true or false. Tell me which step you reject, why that step looked compulsory, and what each of the other exits costs. If it matters: I would most like to keep (1), and I would also like to keep the thought that "might"-claims and comparative-likelihood claims are the kind of thing that can be true or false; I am willing to give up the picture of propositions in (4) if I must, but then I want to know what replaces it and what happens to Boolean compounding and to the idea that credence is a measure over propositions. Two things I have tried and cannot evaluate: the move that the contents of these sentences shift with the agent's information state, so that (4) never applied to them in the first place (which worries me because it looks available for any sentence, in which case the argument has no bite anywhere and I have not found an exit but dissolved the result); and a friend's complaint that the whole thing assumes the admissible credence functions are closed under mixing, which I cannot tell is deep or evasive.
