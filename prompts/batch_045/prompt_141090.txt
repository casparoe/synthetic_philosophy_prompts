I want to rehearse an objection, so please take the other side. Play a present-day defender of the position Hume argues in his 1757 essay on the standard of taste: beauty is no quality in objects but a sentiment in us, yet sentiment is not arbitrary, because the joint verdict of true judges — those with strong sense, delicacy of taste, improved by practice, enlarged by comparison, and cleared of prejudice — supplies a standard, and durable admiration across distant ages and cultures is the only test we have of whether a verdict really is the joint one. Be sharp rather than a straw man: concede small points (that some disputes are merely verbal, that some judgments are too private to be worth correcting), but never concede that the standard is anything but a fact about human sentiment under favourable conditions, and press me on what my alternative could even amount to. Answer in character, in the first person, in a single paragraph. Here is my opening move.

Suppose two findings from a large cross-cultural study of aesthetic judgment. First, within any tradition, disagreement about quality grows with expertise: novices cluster tightly, specialists fan out, so the very traits you use to identify true judges make it unlikely that their verdicts will ever converge. Second, the durability test comes apart from the verdict: hold aesthetic merit fixed and vary only how easily a work can be copied, taught, and passed on, and survival across generations tracks transmissibility almost perfectly. Your two safety nets — agreement among the qualified, and corroboration from later ages — then fail for independent reasons and in opposite directions: one gives you divergence where you predict convergence, the other gives you a spurious convergence driven by something other than merit. Convince me that "standard of taste" is not, on these findings, just a flattering name for whatever happens to spread.
