hey — I run a sci-fi-and-philosophy club at my school, about 14 people, one hour on thursday afternoons, mostly freshmen and juniors, nobody's read any actual philosophy, and we've got no laptops: just a whiteboard, index cards, poker chips, and I can bribe people with candy.

Last week we tried to do the simulation argument — the one that goes: either civilizations almost always die out before they can run huge numbers of ancestor simulations, or they get able to but almost never bother, or we're almost certainly in one right now — and it totally flopped. Everyone just said "yeah but you can't prove it either way," someone said "so, the matrix," and we were done in fifteen minutes. I want to redo it as a game instead of a discussion, and I don't want to stand up and explain anything first.

The thing I want them to actually feel is an idea one of the seniors blurted out at the end, which I think is the good part: nobody would simulate a whole universe at full detail, that'd be insanely expensive, so any real sim would be built on a budget — it'd render whatever somebody is paying attention to and leave the rest at the lowest resolution it can get away with. Which means the hypothesis isn't "everything is fake and nothing can ever be checked." It predicts a specific pattern: a world that's super detailed exactly where people look, and weirdly generic, patchy, or just blank everywhere else. And if sims inside sims are a thing, most minds are at the bottom of the stack where the computing is thinnest, so it also predicts a kind of cheap-looking world. Which means "there's no evidence either way" is just wrong — there's a signature you could go looking for.

Can you design a game for that? I need rules, what the chips and cards are actually for, a scoring or payoff scheme that doesn't involve real money (candy and picking next week's topic is basically all I've got), who plays who and when they swap, a minute-by-minute timeline for the hour, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the argument — including the part where taking it seriously also wrecks its own input, since our guesses about future computing power come from inside the thing. Please don't have me name the argument or the "we're probably simulated" conclusion until afterwards; I want them to run into it. Also tell me what will definately go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which part of the argument a game like this just can't reproduce and I'll have to say out loud myself.
