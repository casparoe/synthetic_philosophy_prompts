My reading group on the history of utilitarianism (we meet at the weird little bar near the philosophy building, and this came up because someone there said Bentham was basically Mill before Mill) has been working through his writings on sexual offenses — the essay on pederasty he never published, the chapters on offenses in the big 1789 book, and a bunch of manuscript material I only have through secondary summaries — and I've been keeping a table of his verdicts to see whether one rule runs through all of them. As far as I can tell he says: consensual adult male-male sex in private, don't punish, since the only real pain is the punishment itself plus other people's disapproval, which he won't count; masturbation, don't punish, at most a matter of prudence if it crowds out other pleasures; rape, punish and punish hard, worse when it comes with injury or terror; sex with someone who can't consent because they're a child, punish; the very same acts done where unwilling people will see them, punish, apparently as a nuisance or alarm thing; a vow of permanent celibacy, don't punish, but he clearly thinks it's a pointless destruction of pleasure and the anti-ascetic side of him wants the legislator to make pleasures easier to come by; commercial sex between consenting adults, I think don't punish, though I'm shaky on that one. My friend in the group says he's just running the harm principle and everything falls out of that. I think the alarm and danger machinery plus the anti-ascetic principle make it messier.

So here's the case I actually want. Adult brother and sister, both in their early thirties, raised in the same house from birth, no coercion and no dependency — neither is the other's caretaker, creditor, or employer — and one of them is surgically sterile, so no pregnancy risk. The acts are entirely private, both of them say it's among the best things in their lives, and the realistic alternative for each is lifelong celibacy, because neither is attracted to anyone else. Nothing else follows: no disease, no children, no reputational damage, no effect on anyone's property, prospects, or care arrangements. For years nobody else is affected at all. When the parents definately do find out, they're devastated — and their distress is entirely owed to the belief that the act is shameful and sinful, a belief they'd hold whether or not the law punished it and whether or not anyone else ever learned of it.

What verdict do you predict — punish, don't punish but don't exactly commend, or positively treat as a good thing the legislator should leave room for? If one of the entries in my table is wrong or won't fit any of the rules, flag it. Please skip citations and reading lists, I'll fill those in myself; I want the argument.
