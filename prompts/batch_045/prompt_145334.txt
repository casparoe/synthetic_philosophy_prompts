You are a formal epistemologist with a working command of the accuracy-first program in epistemology: the domination argument for probabilism, the expected-accuracy argument for conditionalisation, the theory of scoring rules, and the standing dialectic about whether the framework's deliverances are artifacts of the chosen inaccuracy measure. Please think carefully and in stages. First, reconstruct what I am about to propose in its strongest form, granting me everything except what is genuinely in doubt. Second, work the two-cell and three-cell cases numerically and explicitly before telling me anything general — I would far rather see one honest computation than three paragraphs of programmatic hand-waving. Third, and only third, say what follows for the larger dialectic. Do not soften your verdict: if the manoeuvre is a non-starter, I need to know now, before I build a paper on it.

Here is the context. I am writing against what I call the arbitrariness objection. The objection concedes that accuracy-first epistemology derives a great deal from very little — that incoherent credences are accuracy-dominated, and that conditionalisation maximises expected accuracy — but insists that these results are properties of a stipulation. The quadratic (Brier) score, the objection says, is a modelling convenience, not a normative datum; and since the framework's theorems are sensitive to which inaccuracy measure you plug in, the framework has not derived probabilism from the nature of belief at all, but has read it off a choice of metric.

My proposed answer — call it the Ordinalist Reply — is that the objection overstates the role of the metric. The two central deliverances are both stated as world-by-world comparisons of credence functions: one credence function is dominated by another just in case it is less accurate at every world. Dominance is invariant under any strictly increasing re-description of the accuracy ordering at each world. So, I want to argue, the framework needs no more than an ordinal ranking of credence functions by accuracy-at-a-world, and the choice among monotone increasing re-scalings of a distance measure is merely representational — no more normatively loaded than a choice of units. That, I claim, is where the dialectic should be located, and not in the spurious question which metric nature prefers.

Accordingly I have been working with the following inaccuracy measure on a finite partition with cells indexed by i. Let v_w be the truth-value vector for world w (1 in the true cell, 0 elsewhere) and let c be a credence function on the partition. Set

A(c, w) = − Σ_i sqrt( |c_i − v_w(i)| ).

Two features recommend it, I thought. It is built on Manhattan rather than Euclidean distance, so it does not impose the quadratic's disproportionate penalty on large errors in a single coordinate — diminishing marginal disutility of error strikes me as the epistemically natural shape. And the concave square-root transform makes total inaccuracy bounded, which lets me sidestep the pathology of the logarithmic score, whose infinite penalty for credence zero in a truth is, I had assumed, the sole reason the log score appears to impose a requirement of regular, open-minded credence.

Here is my problem. Before writing any of this up I tried to run the domination argument on a two-cell partition, and I got a result I cannot make sense of: on my measure, the maximally incoherent credence function that assigns 1 to both cells strictly dominates the coherent (0.5, 0.5) at both worlds, and I cannot find any credence function that dominates it in turn. This is of course exactly the verdict the framework exists to exclude. I assumed I had botched the algebra, redid it twice, and got the same thing.

So I need you to adjudicate four things.

One: is my invariance claim correct — that because accuracy-first verdicts are dominance verdicts, and dominance is invariant under strictly increasing transformations, any monotone re-scaling of an accuracy measure is admissible? If not, what exactly does the framework require beyond ordinal rankings, and what goes wrong with expected accuracy, specifically, under a non-affine transformation? I suspect I need accuracy to be measured on an interval scale and not merely an ordinal one, but I want the reason stated precisely, in a way I could put in a footnote.

Two: is the failure I found an artifact of the square-root damping, or does it already arise for undamped Manhattan distance? And what is the right diagnosis? I had taken truth-directedness — the true credence function uniquely maximising accuracy at each world, which my measure satisfies — to be the substantive constraint, and strict propriety to be a technical convenience. I now suspect I have that exactly backwards. Tell me why propriety is the load-bearing condition, what it is doing informally, and whether it is doing epistemic work or merely decision-theoretic work.

Three: I have a half-memory of a representation result to the effect that the strictly proper scoring rules on a finite partition are, up to positive affine transformation, exactly the negative Bregman divergences generated by strictly convex functions. Is there such a result, and is that a fair statement of it? If it holds, then my measure is not an exotic member of the admissible class but not a member of it at all, and the choice the arbitrariness objection complains about is constrained to a far smaller family than the objection supposes. Granting that, does it answer the objection? Does that remaining family — quadratic, logarithmic, spherical and the rest — still diverge on genuine first-order norms, or are the residual differences cosmetic? In particular, is the apparent disagreement about whether rationality requires regular, non-dogmatic credence a real decision point, or only an artifact of bounded versus unbounded measures, as I assumed?

Four, and most useful to me: is the Ordinalist Reply salvageable in any neighbourhood of this one, or must I concede that the choice of inaccuracy measure is doing substantive normative work? If the latter, say plainly what the honest options are for someone who wants to defend accuracy-first epistemology against the arbitrariness objection — robustness-across-the-admissible-class, constraints on admissibility, or something else — and which of them you think has the best prospect.
