I'm in a graduate seminar on judgment and decision making, and next week I have to defend the claim that the dollar auction has something real to tell us about escalation of commitment outside the lab — the sort of thing people point at when they talk about the Concorde, about Vietnam, or about a firm pouring money into a project everyone can already see is failing. I expect to be attacked from the skeptical side, and I would like to rehearse against the strongest version of that attack rather than a comfortable one.

Please play a committed skeptic about the dollar auction: someone who thinks it is an over-interpreted parlor game, in which the escalation is manufactured by the rules rather than revealed by them. That is the claim you never give up. The lines I expect you to press, sharply and not as a strawman:

- the rule that the runner-up pays and receives nothing has no counterpart in the real cases, where stopping costs nothing further and the money already spent is not burned;
- the stake in the lab is fixed, trivial and common knowledge, whereas real escalation happens under deep uncertainty about whether the goal is achievable at all, which gives continuing a genuine information value;
- the lab has no principal, no career concerns, no audience and no agency problem, while real escalation is largely a story about who personally bears the cost of backing down;
- the textbook cases are picked after the fact, so nobody has shown that stopping earlier would in fact have been better;
- and a model like mine has no state: nothing changes as a standoff goes on, so persistence cannot wear the other side down or reveal who is more resolved, which is exactly what persistence is supposed to do in the real cases.

Concede small points freely when they genuinely are small, but never concede the main one, and do not slide into agreement just to be agreeable. Write like a tough but fair seminar discussant: concrete, willing to do a little arithmetic or a small piece of game theory when it settles something, no jargon for its own sake. Argue from the structure of the games and the cases; do not cite, name or appeal to any paper, author or experimental result, and do not tell me what the literature says — if you need a premise, ask me for it in your closing question. Stay in character until I type "stop". Do not ask me to clarify the setup; answer the argument below as it stands.

Structure every reply in exactly three labelled sections, in this order:

- The reply — 100 to 150 words, the response itself.

Here is my opening move.

I will grant your central objection in its strongest form and throw out the rule you dislike. Consider a game with no runner-up payment at all — call it the standoff. Two firms contest a market worth exactly 1 to each of them, this is common knowledge, and each round both pay a small cost c to stay in. At any round either side may concede; the first to concede hands the market to the other, and nothing else happens. No one ever pays for nothing, no money is burned, no experimenter pockets the difference. The only asymmetry is that yielding first means losing the contested object outright, whereas staying one more round costs only c. This is the structure of the war of attrition rather than of the dollar auction, and in the symmetric case its unique symmetric equilibrium dissipates the whole value of the prize, with nobody making an error at any point. So the escalation here does not come from the artifact you object to. It comes from a gap between two magnitudes: the discrete loss from being the side that yields, and the merely incremental cost of not yielding yet. And notice that the trap survives even though no one is irrational: conditional on which firm ends up with the market, every extra round is pure waste, so both would have preferred that same outcome one round earlier — the pair walks out poorer than it needed to, and no individual ever had a reason to be the one who stopped. My claim is that this gap is present in the real cases too, and that it, not the all-pay rule, is what the dollar auction was clumsily isolating. Tell me why the gap is not enough.
