I'm drafting the agenda document for a small interdisciplinary group I've been pulled into as a graduate student representative: four philosophers, two machine-learning people, roughly three years of funding, under a heading vague enough to be either a gift or a trap — something like "conceptual foundations of extrapolation in artificial systems." The document keeps circling back to Goodman's new riddle. Grue, fixed relative to some future time t, applies to a thing examined before t just in case it is green and to anything else just in case it is blue; so the evidence that all examined emeralds have been green confirms the hypothesis that all emeralds are grue no less than the hypothesis that all emeralds are green, and the problem becomes one of saying what separates projectible from non-projectible predicates. I do not want a survey of positions and I do not need citations or a reading list — we can find the literature ourselves, and half of it is in the footnotes of the other half. What I want is a defended allocation of effort.

So: if you were spending that group's three years, which four or five questions in this neighbourhood should it actually work on, in what order, and by what criteria? Please make the criteria explicit and then argue about them — tractability for people whose training is logic and formal epistemology rather than machine learning, how many other questions turn on the answer, whether the area is crowded or neglected, and how much difference the answer would make to what anyone should do about a system that extrapolates badly — and say plainly where these criteria pull against each other and how you are resolving the conflict, since I suspect some of the most interesting questions are the least tractable for us. Here is my own unordered shortlist, which you should feel free to cut, merge, or replace, and which I give partly so you can see the kind of grain I'm after: (i) whether the riddle is genuinely about predicates at all, or whether, as Goodman himself in effect noted, grue-style pathologies can be generated over any partition of a domain into what has been examined and what has not — spatial regions, an ordering over individuals, anything that plays the role of t — in which case the real question is which structural feature is doing the work: indexicality to the evidential situation, disjunctiveness relative to a privileged base vocabulary, or dependence of a predicate's extension on facts about our own investigative history; (ii) whether a criterion that gets the cases right is enough, or whether an adequate account has to explain why projecting the entrenched predicate is justified, and hence what should count as a solution in the first place, so that we could tell whether we had one; (iii) an idea I want your verdict on rather than my colleagues', which I've been calling deployment divergence: take a learner that has no human vocabulary at all, only latent features fitted to data with a deployment cutoff, and ask after the class of hypotheses that agree with the target on everything available before cutoff and diverge arbitrarily thereafter — is the new riddle, in that setting, simply the familiar underdetermination of model choice by data plus whatever inductive bias the architecture supplies, so that our group would be reinventing statistical learning theory badly, or is there a residue that is specifically about projectibility and that survives when there are no predicates in sight; (iv) whether we should try to operationalize entrenchment — say, by how often a predicate has in fact been projected in some body of recorded usage, or by how strongly a trained model's own continuation probabilities favour going on in the same way — and whether evidence about which predicates people actually project would bear on the normative question at all, or whether it quietly changes the subject from justification to description; (v) whether the Bayesian relocation, putting the whole weight in the prior over hypotheses, is a solution, an honest restatement, or a refusal, and how much else in the neighbourhood depends on how that comes out.

Two more things would make this genuinely useful. First, tell me which questions you would deliberately leave alone for three years, and why: which are dead ends, which are insulated from any evidence we could gather, and which would be intellectually satisfying to answer but would not change anything downstream — I would rather drop an appealing question early than discover in year three that it was downstream of nothing. Second, give me the conditions of defeat for your own ordering: what result, argument, or empirical finding over the next two or three years should move an item up or down your list, and which single item is most likely to turn out to have been a mistake to prioritize. Where a question bottoms out in Hume's older problem of induction, or in general worries about underdetermination that have nothing specially to do with grue, say so, because I want to know how much of our budget is really being spent on the new riddle rather than the old one.
