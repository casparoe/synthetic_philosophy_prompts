Takeoff speed is a latency problem, not an intelligence problem

[1] In the takeoff arguments I've run into, speed gets treated as a property of the AI: how steep is the capability curve, how fast does it get from "pretty good" to "way past us". I think that's the wrong variable. The interesting thing isn't how fast the system gets smarter, it's how fast a capability gain can be cashed in for the next capability gain. Every self-improvement step runs through a loop: form a hypothesis, build the thing, test it, ship it, watch what happens, feed the result back. The wall-clock minimum of that loop is what I'll call the latency floor of the domain. Intelligence can compress the thinking parts of the loop to almost nothing. It can't compress the parts where you have to wait for the world to answer you back. Amdahl's-law vibes: your iteration rate is your slowest stage, not your fastest engineer.

[2] In the real world those floors are wildly unequal. Writing and testing code: minutes to days. Proving a theorem, designing a chip, running another training experiment: hours to weeks. Getting a new fab actually built: a couple of years. Running a real clinical trial: years, because bodies and disease progression run at their own speed and don't care how smart you are. Permitting and building a power plant or a factory: years. Getting a human institution to say yes: months to years, and it's not obvious that speeds up much just because the applicant is smarter.

[3] So takeoff should be multi-speed, and the speed is set by which domain's loop is doing the compounding. Digital domains have short floors: the output is bits, the test is cheap, the feedback comes back fast. Any domain where feedback has to come from atoms — biology, robotics, energy, manufacturing, construction — has long floors, and there the compounding crawls no matter how smart the thing doing it is. A system a thousand times better than our best researcher still waits the same couple of years for the fab.

[4] Obvious objection: simulation. If you can simulate the experiment you don't wait for the real one, and smarter systems simulate better. I think this buys speed but can't remove the floor, because a simulation is only trustworthy where it's been checked against reality, and every check is a real experiment paid at the floor rate. Call the unpaid bill the grounding debt: you can borrow speed from simulation, cheaply and temptingly and maybe correctly in expectation, but the debt comes due in real-world runs and it's paid at the floor rate. So simulation lets you front-load the digital part of a physical problem — and then you hit the same wall, just later, with more compounding already banked.

[5] Here's the part I actually care about. Suppose capability at ML research itself takes off, because ML research is a digital domain with a very short floor — you run the experiment in hours. That bit can be genuinely fast. But the part where the world visibly changes is rate-limited by floors, so my guess at the shape is: a sharp spike in digital capability, then a stretch where the physical world looks almost unchanged except inside the digital systems it already runs on (markets, software, media, coordination, logistics), then much faster change in whichever physical domains the system managed to get floors down in. Note that lowering floors is itself the obvious early project — build the fabs, automate the wet labs, get the permits, instrument everything — and that project is denominated in physical time. The thing that looks like it should be the fast part is actually the slow part.

[6] Which makes me think some of the fast-vs-slow takeoff fight is a fight about axes rather than about the world. With calendar time on the x-axis and "control over the physical world" on the y-axis you get a knee. With compute or money already sunk into lowering floors on the x-axis, the same curve looks smooth and continuous. With benchmark performance on digital tasks on the y-axis you get something close to vertical. Then everyone talks past each other while being right about their own graph. I'm genuinely unsure whether this dissolves part of the disagreement or just relabels it: if the question anyone cares about is "how much warning do we get", the answer depends on which floors matter, and that's an empirical question about floors, not about intelligence.

[7] Where I suspect I'm cheating: I've defined floors to include more or less everything that is slow, so "takeoff is limited by floors" may be nearly empty — a fancy way of saying things take as long as they take. The version with content in it is the claim that floors are systematically unequal in a way that tracks whether feedback arrives as bits or as atoms, and that this inequality predicts the shape of what happens first. And I might also have it backwards on importance: if most of what matters — money, coordination, software, persuasion, markets — already lives in short-floor digital domains, then physical floors don't constrain the part anyone should worry about, and my whole framing is answering a question about robots and vaccines while the real action is somewhere else entirely.
