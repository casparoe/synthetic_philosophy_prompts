I'm a fourth-year PhD student in machine learning, and I've been asked to write the operating procedure for our lab's policy registry before it goes live next term, so this needs to be something a colleague can actually run rather than something that displays a position.

The registry is a small piece of infrastructure. For certain recurring decisions, a researcher submits a *policy* — a mapping from the observations they might make to the action they would take — together with an ex ante model of the situation. The registry evaluates the candidate policies against that ex ante model, records the winner, and then executes it mechanically once the observation arrives. Two or three decisions a month go through it, usually with real money attached: compute, annotation budgets, occasionally the difference between shipping and killing a three-month run. The person running the procedure will be a rotating graduate student who has read one paper on this and has about forty minutes; the PI signs off. Assume this person has no access to the literature and will not look anything up, so every distinction the procedure uses has to be stated inside the procedure itself.

I want the procedure built on the updateless framing, by which I mean: choose among whole policies by their expected value computed from the ex ante prior, without conditioning on the observation you in fact end up with, on the grounds that what you control is the output of the decision procedure rather than the single act in the branch you happen to occupy. I want that turned into an actual procedure, not expounded. Specifically:

- The ordered questions, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and the points where the procedure stops early or hands the decision to a person.
- A test for whether a decision belongs in the registry at all. As I understand it, the framing earns its keep only where the decision is entangled with something else — an external predictor whose behaviour tracks the policy rather than the act, or a set of instances of the same decision computation that the agent cannot tell apart — and collapses back into ordinary expected-utility maximisation otherwise. I need that test stated so a graduate student can apply it to a real ticket in five minutes, including what to do when the answer is genuinely unclear.
- How to fix the prior, and the prior of whom as of when: our priors at registration, our priors now, or something else — and what to do when the two have diverged.
- What the entangler is simulating when it predicts us: the rulebook as submitted text, the procedure that generated it, or our actual future behaviour. This is the step I expect to be load-bearing and the one I find hardest, and the answer seems to change depending on whether we can rewrite the rulebook afterwards.
- What an operator should do when the executed policy mandates an action that is plainly wrong given everything now known: who may override, on what grounds, and what an override costs the registry.
- Where full policy comparison is computationally hopeless, what approximation to substitute, and which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.
- What the procedure cannot do.

Then run it, in order, on three cases.

1. Our lab belongs to a compute consortium. Each quarter we submit a contingency rulebook; the consortium's allocator, a fixed and published algorithm, simulates the submitted rulebook against a battery of hypothetical scenarios and awards compute roughly in proportion to the share of the allocation that rulebook would spend on long, high-variance runs in scenarios where they look unpromising. This quarter's allocation is already granted and largely spent. The scenario that actually arose is one where a high-variance run looks unpromising and would consume about a third of what is left, and nothing we can identify now depends on whether we run it.
2. A one-off decision: whether to spend $4k on an annotation job for a single project. No allocator, no recurring structure, and no way of confusing this decision with any other.
3. A registered policy from a run begun six months ago says: if the pre-registered safety threshold is crossed, halt, delete the checkpoint, and run no further diagnostics first. The threshold was crossed this morning. Within ten minutes we found a bug in the logging library that makes the crossing very likely spurious; the model itself looks fine; the run cost roughly $60k and three months of someone's time; no external party is watching and nothing downstream depends on our compliance.

Please give me a committed answer: one procedure, with definite choices at each step and the reasons for them, rather than a survey of what a defender of each decision theory would say. Where you think the updateless framing is simply inapplicable or wrong for a class of cases, say that flatly rather than hedging, and I expect I will not like what your procedure returns on case 3 — give it anyway, and say whether the correct move there is to override and what would make an override legitimate rather than convenient. Close with a short note, two hundred words at most, on the step you are least confident in, what would change your mind about it, and where your own view is contested. I would rather have something I can argue with than something balanced.
