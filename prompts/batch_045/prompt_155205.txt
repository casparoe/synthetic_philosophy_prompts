I'm retired now — I used to work in atmospheric chemistry, where you never judge a single emission, you look at the whole budget — and I've drifted in to a philosophy reading group, so forgive the home-made terminology. We did a session on paternalism and nudging and I wrote down where I'd landed. I'd like to know whether the set holds together:

(1) Mill's harm principle is roughly right: coercion is justified to stop me harming others, not to stop me harming myself.
(2) Nudging isn't coercion and isn't really even an intervention, because some default has to be set — there's no neutral choice architecture, so "doing nothing" isn't on the menu.
(3) Asymmetric paternalism, if that's the right label: if a rule helps the people who are erring and costs the people who aren't roughly nothing, that's enough, and we don't need to know who falls in which group.
(4) The mechanism matters, not just the outcome. A nudge that works by leaning on a known bias — opt-out defaults, loss-framed letters — bothers me in a way that handing the same people the same numbers in a clear table does not, even if uptake ends up identical.
(5) The "it bypasses deliberation" objection strikes me as empty, since nearly everything around us steers behaviour below the level of deliberation anyway; but I still want a publicity condition — a nudge should be the sort of thing the government could announce out loud without it stoping to work.
(6) My own addition: assess nudges in bundles, not one at a time. Call it net alignment — look at the whole choice architecture someone moves through over a year and ask whether, taken together, it shifts them toward what they'd want on reflection. One bias-exploiting default is fine if other parts of the architecture push the other way.

How it cashes out: I'm relaxed about opt-out organ donation and automatic enrolment in a workplace pension. But I'm against the state nudging people to vote (texts about turnout, notices that researchers will be checking whether you voted), even though it's the same kind of mechanism and passes my own test in (3) — a government shouldn't be manufacturing the participation that legitimises it. The very same nudge run by a university or an employer I'd have no problem with.

My instinct is that the trouble is (1) against (3), but people in the group keep needling (4) and (5) together and I can't see why. Audit the whole set for me: which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly, which only look like they clash, what the cheapest fix is in each case, what that fix would cost me, and whether there's a single revision that would tidy up more than the rest.
