hey — I'm in an applied ethics seminar right now (the module is on collective action: climate, meat, voting, that whole cluster) and for the final handout I'd rather make a decision tree than write another essay. Can you build me one? Audience is first-years who haven't read any of the literature, no citations or reading lists needed, I just want the tree itself.

Start everybody at the same place: my one flight / my single vote / my one pack of factory-farmed chicken makes no difference to anything anyone will ever notice. From there every branch node should be one question someone could answer without already knowing which view it leads to. I want the branches to be about what kind of thing has to go wrong for the act to be wrong — does it need to make a causal difference, is being part of a set that together does the harm enough, does it matter whether you're free riding on other people's restraint, do what the act expresses or what it sustains matter, does it bottom out in what sort of person you're being. Call it 6–8 nodes and 8–10 leaves.

The nodes should be concrete cases rather than abstract questions, and these are the five I definitely want in:

1. the replaceable one — you skip the chicken, but you know the store sells it to someone else and the next order size is unchanged
2. the unknown-size one — your emissions join millions of others and do harm at some threshold, but you have no idea where the threshold sits or how many others there are
3. the norm one — no single act of yours changes anything, but the local convention that people like us don't do X is what keeps the practice small, and your visible noncompliance erodes it slightly
4. the overdetermined one — you're one of a thousand people turning a key and the harm happens whether or not you turn yours
5. the offset one — you can't avoid the trip but you can pay into something that funds an equivalent reduction elsewhere

Leaves should include at least: the view that the inefficacy objection just works and the act is permissible; the expected-difference view; the being-part-of-a-sufficient-set view; a complicity-style view; a fair-share-of-the-collective-duty view; a norm-sustenance view; an expressive/symbolic view; and a character/virtue one. One sentence each on the leaf's most awkward commitment. Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really separate, and note the answer-combinations nobody occupies, with a word on whether that's because they're incoherent or just unfashionable.

Please commit to one tree: pick the branching order, name the leaves, and where two leaves won't come apart tell me which one you think is genuinely a distinct position and why — or tell me the distinction is bogus. Also say if you think any of my cases are rigged or if the branching frame itself is the wrong one. Give me reasons, not a survey of the options.
