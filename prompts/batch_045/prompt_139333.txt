I serve on the content committee of a national history museum that is building a permanent gallery on state violence in the twentieth century, and I have to present a recommendation on one wall text to the committee in ten days. Last night I spent three hours arguing about that text with a friend who spent her career as a prosecutor at an international criminal tribunal, and neither of us moved an inch. Please draft the memo I will circulate.

The case, anonymized because our draft labels are not public. Between 1937 and 1941 the government of a newly consolidated highland state carried out a campaign of forced sedentarization against the Kirzhak, a confederation of transhumant pastoralist clans numbering roughly 120,000 people, who moved flocks along fixed routes between high summer pasture and lowland winter grazing. What is known:

- The trigger was a 1936 revolt in the eastern highlands, in which Kirzhak mounted clans took part and killed government officials. The campaign was justified at the time as counter-insurgency, and there is no doubt the state also had security aims.
- The measures were: dissolution of the clans by decree, abolition of customary courts, closure of the migration routes, confiscation or slaughter of about four-fifths of the flocks, resettlement of families into fixed villages in a malarial lowland zone, conscription of men into road labour battalions, and removal of children to state boarding schools where use of the Kirzhak dialect was punished.
- Census and camp records indicate something like 22,000 to 30,000 deaths across four years, overwhelmingly from hunger, exposure and disease rather than from shootings. Recorded births fell by roughly seventy per cent during the campaign and did not recover for a decade.
- The dialect now has almost no speakers under seventy. The pastoral economy is gone. A reduced population remains in the lowland villages, and there is a diaspora.
- No document in the captured archives orders the destruction of the Kirzhak. The closest materials are cabinet minutes describing the aim as finally settling the clan question; a ministerial note remarking that a people that cannot move will not remain a people; and administrative correspondence that treats the mortality as regrettable but acceptable, written by officials who plainly knew the policy would kill people in large numbers.
- The Kirzhak share the religion of the surrounding majority and speak a dialect of its language, but have descent-based clan membership, their own customary law and a pastoral economy; the census of the period counted them as members of the titular nationality, and the state itself described them in economic terms, as an anachronism to be modernized. Some scholars treat them as a distinct ethnocultural group; the Kirzhak's own associations insist on it.
- The successor state has paid compensation for confiscated livestock and apologised for the suffering caused by the settlements, but refuses the word. A foreign parliament has resolved by a large majority that the campaign was genocide. No court has ever ruled on it. Two respected historians of the region have written that applying the label here is anachronistic and polemical, and a Kirzhak diaspora association has told us it will picket the opening unless the word appears.
- Our own constraints: the museum's labeling policy says gallery texts may characterise events but may not assert legal findings that no competent court has made; and our board wants the gallery internally consistent, because we already use the word for a neighbouring case in which a court did convict defendants, and we decline to use it for another case in which deaths were far higher but the available evidence of intent is purely military.

My friend's position, as strongly as I can put it: the word has a hard-won legal core, the purposeful destruction in whole or in part of a national, ethnical, racial or religious group as such, and the specific-intent requirement is the whole point, since it is what separates genocide from crimes against humanity and from the ordinary cruelties of state-building. On her reading this is a brutal assimilation-and-development campaign with lethal consequences the government accepted but did not aim at; the right labels are crimes against humanity, forced displacement, and cultural destruction, and if we use the word here we will have no way to withhold it from half the modernization campaigns of the century, which will leave us with nothing to say about the cases where the object really was extermination.

My position: the 1948 text is a diplomatic compromise, not the last word on the moral category, and Lemkin's own coinage was meant to capture the destruction of a people's foundations, not only mass killing; he largely lost that drafting fight. The philosophical crux as I now see it is that destroying the material conditions under which a form of life can be transmitted may destroy a group as a continuing entity even if most of its members survive, and that is exactly what the slaughter of the flocks, the closure of the routes and the removal of the children were for. Crimes against humanity, I argued, is about attacks on individuals and does not capture that a group's future existence was what was targeted. She replied that this argument is immensely attractive and also proves far too much, and there we stopped.

Please draft the memo I can send out. Reason from the concepts and from our institutional situation rather than citing case law, UN documents or scholarship, since I cannot check them and the committee will not read them. It should be about 1,700 words and should have exactly these sections, in this order:

- Recommendation — about 250 words. One bottom line, stated in the first sentence, with a concrete course of action and the timeline for it, given that the committee meets in ten days and the gallery text is locked six weeks after that.
- What the committee is actually deciding — about 250 words. Distinguish the different jobs the word might be doing in a museum label, for example as a legal trigger with downstream consequences, as an honorific owed to the victims and their descendants, and as an explanatory category that tells visitors what kind of event this was, and say which of these our institution is and is not in a position to perform. Also say whether we are deciding one case or a rule, and what the rule should be, given the consistency problem above.
- The strongest case for using the word — about 350 words.
- The strongest case against it, including my friend's — about 350 words. The section you are not recommending should still be written strongly enough that its proponents would recognise their own argument in it.
- The middle options and why I am not recommending them — about 300 words. Consider at least hedged formulations, attribution of the word to the foreign parliament or to some scholars, and the term cultural genocide.
- What I should expect at the meeting, and what would change my mind — about 250 words. Include the objection most likely to be made by our own historians, and state honestly what further evidence would move you off the recommendation.

If you think my friend is right, say so in the memo and recommend accordingly; I would much rather be corrected by a draft than by the board.
