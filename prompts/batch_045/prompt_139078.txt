I'm in an advanced undergraduate seminar this term on aggregation in ethics — we opened with Taurek's classic paper on whether the numbers count, spent three weeks on the replies to it, and I've come away less sure than when I started that everyone in the literature is arguing about the same question. My term paper is supposed to take a stand, but before I take one I want the specification. What would an adequate account of the numbers question in life-and-death cases actually have to deliver in order to count as a solution? I want the list of requirements — the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it has to respect rather than collapse, the results in neighbouring areas it isn't allowed to contradict — each with the case or consideration behind it, because I've noticed that a requirement offered without a case attached to it is usually somebody's intuition wearing a suit. Fix the theory's job as follows, and tell me if fixing it that way already begs something: it is to tell a single rescuer, a stranger to everyone at risk and not herself at risk, what she morally ought to do and what she is permitted to do when she can save either a larger group or a smaller one from death but not both. I also want to know whether a theory adequate for that job thereby has to be adequate for a hospital triage officer or a ministry dividing a fixed budget between a common disease and a rare one, or whether it is respectable to have one answer for individual rescuers and another for institutions.

Here are the materials I keep tripping over; treat them as candidate desiderata rather than as settled ones, and add the ones I've missed. First, the disjoint case — six people in one place, one in another, one trip — as against a nested case where the smaller group is a proper subset of the larger: you can rescue Ada, or you can rescue Ada together with five others, so that saving the many is also better for Ada. Does a view have to answer both the same way, and if it answers them differently is that a feature or a symptom? Second, a shrinking-crowd version: one against one, then two against one, then three against one and upward. If numbers begin to matter somewhere, where do they begin, and why isn't the cut-off arbitrary — so does continuity, or a principled threshold, belong on the list at all, or is it just a debating trick? Third, the lottery cases: one in which everyone at risk agreed in advance to a fair lottery, one in which nobody agreed, and a risk version in which the rescuer can give each of six a one-in-six chance or give one person certainty. Must a theory say something separate about chances and about consent, or does the certainty case settle them? Fourth, the small-benefit contrast: six mild headaches against one severe one, where nearly everyone aggregates without hesitation. If the theory refuses to aggregate lives, must it refuse to aggregate headaches too, or must it say what is special about the goods at stake — and is that marker a genuine requirement or a rescue attempt? Fifth, is the theory a theory of the betterness of outcomes, of permissibility, of reasons for acting, or of what a bystander may demand of the rescuer, and can numbers count in one of those registers while being inert in another? Sixth, the equality point that cuts both ways: every anti-aggregation argument I've read leans on each person mattering equally, and the aggregator replies that this is precisely why six equal claims come to more than one. What must a theory say about equal concern so that this move doesn't simply run in both directions?

For each requirement, say what it is for and mark it non-negotiable or a preference of one camp. Then tell me which pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, whether the list as a whole is satisfiable at all (if it isn't, that is itself a result I'd be happy to report in the paper), and which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate views. Keep the scorecard short — a few lines each for straight aggregation, the no-numbers-with-a-lottery view, a greatest-claim or greater-burden view, and a priority-to-the-worst-off view — since the criteria are the part I need, but do say which requirement each of those four trips over. And please don't give me a reading list; I already have one.
