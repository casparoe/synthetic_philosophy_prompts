I want a case built to order, for a seminar I am teaching next term on the situationist critique of virtue ethics — specifically on whether that critique's operationalization of a virtue as cross-situational behavioral consistency can do the work its proponents need from it. The dialectic I want inverted is the familiar one: the critique normally runs from observed behavioral inconsistency across variations in features that look morally irrelevant (how hurried the subject is, their mood, whether they have just found a coin, whether the person in need is identifiable or merely statistical) to the denial of robust character traits. I want a case in which that very measure ranks two agents in the wrong order: the agent it scores as consistent is the one the virtue theorist should deny has the virtue, and the agent it scores as inconsistent is the one who has it.

The distinction I want the case to isolate is one I have been calling discriminative reliability: not reliability in the sense of producing the same behavior across occasions, but reliability in the sense of producing it because of the features that actually make it called for. My suspicion is that the two come apart, and that the standard helping battery — precisely because it varies only what its designers took to be irrelevant — is blind to the difference. Show me that in a case.

Constraints. One paragraph, realistic setting, ordinary adults; no brain lesions, no compulsion, no exotic technology, and I would prefer a hospital ward or a comparable workplace. The same two agents should face the same two requests, and the behavior in question must fall under a single nominal description that a coder working from the case would naturally use — a colleague in distress asking for help — so that the difference between the agents is genuinely scored as inconsistency rather than as a difference in the type of situation. In one of the two requests the help asked for is something an agent with the virtue would refuse, for a reason that is morally relevant and that is available to the agents in the case without philosophical reconstruction: being asked to help cover up a mistake, or to help in a way that subverts a procedure that exists for a reason. In the other, the same nominal help is what the virtue requires. Now the hard part. The agent who helps in both cases must not be cheap to convict: their helping should be ungrudging, responsive to the other person's situation rather than to the credit they will get, accompanied by the feelings one would want, and not traceable to servility, anxiety, or a taste for being needed. Their defect, if they have one, must be a defect of discrimination alone. Symmetrically, the discriminating agent's refusal must not be a case of selfishness in disguise or of principled fastidiousness that makes them unavailable when help is genuinely owed. And because the obvious reply to any such construction is that the virtue theorist has redefined virtue so that no pattern of behavior could ever refute it, I want one further situation appended — not among the original pair — which the virtue-theoretic account predicts in advance and the consistency measure does not, together with a statement of what result in that situation would disconfirm the virtue theorist.

After the scenario, do the work of showing that it meets the specification. Give me the verdict of the consistency criterion as it is actually applied in the helping experiments, and the verdict of a virtue theory on which the virtues require practical wisdom and so require responsiveness to features the coder's scheme does not represent. Say which feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens if that feature is removed — in particular what happens if the two requests differ in a way the coding scheme could easily register, such as one of them being plainly unlawful. Then give the strongest reply available to a defender of the consistency criterion, which I take to be the line that the critique was only ever meant to test consistency across variation in morally irrelevant features and can perfectly well allow sensitivity to relevant ones; tell me whether that reply saves the criterion or concedes what I want it to concede.

Finally, the part I care about most: tell me what would lead you to withdraw the case as a counterexample, and flag where your own construction is most likely to be begging the question against the situationist. In particular, if the discriminating agent's refusal can be redescribed as the manifestation of a more specific disposition — one whose attribution is itself exposed to the situationist's data about how local such dispositions are — say so, and say how much of my argument goes down with it.
