I quit drinking in Febuary, six months ago, and I've relapsed twice since then — and around the same time I finally got assessed for ADHD and started medication, so I've been thinking about self-control more than is good for me. The medication did something wierd: some of the things I could never make myself do I now just do, and some didn't budge at all. Which makes me think my failures were never all one thing. I went and read about hyperbolic discounting because someone told me it explains all of this, and it explains some of it and not other bits, and now I want to know what I actually think rather than what the blog told me to think. Here are my gut verdicts, going case by case.

1. A party, six weeks in, having decided not to drink; someone hands me a beer, I drink it, and then I drink six more. Weakness of will, obviously.

2. The report due in three weeks; I answer email instead. Weakness of will.

3. One in the morning, in bed, scrolling. I judge right then, at that moment, that sleeping is what I most want and what's best for me — there's no "later" involved, both options are now — and I keep scrolling. This feels like the purest case of weakness of will I have, and it's exactly the case the discount-curve story has nothing to say about, because nothing is being delayed.

4. I planned to go to something on Saturday; Saturday comes, I think it over again and genuinely decide staying home is better, and I stay home. Not weakness of will — that's just changing my mind, even though from outside it's identical to breaking a resolution.

5. I deleted the food delivery apps and asked my wife not to let me order anything after nine. I want to order and I don't. Not weakness of will; the opposite, if anything. But my gut also says the guy who has to build the cage is more weak-willed than the guy who doesn't need one, and I can't see how both of those are true.

6. On Thursday I agreed to go to the thing on Saturday knowing perfectly well I'd drink there, and I drank. At no point on Saturday did I act against what I judged best at the time; at eleven pm I judged drinking best and I drank. But I think this is worse than case 1, not better, and I want to call it weakness of will. Every definition I try tells me I can't.

7. Standing at the bar actually fighting with myself and losing, versus sitting down and ordering with no fight at all. The second is the clearer case of weakness of will to me, which seems backwards — I'd have assumed struggling and losing was the textbook one.

8. I judge I should give my daughter the last portion and I eat it myself. Weakness of will, I think, except it isn't about my own good at all.

9. I know the statistics, I know flying is safe, and I'm still terrified on the plane. Not an action at all, but it feels like the same shape of failure. Whatever account I end up with, does it say this is the same thing or a different one?

10. Waking at 5:55, judging that staying in bed is best and that I have most reason to stay there, and getting up and going anyway. Structurally it's acting against my better judgment, but it's about the one thing in my life I'd point to with pride. I think I saw the phrase "inverse akrasia" for this, or else I made it up.

The principle I half-believe is that it isn't the shape of the discount curve that matters but how far away the self who pays the bill feels: I don't stay up late because the reward is now, I stay up because the cost lands on somebody who doesn't feel like me. That handles 1, 2, 4, 5 and maybe 6, but it predicts that 3 isn't weakness of will, and 3 is the one I'm most sure about. The other thing I half-believe is the boring one — you judge A better, you do B, and no new information came in — which handles 3 and 8 fine but can't tell 4 from 6, and 4 and 6 are the pair I most want separated.

So: give me one or two principles, stated precisely enough that I could actually apply them to a new case, and for each clause say which of my cases it's in there to deal with. Then run them on the three cases I haven't given a verdict on — the "well, today's blown" one where I have a single drink, judge that drinking more is bad, and drink six precisely because I've already had one; the one where I stay sober only because there's money riding on it with one of those commitment-contract websites; and my dad, who drinks every night and sincerely judges that it's fine for him, and is wrong about that. And tell me straight which of my verdicts I have to give up, because I suspect at least one of them is self-flattery. If the principle ends up cutting my failures into two kinds, I'd also like to know which kind the meds were apparently fixing. And please, no headings, bullets, bold or tables — just continuous prose, the way you'd write an email.
