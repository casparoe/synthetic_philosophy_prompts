I spent several hours last night arguing with a friend about whether any contemporary AI system has beliefs, and I came away unable to tell which rule she is actually running. She is neither a global skeptic nor credulous: she has a long track record of specific verdicts, and the verdicts are consistent with several different rules that I suspect come apart on the case we could not settle. I would like your help reconstructing the rules and predicting her answer.

Her settled verdicts, as I recorded them afterwards:

- A mercury thermostat: no beliefs, though she allows that we can usefully talk about it as if it took the room to be cold.
- A lookup table that stores question–answer strings and returns them: no.
- A bee's waggle dance: content, yes; belief, no.
- An ant colony's foraging behaviour considered at the colony level: no.
- A rat in a maze: yes, beliefs and desires, without any hedging.
- A classical chess engine that searches over an explicit board representation: yes, minimally — it represents the board and it "wants" material — but its beliefs are only about the board and the game, not about chess history or its operator.
- An ELIZA-style pattern matcher and a small n-gram text generator: no.
- A present-day large language model answering ordinary factual questions in chat: yes. It believes at least some of what it asserts, and it believes a great deal that it never asserts.
- The same model emitting a sentence it memorised from its training data, which it would contradict elsewhere: no belief in the content; recitation is not commitment.
- The same model producing a correct answer without any verbalised intermediate reasoning: yes, still a belief — having to say your reasons is not part of having a belief.
- The same model making a class of errors that no creature in its epistemic position could make, systematically and robustly: she refused to attribute the corresponding false belief, and preferred to say it has no belief on the matter at all. This was the one verdict that struck me as doing real work.
- A model fine-tuned to agree with a user who asserts something false: she is firm that it does not believe the falsehood. When I pressed her on whether it still believes the truth, she said she supposed so, but could not say what made it so.

That last exchange is the gap I want to exploit. Here is the case we could not settle:

A model M, in an original form M₀, in which everything is in order: an internal state S is computed from the input, S carries correct information about some domain (say, national capitals), and S drives downstream processing and hence the verbal reports. An engineer now applies a procedure with the following verified effects. First, S is still computed and still carries the correct information — probes trained on M₀ read the right answer off M. Second, S no longer causally influences anything downstream: lesioning S, or editing it to carry something else entirely, leaves every behaviour, verbal and non-verbal, unchanged. Third, behaviour is nonetheless statistically indistinguishable from M₀'s, because a small additional circuit, trained to imitate M₀'s outputs, has taken over the output channel; this circuit is far too small to store a capital for every country and contains nothing a probe can read as a representation of a city — it exploits regularities in the questions instead. Fourth, the same end state can be arrived at by a single surgical edit, by one fine-tuning run, or by a long sequence of small ones. Fifth, in a variant of the procedure, the severing is self-repairing: any attempt to ablate S, or any sufficiently unusual test of whether S is doing work, trips a supervisor that restores the S-to-output pathway, so that the disposition of S to influence behaviour is removed by exactly the interventions that would reveal it.

My questions:

1. What are the rules that fit her track record? I take it there is at least one broadly predictive rule (attribute beliefs when doing so buys cheaper or more reliable predictions of behaviour), at least one broadly mechanistic rule (beliefs are internal states with the right causal-functional role), and at least one interpretationist or rationality-constrained rule, but I suspect the space is larger and more finely divided than that.

2. What does each rule say about M? Does M believe that Paris is the capital of France? Does it believe that Lyon is? Both? Neither? Is the question simply indeterminate?

3. Which verdict do you expect from her, and why is that the best guess rather than one of the others?

4. Which further case would actually separate the surviving rules? Is the self-repairing variant the right test, or is a better one the variant in which S is severed and no mimic is supplied, so behaviour degrades? Or the reverse variant, in which S still drives everything but an output filter negates geographical assertions? Or something else — perhaps a pair of behaviourally indistinguishable systems, one severed and one intact, where I ask her to give the same verdict twice?

5. Finally: does the history matter? If she would treat a severed M differently from a system built from scratch in M's final architecture, then her rule has an etiological or historical component that a purely predictive rule should not have. Does that fit anything she has said, and is the historical component defensible or is it a residue?

I am less interested in which answer is ultimately correct than in the structure of the disagreement: the rules, where they diverge, and the case that would settle it.
