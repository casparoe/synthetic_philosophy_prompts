I am finishing a novel set in a fictional river republic of the 1840s in which debt is inherited and a household can be "unnamed" — struck from the register — for a father's default. I have been asked to contribute an author's note on the ethical commitments the book quietly relies on, and I would like those commitments audited before I commit them to print, because I cannot locate the fault myself. They are:

(a) Moral judgments are true or false only relative to a moral framework, and no framework is correct independently of all frameworks. I am a cognitivist about this, which is why I call myself a relativist rather than an expressivist.

(b) Disagreement across frameworks is nonetheless genuine disagreement: when my twelfth-century character and my 1930s character contradict one another about the same practice, neither is merely reporting her attitudes, and they are not simply talking past each other.

(c) Moral progress is real. When my republic abandons un-naming, that is an improvement, not merely a change its former citizens happen by then to favour.

(d) Methodologically I hold what I think of as the no-external-standing rule: a novelist earns the right to judge a practice by rendering it thickly from inside the form of life it belongs to, not by importing principles from outside that life.

(e) My protagonist's condemnation is nonetheless just correct. She has no available vocabulary in which inherited debt can be called unjust and has to improvise one; I want to say she is right, and right in a way that binds even a reader who rejects the vocabulary she invents. I have been calling this proleptic condemnation.

(f) The novel is a mechanism for what I call borrowed endorsement: a reader occupies a framework she does not hold and thereby comes to know something — not merely to feel with someone or to learn what others believe.

Readers of earlier drafts tell me the package collapses at the point of tolerance, that on my own terms my condemnations carry no authority for anyone outside my framework. I have never found that worry persuasive, and I suspect the damage lies elsewhere. Which of (a)–(f) genuinely pull against each other, and which only appear to? For each real tension, say what would have to give and what the cheapest revision would cost me; rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equal; and tell me whether any single revision would settle more than one of them. Please skip any general map of the landscape of relativism — I want the audit, not the survey.
