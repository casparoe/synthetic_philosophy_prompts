I have been reading psychiatric-taxonomy threads on the rationalist blogs for years — the long ones about whether ADHD is real, whether depression is a natural kind, whether the autism spectrum is an artefact of diagnostic substitution — and I finally went back to the source everyone gestures at, Ian Hacking's idea of looping effects: classify people under a kind, the people so classified change in response to being classified, and the kind then has to be revised to fit the changed people, indefinitely. I read him second-hand, mostly via blog summaries and a couple of essays, so I am not wedded to the letter of his account; what I want is the principle behind my own verdicts, which I have never managed to line up. My cases, with my gut verdicts: a diagnosis of Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder received at thirty-four loops hard, and loops in a distinctive backward direction — it reorganises the person's autobiography (so that is why school was like that) at least as much as it changes anything they will do next. A dementia diagnosis that the patient is never told about and could not grasp does not loop at the level of the patient at all, though it loops plenty for the family and the carers. Telling a research volunteer that a scan shows amyloid-positive, pre-symptomatic Alzheimer disease loops hard the moment it is disclosed, because ordinary forgetfulness gets re-read as symptom. A Type 2 diabetes diagnosis loops a little, a fractured femur not at all, so there is a gradient even inside somatic medicine. Hacking's own fugue cases from the 1890s look like looping at maximum: available diagnosis, available behaviour. A category that exists mainly so services can be billed or extra exam time granted — and here I include Pathological Demand Avoidance, which as far as I know never made it into the main diagnostic manuals but has an enormous parent-led following in Britain — loops, but shallowly and instrumentally: people learn to present for it rather than to be it. And the broadening from Asperger syndrome to the autism spectrum looks like a case where looping made the category worse-behaved and yet, I want to say, more valid rather than less.

The pair I cannot reconcile is this. I keep wanting to say that looping requires uptake by the person classified: no uptake, no loop, which is exactly why the undisclosed dementia diagnosis is my clean negative case. But I also think that a polygenic risk score for schizophrenia handed to the parents of a four-year-old loops, even though the child takes up nothing; and I think a label a person explicitly rejects still loops, because refusing a description is one way of being shaped by it. So either uptake by the classified is not required, or my negative case is not really negative. My own patched-together principle currently has three conditions, and I would rather have it tested and repaired than replaced wholesale if it can be saved: a classification of people loops to the extent that (i) it is learnable and applicable by the person classified or by those who act on them, (ii) being so classified makes a practical difference to what anybody does, and (iii) — my own addition, which I have never seen made — it is narratively incorporable, meaning it can be woven into a first-person life story and so reorganise memory and self-description retroactively, not merely redirect future behaviour. I also suspect I need a separate clause for incentive-driven looping, where the feedback runs through what is rewarded rather than through what is believed, but I cannot tell whether that is a fourth condition or a different phenomenon entirely.

So: is there one account of when a human classification loops, and how hard, that delivers all of the above? If so, please state it precisely, say which of my cases motivates each clause, and tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to drop — I would rather give up a verdict than accept a principle with a clause that exists only to save it. I would also like it applied to three cases I have not listed: obesity being classified as a disease, schools labelling children as gifted, and an employer's burnout-risk score produced by an algorithm nobody is allowed to inspect. One further thing I care about, and which the comment sections never settle: does any of this tell us anything about whether these kinds are real, or only that they are unstable? If your answer is that looping is neutral on realism, say so plainly, and say what would have to be true for it not to be. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the principle, or the statement that no single principle exists — then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
