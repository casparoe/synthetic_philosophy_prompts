I'm three years into a PhD and I've now watched the same argument blow up in the common room maybe half a dozen times, and it always lands in exactly the same place: one side says philosophy obviously doesn't make progress, we're still arguing about free will, the external world, and mind-body two and a half millennia on and nobody has been forced to give anything up; the other side says that's like concluding medicine doesn't work because there's still cancer, the tractable questions got answered and then left and started their own departments (physics, psychology, computer science), and besides, everyone standing around in 2024 has arguments available that nobody in 1824 could even formulate. Both sides know the other's moves in advance, both accuse the other of rigging the definition, and nobody ever shifts an inch. I want a diagnosis of the stall, not a verdict on who's right.

Here's my hypothesis, and I want you to tell me how much of it survives. I think there are three disagreements stacked under one question. First, a metric disagreement nobody makes explicit: is progress convergence on true answers, or growth in the stock of distinctions and arguments available, or something like an increasing capacity to answer objections earlier people simply couldn't handle? Those come apart, and I've never heard anyone say which one they're scoring with. Second, a hidden empirical question nobody can settle from an armchair: how many live philosophical questions have actually settled in the last century, whether the number of live positions per question is growing or shrinking, and whether what philosophers believe at a given time tracks anything like argument quality rather than fashion. The one big international survey of philosophers' beliefs that always gets cited looks like the beginning of data on this and doesn't obviously answer it. Third — and this is the bit I keep coming back to — I suspect the metrics trade off rather than merely differ. If a field rewards generating new live positions, i.e. mapping the space of coherent views, then producing one is an achievement and it simultaneously makes consensus less likely. A field can then be getting better in one sense and thereby look like it's failing in the other, which would make the 'no progress' verdict partly an artifact of what the field incentivises.

So what kind of impasse is this actually: verbal, a substantive disagreement about the historical record, an empirical question we just lack the data for, or two different questions sharing a label? Argue for your read using what each side commits itself to when you press on it, say which layer is doing the real work, and then say what would count as progress on the meta-question itself — a distinction that makes it well posed, a way of getting the data, some formal result about the trade-off — or argue that we shouldn't expect any and why. And please flag how confident you are in the diagnosis and what would change your mind about it; I'd rather have the weak joint pointed out than a tidy answer I can't trust.
