I am drafting a paper on moral testimony, and I want the specification settled before I measure any existing view against it. Fix the purpose first, so that adequacy has a target: the theory is to serve as a guide for a competent but morally unexceptional agent who faces a concrete situation and must decide whether to take someone else's word on a moral question, whose word to take, how far to take it, and what she ends up with if she takes it. A theory adequate to that purpose should also be able to say what is supposed to be wrong with the kinds of deference we find objectionable, and why that wrong is not simply one more case of believing an unreliable source.

Write the desiderata such a theory must meet. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that earns it a place on the list, and mark it as non-negotiable or as something only one camp has insisted on. I would rather have eight requirements with real cases behind them than twenty with none.

The following cases are the ones I want adjudicated, since they are where I suspect existing views quietly cheat. Say, for each, whether it generates a genuine desideratum, what that desideratum is, and whether the case is really doing work or is cheap:

1. A trusted person says the act is wrong, offers no reasons, and cannot offer any; the agent defers anyway.
2. The person you have wronged tells you what you owe her. Her say-so seems partly to constitute the answer, and withholding deference here looks like a moral failing rather than an epistemic virtue.
3. Someone with relevant lived experience delivers a moral verdict whose authority seems to come from better access to non-moral facts rather than from superior moral judgment.
4. A testifier whose predictions reliably track the truth but whose stated reasons are confused or worse; reliability and articulate justification come apart.
5. A testifier whose fundamental moral commitments you reject entirely nevertheless tells you that an act is impermissible, and is right.
6. Ten thousand aggregated strangers against one person you regard as a genuine moral expert.
7. The learner's case: a child asks whether lying is wrong and is told that it is. Deferring looks not merely permissible but required, and yet the theory must say what, if anything, changes as the learner matures.
8. An artefact that has no experience, no understanding, no stake, and no capacity to weigh reasons, but which predicts your own considered verdicts better than you predict them yourself.
9. A moral question that is open for you only because the empirical facts are not yet in: separate the deference due to the prediction from the deference due to the verdict, and say whether a theory that handles this by reclassifying it as non-moral testimony has thereby explained anything.
10. The agent with no view of her own at all, who has simply never thought about the matter.

I also want an explicit ruling on which distinctions a theory may not collapse: deferring to a person versus treating her as evidence; believing on testimony versus understanding why; reliability versus expertise versus authority; the moral content of a verdict versus the empirical claims embedded in it; the first-personal deliberative standpoint versus third-personal appraisal; the epistemic question of what you may believe versus the moral question of whether deferring here is itself a failing versus the pedagogical question of what deferring does to a learner; deference to a person versus deference to a procedure such as a tribunal, a lottery, or an aggregation; and taking a verdict now versus coming to understand it later.

Three meta-questions matter more to me than the list itself. First, which items that people standardly demand are really camp commitments dressed up as data, and which are forced by the cases. I have in mind, as candidates for demotion, the demand that the theory vindicate the claim that there are no moral experts, and the demand that it produce a special explanation of cases involving testifiers with odious views; if such cases turn only on the speaker's unreliability, they are easy for every theory and should not be allowed to earn it credit. Second, whether any subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable, so that the list itself becomes a result about what no single theory of moral testimony can be. Third, which single requirement does the most exclusionary work, knocking out the most otherwise attractive views.

Close with a short scorecard, no more than a line or two each, for four generic contenders: a straight deference view on which moral testimony works like any other; a view on which it transmits knowledge but not understanding; a view on which the deliberative standpoint makes deference in principle inappropriate; and a view that explains whatever asymmetry there is by appeal to the nature of moral disagreement rather than to the nature of moral judgment. Keep the weight on the criteria. Do not cite or name authors, do not survey the literature, and do not reach for examples beyond the ones I have given unless one is doing real work.
