I am writing a chapter on counterfactual argument in the historiography of the Third Reich, and I have run into a distinction that my field keeps blurring and that I cannot name. I would like to know whether there is an established concept for it in the theory of causation, the philosophy of history, or the methodology of the social sciences — and, if the nearest established concepts do not fit exactly, how what I am describing differs from each of them and what you would propose to call it.

The pattern comes in two versions, which I suspect are related but which may not be the same thing.

First version. Asked whether Hitler was necessary for the outcome, one historian says no: given the settlement after the First World War, the Depression, the weakness of the Weimar parties, and the German elites' fear of the left together with their commitment to rearmament, a violently expansionist, antisemitic dictatorship would have taken power in Germany in the 1930s with or without him. Another says yes: without Hitler's specific obsessions, his standing inside the movement, his personal hold over the party and the SS, and his willingness to gamble in 1939–41, there is no war of annihilation in the East and no extermination camps, whatever else Germany becomes. What strikes me is that these two claims look compatible, and that they are compatible because they describe the outcome at different grains of detail — a radical and murderous German regime in that decade, on the one hand; the Holocaust as it in fact occurred, with those camps, that timing and that chain of decisions, on the other. The disagreement is not about the causal facts. It is about how finely the effect has to be described before the counterfactual is evaluated. I notice the same move in arguments about Sarajevo in 1914 and about the missile crisis of 1962.

Second version. Even where both sides agree on the grain, there is a further slide. A structuralist will concede that this particular individual was indispensable to what happened and then add that the slot he occupied was not: had he died in 1930, some other suitably placed demagogue would have filled it and the outcome would have been recognisably similar. So the individual is treated at once as a cause that makes a difference and as something replaceable.

What I want is not a label but the shape of the thing. Does the first version have a name of its own, or is it a special case of something more general about the dependence of causal claims on how the effect is described? Is the second the same phenomenon in another guise, or a distinct one that needs its own term and its own test? Where are the boundaries — what would have to change in a case for it to count as clear rather than borderline? And is there a diagnostic I could apply to a counterfactual claim in a historian's prose to tell which of the two questions it is answering, since my suspicion is that a good deal of the Great Man versus structure dispute is a collision between claims that were never about the same question?

I can chase references myself. What I need is the distinction, its edges, and a vocabulary for it.
