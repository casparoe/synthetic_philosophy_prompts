THE QUESTION (30 marks)

Nāgārjuna runs the tetralemma on a number of questions, including whether the self is identical to the aggregates, distinct from them, both, or neither — and he rejects all four.

(a) What is emptiness (śūnyatā) a denial of, and how is it related to dependent origination? [8 marks]
(b) Explain why the rejection of all four lemmas is, or is not, itself a fifth position. Use the lecture's distinction between negative and therapeutic rejections, and answer the fifth-koti objection. [12 marks]
(c) Distinguish Nāgārjuna's position from (i) nihilism and (ii) the claim that ultimate reality is ineffable — beyond anything we can say or think. If you think the ineffability reading is correct, argue for it rather than assuming it. [10 marks]

THE STUDENT'S ANSWER

The tetralemma (catuṣkoṭi) sets out four options: X, not-X, both X and not-X, neither. On the self: the self is identical to the aggregates, it is distinct from them, it is both, it is neither. Nāgārjuna rejects all four.

(a) What emptiness denies is svabhāva — intrinsic nature, existing on ones own, independently of causes, parts and conceptual imputation. Emptiness is not a separate reality behind things and it is not nothingness; it is just dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda) described from the other direction. To say that a thing is empty is to say that it arises dependently, and vice versa. That is why the stock objection — if everything is empty there are no four noble truths, no path, no attainment — misfires: if things had fixed natures nothing could change, be practised, or be accomplished, so emptiness is what makes the path possible in the first place. It is also why emptiness is like a medicine that turns to poison when you grasp it as a view.

Conventionally (saṃvṛti) there are persons, pots, and true and false statements about them; ultimately (paramārtha) nothing possesses its own nature. These are not two worlds or two inventories of objects — they are the same items under two descriptions, one of which is the everyday one and one of which is the correct analysis.

(b) On the fifth-koti objection: Nāgārjuna's rejections are therapeutic rather than negative. He isn't saying, of the self, that it has the higher-order property of failing all four descriptions — that would leave the question "what is the self, really?" in place and answer it, and it would be a thesis, and he says outright that he has no thesis. The rejection instead withdraws the terms: once you see that there is no self with a nature to be tracked, there is no subject left for the four predicates, and the question dissolves. Nāgārjuna was himself a Prāsaṅgika — he thought the Mādhyamika may argue only by prasaṅga, drawing out consequences his opponent is already committed to rather than asserting anything of his own — and Candrakīrti later defended this against Bhāviveka, who wanted autonomous inferences (svatantra) whose terms both parties accept.

(c) So he is not a nihilist: he is not denying that anything exists, only that anything exists independently, and a nihilist who says nothing exists is still helping himself to a notion of existence with a fixed nature. He isnt a Pyrrhonian either: the Pyrrhonist suspends judgement and goes on living by appearances, whereas Nāgārjuna denies all four. What the tetralemma shows is that reality as it really is cannot be captured by any of the four conceptual options. Emptiness is empty too — it is not a thing or a ground — but it remains the ultimate truth about everything: how things are in themselves is ineffable, beyond what can be said or thought, and seeing this is what loosens the grasping that keeps us stuck.
