I'm putting together a short series of dialogues where a dead philosopher gets dropped into the present and has to react to something that didn't exist in their lifetime. This episode it's Epicurus being walked through a menu of mind-uploading procedures by a very patient neuroengineer, and I need to know what he'd actually say about each one — not what a present-day physicalist would say with Epicurus's name stappled onto it.

I've made four variants of the gradual-replacement case rather than using the standard textbook one:

(a) the straight swap — one neuron at a time gets replaced by a silicon unit that reproduces its input-output behaviour exactly, over about ten years, patient awake and holding a conversation the whole way through, no reported discontinuity;

(b) the overnight job — the exact same swaps, in the same order, the same ten years' worth of them, but compressed into one session under general anaesthesia, so there's no continuous stream of experience running through the change;

(c) the shadow run — nothing in the brain is replaced at all; a full emulation is built from a scan, switched on, and run alongside the biological person for a year, with all her memories, reporting being her, indistinguishable in conversation; at the end of the year the biological brain is destroyed painlessly while the emulation carries on;

(d) the tolerance swap — as in (a), except each replacement unit matches the original only to within manufacturing tolerance, so by year ten the system's behaviour has drifted a little from what the untouched brain would have done.

What I want from you: for each of the four, would Epicurus count it as the death of that person, and — this is the part I keep going in circles on — does he have the resources to say so without wrecking his own argument? If death is the privation of sensation, and every good and bad thing lies in sensation, then I can't see why he'd care about any of these four. But it also feels like his materialism about the soul (fine atoms that need the body as a kind of container to have any sensation at all) should make him specially hostile to (c), maybe more hostile than to (a). Which of the four would he think is the worst thing that could happen to her, if he's willing to rank them at all? And name the one variant where you think he'd have to admit his own machinery doesn't reach the case, and say what exactly runs out on him there.

Also tell me if I've built the variants badly — if two of them collapse into the same case for his purposes, say which and why.
