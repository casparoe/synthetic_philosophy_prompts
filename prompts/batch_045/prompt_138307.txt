Hey — I'm a political theory PhD student and I've been sitting in on a reading group on counterfactual history (mostly historians, two philosophers, everyone slightly talking past each other). There's one move that keeps getting wheeled out and I want to audit it properly before I put my foot in it again. It goes like this: whenever someone asks whether there would still have been something recognisably like Nazi Germany if Hitler had died in, say, 1925, or never got his citizenship, or been shot in the street in 1932 — pick your deletion point — the answer comes back as an analogy. Hitler was the spark; the structural conditions were the powder keg. Versailles, the Depression, the fragility of the Weimar party system, the conservative elites and the army, fear of Bolshevism, the existing völkisch and antisemitic substratum: the tinder was stacked and drying for a decade, so of course something was going to go off, and some other demagogue would have lit it. My supervisor used it last week to argue two further things: first, that a right-authoritarian, anti-Versailles, expansionist regime was close to overdetermined and that the interesting explanation is entirely on the powder side; second, that obsessing about the individual is a kind of moral self-flattery, because if any spark would have done then the blame is diffuse and structural rather than located in one man. A friend of mine in the group went further and said there's no real difference between deleting Hitler and swapping him for a generic stand-in, and that it's only because we can treat them as equivalent that the counterfactual is testable at all.

What I want is the audit rather than the verdict. Give me the mapping explicitly, table form is fine: what plays the spark, the powder, the ignition, the explosion, the other available sparks, and the person who notices the smoke. Then the disanalogies, and — this is the part I care about — which of them are load-bearing for the jobs the analogy is being asked to do and which are just decoration. The ones I keep circling myself: a spark is external to the keg and unaffected by it, and the explosion doesn't rearrange the powder on its way to going off, whereas Hitler spent the years before 1933 altering the composition of the fuel (building a mass-mobilisation party whose base couldn't easily be demobilised, destroying the credibility of the conservative alternatives, getting the elites to think they were hiring a useful instrument). And sparks are interchangeable in a way that catalysts or enzymes are not: the rhetorical force of the image comes entirely from the interchangeability, which is exactly the substantive claim in dispute, so it looks to me like it's being smuggled in as a premise. I'd also like the ambiguity in "there would have been a Nazi Germany" pulled apart into tiers rather than smoothed over — (a) some right-authoritarian, revisionist, probably antisemitic regime; (b) a plebiscitary, totalitarian, mass-mobilisation fascist regime of roughly that shape; (c) that regime with that leadership style, that internal structure, that timing, and that genocide. My hunch is the analogy looks strong for (a), shaky for (b), and empty for (c), and that most of its apparent power comes from sliding between the tiers — but I'd rather be told the tiering is a fake distinction than have it politely endorsed. And separately: was he filling a role that already existed, the strongman the conservatives thought they were hiring, or inventing one that had no precedent and no obvious successor? I can't tell whether that question is the whole ballgame or a distraction.

Last bit: if the spark-and-keg picture can't carry what my supervisor and my friend are loading onto it, is there a better image, or is the honest answer that every candidate quietly presupposes an answer to the interchangeability question? I've been kicking around catalyst-versus-reagent (a catalyst isn't consumed and it's highly specific, a reagent changes what the products even are), keystone species, a prism that splits diffuse white light into a particular spectrum, a lens that concentrates scattered heat into one burning point, a selector rather than a trigger. I don't know if any of those survive contact with the case or if they just relabel the problem. No need for citations or a reading list, I'll chase the literature myself later — I just want the arguments and the places they break, and I'm happy to be told I've got the tiering backwards.
