I have been reading about pacifism on my own, without any academic background, and I keep getting stuck not on an argument for or against it but on an institution. In countries with conscription, a person recognised as a conscientious objector is not simply excused: he is assigned alternative civilian service instead, often in hospitals, nursing homes or retirement homes, for a comparable stretch of time. To be recognised, he has to satisfy a board that his objection is sincere and that it comes from a deep and serious conviction; in many systems a purely political objection is not enough, and the objection has to be to war as such. Courts in most countries that distinguish the two allow blanket objection but not selective objection, and I gather that in the United States the Supreme Court held in Gillette v. United States (1971) that a recognised objector must refuse all wars. Meanwhile some objectors, the ones usually called absolutists or total objectors, refuse the substitute service too, on the grounds that nursing and similar work just frees up other people for the war effort, and they are punished for it.

Three things about this puzzle me. First, the more demanding and, as it seems to me, less plausible view — that all war is impermissible — is the one the state accommodates, while the more modest and better evidenced view — that this particular war is unjust — gets nothing but prison. If the point of the exemption were to avoid forcing people to do what they have most reason to refuse, the ranking should surely be reversed. Second, the substitute service looks inconsistent with the objector's own stated reason: if the wrong lies in killing, then working in a hospital that releases somebody else to serve looks like cooperation; yet the state treats the refusal to wear a uniform as the whole of the objection. Third, the objector still pays taxes and remains bound by every other legal duty, and the state obviously does not act as if pacifism might be true; it looks less like a concession that war is wrongful than like a dispensation granted to a minority from a rule assumed to bind everyone else.

My own guess, which I would like tested, is that the practice treats a conscientious refusal as an integrity-like commitment that constitutes who someone is, rather than as a truth-tracking judgement about a particular war; that would explain why the state inquires into the source and depth of the belief and never into the justice of the war, and why the institution sits so comfortably with the idea that ordinary soldiers are not culpable for an unjust war their leaders chose. I have also invented a small test of my own: imagine someone who has no objection to fighting as such and would happily volunteer, but who objects to being conscripted, to being forced. As far as I can tell he gets nothing, which suggests the protected interest is specifically about killing and not about coercion or autonomy.

So my questions are these. What view of pacifism, and of what a conscientious refusal actually is, does the practice only make sense under? Which features strictly presuppose that view and which merely fit well with it — does the substitute service really commit the state to an act-centred morality of the sort that distinguishes what I do from what I merely contribute to, or could a complicity-based pacifism accept it as a workable compromise? Is the package internally coherent, given that the state simultaneously reviews the content of the objector's belief and refuses to assess the war? Is the institution perhaps better explained by something other than a moral view at all — administrative convenience, the state's interest in preserving its capacity to wage war, or a cheap purchase of legitimacy — so that the same institution would be licensed by several quite different theories? And finally: what would have to change if the underlying view were replaced, first by a complicity-based pacifism, then by a just-war view that recognises selective objection, and then by the view that the pacifists may simply be right?
