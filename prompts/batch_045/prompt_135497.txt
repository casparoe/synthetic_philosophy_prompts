The question. I know the statement of the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem well enough: with three or more alternatives, an unrestricted domain, and a social choice function that is onto (every alternative can win at some profile), strategy-proofness — truthful reporting weakly dominates every misreport, for every voter, at every profile — forces dictatorship. What I lack is a gut-level grip on why moving to randomized rules, which return a lottery over the alternatives rather than a single alternative, lets you escape it. Random dictatorship (draw a voter uniformly at random and implement that voter's top alternative) is anonymous, neutral, ex post efficient, and strategy-proof once preferences over alternatives are extended to preferences over lotteries by first-order stochastic dominance. That is a great many desiderata recovered at once, and I want to understand what was actually paid for them.

Here is my half-formed picture, which I want you either to turn into a real explanation or to dismantle. As I understand it, the deterministic proof turns on a discreteness: as a voter moves some alternative x upward through their own ranking, holding everyone else's ballots fixed, the winner either does or does not switch to x; so there is a first position at which it does, and that first jump hands the voter a kind of decisive power over a pair, which the rest of the argument leverages into full dictatorship. Randomization, on this picture, smooths the jump into a marginal shift of probability mass, and a marginal shift of probability mass is never a first-order-stochastic-dominance improvement when truth-telling is already SD-maximal. If that is right, the escape has nothing essentially to do with preferences: it is that the outcome space has become convex. If it is wrong — if the real reason lies elsewhere, in what "onto" now means, or in the fact that we have weakened the incentive notion rather than enlarged the design space — say so, and say where.

Please also settle a worry I cannot settle on my own. A skeptic says the escape is a cheat: we have not made truth-telling safe, we have only made the ordering over outcomes thin enough that fewer misreports register as improvements. To test this I invented a strengthening. Call a randomized rule extension-robust strategy-proof if it remains strategy-proof no matter how each voter completes their ranking over lotteries, subject only to two constraints: (i) the completion ranks degenerate lotteries by that voter's ranking of the alternatives, and (ii) it never contradicts first-order stochastic dominance, in the sense that whenever L dominates L′ in the FOSD order induced by the voter's ranking of the alternatives, the voter ranks L at least as high as L′. A second version of the same idea restricts attention to completions that are expected-utility orderings for some utility function consistent with the voter's ranking of the alternatives. Does either version bite harder than ordinary SD-strategy-proofness? My suspicion is that both collapse back into it — informally, if truth-telling fails to SD-dominate a misreport, then some admissible completion, and some admissible utility function, makes the misreport strictly better — but I cannot tell whether that suspicion is an actual argument (something like: FOSD domination is equivalent to agreement across all monotone completions, and across all increasing utility functions) or just hand-waving.
