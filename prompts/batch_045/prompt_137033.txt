I'm a novelist — two books out, a third one due to my editor in March, and a writing group that meets on Sundays — and I've managed to write myself into a philosophy problem I can't write my way out of, so I was hoping you'd write me an essay about it. Here's my setup. My narrator is a freelance literary translator named Odile who gets hired by a reclusive, dying novelist to "improve" a huge, messy manuscript. Every week she hands back a new version, he reads it and says yes, this is better, pays her, and sets her going again. Nine hundred rounds later the book is smooth and completely dead, and everyone including Odile agrees it is much worse than the first draft — but nobody can point to the round where it went wrong. Offered all her fees back to restore draft one, she says no. Half my writing group says she's being money-pumped and is straightforwardly irrational; the other half says this is one of those cases where "better than" just isn't transitive, so there's nothing for her to be irrational about.

The reason I'm asking here instead of settling it over wine is that my case is a bit different from the textbook versions I've half-glimpsed, and I can't tell whether that difference helps or just makes it a muddle. In the standard spectrum argument as I understand it, there are two fixed dimensions: each step trades a tiny loss along one axis for a slightly bigger gain along the other, and by the end you've drifted somewhere that loses massively on the first axis. Mine doesn't work like that. Each of Odile's rounds wins on a *fresh* axis — round 12 fixes an anachronism, round 13 gives a minor character a motive, round 14 kills a cliché — and the quality that made round two better than round one, the ragged live voice of the thing, is exactly what the final draft has none of left. The axes rotate rather than repeating. Is that actually a structurally different argument, or is it the same one dressed up? And there's a second wrinkle: she's being paid every round. Doesn't that mean the relation doing the work is "worth swapping, fee included," and not "better than" simpliciter — in which case my whole plot rests on an equivocation and Odile's stubbornness is about something else entirely? I really don't want the essay to wave either of those off.

So: can you write an essay on whether "better than" has to be transitive? I'd like the strongest version of the spectrum-style case that it doesn't, and then the strongest replies — the one where you deny that each tiny step is a genuine improvement, the one where you say the relation is vague or indeterminate rather than intransitive, the one where you let "at least as good as" stay transitive while "better than" fails, the one where you say the real phenomenon is incomparability or being "on a par" rather than intransitivity, and the one where the argument equivocates between better-in-some-respect and better-all-things-considered — and then I want you to actually pick a side and apply it to Odile: is she irrational, and if not, what exactly is she doing? I keep half-remembering names attached to this stuff, Temkin? someone named Rachels? Broome? and something about Parfit's repugnant conclusion, but I've definately got at least some of them stuck to the wrong positions, so only bring them in if you're sure who holds what, and please don't give me a reading list or citations, I won't follow them up. More important to me than the verdict itself: tell me how confident you are in the big claims you make, single out the one you're least sure of, and flag anywhere my own set-up is confused rather than just answering the question I asked.
