So I've somehow become the person on my town's memorial committee who is expected to have opinions, which is awkward because my actual job is crisis and change communications — about twelve years of it, mostly mid-size companies and two universities after various scandals, layoffs, and one genuinely botched merger. My working vocabulary is narrow and shamelessly instrumental: stakeholder maps and audience segmentation, who currently owns the frame, the three-beat narrative arc (what happened, what it means, what we are doing about it), symbolic repair gestures versus operational ones, message discipline, why some apologies land and others read as lawyered, and always the question of what a story is *for* right now. That's not a professional mask, it's how I think; I have no philosophy or history training past first year and I'd rather you not hand me reading lists or citations, I can't get to a university library and I wouldn't know what to do with them anyway.

The reason I'm asking: we're deciding what to do with a monument and an accompanying heritage plaque from the 1910s, and reading around a bit I realized the Confederacy case is one instance of a pattern — a cause that was thoroughly lost, and a defeat that later got made into a founding story. The comparisons people keep reaching for are Masada, Kosovo, and Dunkirk. And my toolkit seems to flatten all four into a single move: reframe a loss so that it pays out in the present. But they obviously aren't the same move, and the differences look like exactly the differences that should decide what goes on the plaque. Here's my own attempt at a distinction, which please feel free to tell me is garbage: call them moral victory (we lost, but the loss proves we were faithful to something that matters more than winning), postponement (we lost this round, the struggle isn't over, the defeat is a phase not an ending), and entitlement (the wound itself is now load-bearing — it licenses present claims to land, precedence, deference, or immunity from being argued with). As far as I can tell, Dunkirk is mostly the first with some second, Masada was the first and later got pressed into the third, Kosovo is the third propped up by the second, and the Lost Cause started as the first and slid into the third. My tentative rule is that a myth turns poisonous when the preserved wound is load-bearing for entitlements rather than only for self-respect.

What I want is a translation, not a lecture. Write me a memo in my vocabulary: restate each of those three operations as a piece of communications practice I'd recognise from work — the move, the audiences it plays to, the repair gesture it corresponds to, how you'd know if it was working. Then give me the part I actually care about, the residue report. For each operation, say what translated cleanly, what you had to bend or oversimplify to fit my terms, and what has no expression in them at all. My guess is the residue will cluster around two things: that my vocabulary has no way to distinguish self-respect from entitlement except by asking who objects, which is just audience research with a moral label stapled on, and that it treats truth and justification as purely downstream of whether a story works, so the question of whether the plaque should *say something false* isn't a question I have words for. Then give me your verdict on that residue: is it telling you my framework is broken for this job, or is it telling you my three-way distinction is fuzzier than it looks and that the historians are quietly relying on a normative claim they haven't earned? I want you to actually pick a side on that, not tell me it depends.
