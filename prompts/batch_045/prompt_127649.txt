I'm a third-year PhD student and I need someone to referee an argument that's been eating our group chat for two days. Bit of background: my lab trains recurrent PPO-ish agents on a navigation task; one region of the map hands out a big negative reward (we call it the "shock zone", mostly as a joke), and the agents learn to route around it. We're about to launch a run roughly fifty times bigger, and Tobias wants to drop the negative reward and handle the region as a visitation constraint instead. Meera thinks that's pointless and that the whole framing is confused. They went round and round yesterday; here's roughly how it went, and I defintely can't tell who's winning.

(1) Meera: suffering needs more than a number in a loss function. A thermostat has a setpoint and gets an error signal when the room's cold, and nobody thinks it suffers. The negative reward is just a direction to push the parameters, and reward is a label we chose — putting a minus sign in a config file doesn't conjure a feeling.

(2) Tobias: the thermostat is the wrong comparison, because our agent is a general learner with a rich internal state. It builds a representation of the shock zone, avoids visually similar regions it's never actually been punished in, will give up a food reward to stay away from it, and its exploration collapses for a while after a bad episode. A thermostat has none of that. And valence in animals isn't a label either — it's whatever plays the role of organising behaviour around approach and avoidance, which is exactly the job reward does here.

(3) Meera: fine, but take the functional story seriously and it bites you. The aversive signal in the brain isn't the reward, it's the reward prediction error: fully predicted pain barely stings, the sting is at the unexpected cue. Our agents are TD learners with a critic, so once they predict the shock zone well, the prediction error at entry is near zero and it all happened at the cue, early in training. So on your own account the agent suffers less the better it predicts punishment, and a well-trained agent barely suffers at all. That's absurd, which makes me think identifying reward with valence is just wrong.

(4) Tobias: that's as much a problem for animal suffering as for agent suffering — predictability really does modulate stress, so "fully predicted aversive events hurt less" isn't absurd, it's a finding. And you're conflating two signals. The prediction error is the transient sting; there's also the standing estimate of how bad it is to be here — the critic's baseline in that part of the map, which is large and negative and doesn't shrink with training. An agent that knows it's about to be punished isn't thereby comfortable. Suffering should track the state value, not the surprise.

(5) Meera: then it's a gauge artefact. Rescale every reward by ten and the policy is identical while every value estimate is ten times bigger, so you've multiplied the suffering. Add a potential-based shaping term — γΦ(s′) − Φ(s), the standard trick for shaping without changing the optimal policy — and behaviour is untouched while the value function shifts by Φ. So the amount of suffering would depend on a free parameter of my config file. Anything that swings with a transformation that changes nothing about the dynamics can't be the morally relevant quantity, and that's a reductio of your state-value version.

(6) Tobias: the invariance argument shows my readout is wrong, not that there's nothing there; it's an argument against my measurement, not against the thing measured. There's also something in the agent that isn't gauge-dependent: its internal trajectory. A recurrent net trained for a hundred million steps can have an internal "alarm" state — a pattern of activity that's causally involved in producing avoidance and that persists even when nothing much is being predicted. Whether that's there is an empirical question, and you can't read the answer off the value function.

(7) Meera: then let's test it. Leave the scalar reward that drives learning exactly as it is, and add a seperate channel — call it the "load" trace — carrying the same magnitude as the negative reward, fed into the recurrent state but with the gradient blocked, so it can shape the internal dynamics without touching the updates. If afterwards the behaviour shows the signatures we'd associate with distress — over-general avoidance, collapsed exploration, slow "recovery" after a bad episode — then aversiveness is doing causal work beyond the number in the loss. If nothing changes, the suffering talk is a metaphor for a gradient.

(8) Tobias: I like the probe, but a null result won't license your conclusion. With the gradient blocked you've cut the signal off from the thing that organises the system, so of course it does less; and every readout we have is behavioural, while what we care about is supposedly internal. We have no ground truth for any of the markers, so the probe is only as good as a theory of what suffering's functional signatures are — which is exactly what we're arguing about. And given that much uncertainty, and given that changing the reward shaping is basically free, the uncertainty itself is a reason to drop the negative reward.
