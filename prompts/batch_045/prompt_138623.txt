I've been reading the rationalist and EA blogs for about fifteen years, and next month I'm running a two-hour seminar for a reading group of a dozen or so software engineers and operations researchers — all fluent in expected-value reasoning, error bars, and the worry about fanaticism, none with any philosophy training. The topic is consequentialist cluelessness, and what I want from you is a plan for teaching it, not an explanation of it.

The obstacle is that this audience will hear the claim that we can't know our actions' downstream effects as ordinary imprecision that more data would fix, and at least one of them will say that the expected value of an action is perfectly well-defined even when we can't compute it, so nothing of philosophical interest is going on. Relatedly, I've been toying with a framing and want your verdict on whether to build the session around it: I call it sign fragility — the phenomenon where individually defensible bookkeeping choices about a causal pathway, such as which effects count as the same effect, where you stop counting, or whose counterfactual baseline you use, flip the sign of the estimated net effect, so that the number is not so much noisy as an artifact of how the accounting was done. Is that the right thing to center, or does centering it make cluelessness look like a defect in the model rather than in the world?

Give me the order the components have to be introduced in and what depends on what; the single case that should carry the session, including what it must have — I assume a large, well-measured near-term effect plus a long unmeasurable tail — and what it must lack, which I assume includes existential stakes, so the discussion doesn't get hijacked by fanaticism; the misconceptions in the order this group will actually raise them and the point in the sequence where each has to be defused; whether the "what do you do about it" responses belong before or after that defusing, given that offering an escape hatch too early may stop them taking the problem seriously; one question I can put to them at the end that would show the idea had landed rather than merely unsettled them; and what I should explicitly decline to settle at this level.
