Hey — I'm a PhD student in philosophy of physics (I mostly work on symmetry and gauge stuff, but I sit in on the time reading group), and I need someone to referee an argument I watched happen over dinner after a workshop talk and honestly cant call. It was between Marek, who thinks the arrow of time has to be anchored in something global, and Dana, who thinks it's nothing over and above a local entropic gradient. I'm not going to tell you which one I'm sympathetic to because I keep flipping, and I don't need reading suggestions — I've read the usual stuff, I want the argument picked apart. Here's roughly how it went.

1. Marek opened with this: entropy goes down locally all the time — inside your fridge, in a growing crystal, in you. If the arrow just were the direction of increasing entropy, then inside the fridge the future would be the direction of lower entropy and you'd be remembering the fridge's future. Nobody talks that way. So the arrow has to be fixed by something bigger: the gradient of the largest isolated system, and ultimately by whatever makes the early universe special, not by whatever the nearest box of gas is doing.

2. Dana said she's fine with 'the largest relevant isolated system' — but that that concession cuts against him. It makes the arrow relative to a system, not global. And she gave a case: picture a cosmos with a single low-entropy waist in the middle of some global time coordinate, with entropy rising as you move away from the waist in both temporal directions, and the two sides causally walled off from each other. Observers on each side call opposite directions 'the future' and each is right from where they stand. So the arrow is perspectival, like 'here', or like simultaneity in relativity. There's no further fact about which side got it right.

3. Marek said the case hasn't paid its own way: to even draw the picture you need a single time coordinate with respect to which the two arrows are opposed. If the arrow is perspectival all the way down, there is no view from outside the cosmos from which to say the arrows point oppositely. So either the case is incoherent and shouldn't move anyone, or it's smuggling in a direction that isn't entropic.

4. Dana said that's a limit of our notation, not of the view. A four-dimensional description of the whole block is direction-neutral: lay out the block, note that entropy rises as you move away from the waist in both directions, done. The block has no arrow; only the inhabitants do.

5. Marek conceded the block point, then said his real argument is different and involves cracking the wall. Open a narrow, slow channel between the two sides, and push a small record-bearing object through it — a hard drive, a photograph, whatever. Records are asymmetric, and the standard story about why is statistical-mechanical: a record at one time carries information about an earlier state. Now, the object formed on side A records A's past. But A's past is side B's future. So B gets handed an object whose internal structure encodes, in its own time, events that haven't happened yet. His claim: the object carries its own arrow with it, stamped in by the sector it formed in. So what fixes the arrow for a subsystem is its history of formation, not the gradient it's currently sitting in — and that's a feature of the world that 'local entropy gradient' doesn't capture.

6. Dana said she can live with all of that and it still doesn't get him a global arrow. The object is just an exotic boundary condition: a low-entropy, record-bearing intruder that B's own processes could never produce. Its correlations don't stop being correlations, they're merely anti-thermodynamic relative to B's gradient, and B's physicists will describe it as a fluke import rather than a message from the future. Nothing there requires a fact about which side really has the future.

7. Marek then pushed the observer version, which is where I got lost. Take an agent whose memory-writing and deliberating machinery was built under A's gradient, and bring them into B gently enough that they survive. Which way do they experience time? If it's their own way, then you've conceded that the arrow for a system is settled by its formation history, and you now owe a story about an observer running thermodynamically backwards relative to their entire surroundings — which he says is either incoherent or a much bigger deal than she's admitting. If it's B's way, because the ambient gradient now decides, then the gradient theorist owes an account of how the same physical system flips the direction in which it remembers and anticipates, and he doesn't see one.

Two loose ends nobody picked up. First, someone at the table said Dana's two-sided cosmos might not be a case of two arrows at all — 'away from the waist' could be one arrow pointing outward in both directions, so that a real case of two local arrows would need two independent low-entropy bottlenecks. Second, Marek muttered that a transported object and a B-observer would cross at only a single instant, so 'what B sees' might not even be well defined; he didn't develop it.

What I want: go through the seven moves and tell me who's right about what at each one, rating each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and flag any place where someone concedes something that's actually dialectically free for them. Then say who's more right overall and why — please don't just split the difference — and say what you think both of them are missing, whether that's a distinction the argument needs or a possibility neither considered. And if you have a view on either loose end, I'd like that too.
