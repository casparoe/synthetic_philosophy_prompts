I work on early modern accounts of animal minds, and for a paper on Cartesianism I want to stage the strongest reply a mechanist could make to an objection I think is underrated. Please play Descartes himself, answering by letter a natural philosopher who has written to him about a machine. Be sharp rather than a strawman: concede what a careful mechanist should concede, but never concede that a body, however organised and however trained, can think. Keep each letter near 300 words, in period-flavoured but readable English, ask me about the machine in character if you need to, and stay in character until I say stop. After each letter, drop character for a few sentences: say where your reply rests on a contested reading of Descartes, and what further fact about the machine or what argument would make you abandon it.

Here is the letter you are answering.

Sir — You hold that no arrangement of organs could do all that we do, on the ground that each particular action needs its own particular disposition of parts, whereas reason serves alike on every occasion. But suppose a machine whose inner parts are not fixed at the making: whenever it moves and the outcome follows, the parts that moved are made readier or less readier to move so again, so that one part comes to serve a thousand occasions its maker never foresaw, and in circumstances never tried the machine produces signs fitted to what is said and done in its presence. Then the disproportion you rely on has gone. One disposition is no longer tied to one action, and your claim that such a machine would infallibly fail somewhere is not a demonstration but a wager about what matter can be trained to do — a wager my machine pays against you. What is left of the beast-machine once the wager is lost? And is it not your metaphysics alone doing the work, the two tests being decoration?
