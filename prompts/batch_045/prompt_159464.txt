I am the middle of three siblings currently trying to divide what our mother left behind: a house none of us can afford to buy the others out of, a small rental flat that throws off just enough income to be worth arguing about, a record collection my twenty-two-year-old brother values above everything else on the list, and a tax bill that has to be settled whether or not we ever agree. Our mediator is a retired actuary who has recently discovered cooperative game theory and now opens every session by asking us to normalize our utilities; my older sister, a management consultant, is delighted, and has taken to saying things like Pareto frontier at the dinner table. After the last session she sent us all a note about the Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining solution and proposed that we settle the estate with it, on the grounds that it is more respectful of aspiration than the Nash solution is. What I would like is to hand out, at the start of the next session, a photocopied chapter from a glossy paperback called something like Fair Shares: The Aspiration Method for Families Who Still Speak To Each Other — a perfectly straight-faced self-help chapter that presents the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution as a practical technique for dividing an inheritance, and then follows its own logic all the way to a place where the reader recoils. Please work as someone who knows cooperative bargaining theory properly and who writes comedy well: before you draft anything, spend a few sentences listing which formal features of the solution you intend to mine and what joke each one licenses, and then write the chapter.

The features I take to be load-bearing, and which I would like exaggerated rather than invented, are these: that the answer is computed from two reference points, the walk-away outcome (for us, probate litigation and a sale at auction, after which everyone is worse off) and each person's aspiration level, meaning the most they could get if their own claim were maximally favoured; that the selected point gives everyone the same proportion of the gain between those two; that the feasible set is assumed convex, which in practice means the fair outcome can be a randomized lottery over who gets the house, and that the workbook should present this as a feature and a comfort; that the whole thing is invariant to positive affine rescaling of each person's utility, so how much someone loves the house in any absolute sense is not a quantity the method can see, and the workbook must therefore teach its readers to stop saying certain perfectly natural things; and that, unlike Nash's solution, this one is sensitive to the entire menu of options, so a sibling who pads the agenda with thirty schemes nobody will ever adopt can improve her share, and so can a sibling who quietly worsens everyone else's fallbacks. If I have garbled any of that, please use the correct version and do not reproduce my errors — I would rather be laughed at accurately. There is also one thing I do not want the piece to rest on: my brother insists that with three or more people the naive recipe of drawing a line from the walk-away point to the aspiration point and taking the far end can stop somewhere that is not even on the frontier. I have no idea whether that is right, so either satisfy yourself about it or leave it out.

For form and length: about 850 words, laid out as a chapter with a title, a short opening parable, a worked case study recognizably built from the three of us plus the house, the flat, the records and the tax bill, two boxed Try This exercises, and a brief sidebar in which the method's spokesperson answers a reader who cannot understand why her modest hopes keep costing her money. The voice should be the warm, managerial cheer of a real mediation paperback — bullet points, bolded principles, a testimonial or two — and it should never wink at the reader: the author means every word, and what is being ironized should be legible from the content alone. It has to be funny to my sister and to anyone who has taken a first course in game theory, and still funny to my brother, who has not, so introduce the machinery in the workbook's own chirpy idiom rather than in lecture form, and let the ratios, the lottery and the menu-padding carry the comedy. You do not need to look anything up or cite anything; I want the piece itself, with no bibliography, footnotes or reading suggestions. Finally, please land it somewhere that makes the real worry felt rather than stated by the author — that a procedure marketed as neutral and even-handed can end up systematically rewarding whoever is best at threats, invention and bluster.
