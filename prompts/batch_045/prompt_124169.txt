OK here's the setup: fair coin, tossed until it lands heads. If the first heads is on toss n you get $(-1)^{n+1}·2^n/n — so +2, -2, +8/3, -4, +32/5, ... — and the probabilities halve each time, so summing in that order gives 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ... = ln 2 ≈ 0.69. Sum it in other orders and you get anything you want, including diverging to +∞ or -∞.

The proposition I want a number on: there's no determinate fact about what this game is worth. The payoff function and the probabilities don't fix a value; you need extra structure the setup doesn't give you — a summation order, or a different decision rule entirely. My credence is around 0.65. My friend's is 0.2: she says the value just is ln 2, because the game hands you a canonical order (sum over states in order of how many tosses it takes, i.e. take the limit of the finite truncations), and the reorderings that break it are illegitamate. I tried a wrinkle of my own and it half-worked against me: if you only allow permutations that move each term by a bounded number of places, the limit provably doesn't change — the partial sums differ by only a fixed number of terms, each going to zero — so even swapping every adjacent pair leaves you at ln 2. Her claim may not need the order to be canonical at all, just the rearrangements to be tame; the paradox needs wild, unbounded shuffles. Is that right, and does it actually help her?
