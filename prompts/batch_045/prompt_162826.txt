I'm in the third year of a political theory PhD and I finally have to decide what my one remaining free paper slot goes on. The constraints: roughly one semester, decent but not great formal training (I can follow an Arrow-style proof if I go slowly, but I definitely don't want to spend four months trying to prove a new impossibility theorem), and a slightly odd second audience — I do some paid consulting on the side for a team building shared household assistant software, the kind of thing where one device serves three or four people and has to collapse their preferences into a single action. So the end is twofold: one paper a social choice or political philosophy journal would take, and something that actually says something usable to engineers deciding who gets an override over what.

The area I keep circling back to is the liberal paradox: Sen's 1970 result that you can't have unrestricted domain, Pareto, and a minimal liberalism condition (everyone gets to be decisive over at least one pair of outcomes differing only in their own personal sphere — the old example being two people and one copy of a scandalous novel, where the prude would rather read it himself than let the lewd enjoy it) all at once. There are a few familiar-looking escape hatches: restricting the domain so people don't get to have meddlesome preferences about others' spheres, reformulating rights over game forms or strategy spaces rather than over outcomes, weakening or conditioning Pareto, or denying that rights were ever the sort of thing a social choice function could represent. But I also have two wrinkles of my own that I can't tell are real questions or just relabellings. First, suppose we separate outcome-liberalism (I'm decisive over which outcome in my sphere happens) from rule-liberalism (I'm decisive over whether a standing rule governing my sphere is adopted — a constitutional-level rather than outcome-level veto). Does the paradox survive at the rule level, and does it matter that rules get applied repeatedly rather than once? Second, suppose the personal sphere isn't a consumption act at all but delegated authority: what an agent does on your behalf (which invitations your calendar accepts, whether your data is used for a household model's next update), where the offended third party is another user of the same system. That seems structurally different from reading a book, but I can't say why yet, and I suspect it may just be the two-person case with extra steps.

So: for someone with my time, tools, and two audiences, which two or three lines of work here are actually worth the semester, and in what order? I care much more about the defence than the list. And please flag which of the alleged implications of the paradox are overrated or just don't follow: I keep hearing versions of "it shows rights and efficiency are in tension" or "it shows liberal rights are incoherent", and I want to know which of those really carry weight and which collapse once you look at what the theorem actually assumes. Don't give me a literature survey or a reading list; I want a defended agenda. Oh, and please write the answer in British English.
