I am writing a novel in which the central character is a composer who works almost exclusively by producing sets of variations, and in which her younger rival accuses her of not creating anything at all: on his view, once a theme is fixed, rotating the available parameters is a kind of systematic doodling that merely automates a search, and whatever is genuinely creative must already have happened in the choice of the theme and of the constraints under which it is varied. I want to stage that argument accurately, so I need a clear account of Hofstadter's knob-twiddling model of creativity.

Three things in particular. First, what holds a theme together across variation: what makes a given change the turning of a knob rather than the substitution of a different theme, and what prevents the underlying harmonic or structural skeleton from being counted as just one more knob? Second, whether the model can distinguish variations that deepen or expose the theme from variations that only decorate it, and whether it has room for the discovery of a new dimension of variation — a new knob — as opposed to a new setting of an old one. Third, whether on this picture a larger space of knobs makes creation easier, or whether it simply relocates the difficulty into judging which settings are worth keeping; and whether my rival character is right that the model pushes the creative act back into the prior choice of theme and constraint.
