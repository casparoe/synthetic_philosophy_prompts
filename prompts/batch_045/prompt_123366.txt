I work in computational statistics — heavy-tailed loss distributions, Monte Carlo error bounds, trimmed and winsorized functionals, the usual measure-theoretic graduate background. I have been reading about the Pasadena game and the claim that it is a harder problem for expected-utility theory than the St. Petersburg game, and I would like that whole debate restated in the vocabulary I actually think in.

The game as I have encountered it: a fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads; if that happens on the nth toss the payoff is (-1)^(n-1) · 2^n / n. The payoff-probability products are therefore the terms of the alternating harmonic series: summed in the order given by the toss-count they converge to ln 2, but the absolute values sum to the harmonic series, so the series is only conditionally convergent and, by the rearrangement theorem, can be summed to any real value at all, or made to diverge. The philosophical claim I want translated is stronger than "the integral does not exist": it is that this particular game has no determinate value, in a way the St. Petersburg game does not (there the value is at least determinately infinite), that this is a genuine failure of the theory rather than merely an inconvenient idealization, and that the failure spreads from the absolute verdict to comparative ones — so that even the question whether playing is better than a guaranteed dollar is supposed to have no stable answer.

Before translating, please check three computations of mine, because what I want to know is whether they change the shape of the debate:

1. Symmetric truncation. E[X · 1_{|X| ≤ c}] is a functional of the distribution alone, requiring no ordering; and as c → ∞ it converges to ln 2, because the cutoff on |X| is equivalent, up to the slowly varying correction, to cutting the alternating harmonic sum at an index that grows like log c. So there is a distributionally definable, rearrangement-free quantity here that is finite and equals ln 2.

2. Asymmetric truncation. If the positive and negative cutoffs go to infinity at different rates, with the positive cutoff growing like the α-th power of the negative one, I get a limit of ln 2 + (1/2)ln α rather than ln 2. If that is right, then the truncation route does not save us from a free parameter; it only moves the arbitrariness from the ordering of a series to the relative growth rate of two cutoffs, and symmetry (α = 1) is a choice, not a theorem.

3. Sampling behavior. The tail of |X| is, up to constants, of order 1/(t log t), so n·P(|X| > n) → 0 and the truncated second moment over |X| ≤ n is o(n²); the standard weak law for triangular arrays then gives that the sample mean of n independent plays converges in probability to ln 2 — a quantity that is estimable and that a simulation would appear to confirm, even though the expectation does not exist. But the rate is on the order of 1/log n, which is absurdly slow, and since the sum of P(|X| > n) diverges, Borel–Cantelli suggests the running average is kicked by jumps of order one infinitely often and so converges in probability but not almost surely — the ensemble settles down, the single sequential agent never does.

Assuming those check out (tell me plainly where they do not), here is the actual request. Restate, as faithfully as you can, both sides of the dispute about whether the Pasadena game has a value — the "no determinate value, decision theory goes silent" side and whatever the best available rebuttals are — in my vocabulary: estimand versus estimator, integrability and failure of L¹, the Lebesgue integral as opposed to improper or principal-value limits, symmetric truncation as a regularization, regular variation, convergence in probability versus almost surely, robust functionals that are well defined on distribution classes where the mean is not, identifiability, and what it means for a statistical functional to be well posed. Do not quietly substitute my framework's assumptions for the philosophers' claims and call it a translation; where you have to make a choice they do not face, say so.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the debate map cleanly onto distinctions I already have? Which survive only after being reformulated into something weaker or different than what was claimed? And which parts have no expression in my vocabulary at all — in particular, is there anything left over once I have said "the mean does not exist, but this other distributional functional does, and it is consistently estimable in the ensemble though not along a single path"? Finally, and this is where I want you to commit rather than survey: does the residue indict my framework — for instance because statistics has no way to express the value of a one-shot option as opposed to the long-run behavior of a repeatable experiment — or does it indict the original claim, because "has no value" turns out to conflate several different failures that my vocabulary separates? Take a definite position on whether the Pasadena game has a value and on what, if anything, it is rational to pay to play it, give your reasons, and tell me how confident you are and what would move you. Flag explicitly any step where you are guessing, filling in a constant, or asserting a theorem you cannot reconstruct.
