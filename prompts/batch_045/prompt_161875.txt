So my PhD advisor Miriam is retiring — well, not retiring exactly: her last grad student defended in May, the lab is being folded into someone else's group, and a dozen of her former students are throwing her a dinner next Friday. I've been asked to give the toast. Four minutes, roughly six hundred words.

Miriam spent her whole career on the timing question in perception. You probably know the case she cared about: a blue dot is flashed near the top of a screen, then a red dot near the bottom, fast enough that you see one dot travel between them — and people report the color flipping abruptly halfway along the path, before the second dot has even been presented. Two stories get told about that. One says you had the un-flipped experience first and memory quietly rewrote it afterwards. The other says the system waited to see what was coming and then wrote the finished version straight into the record, dated earlier than it was actually produced. The usual labels for them are Orwellian and Stalinesque. Miriam's line, for thirty years, was that the fight between those two stories is empty: no experiment could separate them, so there is nothing there to be right about.

The closest she ever came to budging was a protocol she ran years ago that people in the field still argue about. Subjects kept up a running spoken commentary while watching the display, and were then asked again an hour later what they'd seen. The idea was to catch the earlier, unrevised draft in the live report. The reports never diverged. Her critics said that was because reporting on the percept while you're having it changes the percept, so the protocol destroys the thing it was built to measure — you cannot narrate the draft without editing it. She thought that objection proved her point, not theirs. I'm telling you this because I want the toast to use it: a toast is a diary protocol. Saying the feeling out loud changes the feeling. That has to be in there somewhere, as one line the lab groans at and her sister thinks is just a sweet thing to say about a party.

The room: about half are her ex-students and postdocs, plus two people who spent years arguing with her in print, one of whom now says the first story is testable after all and will wince if the toast implies there's no fact of the matter. The other half are family — her sister, who thinks she spent thirty years proving that people see a dot; her two teenage kids; her brother-in-law, who is a contractor and will assume any silence is a technical problem. So: no explaining the illusion, no lecture, and nothing that only lands if you know the literature.

Here's the constraint that matters to me. I dont want the toast to pull either of the two moves on Miriam's own record. No we always knew you were right — there were years when half the lab thought her view on this was empty-headed and said so to her face, and I'm not letting a nice evening retroactively smooth that into a straight line. But also no backdating the room's affection: I dont want to claim a warmth that is being manufactured at the dinner and then dated back across the whole career, as if it had been there all along. Except — and this is the part I keep tying myself in knots over — I also don't want the toast to sound like I think there is a clean fact about when a feeling begins, because that's precisely the assumption she spent her life denying. So the scruple has to be real without pretending it tracks anything, and she should get the last word on it. She is completely fine with being teased; she hates being called brave; she will forgive a bad joke and not a false one.

Write the toast. Then, separately, a short note of a couple hundred words on where you've taken liberties: which reading of the two stories the toast quietly commits itself to, whether the scruple I gave you is even coherent if she's right that the distinction is empty, and what would change your mind — what argument would convince you the whole distinction collapses and that I should just get up and say we've always loved you without any of this hedging.
