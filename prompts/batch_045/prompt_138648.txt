You are a philosopher of mind who also works on the foundations of machine-learning interpretability. Think the problem through carefully: before settling, sketch two or three candidate scenarios and say in a line each why you discarded them.

Construct a single-paragraph, realistic case in which two criteria for attributing a belief to an AI system come apart in the direction that is normally thought hard to arrange.

Setting constraints: a present-day machine-learning system in an ordinary institutional setting — a hospital, a benefits office, a warehouse, a bank's fraud desk. No science fiction, no superintelligence, no perfect predictors, no stipulations about consciousness in either direction, and no internals that could not plausibly belong to a system built today.

The two criteria:

(A) The stance criterion, Dennett-style: we are warranted in attributing to a system the belief that p only if predicting and explaining its behaviour by way of that attribution beats the best non-intentional (design- or physical-stance) description of the same system that is actually available to a human with ordinary time, attention, and access.

(B) The etiological criterion: a system has a state that represents that p iff the state was produced by a learning process whose success-condition was to make it covary with whether p, the state's downstream influence on behaviour is explained by that covariance, and the state can therefore misrepresent p.

Requirements: (A) must deliver no attribution, (B) must deliver a genuine representation of p, and both verdicts must be generated by the criteria rather than stipulated. The refusal in (A) may not come from the system being too simple, too alien, unreliable at its task, or closed to inspection: it must come from a mechanistic description of this very system being available and strictly better than the intentional one for predicting the behaviour at issue, by a margin that matters in that setting. The verdict in (B) must hold on the criterion's own terms.

Then: give each verdict with the reasoning that produces it; isolate the single structural feature doing the work; show what happens to both verdicts if that feature is removed while the system's task accuracy is held fixed; and give the strongest reply open to a defender of whichever view comes out worse.

The case is for an argument I am making — that successful mechanistic interpretability could remove the warrant for intentional talk about AI systems without showing that those systems have no content — so close with one sentence on whether the disagreement your case exposes is substantive or merely verbal.
