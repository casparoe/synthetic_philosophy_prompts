Hey — I'm a TA for the discussion sections of a decision theory / behavioral econ class (cross-listed, mostly sophomores, 16 people in my section, 50 minutes this Thursday). We just covered escalation of commitment and I'm supposed to run something that makes it land. The problem is my professor won't let me just run the standard dollar auction. His objection is that it's a rigged trap: with a fixed prize that everyone can see and everyone knowing the rules, the smart move is to never bid in the first place, so either nobody bids and the demo dies, or one person bids as a joke to be funny and then we've only shown that that one guy is confused. He says students walk away feeling the experimenter tricked them, not that escalation is something real people actually do. I think he's probably right but I don't know how to fix it.

What I want them to feel, in their gut and not from me saying it, is that every single step can look locally fine — each extra bid really does protect the bids you've already made — and you still end up having burned way more than the thing is worth. And ideally they should also get that you can't tell from the outside whether an escalation was stupid without knowing what the person knew, because in real cases some of the sunk spending does buy you something.

Two things I was kicking around and want you to build into the design (or tell me why they're wrong):

1. Make the prize a private value. Each pair draws an envelope with a hidden number of points on it, only they know it, and the winner gets whatever's in their own envelope. My hope is that this makes bidding genuinely rational up to a point instead of a pure trap, so escalation comes from uncertainty rather than from confusion. Does that actually work, or does it turn into something else entirely?
2. Run a second version with half the room at the same time where the prize grows as they spend — say the pot picks up half of everything both players have put in, so the thing you're fighting over gets bigger the longer you fight. That's meant to mirror the cases where the analogy to a "sunk cost" is murky: finishing a degree you've half-paid for, a startup that's already burned its seed round, staying in a war. Then we compare total points burned across the two versions, and I want a prediction ahead of time about which one blows up more and why, so if the result surprises me that's informative too.

No lecture before the game — they shouldn't hear the words "sunk cost" or "escalation" from me until after. Pairs, 16 people, and the whole thing including debrief has to fit in 50 minutes with a bit of slack.
