I'm putting together a two-page proposal for a small summer research fellowship in my department (four months, and the committee always dings people for projects that sound like they'd take a decade, so I need it to look genuinely finishable). Here's the half-formed idea I want turned into one. There's a fairly standard distinction floating around between epistemic bubbles and echo chambers: a bubble is the ordinary, unplanned situation where your friends and your feed just happen to never expose you to the other side; it's fragile, because once someone actually shows you the missing material it tends to burst. A chamber is supposed to be the worse thing, because it doesn't merely omit outside voices, it actively discredits them in advance — members are taught to distrust the very people who would correct them, which is why chambers are self-sealing and why leaving one usually has to happen for non-evidential reasons like falling out with a friend. My roommate runs media-literacy workshops for high schoolers, and her whole curriculum is essentially teaching blanket suspicion toward every source: ask who benefits from you believing this, assume motives, check who funds it, nobody gets a free pass. It hit me the other night that she is deploying what looks like exactly the mechanism that's supposed to make chambers bad — systematic, pre-emptive discrediting of sources — except she aims it symmetrically at everyone, there's no doctrine, and there's no in-group she's protecting. And I genuinely can't tell whether the standard account predicts that she's doing something chamber-like and epistemically dangerous, or predicts that she's fine. The reason I can't tell is that I don't know which feature is doing the work: is it the discrediting mechanism itself, or the asymmetry between how insiders and outsiders get treated?

So I want a proposal, not an essay. Give me a precise statement of the question (I'd phrase it as something like: is the wrong-making feature of an echo chamber the discrediting of outside sources, or the asymmetric application of discrediting? — but fix my phrasing), a short section on why the existing positions leave it open rather than settling it by definition, and then the method, which I think has to be the construction of test cases: a group that discredits its own leaders and texts as aggressively as it discredits outsiders, a community with a full-blown discrediting mechanism but no shared doctrine at all, a chamber whose members are all in on it and know it's a chamber, maybe one where the asymmetry is inverted and insiders are trusted less than outsiders. Say what the two or three possible conclusions are and what considerations would decide between them, and — this is the part I think the committee will actually read — an honest statement of what would count as the project failing: if all the test cases quietly collapse back into plain bubbles, if the asymmetry/discrediting split turns out not to track anything, if the whole thing is really an empirical question about what makes people update rather than a conceptual one, or if the answer, once stated, has no payoff for anyone deciding how to design a media-literacy curriculum. No bibliography and no literature review by name, please — I'll fill in the citations myself, so refer to positions by describing them. And please build in a short bit on how I'd keep it inside four months, since my last two proposals got rejected on scope and I'd rather admit the risks up front.
