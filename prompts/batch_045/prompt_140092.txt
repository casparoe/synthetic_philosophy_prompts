I'm finishing a dissertation in Buddhist philosophy on the logical form of the fourfold negation (catuṣkoṭi) in Indian Madhyamaka, and I keep running into one sentence that does an enormous amount of dialectical work without ever being cashed out: "Nāgārjuna only refutes; he never asserts."

Here is where it bit me. At a conference panel last spring I asked whether the fourfold that opens the Root Verses — the denial that anything arises from itself, from another, from both, or from no cause — should be formalised as a logic with four truth-values, with the last lemma taken as a genuine additional option (there is a reading on which the fourfold is treated as genuinely conjunctive and the logic has to be made to tolerate contradiction), or instead as four different ways of denying one and the same thesis. A senior scholar replied that the question is ill-posed, because Nāgārjuna only refutes and never asserts: there is, on his view, no thesis there for me to be asking about the logical form of. The same line is often backed by appeal to a verse in which Nāgārjuna is made to disclaim having any thesis of his own, so that there is nothing for an opponent to attack.

I think something in the neighbourhood is right. But the sentence as I heard it seems to me to be doing at least five different jobs at once, and I cannot tell which of them is supposed to make my question unaskable — or whether any of them really does.

What I want is a disambiguation with verdicts. Please set out, as a list, the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing, ordered roughly from the trivial to the radical. For each one say what exactly it claims, whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false or self-undermining, and — this is the part I care about — whether it actually gets the speaker his conclusion that my question about logical form is ill-posed. The readings I can see crowding together include at least these, but please add any I have missed: a purely dialectical one, on which the arguments simply proceed from premises the opponent already grants and nothing is asserted on one's own account; a semantic one, on which the negations are non-implicational, so that denying a claim carries no commitment to any positive replacement; a doxastic one, about what Nāgārjuna believed or taught; a strong ontological one, on which nothing could be asserted truly because no statement could be made true by how things are intrinsically; a quietist one, on which the utterances are instruments for stilling conceptual proliferation and are not in the truth-evaluable business at all; and the self-applied version, on which the disclaimer covers itself, so that the slogan is not being asserted either — which I would like to know is coherent.

I should also declare my own stake, because I would like it stress-tested rather than politely accommodated. In my own work I have been defending what I call the empty-negation reading: the fourfold, I argue, does not deny four candidate claims but exhibits the failure of negation itself to generate a determinate complement when the subject term lacks intrinsic nature — so that the negation of a negation does not return the original claim, no accumulation of lemmas can restore exhaustiveness, and the fourth lemma is not a conjunction of two denials but a marker that the complement of the complement is itself indeterminate. On which, if any, of your readings does the slogan entail this reading, exclude it, or sit badly with it? And does my proposal collapse into the doctrine that emptiness is itself empty — in which case I need to know whether it leaves the slogan self-undermining or leaves it untouched?

Please commit to a verdict rather than leaving me the menu: tell me which single reading is most plausibly the one being invoked in a case like mine, whether on that reading my question really was ill-posed or was merely unwelcome, and which reading you think is actually the strongest, with your reasons. Flag the points where your verdict depends on a contested interpretive or logical assumption rather than on anything the sources settle. I have the bibliography and can find the commentaries myself, so please spend no part of the answer on recommendations for further reading.
