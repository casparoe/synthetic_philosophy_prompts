I have just come away from a long argument with a friend about a proposal to abolish our university's core-curriculum distribution requirements, the rules that oblige every student to take a fixed number of credits in areas such as literature, history, and the natural sciences regardless of their interests or plans. I argued that I cannot see what good the requirements now do: students often forget the material, the courses are uneven, and the requirements largely operate as a bureaucratic hurdle. My friend, who has been reading about Burkean conservatism, invoked Chesterton's fence against me. She said that the reformer's principle is do not tear down a fence until you know why it was put up, and she concluded that I am therefore not entitled to abolish the requirements until I can state exactly why the people who originally instituted them put them there; if I cannot supply that explanation, she claimed, the requirements must stay. She seemed to regard this as decisive.

I am not satisfied. It seems to me that the slogan can be making many different claims, and that the strong work she wanted it to do depends on a specific and contentious reading rather than on something self-evident. Could you lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses that this Chesterton's-fence slogan could be expressing? For each reading, I would like you to say whether it is a mere platitude, a substantive and defensible principle, or straightforwardly false; whether it is genuinely Burkean in spirit or only superficially so; and, crucially, whether it would actually support my friend's conclusion that I must identify the original rationale for the requirements before I am permitted to abolish them, and whether it would also forbid reforms that are staged, reversible, or experimental.

In laying out the menu, please separate readings that concern the fence's original purpose from readings that concern its present effects, and include some of the less obvious possibilities rather than only the standard ones. For instance: that the fence was erected for a reason that has since lapsed, but now happens to be load-bearing for some other valuable equilibrium; that the fence is a coordination device or commitment device whose value lies partly in its resistance to easy revision; that the mere persistence of the practice is weak evidence of a hidden function because it has survived a kind of social trial and error; that the fence may have been erected for a bad or bigoted reason and yet still be worth keeping for unrelated reasons; that the slogan is only an epistemic injunction to investigate before acting; and that it is a procedural rule about who bears the burden of proof rather than a substantive ban on reform. I also want to know which of these readings the people who repeat the slogan most likely have in mind, and which reading, if any, would convict me of mere status quo bias if I reject it. My aim is to bring a clear disambiguation back to my friend rather than simply accuse her of begging the question.
