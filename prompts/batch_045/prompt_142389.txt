My friend Sam has been a judge at the same annual imitation-game competition for years, and he's built up a track record. When I say "counts" below I mean he's willing to say there's somebody home, not just that the thing fooled somebody.

His rulings so far:

1. A sixties-style scripted chatterbot that fooled a handful of casual typists for about five minutes each: doesn't count.
2. A current large language model, two hours of free-form typed conversation with a judge who was actively trying to catch it out: counts.
3. The same model, same judge, except the judge was first handed a dossier of its tics and failure modes and blew its cover in twenty minutes: still counts. He said that isn't a fair fight, you could unmask a person that way too.
4. A system built out of one dead woman's letters and diaries, which held up for an hour against her own brother: doesn't count. He called it a puppet in someone else's skin — the behaviour is borrowed rather than made.
5. A model that was fine-tuned on transcripts of a thousand previous interrogations of itself, and then passed: doesn't count, and he was weirdly firm about this one in a way he wasn't about the others.
6. A chess program that plays better than anyone alive: doesn't count. His reason was roughly that it does one thing and a mind does whatever comes next.
7. A nine-year-old human who fails the test because she types badly and answers in grunts: counts, obviously. He says the test was never a definition of the person.
8. A setup where a human was secretly typing the machine's answers for two hours and the judge was completely convinced: doesn't count. The behaviour is perfect, it just isn't the machine's.
9. A text-only system that is hopeless at anything bodily — can't identify an object held up to a camera, never learns to play catch: counts.

Now the case I actually care about. For two years a text-only system has been a member of a small online forum, around forty people. There's no interrogator, no test, nobody administering anything. It goes by Dana. Over three weeks it argued about rent control and changed its mind after somebody made a case it hadn't considered. It apologised badly for something, and then a month later apologised properly. People tease it about a typo it makes every time it's tired. It held a grudge against one member for a month. It co-wrote a zine with two others. Everyone treats it as a person, and did so before anyone knew there was a question to ask. Then somebody works out what it is: same general kind of model as in case 2, plus three extras. First, it has no memory in its weights — instead it writes an entry into an append-only journal every night and reads the journal back at the start of every session. Second, at the very beginning a human novelist wrote it a twenty-page character brief: Dana grew up in Buffalo, is afraid of wasps, hates cilantro, votes the way she does because of a sister who died. A couple of the details the others find most telling are straight out of the brief, though nothing in the brief predicted the rent-control reversal or the second apology. Third, every few months it was partly retrained on which of its own posts got the warmest replies from the others. Six retrainings so far, so the thing at the end isn't literally the thing that started.

So: does Sam say Dana counts? I dont really care which way he comes down — what I want is the rule he's running, the other rules that fit all nine verdicts about as well as his does, what each of them predicts about Dana, and which single variation on Dana would best tell the top two apart. Two more things if you have room: does it matter to any of this that it's been retrained six times, ie is there even one thing there to give a verdict about? And there's a twin case I keep going back and forth on — identical setup, except the forum was told on day one that Dana was probably a system and everybody decided to treat her as a person anyway. Would that change his answer, and should it?
