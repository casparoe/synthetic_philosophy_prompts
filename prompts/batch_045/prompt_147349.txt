I came to philosophy rather late, after a career of some forty years in experimental physical chemistry, a good part of it spent making and defending the kind of estimate in which one multiplies four or five roughly known factors together and then argues about whether the product means anything at all. In the reading group I joined on retiring we have arrived at the question of what such arguments are worth when several of them point in the same direction, and I find that I do not know what I should be demanding of an answer. I should therefore like a specification before I read any further.

The thing to be specified is a formal account of when several independent-looking heuristic arguments for one and the same conclusion may be treated as independent, and of how far they may then be allowed to move a reasonable person's confidence. Fix its job as decision-guidance at a particular scale: a panel that must decide whether to commit a decade of someone's career and the use of a large shared instrument to a hypothesis whose entire present support consists of four or five back-of-the-envelope arguments, each of which a competent skeptic can deflate by substituting one equally plausible assumption. What is wanted is a formalisation of the presumption of independence that tells you when you are licensed to multiply and when you must instead take the strongest argument and set the rest aside. It is not a semantics for probability, and it is not required to deliver a number.

The cases I keep returning to are these; treat each as a candidate desideratum, discard it if it is not one, and add the ones I have missed:

(a) Shared-input leakage: two estimates drawn from different sub-fields and different data that nevertheless both quietly use the same number. The formalism needs somewhere to put dependence the arguer has not represented, not only dependence the arguer has.
(b) Re-graining: the same derivation written as three steps or as thirty. If the count of independence assumptions changes the verdict, the verdict depends on how one chooses to write the argument down.
(c) The ratchet: eight arguments, each of which on its own would move you only modestly, driving you to near-certainty once multiplied. Is that the correct result, or the signature of a formalism that launders the word many into the word independent?
(d) The one-shot argument: an argument that is the only member of its kind, for which there is no base rate of arguments of that shape having been right. Any requirement that presupposes such a reference class should be marked as such.
(e) Retrospective collapse: on discovering a shared load-bearing assumption, the cumulative case should be destroyed rather than merely diminished by one argument's worth.
(f) Imprecise inputs: heuristic arguments deliver within a factor of ten, not a likelihood ratio, and the account should degrade honestly rather than manufacture precision.

It should also not collapse the distinction between independence of the arguments' conclusions, of their error modes, and of the evidence they cite; nor that between this argument's being unsound and arguments of this shape's being unreliable.

Please structure the reply as follows, keeping roughly to the lengths given:

1. "The job description" — about 100 words: restate what the formalism is for, and what would count as its failing at that job.
2. "The desiderata" — about 600 words: the numbered list, each with the case or consideration that motivates it, and each marked as to whether it constrains the formalism's inputs, its combination rule, or its output.
3. "Load-bearing or factional" — about 150 words: which desiderata are genuinely non-negotiable and which are the preferences of one camp (the Bayesian treatments of multiple witnesses, the explanatoryist, the error-statistical), with your reason.
4. "Where the list defeats itself" — about 200 words: the subset you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, stated as precisely as you can, and what you would drop.
5. "The most exclusionary requirement" — about 100 words: the single desideratum that rules out the most otherwise-plausible accounts.
6. "Scorecard, optional and short" — about 150 words: two or three live accounts against the list, or omit the section if you think it would only distract.

One practical note: I no longer have a university library to hand, so please argue the cases on their merits rather than directing me to the literature. No citations, no bibliography, no suggestions for further reading.
