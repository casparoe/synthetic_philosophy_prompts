I need help figuring out what I actually think about bluntness vs. white lies, and I'm doing it the only way I know how, which is by staring at my own gut reactions to cases. My verdicts:

2. My Dutch mother-in-law makes stamppot, it's bland, she asks if I liked it. I say it was lekker hoor. Fine — and I mean really fine, I don't feel even a twinge. But note this is the same shape as case 1: a positive I don't fully mean, about something a person made.

5. A junior developer on my team submits messy code. In our one-on-one I say it needs a refactor before review — fine. The exact same words, posted in the team channel where her old manager from another department can see it — not fine.
6. "Have a nice day" to the Albert Heijn cashier, said with zero feeling behind it — fine, so trivially fine it barely registers as a case at all. But ending an email to a colleague I can't stand with "warm regards" — also fine, and yet it costs me a little. Why does one cost nothing and the other cost something?
7. My Japanese father visits Amsterdam, has a miserable time, complains the whole week, and at the airport asks whether I'm glad he came. I say yes. Fine. I'm sure it's fine. But I can't tell you why it's fine when case 3 isn't.

And case 7 satisfies (b) — he's on a plane, I'll never have to repeat it — which feels like completely the wrong reason for it to be fine.

A friend offered me a different one: the real line is whether the falsehood is *coded*, i.e. whether the people in that setting already discount it the way everyone discounts "how are you," so it doesn't actually deceive anybody and isn't really a lie. I like that, but then I don't know what to do with case 3 versus case 2, because in both families the phrase is pretty coded.
