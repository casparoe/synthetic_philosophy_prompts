I spent most of my working life in numerical weather prediction, and since retiring I have been sitting in on a graduate seminar in formal epistemology — I am the only person in the room who has ever written Fortran, and the seminar's reading list sits behind a university login I no longer has access to, so I would be grateful for argument rather than for pointers to papers. The topic of the term is whether computational limits are merely practical obstacles to inquiry or genuine constraints on what we ought to believe, and I have drafted a short paper defending the stronger claim: that worst-case intractability does not simply make Bayesian conditionalization hard for creatures like us, but disqualifies it as a requirement of rationality. My own argument now seems to me to overreach, and I cannot locate the fault; two of the graduate students diagnosed it in incompatible ways — one says it is valid with a false premise, the other that a key term shifts meaning between steps — and I trust neither of them.

The argument, as I have written it:

(1) A rule of reasoning is a genuine requirement of rationality for an agent only if the agent can comply with it; and an agent can comply only if the computations the rule demands are ones the agent can actually carry out, to the accuracy the rule demands, in the time and with the hardware available. ("Ought" implies "can," in its computational form.)

(2) Exact Bayesian conditionalization is in general intractable: as I understand the standard results, computing a posterior over a space of composite hypotheses requires solving a #P-hard problem — at least as hard as counting the satisfying assignments of a Boolean formula — and even computing a merely approximate posterior is NP-hard in the worst case, so the resources required grow faster than any polynomial in the size of the problem.

(3) Agents like us face problems of just that shape constantly — medical and mechanical diagnosis, planning under uncertainty with many interacting variables, and, in my old field, assimilating observations into a model whose state space runs to tens of millions of dimensions. At my former centre we never computed a posterior exactly: we used variational and ensemble approximations, and it would have been absurd to call the forecasters irrational on that account.

(4) So Bayesian conditionalization is not a requirement of rationality for agents like us in those cases.

(5) And if intractability disqualifies a norm in those cases, it disqualifies it wherever it bites: rationality does not switch off when a problem gets large, and a norm cannot be binding for the easy instances and merely ornamental for the hard ones unless someone can say where the line between them falls.

(6) Nor does the argument stop with Bayesianism. Checking whether a sizeable body of beliefs is even consistent is NP-hard; inference to the best explanation across combinatorial spaces of hypotheses is NP-hard; and even the modest instruction to proportion confidence to the evidence becomes intractable when the evidential proposition is itself expensive to evaluate. So every substantive epistemic norm is disqualified, and what survives are the computationally trivial ones — believe what you see, believe what the nearest placard says.

(7) Those are worthless as norms; someone who followed them would be irrational. The argument therefore ends in full normative skepticism: nothing is ever rationally required of anyone, and the only counsel left is to believe whatever is cheap. That I cannot accept.

There is one further thing that troubles me, which I have been calling the laptop point: on my own argument it seems to follow that whether Bayesianism binds me depends on how fast my machine is, on how many observations happen to be in front of me, and on whether anyone ever builds a large quantum computer — the normativity of a rule of inference becomes a contingent, time-indexed, hardware-relative matter. I take that to be a reductio, but I cannot say where the dependence slipped in.

Which step do you reject, and why did that step look safe when I wrote it? Please commit to a single diagnosis: tell me whether the argument is valid with a false premise or invalid through a shift in the meaning of a term, name the premise or the transition, say what I must give up in order to reject it, price the alternatives, and defend your choice rather than setting the options before me and leaving me to choose. If two steps share the blame, rank them. For what it is worth, the premise I am least willing to surrender is (1): I do not want to turn rationality into a standard that no one can meet, though I grant that "can" may be doing more than one job in it and that I would not know how to tell; and I would sooner abandon the argument than the conclusion that Bayesian updating is not literally required of us.
