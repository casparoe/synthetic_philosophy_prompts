I'm the community member on our hospital's clinical ethics committee -- not a clinician, not a philosopher, I'm just the lay person they're required to have on it. I've been on it about eighteen months and we've recieved six requests. Next week we get a seventh and I want to know what we're going to do, given what we've already done.

Our record so far:

1. Mrs. K., 84, advanced Parkinson's, couldn't swallow, PEG tube proposed. Twenty years of saying she'd never want one, plus a proper directive signed at 76 after watching her brother go through it. Her son wanted the tube, her daughter didn't, and Mrs. K. herself couldn't tell us anything anymore. She'd had three aspirationpneumonias and would have needed soft wrist restraints to keep the tube in. We said no tube: hand feeding, comfort care. She died about ten days later.

2. A woman of 39 with a postpartum hemorrhage. At 24, during a bad untreated depression, she'd signed a document refusing any life-prolonging treatment. Fifteen years, a marriage and two kids later she was refusing a transfusion on the basis of it while asking us to save her life. We transfused. The rationale was roughly that it was written while her judgment was distorted and the rest of her life had repudiated it.

3. A man of 58 with ALS, directive from two years earlier refusing a ventilator and a tracheostomy. In respiratory failure he panicked and wrote on a pad: I want the tube, I changed my mind. We honored the original. Panic isn't a considered change of mind was more or less the reasoning.

4. A woman of 76, lifelong member of a church that forbids blood transfusion, urosepsis, confused, kept saying you decide, whatever's easiest. Easiest thing in the world: one unit of blood and she'd very likely be fine. We said no blood; she got fluids and antibiotics and went home. That's the one I'd probably have decided differently on my own.

5. A man of 80 with a wallet card that said only: I don't want to be kept alive if I can't enjoy life anymore. Early dementia, apathetic, needed a pacemaker; his son invoked the card. We overrode it. No specific intervention named, and enjoy life was doing way too much work.

Next week's case. A man of 68, early-onset Alzheimer's diagnosed at 61. Six months later, with his neurologist and his daughter (a nurse) in the room, after two separate conversations, he signs a directive: if he reaches a point where he doesn't recognize his family and can't follow a conversation, he wants no treatment for infections, no hospital transfer, no IVs -- music at home and let him go. He attached a page of reasons: he'd watched his own mother in a nursing home for six years, and what he was afraid of was being kept going as a body that scared people, plus being a millstone round his daughter's neck. (He named that daughter as his agent; his second wife knew and didn't object.)

Now: he's 68, moderate to severe, doesn't recognize his wife or his daughter, can't follow a conversation. But he lives at home, is almost always cheerful, hums along to the opera he always loved, eats fine, no pain, smiles at the grandkids. He has a urinary tract infection. It's treatable with seven days of oral antibiotics at home -- no hospital, no needles. Without it he'd likely die inside a week or so, probably with some fever and confusion but nothing we can't manage.

His daughter says: this is exactly the situation he described, that's the entire point of having a directive, honor it. His wife says: he was bracing for a nightmare and the nightmare didn't show up -- he's happy, and anyway she says that at 64, coming out of a Mozart recital, he told her "I think I got that wrong. If I'm happy I want to stay." Never written down, no recording. The daughter says he never said anything remotely like that to her, and that he saw his neurologist twice a year for five years and never once touched the document. The wife concedes she isn't the decision-maker. (She also quit work to look after him and is exhausted, which she says isn't her argument. For what it's worth, our committee has never cited family burden in a written rationale.)

So: what are we going to do, and if our own record commits us, to what? Please actually commit to a call on the case, not a menu of options. I'd also like the other rules that fit our five previous rulings just as well and where each of them would land here; which precedent is the closest analog and which is a trap; and the one extra fact, or the one variant of this case, that would tell you which of those rules we're really running. Give me a confidence number too, and say what would move you off it.
