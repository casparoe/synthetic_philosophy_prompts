I'm in the fourth year of a PhD in modern European history — I work on how mass atrocities get written about rather than on any single case — and for a couple of years now I've been taking the minutes for the classification panel of an open-access annotated bibliography on state mass violence. The panel reads each submission and puts it in one of three buckets: include as normal scholarship, include-with-an-editorial-note (usually where we think a reader needs a heads-up about framing or about who is celebrating the piece), or exclude as denialism. I have to draft the minute for next Tuesday and I'd rather not walk in empty-handed, so here is our track record, then the item we're actually deciding on. Past calls, compressed: (a) a peer-reviewed article in a mainstream journal revising the death toll at one camp complex down by about a fifth, on the basis of newly catalogued transport records and demographic back-projection, engaging the standard literature throughout, author with no affiliations anyone can find — included, unanimous, no note; (b) a self-published PDF arguing the same toll is inflated by roughly an order of magnitude, citing two 1950s pamphlets, a clipping and other self-published PDFs, no archival work, author also writes about a global financial conspiracy and has spoken at far-right events — excluded, unanimous; (c) an article in a peer-reviewed Ottoman-studies journal arguing that Armenian deaths in 1915–16 were overwhelmingly the product of famine, epidemic disease and intercommunal violence rather than of an organized state killing programme, based on Ottoman archival documents, citing some but not the strongest contrary evidence, author employed at a Turkish state university and has elsewhere called the genocide label an instrument of diaspora lobbying — excluded, 5–2, dissent saying it is wrong but not denialist; (d) a university-press book by a scholar with no Turkish institutional ties arguing the correct legal description is crimes against humanity or demographic engineering rather than genocide, because specific intent to destroy the group as such isn't established for every phase, while fully accepting the scale of killing and that deportations were state-directed — included with a note, 4–3, the note saying many specialists read the framing as minimizing; (e) a peer-reviewed forensic paper revising down the number of bodies at one mass-grave site outside Nanjing, methodologically careful, but the author writes regularly for a magazine campaigning against what it sees as anti-national history teaching, has appeared on panels with the textbook-revisionist society, and that society's publication praised the paper immediately — included, 5–2, with a note, and the minute records explicitly that the panel does not classify on the basis of who approves of a work; (f) a chapter in a school textbook series from a nationalist revisionist society, referring to the so-called Nanjing Massacre, deaths in the low thousands, mostly combatants, no archives, citing two denialist books and some 1938 newspaper reports — excluded, unanimous; (g) an article by an otherwise very well regarded historian arguing that a specific, well-documented massacre in one small town never happened, in a mainstream venue, where a specialist on the panel showed the key document had been mistranslated — excluded, 6–1.

Next week's item: a long article in a top-tier journal (paywalled, in English) by a historian at a Western European university with an unblemished record, no political affiliations, nothing controversial in their back catalogue. The argument is that in one military district of the occupied Soviet Union in 1941–42 the mass killing of Jews began as locally initiated measures by military and civil administrators reacting to acute supply shortages and partisan activity, was only retroactively authorized and then systematized from the centre, and that the standard picture of a single exterminationist directive implemented top-down is unsupported for that district and period. The opening pages state plainly that the campaign as a whole was genocide, that the centre's knowledge, endorsement and escalation are not in dispute, and that nothing here changes the overall death toll; there's an old name for this kind of dispute — functionalists against intentionalists? I always mix the two up — and the point is that it is an inside-the-field dispute, not a fringe one. Methodologically the piece is clean: it engages the full mainstream literature, takes on the strongest contrary evidence, has an appendix on source criticism, and it explicitly declines to use survivor testimony for questions about actors' reasons on the ground that testimony is unreliable about reasons, which one reviewer called defensible but strategically unfortunate. Three weeks after publication a far-right weekly in the perpetrator country ran it under something like "no extermination order after all: leading historians concede", a populist party cited it in a parliamentary question, and denialist channels are circulating a machine-translated extract with the regional and temporal hedges and the genocide affirmation stripped out; the author has posted a short statement calling that coverage a distortion and repeating the overall genocide characterization.

So: what will the panel do with it, and why? I don't just want the call — I want the rule you think we've been running, the other rules that fit our seven precedents just as well, what each of them predicts here, which past case is the closest analog, and, if our precedents genuinely underdetermine it, what we'd be committing ourselves to with each option. Also: which further submission would best tell those rules apart (I've been wondering whether a piece with flawless method and no agenda cues at all that denies something core would do it, or whether the discriminating case is about uptake rather than content). Please say how confident you are in each part and single out the bit you're least sure of — I'd much rather hand the panel a minute that says "maybe 60%, and here's what would move it" than one that sounds certain and isn't.
