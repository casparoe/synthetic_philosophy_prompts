I retired from physical chemistry six years ago and somehow ended up convening a translation workshop — eight amateurs, sixteenth-century French lyric. We spent a year on the short Marot poem that Hofstadter built Le Ton beau de Marot around, and it split us cleanly in two:

- form-first: the rhyme scheme, the syllable count, the stanza shape, whatever else the poem does structurally, are part of the poem. Drop them and you've produced a gloss, a crib, or a new poem "after" — not a translation. Bend the sense before you break the skeleton.
- content-first: what it says, to whom, in what register, with what allusions — that's the cargo; the formal pattern is the box it shipped in, and boxes are replaceable. A rhyme that makes you assert something the original doesn't assert isn't a feat, its a mistranslation in a costume.

We can't hear each other any more. What I want is the document the two camps should have produced: something both could put their names to. I should flag that I'm the form-first one — forty years on systems whose interest is all in the constraint rather than the stuff filling it — so if somebody here is being thick its probably me, and say so if you think so.

Give me:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims. Not "translation is hard": the real ones. What kind of relation a translation is between two texts; whether and when formal features carry meaning themselves; whether preserving a constraint normally costs you something; what counts as a distortion; whether both camps actually want the same thing out of a translation.
2. A table of what's left over. One column with the form-first claim stated the way that camp would sign it, one column with the content-first claim stated the way that camp would sign it — each wording fair enough that the other side would concede it's a honest statement of the position — and a third column saying whether the row is a real disagreement about something or just two ways of using a word. If part of this is verbal, tell me which row and why.
3. For every row that's real, the concrete case or measurement both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down before we look.

The cruxes I think I see — swap them out if I've got them wrong:

(a) The poem we're stuck on now is an acrostic as well as being in strict alternating rhyme with a fixed syllable count: the first letters of the stanzas spell the name of the woman it's addressed to. In English that name has different letters, a different length, and almost nothing that rhymes with it, so you genuinely cannot keep both features. Is the identity of the addressee part of the poem's content, or part of its occasion? Each camp has to state in advance which feature it gives up here, and what giving it up costs it.

(b) My pet idea, which the other half detests: you don't have to preserve the tokens of a constraint, only what the constraint does. In the French, the rhyme welds two words of nearly opposite meaning into the same sound and the reader feels the clash; a translation that welds two different opposed words by assonance, syntax or line position has inherited the constraint's job while keeping none of its surface. Is "functional inheritance" translation or cheating, and what case would separate the two?

(c) The bit I keep trying to make quantitative and probably bungling. Score a translation's formal fidelity C and its semantic fidelity S — S measured crudely, by handing the translation to a bilingual reader who hasn't seen the source and asking them content questions about the source, or by back-translating it. Plot S against C over all the existing versions of the poem. A flat stretch means slack: somebody could have raised C at no semantic cost and didn't, and the form-first camp is right that a lot of what gets complained about is laziness. A steep frontier means every unit of form costs meaning. I predict appreciable slack; they predict steepness. Is the measurement even well-posed — can C and S be defined so both camps would accept the numbers — or does choosing the metrics rig the outcome?

(d) A process question, maybe the only one we could settle in an afternoon: a form-first draft followed by a semantic repair pass, versus a content-first draft followed by a formal repair pass. Which ends up higher on both scores? I say skeleton-first, they say content-first. Both agree this bears on whether a constraint is a cage or a scaffold.

(e) Whether the target reader even perceives what the form-first side is paying for. My guess is readers hear "it rhymes" but not "the scheme turns over here because the argument does", so some formal features are expensive to preserve and invisible once preserved — and if that's right, part of the dispute dissolves into a question about which formal features are cheap and loud.

First sentence: the bottom line for the group, one sentence, where this actually lands. Then the document itself. No preamble, no restating what I asked, and don't tell me translation is a delicate art.
