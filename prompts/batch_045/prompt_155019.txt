My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about the Gettier problem, and I would like the disagreement restated in the vocabulary I actually work in. She has graduate training in epistemology; I do not. I am a quantitative researcher: my working day consists of estimators and their sampling distributions, point estimates and standard errors, identification strategies, measurement error and confounding, model misspecification, priors and posteriors, and the standing distinction between a procedure being reliable and a particular result being correct. Assume I am fluent in all of that and do not explain it back to me.

Here is the case we got stuck on. A researcher estimates a treatment effect whose true value is 0.4. His estimator is subject to two errors he has no way to detect: an unblocked confounder pushes his point estimate up by 0.3, and a coding error in a covariate pushes it down by 0.3. The estimate comes out at exactly 0.4. The study is large, pre-registered, adequately powered, and every diagnostic he knows how to run comes back clean; his standard errors are an honest report of sampling variation, and both errors are structural, so neither can surface in any check available to him. He concludes that the effect is 0.4. My friend says this is a Gettier case inside statistical inference, and that it shows the justified-true-belief analysis fails for the same reason the original cases do. I said it is not: it is just an unreliable procedure that happened to land on the truth, which is a different and much less interesting phenomenon. She then offered a general formula I could not answer: every Gettier case, she claimed, has what she called a double-payment structure, in which the world pays for the truth and the evidence pays for the justification, and the two payments are made along routes that never meet.

Please do the following. First, restate the Gettier problem and the main families of response to it — the reliability and tracking conditions, safety and sensitivity conditions, virtue or credit theories, defeasibility theories, and the knowledge-first approaches — as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary of estimation and inference, saying for each what its counterpart is in my terms. Second, translate the cancellation case above into that vocabulary and adjudicate it: is it a genuine Gettier case, a case of mere unreliability, or something my vocabulary cannot sort? Translate her double-payment formula too, and tell me whether it has any content once translated. Third, and most importantly, give me the residue report. Which claims survive the translation cleanly, which survive only by being distorted, and which cannot be expressed at all — for instance, is there anything corresponding to justification as opposed to reliability, to an internal standpoint as opposed to an external one, to the difference between the estimate being right and the researcher knowing, or to how near the error cases are? Where you have to add a formalism that is not part of my everyday toolkit, such as counterfactual distributions over nearby data-generating processes, say so explicitly rather than presenting it as part of the translation. Finally, tell me whether the residue is a defect of the statistical vocabulary or evidence that the epistemologists' debate is thinner than it looks.
