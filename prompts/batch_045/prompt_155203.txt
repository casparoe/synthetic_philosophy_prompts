The thesis I am defending: effective altruism's cost-effectiveness-driven allocation is subject to a systematic, correctable bias that I call measurability bias, and the correction is large enough to justify a standing structural sleeve of roughly a fifth of the grant budget for institutional and systemic work whose effects are hard to measure but whose upside is large and whose main byproduct is information about structural causes.

My argument, so you have something to aim at. Step one, the diagnosis: cost-effectiveness estimates are not produced under uniform conditions of evidence. Some interventions are cheap to measure well — a bednet distribution with a mortality or parasitemia endpoint; others are inherently expensive to measure, because the causal chain is long, the unit of treatment is a policy or a coalition, and the counterfactual is a legislature rather than a village. I define the measurement elasticity of an intervention as the ratio of how much its measured cost-effectiveness moves to how much its true cost-effectiveness moves, as measurement effort varies; interventions with high measurement elasticity get measured more, and measured better, and therefore rise to the top of the ranking partly because they are legible. Call the resulting distortion the measurability factor: the ratio by which ranked estimates systematically misrepresent true impact. I will argue it is bounded but large, plausibly on the order of two to twenty for the systemic end of the range.

Step two, the portfolio framing: measurability is not an independent risk factor, it is a shared exposure. Everything at the top of a cost-effectiveness ranking is top-ranked partly for the same reason, so the portfolio is undiversified along a dimension nobody is tracking. That is an argument for deliberately holding assets whose value is not driven by that factor.

Step three, the information externality: systemic interventions produce, as a byproduct, evidence about which structural causes matter and how pliable they are. That evidence is a public good for the whole funding ecosystem, it is not captured by the intervention's own cost-effectiveness estimate, and it is exactly the kind of evidence the measurable end of the portfolio never generates. I call this the structural-learning option, and I treat its expected value as part of the intervention's value.

Step four, a defensive move I expect to need on earning to give: the standard replaceability objection — that the counterfactual impact of one more high earner is near zero because someone else would have taken the job — applies symmetrically to measured and unmeasured interventions, since both are funded at the margin by the same pool of donors, so it cannot be used to defend the measurable end of the portfolio against my argument without also dismantling the case for earning to give itself.
