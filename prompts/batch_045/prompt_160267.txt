I'm writing a paper with a metaphysician who has fast, confident intuitions about material constitution, and over two terms I have logged her verdict on every case I have put to her. I now have a new case to bring her, and I care more about identifying the principle she is running than about guessing correctly.

Her verdicts so far:

1. A lump of clay worked into a statue over a week: the statue and the lump are numerically distinct; the lump existed for a week before the statue did; the lump would have survived being rolled into a ball, the statue would not.
2. A bell cast from a melted-down cannon: the bell and the piece of bronze are distinct; the bronze pre-existed the bell and would survive being melted again; the bell would not survive being melted.
3. A human person and the human organism: distinct; in a permanent vegetative state the organism persists while the person does not.
4. Ship of Theseus, planks replaced one by one over thirty years, the old planks stored and finally reassembled: the vessel in the harbour is the original ship, the reassembled vessel is not.
5. A pocket watch taken entirely apart, its parts boxed for a month, then reassembled by the same watchmaker: the watch did not exist during that month; the reassembled watch is a new watch. She rejects objects that go out of existence and come back.
6. A carved wooden piece that a household both uses as a chess piece and keeps on a shelf as a shrine figure: one object with two uses, not two coinciding objects.
7. The statue on the plinth together with a pebble across the room: nothing is composed of the two; composition is restricted, not unrestricted.

The new case. A cathedral commissions an ice sculpture of a bishop for a January festival. The sculptor fills a bishop-shaped silicone mould with supercooled water and triggers crystallisation: one moment the contents of the mould are entirely liquid, a few seconds later they are entirely solid ice in the shape of a bishop. The piece stands in the nave for nine days; on the fifth day the sculptor shaves a slice off the base so that it stands level, removing some of the ice; by the end of the ninth day it has melted completely and the meltwater is poured into the courtyard. I am asking about the solid ice and the sculpture, not about any portion of water that may have been in the mould beforehand or in the basin afterwards. The point of the case is that the material thing and the artefact begin together and end together: unlike the clay in case 1, there is no stretch of time in which the piece of ice exists and the ice bishop does not, or vice versa. The two still differ modally: had the heating failed on the third day and melted off the mitre and the crook, leaving a rounded mass of ice on the plinth, the piece of ice would have survived and the ice bishop would not.

My question: during the nine days, will she say that the piece of ice and the ice bishop are two objects, one constituting the other, or one object under two descriptions? Before answering, please set out the candidate rules that fit all seven of her verdicts — I can think of a modal test keyed to persistence conditions, a test keyed to kind or to a two-tier distinction between mere material things and things with an imposed form or function, and something perdurantist keyed to actual temporal extent, but I want the ones that genuinely fit — and say what each predicts here. Note that cases 4 and 5 look like a separate doctrine about persistence through change that any of these rules will have to borrow, so say how each rule accommodates them. Then give me your best guess at her verdict with the reasons for preferring the rule you think she is running, and name the further case that would settle it. My own candidate is a wedge of clay that has been jammed under a lecture-hall door as a doorstop since 1994: the lump and the doorstop seem to differ in kind but not in persistence conditions, since both survive being squashed, chipped or flattened and neither survives being dissolved. Is that the right probe, and does it discriminate the rules the ice case leaves standing? Please also state how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the claim you are least sure of.
