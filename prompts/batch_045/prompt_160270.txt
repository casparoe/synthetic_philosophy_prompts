hey so I got picked as one of the two senior speakers at graduation and I have basically no idea what im doing. Its outdoors, like 600 people in the sun, they told me 4 minutes, I think I can get away with 5.

My philosophy teacher did a unit on free will at the end of the year and showed us this thing about the sphex wasp: the wasp stings a cricket, drags it to its burrow, leaves it at the entrance, goes inside to inspect the nest, comes back out and pulls the cricket in — but if you move the cricket a couple inches while its inside, it comes out, puts the cricket back where it was, and goes in to inspect again, and you can make it run that loop over and over (like 40 times? whatever the number is). The point being that what looks from the outside like flexible, purposeful, "she knows what shes doing" behavior can actually be a rigid loop that gets reset by one tiny change in the world. I think the philosopher was Dennett, or maybe he just made the word famous — dont hold me to it. The word was "sphexishness." Also I googled around after and saw people saying the wasp story is exaggerated or possibly made up, which my teacher kind of waved off, so idk where that leaves me.

Heres why im stuck. Every graduation speech I've ever sat thru says some version of "you chose your own path," "you can be anything you want," "you made this moment happen." After that unit I cant say that straight anymore — if sphexishness is a real thing, or even if its a real *possibility* for anything with a brain, then "you chose" is doing a lot more work than it looks like it does. But I also cant get up in front of 300 eighteen year olds and their parents who paid for all this and tell them nobody chose anything. And I really dont want to do the smug move where I go "well, actually, philosophers have a technical fix for this, you're free anyway" — half the room checks out and the other half thinks im being clever at them.

Two ideas I want to build it around, tell me if they're dumb:

1. The grain thing. The wasp looks totally rigid if you describe the whole routine, but its actually tracking the world really closely the whole time (where the antennae are, where the cricket is) — so maybe "rigid" and "flexible" are properties of a *description*, not of a creature. And you can apparently get super flexible behavior out of completely rigid parts, like a jukebox or a chess program, so "my parts are mechanical" doesn't immediately mean "im not free." My teacher called it the level-of-description problem I think. I dont really know how far that actually gets you.

2. The opposite failure mode, which nobody ever talks about: not being able to *close* a loop. The wasp cant stop re-inspecting. But there's also the person who re-opens every decision forever and never commits to anything, and that doesn't feel free either. So maybe the thing worth wishing people isn't unlimited flexibility, its being able to stop re-checking and actually commit — having a working exit from the loop, not just more options. I basically made this up, idk if anyone defends it, but I like it and I think my class would get it.

So: write the speech. 4–5 minutes, meant to be said out loud, no jargon (no "compatibilism," no "determinism" if you can help it, definitely no "incompatibilism"), no "the real lesson is..." framing, not a lecture. It has to land for my classmates who want to be told they're special and also want to get out of the sun; for parents and grandparents; for my AP Bio teacher who actually knows insects and will know whether the wasp story is true and will side-eye me if I say it too confidently; and for my philosophy teacher, who will hear every move I make. It should still be a graduation speech — about leaving, and about whatever comes next.
