I work on the payments side of a mid-sized public program that hands out money to organisations for things we want more of -- restoring wetland, retrofitting old buildings, running job training, that sort of thing. The rule we apply, and as far as I can tell every program remotely like ours applies some version of it, is that we are only supposed to pay for the bit that would not of happened anyway. Internally we call it additionality. What I want from you is: what is a program like ours actually committed to, philosophically, by running that test? Because the longer I do this job the more it looks like the test is doing three or four different jobs and only one of them is the job we say it's doing.

Here's what I mean. On the application form, before anything has happened, we ask applicants the present-tense or future-tense question: if we don't fund you, will you still do this? On audit, two years later, after the thing is done and everyone knows how it turned out, we ask the past-tense question: would you have done this without our money? And I've started to think these are not the same question at all. The first one is asked while the decision is still open, and applicants answer it like they're deciding or negotiating -- a lot of them answer it like a threat, and our own training tells us not to reward that, because someone saying pay me or I walk is just bargaining. The second one is asked when the thing is settled, and people answer it like a question about a fixed fact, and they seem to reason backwards from what actually happened: we did it, so I suppose we'd of found the money somehow. I've watched the same outfit give genuinely different answers to those two questions and I don't think it's only self-interest, though obviously it's partly that.

Then there's a third move. When we can't get a straight answer out of anyone, we quietly switch to a completely different question: is this the sort of thing that people are doing anyway? Is it already legally required? Would any outfit in this line of work be doing it without a subsidy? That's not about the applicant at all -- it's a generalisation about a class of cases -- and everybody in the office treats it as if it were the same question, just easier to answer.

And then the fourth thing, which is the one that really bugs me. Even when we've been extremely strict about the individual would-have question at the eligibility stage, the size of the payment is worked out against a regional average, not against what this applicant would have done. So the yes/no is counterfactual and the how much is statistical, and nobody seems to notice the whiplash.

So: reconstruct the theory that all this presupposes. Does it presuppose that there is a determinate fact of the matter about what a particular organisation would have done? Does it presuppose that the future-tense question and the would-have question have to have the same answer, and if it does, is that presupposition one anybody has defended? Does it need causation to be a matter of difference-making for the individual case, or would a purely probabilistic or statistical view run the whole thing? Is there anything special or dodgy about the fact that the antecedent is about the applicant's own decision -- that the thing we're hypotheticalising away is the very choice we're trying to buy, and that asking about it might change it or make it uncheckable? Tell me which parts of our practice strictly require the strong counterfactual machinery and which parts would survive just fine if we replaced it with averages, and which of the four things I described are flatly in tension with each other. And if we dropped the test entirely -- paid everyone who did the thing, or paid a rate calibrated off randomised trials of the whole program -- what would we actually be giving up, and what would we gain?

One more thing about how I want the answer pitched: I don't want a reading list or citations, I want it in your own words, and I want you to mark clearly which bits are settled and which are genuinely fought over by people who work on this, and not quietly present one side of a live fight as the consensus. Where you're going past what's actually established and into your own reconstruction, say so and say how confident you are.
