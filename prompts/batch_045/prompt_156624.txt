Write an internal memorandum of 700 to 850 words, composed by a mid-level official in a fictional agency — call it the Office of Recurrence Certification — and addressed to her superiors, proposing a reform in how the office decides whose life is fit to be repeated. The setting is a world in which the eternal return has ceased to be a philosophical thought-experiment and is instead a confirmed physical fact: astronomers have verified that the cycle is closing and that every life, including the lives of the officials who administer the certification, will recur in identical detail, forever. The office's statutory task is to certify in advance which lives are affirmable, that is, which ones their owners could will again without remainder, and the piece should be set at the moment when that task has quietly become unworkable.

The comedy has to come from the mechanics of the doctrine rather than from the situation around it, and I want at least four specific difficulties to drive the memo. First, the unit problem: whether what must be affirmed is a life taken as a whole or every smallest part of it. Under the office's original ruling a single unaffirmed detail — a toothache, a humiliating afternoon, a badly chosen sandwich — fails the entire file, with the result that nobody has ever passed; the memo should propose some form of aggregation instead, and show how aggregation quietly converts affirmation of one's life into cost-benefit accounting of it, so that the heaviest of weights becomes a spreadsheet exercise. Second, the memory clause: the office has never decided whether the next cycle replays with the subject's recollection of earlier cycles intact. If it does, affirmation ratchets, and eternal return becomes an endless series of the same boredom consciously remembered; if it does not, the person who says yes to the repetition is not the person who lived it, and the certificate is void. Third, the screening problem: officials must distinguish genuine affirmation from what the memo should call Stoic contamination, the yes of someone who has understood that a no is useless. This is where the piece bites hardest, because in a universe where recurrence is a physical law the answer is not chosen but fated, so the office is not administering a test at all but merely taking a measurement, and a measurement of a greatness the subject has no say in. The fourth difficulty is recursive and should surface late: the assessment is itself an event inside the life being assessed, so the assessment recurs, the evaluator writes the same memo in every cycle to the same stubborn recipients, and any persuasion the memo attempts is known in advance to fail identically. The writer may notice this without being permitted to escape it.

Keep the whole thing deadpan and inside the bureaucracy's assumptions: no character should announce that the enterprise is absurd, no passage should wink at the reader, and there should be no redemptive or melancholy final paragraph. The reader should nevertheless be able to tell what is being ironized — the treatment of a thought-experiment about how one stands toward one's own existence as an auditable standard, a credential, a piece of paperwork — and the piece should land for someone who has never read a word of Nietzsche as well as for someone who has. You may include a brief cameo of the doctrine's popular afterlife, in the form of recurrence coaches or a workplace wellness pilot in which employees are asked whether they would will their lunch order again eternally. Do not cite or name any book, section number, translator, or commentator, and do not place any real text in quotation marks; the piece should not depend on any bibliographic detail at all.

Format the response as continuous prose only, in the manner of an email or a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, no numbered clauses. A single date line and a To/From line at the top are acceptable, and nothing else.
