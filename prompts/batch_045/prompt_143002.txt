You are a professional philosopher who specialises in early modern moral philosophy — Montaigne's *Essais* in particular — and who has spent several years advising humanitarian and development NGOs on cross-cultural ethics. Think carefully and do not rush: before you draft anything, do a private brainstorming pass in which you work out which of Montaigne's moves in his essay on the Tupinambá of coastal Brazil, known in English as *Of the Cannibals* (from the first book of the *Essais*, first published in 1580), can carry procedural weight and which cannot. Then write the answer for a colleague who is intelligent and experienced but has never read the *Essais*: no jargon, no citations, no quotations from primary texts, and nothing I would have to look up. I need the reasoning, not the scholarship.

Here is my situation. I chair the Practice Review Panel of a mid-sized international NGO that runs maternal-health and education programmes in about a dozen countries. When our field staff encounter a local practice that troubles them, the panel writes an advisory opinion placing it in one of five buckets: accommodate with no action; accommodate with mitigation; oppose privately through our partner organisations; oppose publicly; or make the practice a condition of continued funding. We handle fifteen to twenty cases a year. I have three people – me, a regional programme officer, and an anthropologist on retainer – about six weeks per case, and no legal training. Every opinion is shown to our local partner staff, and some are quoted in donor reports, so a badly worded verdict can cost us money, staff safety, or a decade-long relationship. The executive director signs off on anything above the second bucket.

I want a decision procedure my panel can actually run, and I want it grounded in Montaigne's argument rather than in the generic cultural-relativism slogans that people reach for in these meetings. Concretely, I expect your procedure to preserve the distinctions that do the real work in that essay: the anti-projection move, that we are apt to call barbarous whatever departs from our own customs and to judge distant peoples by our own example rather than by universal reason; the distinction between the merely unimproved or wild and the genuinely depraved, which lets Montaigne admire a people whose customs he has no intention of adopting; his substantive and non-relativistic criterion, that cruelty and treachery are vices everywhere while difference of custom is not; the comparative test, in which he insists that certain European practices look worse than the ones we find shocking when redescribed without their flattering framing; and his epistemic modesty about secondhand reports of what the practice actually is, given how thin his own sources were. Where a move in the essay is unusable as procedure – his appeal to a state of nature as a normative baseline, his confidence in a single eyewitness, his literary and ironic framing – say so explicitly and say what you would put in its place.

The procedure must be ordered questions, each naming who answers it, what counts as a sufficient answer, and what to do when we cannot get an answer within our six weeks. It must have explicit stop rules that end in "accommodate, no action", escalation rules, and marked handoffs to a named human being where the remaining question is one of values and not of fact. It has to survive the hard cases, so please build in steps for at least these: the risk that the *description* of the practice is the outsider's frame rather than the thing itself, which I want handled by a substitution test – redescribe the practice in terms we would apply to an equivalent practice in our own society, feeding relatives to mourners versus desecrating a corpse, confinement of a disturbed person versus involuntary psychiatric commitment, marriage at sixteen versus our own emancipation law – and send the case back for reframing if the redescription changes the verdict; cases where the practice is contested inside the community, so that deference cannot simply go to whoever currently holds authority; cases where all our testimony, including our own field reports, is gathered under a funding relationship and therefore has a built-in interest structure; cases where public condemnation is itself likely to turn a fading practice into a badge of identity under external attack; cases where the horror lies in the rate or severity of individual instances rather than in the practice as a type, so that condemning the type and mitigating the tokens come apart; cases where the practice is unlawful in the jurisdiction, where I need to be told exactly what work the legal answer does and prevented from letting it stand in for the moral one; and cases where the practice looks ancient but is forty years old, or has recently become a cash strategy or a form of resistance, and where we will usually never get the history right.

Then apply the finished procedure to three cases: a partner-run boarding school whose girls are married at sixteen with their families' consent and where withdrawal would mean the girls leave school altogether; a healing practice involving the confinement and beating of a person believed to be possessed, performed by a practitioner our own local staff respect, after which the person usually recovers and is reintegrated; and a mortuary rite in which relatives consume a small portion of a revered elder's remains as part of mourning, which a field officer reported to headquarters as cannibalism and which a major donor has now heard about. At least one of these must come out as a verdict my panel will not like, because it costs us money or tells our own staff that their framing was the problem.

Finally, be honest about the limits. Mark the steps where judgement cannot be eliminated and no checklist will help me. Tell me what this procedure cannot do: whether it can settle the metaphysical question of whether our judgements about distant practices can be objectively correct, whether it can fix who counts as a member of the community for the purposes of deference, whether it can aggregate when a practice protects some members and harms others, and whether it can answer the political question of whether we should be in these relationships at all. And if you think the Montaignean frame is the wrong one for some step, say where and what you would use instead. Keep the procedure itself to something that fits on two pages and can be run in an afternoon; take as long as you need on the worked cases.
