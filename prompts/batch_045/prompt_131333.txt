I convene a small reading group — two physicists, two philosophers, and me — and we have spent most of a year on the Rietdijk–Putnam style argument for the block universe: the argument that, since any two spacelike-separated events are simultaneous in some inertial frame, and since what is simultaneous with you now is what is real now, everything from your own twenty-second century to the death of the last star is as present as the chair you are sitting in, so no presentist or growing-block story about time can be reconciled with special relativity. We have not moved at all, and I no longer think more discussion of the same material will help, so I want a structural diagnosis instead of another round.

The three positions in the room are stable and well rehearsed. One physicist thinks the argument is simply sound and the resistance to it is discomfort with a consequence of a theory we have overwhelming reason to accept; she treats the presentist's remaining moves as epicycles. One philosopher thinks the whole thing is an equivocation on 'real' and 'exists': the only existence relativity talks about is tenseless existence in the manifold, which has nothing to do with the tensed, temporally-loaded existence the presentist is denying, so the two sides are not contradicting each other but answering different questions under one label. A third member grants that the argument may establish something metaphysical but says what it really establishes is not that the future is 'there' but that it is already settled — that the live conclusion is about fixity and determinism rather than about ontology — and he thinks the rest of us are arguing past that.

Two things I noticed while preparing this last session seem to me to be doing hidden work, and I want your judgement on both. First: timelike-separated events are simultaneous in no frame, so the argument only ever yields pairwise co-reality for spacelike-separated pairs. To get from those pairs to the conclusion that all events are equally real, you have to chain through an intermediate event, and that requires co-reality to be transitive. But the presentist's natural reply is that there is no single relation here at all, only a family of relations — co-real-with-e-relative-to-frame-F — so the chaining equivocates, and the argument's most dramatic premise is also its least examined one. Is transitivity really the load-bearing joint, and is there any argument for or against it, or is it a stipulation each side makes because it fits the conclusion it already likes? Second: consider a variant with a single observer who never needs an alien. Take a traveller at a large distance from Earth who oscillates her velocity back and forth through a small range; the hyperplane she currently counts as simultaneous sweeps forward and back across centuries of Earth's history, and it does so as quickly and as often as she likes, for reasons internal to her. The frame-indexed presentist seems committed to saying that whether some twenty-third-century Earth event is now changes with her whim, which my physicist takes as a reductio and my philosopher takes as no more mysterious than the fact that what is to my left changes when I turn around. Does that variant add any pressure the original argument lacks, or is it only the same argument in a more vivid costume?

My own hypothesis, which I hold loosely, is that this is three questions stacked into one and treated as a single disagreement: whether the structure of relativity affords any frame-independent relation of co-reality; whether being real, in whatever sense survives that first question, entails being settled; and whether either result touches the experience of temporal passage, which is what most people in the room actually care about and which neither of the first two settles. If that is right, then the year of stalemate is not a hard problem being avoided but several different problems being run together, and the fix is a distinction rather than an experiment. But I would also take seriously the answers that this is instead a clash of bedrock intuitions that neither side can argue for, or a disagreement about what a physical theory has to do before it counts as settling a metaphysical question.

Give me the diagnosis with reasons, engaging the three positions as I have described them rather than surveying the options, and tell me honestly what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result, a reformulation of the question — or why nothing should be expected to. Don't send me to the literature; I want your own reasoning. Answer in continuous prose, six to eight hundred words, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write a long email to a colleague you disagree with.
