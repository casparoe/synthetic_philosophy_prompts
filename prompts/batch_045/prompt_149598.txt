I am giving a fifty-minute colloquium paper — twenty-five minutes of presentation, twenty-five of questions — in a philosophy department whose strengths are philosophy of mind and philosophy of cognitive science. What I want from you is a Q&A preparation sheet, not a defence of the view.

The thesis I am defending: illusionism about phenomenal consciousness is best formulated not as the metaphysical claim that we lack a certain sort of property, but as a metasemantic claim — that phenomenal concepts, the concepts at work in first-person judgments of the form there is something it is like for me now, fail to refer, because nothing could occupy the role those concepts assign: an intrinsic, ineffable, privately accessible property whose nature is fully revealed in acquaintance. On this formulation illusionism is not the claim that qualia do not exist, which would concede that the concept is in good standing and merely uninstantiated, but the claim that phenomenal discourse is a degenerate theoretical idiom whose apparent referentiality is an artifact of how introspection works: a quining rather than a denial. Two corollaries I intend to defend. First, the explanatory gap is not a gap to be closed but an artifact of a non-referring concept, although there remains a genuine empirical project, namely explaining why creatures with our kind of introspective self-model make these judgments at all and with this degree of conviction. Second, the view has no distinctive clinical consequences: the question whether a given patient is conscious is to be replaced by questions about specific capacities, and that replacement is as available to the realist as to me, which I take to be a virtue of the view rather than a confession that the dispute is verbal.

My argument for the metasemantic claim is a dilemma: either phenomenal properties make a difference to what a subject can say, do or know, in which case the realist owes an account of that difference which is not simply the familiar cognitive-functional story, and no such account is on offer; or they make no such difference, in which case the posit is idle and the concept has nothing left to be about. I also introduce what I call the depth problem: the illusion is not merely an illusion that we have phenomenal properties but an illusion that this is indubitable, and I argue that a metacognitive story about systematically inflated confidence in self-model outputs can carry the depth. On my view a first-person report is not an avowal whose authority derives from acquaintance but the output of a measurement device with a known and systematic bias, so its authority is reduced rather than abolished.

The audience is as follows. A Chalmers-style phenomenal realist, senior, who holds that consciousness is a brute non-functional feature of the world and regards illusionism as eliminativism with better manners. Two mid-career philosophers of cognitive science working in the predictive-processing idiom, broadly sympathetic, who suspect the whole dispute is verbal and will want to know what my view adds to existing stories about interoceptive inference and self-modelling. A junior philosopher currently writing about the charge that illusionists simply change the subject by redefining consciousness as something functional and then announcing victory over it. An older Dennett-style heterophenomenologist who thinks the illusion framing concedes a datum the realist was never entitled to and amounts to a retreat from the harder line. A palliative-care physician who writes on neuroethics and disorders of consciousness and will want to know what any of this implies for analgesia and sedation in patients who cannot report. And a room of graduate students who mostly want to know what the view implies about whether an artificial system could be conscious.

What I want: the eight or so objections I will actually get, ordered by how likely and how damaging, each phrased in the idiom of the person who will raise it rather than as a generic objection to illusionism, each with a two-minute reply, a note on where the reply is soft, and a line on whether I should concede something at the outset. I especially need three things handled carefully: how to answer the change-the-subject charge without retreating to the claim that we are merely disagreeing about a word; how to answer the predictive-processing sympathisers when they say my view is their view in metaphysical costume; and what to say to the clinician, given that my no-clinical-consequences corollary removes the footing under precautionary reasoning about pain in patients who cannot report — that is the objection I least want to improvise. Finish with the weakest step in my own argument, which I should prepare to defend rather than hope nobody notices, and with the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
