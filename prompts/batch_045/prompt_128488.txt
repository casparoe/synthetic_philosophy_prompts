I'm putting together a set of scripted dialogues for an early modern reading group, and I need one on Hobbes's views about geometry. Two characters with roughly equal airtime, about 900–1100 words, and I need them to actually engage each other — not one of them reciting a summary while the other one nods along.

Here's the background so you don't have to rely on remembering the texts (and please don't have either character quote Hobbes or cite chapter and verse — I don't want invented quotations, paraphrase everything).

Hobbes thinks geometry is just about the only real science we have, and he explains its certainty by the fact that we make its objects: a line is what you get by moving a point, a circle is what you get by swinging a straight line around one of its endpoints, and so on. Because we produce the objects, we know their causes, and a proper definition states that production instead of just listing properties. This pairs with his resolutive–compositive method: you resolve a thing into its most universal causes (motions, for him), then recompose, and the recomposition is the demonstration. Physics could in principle be like this too, if we could get at the tiny motions that produce the appearances. Alongside all that there's his very thorough nominalism: universals are names, truth is a property of speech rather than of things, and the starting points of a science are definitions, which look a lot like stipulations about how we're going to use words.

Now the fight I want staged. Call the two readings the verbal reading and the causal reading.

Marisol takes the verbal reading: geometry's necessity is just consequence-in-speech. Once the names are fixed the conclusions follow, and the constructive bit — the moving points and swinging lines — is psychology or pedagogy, not ground. Theo takes the causal reading: the construction is doing the real work, and without it you can't explain why Hobbes thought geometry was uniquely privileged, since on the verbal reading any old set of stipulated definitions would give you a necessary science.

They need to get far enough that each one restates the other's position in terms the other accepts, and then they disagree about which cost is smaller. Two things I want them to actually answer rather than dance around:

1. Marisol has to explain why Hobbes keeps saying geometry is about bodies, and why he thinks physics could someday become like geometry. If the necessity is purely verbal, that hope looks empty — stipulate hard enough and you get a necessary science of anything. She's allowed to bite the bullet here (maybe the privilege of geometry is only that we get to fix the definitions ourselves, whereas in physics we have to discover them), but Theo should press on whether that's a bullet Hobbes can afford given how he talks about certainty.

2. Theo has to explain the grounding problem. The constructions get performed in the imagination, on phantasms, and phantasms for Hobbes are decaying sense — motions in us caused by external bodies. So geometrical necessity now seems to rest on contingent facts about what we can imagine or what motions we're built to register. Worse, it seems to make geometry a branch of physics at exactly the moment Hobbes wants physics modelled on geometry. Call it the bootstrapping problem, and let Marisol use that name against him. Theo's best reply, I think, is that the admissible definitions aren't arbitrary — a definition that doesn't state a possible production is defective — so nature constrains which definitions you may start from; but then Marisol gets to say the necessity has an empirical hinge, which is precisely what Hobbes denies when he contrasts geometry with merely conjectural natural philosophy.

Then give them a concrete case to fight over: suppose we grant that Hobbes's own attempted squaring of the circle was faulty, as his mathematical critics argued and as he never really conceded. On the verbal reading that's just a bad inference from stipulations, no big deal, and it touches nothing — and note that the refutations were themselves ordinary demonstrations from definitions, which suggests the definitions-only picture is what's working in practice. On the causal reading it's a diagnostic: Hobbes behaved as if having a construction was evidence of correctness, which only makes sense if construction is the source of the certainty, and his inability to take the refutations on board is a symptom of that. Marisol gets to reply that people who are bad at inference make errors and that's all this is.

Casual register, a bit of personality, two different temperaments, no lectures. End with them agreeing on what one thing would settle the disagreement between them but not settling it — and if you genuinely think one side wins on the evidence, let that show in the texture of the exchange, but don't let the loser collapse into an idiot.
