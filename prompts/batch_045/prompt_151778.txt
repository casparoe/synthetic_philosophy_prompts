ok so I'm trying to write down my actual view of truth for a grad seminar and I want to know if the package holds together before I stand up and defend it. Where I've landed:

(1) Truth is whatever an ideally extended, ideally conducted inquiry would settle on at the limit — roughly the Peircean story.
(2) I also want the Jamesian bit: truth is a species of the good, and the reason anyone should care about being right rather than comfortable is that true beliefs are the ones that are expedient in the long run and on the whole. I've been treating (1) and (2) as complementary rather than as rivals: (1) tells you what truth is, (2) tells you why it matters. That division of labour is the load-bearing part of the whole thing.
(3) Truth isn't relative — not to persons, not to cultures, not to historical eras. One right answer per question.
(4) There are determinately true propositions that no inquiry could ever settle even in principle. The last speaker of a dying language says something to a friend one afternoon; every recording and every trace of it is destroyed the next day and no other speaker survives. It's either true or false that she said yes rather than no, and nothing could ever tell us which.
(5) The disquotation schema is a fixed point — any theory has to deliver '"p" is true iff p' for every sentence — but I read that as a constraint a theory has to meet, not as the whole theory, which is why I've never been able to just be a deflationist.
(6) I'm happy to say of some false belief that it was genuinely better to hold it, and I dont think saying that costs me (2).
(7) And one realist-sounding thought I'm attached to: part of the explanation of why my beliefs and practices work is that they're true. Truth earns its keep by explaining success.

What I can't work out is whether I've got one problem here or three. Audit it: which pairs genuinely collide, which only look like they do, and for each real collision, what would have to give and what that costs me. Rank them by severity rather than listing everything as equally bad. And if (1) and (2) really do pull apart under the surface, give me a case that forces the issue — every example I try to build either quietly assumes inquiry would converge on whatever happens to be useful, or makes the belief useful in such a local, short-run way that (6) covers it instead.
