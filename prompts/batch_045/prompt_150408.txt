I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy, and the chapter of my dissertation I'm currently stuck on is about whether the Gregorian level in Dennett's fourfold taxonomy (Darwinian creatures, whose behavioural repertoire is fixed by selection; Skinnerian creatures, which tune that repertoire by reinforcement; Popperian creatures, which test candidate actions in an inner environment so that bad options fail in imagination rather than in the world; Gregorian creatures, which additionally get equipped with mind tools — public, reusable representational artifacts, paradigmatically words — that reorganise their own cognitive routines) marks a genuine discontinuity or is just Popperian machinery with extras bolted on. A reading group here on animal minds has been pushing me on a sub-question I can't settle by argument alone: what exactly has to be true of an external artifact for it to count as a mind tool rather than a mere instrument? I've been toying with two candidate criteria. The offloading criterion says an artifact is a mind tool whenever the agent uses it to carry, fix, or transform information, thereby reducing what its own on-line cognition has to do — a bird that drags a shell to a hard surface and uses it as an anvil to hold a nut steady is, on this view, halfway into Gregorian space. The re-deployment criterion says offloading isn't enough: an artifact is a mind tool only when the very same item, picked up for one job, is later re-used as a representational resource in a structurally different task with no new training for the second use — the anvil becomes a mark-maker, the mark becomes a signal, the signal becomes a term. My supervisor's view is that the two criteria never actually come apart in any case we could recognise, and that the disagreement is therefore merely verbal. I want a case that shows she is wrong, or that shows me why I am.

Please act as a philosopher of mind and animal cognition with a working command of Dennett's hierarchy, and take your time: before committing to a scenario, brainstorm two or three candidates and say in a line or two apiece why you discarded them. Your task is to construct a thought experiment that separates the two criteria for me. Design a matched pair of conditions — same species, same rearing, same species-typical cognitive ceiling, same physical environment, same task, same reinforcement history — in which the only thing that varies is what happens to the artifact between the first use and the second, and where my offloading criterion and my re-deployment criterion return opposite verdicts on whether the creature has crossed into Gregorian space. Constraints. No science fiction, no aliens, no artificial agents, no exotic physics: the creature should be no smarter than a corvid, a parrot, or a chimpanzee, and the artifact should be something an animal could realistically encounter — a stone, a stick, a shell, a leaf, a smear of pigment, a knot, a pile of cached food. One paragraph of scenario, and no longer. The creature must be uncontroversially Popperian at the start: the episode should show it acting on an option it has tried out internally rather than stumbling into success, so that the Gregorian question is live rather than vacuous. And crucially, the verdicts must be generated by the case, not stipulated by it: you may not make a criterion lose by saying that the creature does not really understand, is merely mimicking, or lacks the right inner states. The losing criterion has to lose for a structural reason I could diagram.

After the scenario, please do the check I'd need in print. First, run each condition through all four levels of the taxonomy and say explicitly where the creature stops and why it does not stop one level higher. Second, state each criterion's verdict and the reasoning that delivers it. Third, name the single feature of the case that is doing the work, and show what happens if it is removed — I expect the two criteria to collapse back together, and I want to see the collapse. Fourth, give me the strongest objection a defender of the criterion that comes out badly would raise; I'd rather face it now than in review. Then close with a final line, and nothing after it, in this exact form: Verdict: <exactly one of: offloading, re-deployment, both — collapse> — <one sentence stating which criterion the case vindicates and the feature that settles it>.
