You are a formal epistemologist and philosopher of probability, with research-level command of the interpretation debates: the logical interpretation in the Keynes–Carnap line, objective Bayesianism in the Jaynes and Cox-tradition, frequentism, and the propensity view. Work through this carefully rather than giving a first-pass verdict. I want the analogy itself interrogated — the mapping, what it carries, what it quietly imports — not just a bottom line. This is a pure reasoning exercise: do not cite, do not look anything up, and do not give me a bibliography or a reading list. Argue from the structure of the views.

The setting. I sit on a certification board for one-off civil-engineering projects. The case in front of us is a single tidal barrier: bespoke design, one installation, one chance to be right. We must publish a number for the probability that it fails within its hundred-year design life, and the world will test that number at most once. There is no usable reference class, no series of comparable barriers, no repeatable experiment to run. Most of us treat that as the central embarrassment: the one notion of probability we can cash out without hand-waving, the long-run frequency, has nothing to attach to here.

Our newest member, trained in formal epistemology, denies that this is an embarrassment, and her argument leans throughout on a single analogy: probability stands to evidence as deductive logic stands to premises. In her mouth the analogy does the following work.

1. Probability is degree of entailment. The probability calculus extends logic to partial support; it is not a branch of the theory of measurement, and it owes nothing to repeated trials.
2. Logic needs no long run of valid arguments in order to be objective. An inference is valid on a single occasion, and a single bad inference is simply an error. So the absence of repetitions is no objection whatever to a single-case probability.
3. Two reasoners with the same premises are not entitled to disagree, and likewise the probability of a hypothesis given a body of evidence is fixed by that evidence. Apparent disagreement means someone has reasoned badly, or is not really working from the same evidence.
4. What is objective about a set of degrees of belief is its coherence, and incoherence plays precisely the role that contradiction plays in logic: a Dutch-bookable set of degrees of belief is the probabilistic analogue of an inconsistent set of beliefs.
5. And the part aimed directly at us: because probability is logic-like rather than frequency-like, we should stop treating our number as a guess to be averaged with other people's guesses or hedged in the write-up, and publish the one value the evidence determines. We should also resist the regulator's plan to score our numbers across the board's whole portfolio of projects, since a valid argument is not answerable to a batting average.

The conclusions she wants are therefore two: single-case objectivity without frequencies, and uniqueness — exactly one rational number — so that the board's task is to find it rather than to negotiate one.

Within that audit, I specifically want you to confront the following, rather than gesturing at them:

(a) Entailment is a relation between sentences of a fixed formal language, and its verdicts are settled by the meanings of the connectives. What plays the role of the language and of the connectives on the probability side, and what happens to uniqueness when that choice is not itself settled by logic? I mean the Carnap-style worry about the measure over the space of possibilities, put in plain terms rather than in formalism.

(b) Deductive consequence is monotonic; evidential support is not. Does the analogy then require probability to behave like a non-monotonic logic, and if so, what happens to the claim that a body of evidence determines a unique degree?

(c) In logic the premises are shared, articulated sentences. Our evidence is neither: the same body of evidence can be carved into propositions in more than one way, and facts we already knew before we formed any probability cannot simply be fed in as fresh premises to conditionalize on. Show how this maps, or fails to map, onto anything in the source domain.

(d) Logic has no external success criterion — validity is not graded by outcomes. Probability standardly is: calibration, proper scoring rules, and our regulator's portfolio-level scoring. Does any of that grading belong to the analogy at all? If it does, have long-run considerations returned by the side door, and does that refute the analogy or only delimit it?

(e) A final comparative question.
