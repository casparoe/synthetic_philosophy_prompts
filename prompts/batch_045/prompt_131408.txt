I'm writing an essay for a jurisprudence course on the Hart–Devlin exchange about legal moralism, and rather than rehearse the argument in the abstract I want to see what happens when it is run against a concrete case: the blasphemous libel prosecution of *Gay News* in the late 1970s. Please take the following facts as given rather than as things to check. In 1976 the magazine published a poem by James Kirkup, written from the point of view of a Roman centurion, describing sexual acts with the crucified Christ. Mary Whitehouse, then well known as a campaigner against what she saw as moral decline, obtained leave to bring a private prosecution; at the Old Bailey in 1977 the publishing company was fined £1,000 and the editor Denis Lemon was fined £500 with a nine-month suspended prison sentence; the Court of Appeal struck down the sentence but upheld the convictions; and in 1979 the House of Lords held, by three to two, that the offence did not require any intention to commit blasphemy — the question was whether the material was calculated to outrage religious feeling. The offence protected Christianity alone; a minority of the Lords thought it ought to protect all faiths equally. The common law offences of blasphemy and blasphemous libel were abolished in England and Wales in 2008, but only a couple of years after Parliament had created, over sustained objections from comedians, writers and civil liberties groups, a new offence of stirring up religious hatred, narrowed in the Lords to threatening words or behaviour intended to do so.

What I want is a careful mapping running in both directions. First, the case through the theory: how would a Devlin-style moralist and a Hart-style critic each reconstruct what was at stake here — who the relevant society is whose morality is supposedly in question, what the disintegration thesis would have to predict about the effects of a poem printed in a magazine with a small and largely self-selected readership, and which features of the episode each of them actually explains (the private prosecution, the absence of any mens rea requirement, the irrelevance of how few people read it, the use of the jury as the voice of community standards, the confinement of the offence to one religion, and the willingness of a senior judge to extend it to all). Second, the theory through the case, and here I have three proposals of my own that I would like you to test rather than humour. The first is an endogeneity worry: Devlin identifies the relevant positive morality by the reactions of ordinary members of society, but prosecution and the threat of it shape which reactions get expressed and by whom, and silence the dissent that would reveal a consensus to be partial — so is his test capable of giving independent evidence, or is it self-confirming in a way that no amount of jury verdicts could correct? The second is a distinction between enforcement doing causal work and enforcement doing expressive work: much of the energy behind that prosecution looks declaratory, a community saying what it stands for, and if that is what the criminal law was doing then the empirical claim about social cohesion is idle, which raises the question whether the episode supports a moralism that has dropped the empirical claim, or shows instead that Devlin needs the empirical claim and cannot survive without it. The third is a precautionary reading, on which the point about not knowing which strands of the moral fabric bear the weight is really an argument from uncertainty about social causation: if that is the best version of his argument, does it rescue him, or does it push him into licensing enforcement of whatever the majority happens to feel strongly about, and can he then explain why the precaution should run against moral reformers but not against the majority's own moral innovations? I would also like the reverse verdict: is this episode an argument for legal moralism or against it, does the shift from protecting a faith to protecting believers mark a real move to something like a harm principle or an offence principle, or is it the same moralism in different vocabulary, and does abolition without subsequent social collapse count as evidence against the disintegration thesis or would Devlin reasonably reply that the morality had itself changed so that nothing follows?

Finally, be explicit about confidence. Separate what is settled in the literature — including the point, which I gather is itself disputed, that Hart's reading of Devlin as committed to an empirical claim about social disintegration may be a misreading, and that the distinction between a moderate and an extreme version of the thesis may not hold — from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and from your own reconstruction; tell me which of your claims are interpretive rather than settled, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. Where the framework simply has nothing to say about some feature of the case, say so rather than forcing it to yield an answer, and flag anything where my three proposals above are confused or already answered in the literature. Please write in British English, and skip the standard textbook summary of the debate: I have read it.
