ok so I keep catching myself caring way too much about whether something is "the real thing," and I can't tell which of my reactions are principled and which are just snobbery with a nice coat on. I want someone to tell me what rule I'm actually following here — or that I'm following two rules that don't fit together and I should throw one out.

Here's me going by gut on a bunch of cases.

1. Generic ibuprofen instead of the name brand. Zero loss. Not even a little. If you swapped them without telling me I'd be annoyed about the pricing, not the pill.

2. A counterfeit watch that actually keeps better time than the real one. I'd still feel like a jerk wearing it, but I can't point to a single thing wrong with the object itself. Meanwhile a cheap "homage" watch that's upfront about what it is: totally fine.

3. A painting in the style of a Dutch golden age master, hung in a family's dining room for thirty years, everybody assumes it's real, they love it. Then it's exposed as a 1920s forgery. My reaction: something enormous is gone. But also: those thirty years of looking at it were not worthless, and it feels nuts to say the pleasure was fake. Both of those feel true to me and I can't see how both can be.

4. Same kind of forgery, except a museum paid millions for it and a couple of scholars then built careers on it. This strikes me as much worse than #3, and I think part of what's worse is the scholarship, not just the money.

5. A painter's own later replica of her own early painting — same materials, same care, no intent to fool anyone, signed as a second version. I feel almost no loss here. A little, maybe. Nowhere near #3.

6. A 3D-printed, molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a great 18th-century violin, indistinguishable in blind tests. I'd still take the old one, but the gap feels tiny compared to #3, and I don't trust myself on this at all.

7. Generative art: the artist writes the code and the rules, then never touches or even sees the individual outputs. I want to say those outputs are straight-up originals, no asterisk.

8. A gothic cathedral taken apart stone by stone during a war, every stone numbered, then put back up. My verdict: I honest to god do not care whether they used the numbered originals or quarried fresh stone that matches. Reverence at the rebuilt one isn't misplaced. But then why do I care so much in #3?

9. A museum dinosaur skeleton where every visible bone is a cast and the real ones are in a vault, clearly labeled. Fine. I don't feel shortchanged in the slightest.

10. A genuine 17th-century painting whose signature was added by a dealer around 1900 to bump the price. The value drops a lot, but not to zero, and it feels kind of grubby in a way that an openly-labeled copy doesn't.

11. An AI-upscaled, recolored "restoration" of a 1950s movie that is now the only version you can stream. I'd rather have the grainy original, but I don't think the film has been destroyed and calling it a forgery seems wrong.

12. A hologram-plus-AI-voice concert by a singer who died twenty years ago, advertised plainly as what it is. I'd go. I'd probably have a good time. I would not say I'd seen her.

13. A novel finished by a language model from a dead author's drafts, letters and outlines, published under her name with a big note on the cover. I'd read it, I'd probably love it, and I still would not count it as her novel. Given #7 I'm not sure I'm entitled to that.

And the one I actually go back and forth on: my grandmother's chair burned in a house fire. I had a woodworker build a copy from photographs. It's beautiful, I love it, and there's a small hollow spot in the middle of liking it. Is that hollow spot information or just me being precious? And is it wrong to tell people it's "her chair"?

My tentative principle, which I'm sure is too simple: an original matters exactly as much as what we care about depends on the object's real causal history, and a fake only loses the value that hangs on that history — so where nothing we care about hangs on history, a copy is a perfect substitute.

Test that against my list and tell me where it breaks. Specifically:

- Is there one thing doing the work, or do I need separate clauses for "was made by the person," "touched the person/process," and "wasn't meant to deceive"? Name the case that forces each clause in.
- Which pair of my verdicts can't both survive, and which one should I give up?
- What does the repaired version say about #13 and about the chair?
- Give me its verdicts on three cases I haven't listed: (a) an art work whose entire content is a set of written instructions, executed fresh by whoever is hanging the show each time, with the artist long dead; (b) me cooking a famous chef's recipe exactly right in my own kitchen — did I eat his food or mine; (c) a lab-grown diamond that's chemically identical to a mined one, which I'd wear with zero hesitation.
- And be straight with me about which of my verdicts your principle just can't accommodate, and whether that's a mark against the principle or against me.
