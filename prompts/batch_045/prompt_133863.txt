I do policy work for a small global-health foundation and help run a reading group on the ethics of triage, and recently a colleague used the slogan "the numbers count" to close an argument that I don't think it actually settled.

Our case was the standard one: a boat can reach either five stranded people or one, everything else equal, and I suggested that since each of the six has an equal claim to be rescued, fairness might favour giving each of them an equal chance of survival rather than guaranteeing the five. My colleague replied that everyone matters equally and nobody matters more than anyone else, that this just is what it means for the numbers to count, and that therefore we must save the five.

Please lay out, as a menu rather than a single interpretation, the distinct precise theses "the numbers count" could be expressing here, and mark each as trivial, substantive and defensible, or false. I would like these kept separate: counting as a claim about which outcome is impersonally better versus a claim about what the agent has most reason to do or owes particular people; counting because each person is a separate source of value versus because each person makes a separate claim on us; counting headcounts after the fact versus counting expected numbers under probabilities that are themselves rough; counting as a demand about which outcome occurs versus a demand about how chances are distributed; and whether numbers, if they matter at all, matter proportionally (is the loss of five five times the loss of one?) or merely enough to break indifference. I also can't place two wrinkles: whether the reading changes if this is a one-off rescue rather than a rule applied across many similar cases, and whether the slogan could hold at the level of institutional policy while failing to guide an individual agent facing the six.

Finally, tell me which readings, if any, would take my colleague from the equality premise to the conclusion that we must save the five rather than toss a fair coin — my suspicion is that equal concern supports a claim about chances rather than about guaranteed outcomes, but I would like that tested — and say which reading people who invoke the slogan in this debate, and those who appeal to Taurek against it, are usually relying on.
