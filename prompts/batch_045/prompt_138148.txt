I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've somehow ended up giving a twenty-minute internal talk with ten minutes of Q&A to our ML platform group next week. What I need from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of my position.

The thesis I'm defending: persistent disagreement between our human annotators should be treated as a defect report against the task specification rather than as label noise to be aggregated away. My argument leans on Aumann's agreement theorem, which I know only at roughly the level of a couple of long blog posts — as I understand it, agents who start from a common prior and whose posteriors are common knowledge cannot stably agree to disagree. My move is to read that as an engineering checklist rather than as a philosophical result: if our annotators share a prior (same training, same rubric, same calibration set), have seen each other's judgments, and still disagree, then one of the conditions has failed, and that failure is a bug with an owner. So instead of buying a better aggregation model, we run a disagreement-triage loop. Concretely, I'm proposing that every disagreement get routed into one of three bins — spec ambiguity, private-evidence asymmetry (one annotator caught a tool-call failure the other scrolled past), or prior mismatch — that annotators submit a confidence alongside a single hinge sentence (the smallest span of the item that would flip their judgment), and that we run two blind exchange rounds before adjudication in which hinge sentences are shown but the other annotator's rating is not. I also want to test whether overlapping hinge sentences predict which bin a disagreement belongs to. I'm piloting it on one eval set for six weeks; internally the loop is called Spec-Diff, and I'm open to being told the name is bad.

The audience is about twenty-five people I have to keep shipping with afterward. Roughly a dozen senior ML and data-infrastructure engineers, who think in throughput, cost per label, and pipeline latency, and who currently treat inter-annotator agreement as a threshold to hit (a chance-corrected agreement statistic plus a gold-set pass rate) rather than as a structured signal. One Bayesian statistician on loan from the ads organization, who will be the only person in the room who actually knows the theorem and its assumptions cold. Two product managers who own the launch date. And two annotator-team leads, who will hear anything about annotator error as an accusation against their people.

For context on where I expect to be hit: I've been told there is a follow-up result saying that two agents with finite information partitions who simply keep announcing their posteriors to each other converge to a common posterior in finitely many rounds, and my two blind rounds are a deliberately cheap version of that. I'm not confident I have the details right.
