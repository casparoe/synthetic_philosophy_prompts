I am putting together a session on the frame problem for an upper-level seminar on philosophy of cognitive science, and I want to spend it on Borges's story about Ireneo Funes: the young man who, after being thrown from a horse and left an invalid, discovers that he can forget nothing, and who dies young in a dark room he barely leaves. I would like a serious reading of the story through the frame problem, and an honest verdict on whether it earns its place on the syllabus.

Here is how I have been setting the problem up for the students. I distinguish three things that travel together in the literature and are worth keeping apart: the problem, in the original sense, of how many of the conditions describing a situation can be assumed to carry over unchanged when an agent acts; the ramification problem, of what else changes as a side effect of the action; and the qualification problem, of what has to hold for the intended effect to obtain at all. I then give them the two standard moralities: Fodor's argument that the difficulty is not a bug in a particular reasoning system but a symptom of what unencapsulated, central cognition is like — sensitive in principle to everything the creature believes — and the sequence of robots in Dennett's telling, each of which is destroyed because it cannot stop drawing out the consequences of its plan. I have found that students take the robots as showing that the frame problem is essentially a problem about relevance, and that they then assume a solution would be some mechanism for filtering relevance.

What I want to argue, and what I most want you to test, is that this three-way taxonomy omits a fourth member, and that the omitted one is the one Borges is actually writing about. Call it the generality problem: before you can ask which conditions persist and which change, the situation has to have been typed. The dog seen in profile at one moment and the same dog seen from the front a minute later have to be the same dog; the current predicament has to be a token of a type the agent has already learned to act on; otherwise there is nothing for a relevance filter to filter. Funes, on my reading, is not a creature who computes too many consequences or too few. He is a creature for whom nothing recurs, and so for whom no consequence can be drawn twice. His failure is a failure of abstraction rather than of relevance, and if that is right then the explanatory order in the standard framing is backwards: what we call the frame problem is downstream of the problem of concept possession, and relevance is a by-product of the similarity classes an agent has, not something computed directly by a mechanism that selects it. The practical upshot would be that the interesting work is in the theory of concepts and not in non-monotonic logic.

I also want the reading to be honest in the other direction, because I can see at least three ways it fails. First, Funes is barely an agent at all: he is paralyzed, he lies in the dark, and he does next to nothing, so the practical frame problem — the one that gets the robots blown up — hardly gets to arise. Second, the story is arguably about memory, infinity, personal identity, or the possibility of a mind without concepts, and pressing the frame problem on it may simply be reduction. Third, I cannot decide whether to lean on his paralysis at all: it invites the point that the qualification problem is literally what immobilizes him, and I worry that is a pun rather than an argument. I want you to say plainly how much of the story the frame-problem reading leaves out, and whether the omissions are fatal or whether they are what makes the story useful.

Some more concrete things I would like from you:

- Which two or three moments you would put in front of the class, and what you would say about each. I have in mind his numbering system, in which every number gets its own proper name in place of a place-value notation; his irritation that the dog glimpsed from one side should bear the same name as the dog glimpsed from the other a minute later; and the narrator's reflection that to think is to forget differences, to generalize, to abstract.
- Whether Funes is better described as suffering from too much relevance — everything is equally relevant — or from having no relevance relation at all, and whether that distinction does any work for the frame problem itself.
- Whether the ending, in which he dies young essentially of drowning in detail, is a claim about the frame problem or only a claim about memory capacity and exhaustion.
- What the story does for the debate that the robot thought-experiments cannot do. My guess is that it is something about what a life without a frame would be like from the inside, and that this is a consideration the arguments never supply; I would like you to say whether that is right or whether it is a sentimental extra.

Give me roughly a thousand to thirteen hundred words of criticism, not a plot summary and not a literature review. I would rather you commit to a reading and then say where it breaks than balance three readings against one another. And please do not spend words on citations or suggestions for further reading; I know the literature and I want the reading.
