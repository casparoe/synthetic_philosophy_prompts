I'm drafting a short position paper for a seminar on classical Indian epistemology and I want someone to poke holes in my package before I hand it in. I'm broadly Nyāya-leaning but I've picked up commitments from all over, and I have a feeling a couple of them are quietly fighting each other. Here's where I've ended up:

1. Knowledge is a true, non-memorial cognitive episode produced by a pramāṇa. Realist, correspondence-ish, no coherence theory stuff.
2. Validity is extrinsic. A cognition doesn't certify itself; we establish that it was true by a further inference, paradigmatically from acting on it and having it work out (the water really did slake my thirst). I reject the Mīmāṃsā intrinsic-validity line, and I take the same view about invalidity: you find out you were wrong from outside too.
3. Perception is the base pramāṇa, and I want a strict reading of it: sense–object contact with something that's actually present, plus the old requirement that perception be non-deviating — which I read as part of what makes something perception rather than just a cognition that happens to be caused by a sense.
4. I think the extra-ordinary kinds of perception the later Naiyāyikas admit are ad hoc patches, and I'd rather do without all of them: perceiving a universal across every one of its instances at once, yogic perception of everything, and the rope-snake snake showing up because the memory of the snake itself does the work of the sense-relation. I know the usual motivation for the first one is that it's what gets you from a handful of observed smoky fires to a pervasion covering smoke as such, but I think repeated observation plus reasoning by reductio can carry that on its own.
5. But I also want to say absence is directly perceived: you look at the floor and you just see that there's no pot on it. No seperate pramāṇa of non-apprehension.
6. And I'm attached to the Nyāya story about illusion — the snake on the rope is something that really is a snake, elsewhere, getting presented as right here. A misplacement, not a fusion of two bits of cognition by memory, and not a projection of nothing.
7. Testimony is genuinely its own pramāṇa, not reducible to inference, and I want to keep it even for things you'll never get to check: someone tells me a fact about my great-grandfather, no successful action is coming, and I still want to say I come to know it.
8. Whenever I'm having a cognition I'm immediately in a position to know that I'm having it. I dont need a further inference for that, and I don't think it commits me to anything like self-luminosity.

Which of these really pull against each other, which only look like they do, and what's the cheapest way back to consistency? Rank the tensions by how bad they are instead of giving me six equally weighted problems, and if there's one revision that would clean up the most, say which. I genuinely don't know which item I'd give up — I like all of them — so mostly I want to know what each option would cost me.
