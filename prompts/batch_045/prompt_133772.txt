I am on a small working group that has been asked to draft the consent and succession rules that would govern a gradual neural replacement procedure: the subject's neurons are swapped, a few thousand at a time, for prosthetic units tuned to reproduce their input-output behaviour, so that at no individual step is there a moment at which anyone would want to say the person has gone out of existence, while at the end of the process none of the original tissue remains. The lawyers on the group do not want a verdict on whether the procedure preserves the person. They want to know what a theory of personal identity would have to be able to do in order to be usable as the basis for a statute — and they want to know that before anyone starts citing their favourite theory at them. So the question I am putting to you is this: what are the desiderata?

Fix the purpose as narrowly as that: a theory good enough to be written into rules that will be applied by registrars, judges and clinicians to people who have never read philosophy and who may reasonably disagree about the metaphysics. Given that purpose, give me the requirements such a theory must meet — the cases it has to return a verdict on, the distinctions it is not allowed to collapse, the results from neuroscience and from ordinary biology that it cannot contradict — and, for each requirement, the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a taste. I want the reasoning to sit in the criteria, not in a scorecard.

For orientation, here are the cases I keep coming back to, which you should use but need not be limited by. First, the cold swap: a prosthetic unit is trained in advance on a recording of the unit it replaces and runs from that recording rather than from live input for its first weeks, so behaviour is seamless while the causal link to the surrounding tissue is not what it normally is. Second, the same total replacement carried out over twenty years and over a single afternoon under anaesthetic; I want to know whether a theory may let the rate of replacement change the verdict, and if it may, what work the rate is doing. Third, the dormant duplicate: a complete prosthetic version is built before the procedure begins and then kept inert and unopened while the biological original is gradually replaced, and is destroyed at the end; whether the mere existence of an unused duplicate can matter seems to me the sharpest test I have. Fourth, the re-implantation: the removed tissue is preserved, kept non-functional, and then re-implanted in reverse order after the prosthetic system has been running for some months, so that from a certain date two candidates present themselves. Fifth, the thinning, in which each unit is replaced by two units that share its job between them. And sixth, the institutional questions the statute actually has to answer: does the post-procedure candidate own the pre-procedure person's house, answer for that person's debts and crimes, remain married, and count as a victim of homicide if switched off — and is the theory permitted to answer yes to some of these and no to others, or must it be all-or-nothing?

To that last point, please build in the case that most interests me: a subject signs a directive before the procedure instructing the team to halt if at any stage her capacity to recognise her family falls below a stated threshold; at two-thirds replacement she revokes it and demands that the process continue. A theory that cannot say anything about which of those two expressions of will governs is not usable for my purpose, and I would like you to press on whether any theory can say it without assuming what is in dispute.

I also want your view on which of the demands usually made in this area are genuine desiderata and which are the prejudices of one camp: the demand for a determinate yes-or-no answer, the demand that the theory invoke no further fact beyond the physical and functional ones, the demand that it vindicate the first-personal conviction of the person who is about to undergo the procedure, the demand that it treat the biological and the prosthetic on a par. And I suspect at least one pair of genuine requirements cannot be jointly satisfied — in particular the requirement that no single swap be pivotal, which the gradualness of the procedure seems to force, and the requirement that the verdict track something that could be lost by degrees rather than all at once. If you think that pair is satisfiable, say how; if you think a different pair is the trouble, say which. Then tell me, in a sentence or two, which single requirement does the most work in excluding the theories you are aware of, and which theory it excludes.

Close by telling me which requirement you would abandon first if forced, and what argument or new case would make you abandon it — I want to know where your reasoning is most likely to fail, not just where you land. Do not cite anything or point me to a literature; I need to be able to use this without a library. And write it as continuous prose for a memo I am already assembling — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
