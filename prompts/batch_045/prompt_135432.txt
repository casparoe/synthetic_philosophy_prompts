I'm writing a seminar paper on mechanical rigidity and freedom, and an argument my study group built now looks to me like it establishes far more than any of us wanted. We start from the textbook case, which I'll restate so nothing depends on the biology: a digger wasp drags its paralyzed prey to the threshold of its burrow, leaves it there, goes inside to inspect the nest, comes back out and hauls the prey in. If an experimenter moves the prey a short distance while the wasp is inside, the wasp emerges, drags it back to the threshold, and goes in to inspect again — and this can be repeated many times over. I gather the empirical story about the real insects is messier than this version, but I don't want the argument to rest on that, so please read it as a description of a possible mechanism rather than as a claim about wasps. Call the phenomenon sphexishness. The point we take the case to establish is that the defect is not determinism as such: what is wrong is a kind of insensitivity, the wasp continuing to run a sequence whose purpose has already been achieved because its behavior is driven by an internal schedule rather than by how things now stand. From there we argued as follows.

(1) An agent's behavior is defective in the sphexish way exactly when it is insensitive to a difference in the situation that matters for the agent's goal.
(2) So an action is free only if the mechanism producing it is sensitive to every difference that matters for the agent's goal, whatever that difference turns out to be: what separates free action from the wasp's is flexibility, not indeterminacy.
(3) Goals are themselves just further internal states of the agent, states that cause and organize its behavior; nothing outside the agent's own mechanism gets to say which differences matter.
(4) But no mechanism of the sort an embodied creature could have is sensitive to every difference. For any agent with a finite repertoire there are indefinitely many differences in the world it cannot register, including differences that matter given its own goals.
(5) So every agent is sphexish about something: for each of us there is some intervention an experimenter could perform that would expose rigid, point-missing behavior.
(6) If rigidity in a domain is enough to make the wasp's action unfree in that domain, then by parity each of us acts unfreely in every domain where we are insensitive — and which domains those are depends on which interventions happen to be available to whoever is watching.
(7) So no one ever acts freely. Freedom is at best a matter of degree, and the degree a creature gets is fixed by the curiosity of its experimenter.

I can't accept (7), and the argument has a second consequence I find even harder to take: on this criterion the fumbling novice, who notices everything and is thrown off by every perturbation, comes out freer than the expert whose performance has become automatized, and a system that tracks a very wide range of features of its inputs — a chess engine, a language model responding to prompts — comes out freer than a tired person running on autopilot. That cannot be the right result. So what I want is a diagnosis rather than a survey. Which step do you reject, and why did that step look safe when we wrote it down — what did it appear to be saying, and what is it actually committing us to? Then price it: if I abandon that premise, what can I still say about what is wrong with the wasp, about why the expert is not less free than the novice, and about whether any version of the flexibility requirement survives? If you think the chain is valid and I simply have to deny one of the premises, tell me which denial is cheapest and what else it commits me to. For what it's worth, I would most like to keep the judgment that something is genuinely defective in the wasp's behavior and that the defect is not merely that the wasp is predictable; I am much more willing to give up the claim that flexibility does the separating, but only if you can put something in its place or explain why nothing needs to go there. If one of the familiar styles of account — reasons-responsiveness, hierarchical or mesh accounts, guidance control, or the evitability-style readings — handles the argument better, say so briefly and say what it pays.
