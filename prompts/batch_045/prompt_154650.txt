In my Bayesian reading group everyone leans on this move: ignorance is just like a die. You dont know which face will come up, so you spread credence evenly over the faces; likewise if you know nothing about a parameter you spread it evenly over its range. It gets used to licence two things: that a uniform prior is the neutral, assumption-free default, and that Bertrand's chord puzzle is a parlour trick about a badly posed question rather than a problem for indifference itself (the three classic setups give 1/3, 1/2 and 1/4, and the line in the group is that those are just three different questions).

Someone offered a fix for the paradox that I half believe: discretise. Chop the circle into N chord-slots or a fine grid and you get a canonical counting measure, so the methods stop fighting — meaning the paradox is just an artifact of the continuum, and the die analogy is fine whenever everything is finite.

I'd like a real audit rather than the usual shrug. Give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the faces, of the die, of the symmetry that makes the faces interchangeable. Then the breaks. Specifically: does the discretisation fix actually work, or does it just relocate the choice of parametrisation (and if so, as N grows do the three answers converge or stay apart)? What does the die have that the chord doesn't — a readymade atomic partition, exchangeability, a symmetry group that pins the measure down? And if we patch things by demanding invariance under rotation, translation and scale, is that repairing indifference or quietly replacing it with a substantive assumption?

My own inkling is that the faces come as a partition given in advance while the chords don't, and that this is where it all turns, but I cant say why that matters so much.
