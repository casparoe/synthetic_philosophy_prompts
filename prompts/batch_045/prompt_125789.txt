I am a professor of philosophy and I chair the external ethics advisory board of a mid-sized AI lab that deploys a general-purpose assistant. Each year the board publishes a short public report, and this year I have to write the section that states what the lab's engineering practices commit it to — not in the sense of its mission statement, but in the sense of what would have to be true about value, knowledge, and disagreement for those practices to be a sensible way of building the thing. I can describe the machinery but I cannot get the implicit philosophy to come out sharp, and my draft keeps collapsing into the observation that the contractors' preferences get baked in, which is true but says nothing about which parts of the package are optional.

The practice is a composite of what several labs do; treat it as a stylized description and attribute nothing to any particular company. It has five elements.

First, the base of the pipeline is preference learning from pairwise comparisons: a few dozen paid contractors, mostly from two or three countries, read two short model outputs and pick the better one; disagreements are resolved by majority vote; a reward model is fit to the aggregate and the assistant is optimized against it. Contractors are instructed to apply a written specification rather than report their own reactions.

Second, the deployment system prompt tells the model to answer factual and technical questions with the best-supported answer and to hold that answer even when the user pushes back, but on moral, political, and religious questions to lay out the main considerations and hand the decision back to the user rather than answer. Call this the Socratic default.

Third, certain request classes are refused outright, whatever the user's values and whatever the user says their reasons are: assistance with self-harm, with violence against others, and with a specified band of dual-use technical information. Refusals are tuned to be brief and non-preachy.

Fourth — and this is the element I find hardest to place — the lab practices what I will call disagreement-preserving upweighting. When the annotation pool splits close to evenly on a comparison, that example is deliberately upweighted in training rather than discarded or settled by a supervisor, on the stated rationale that persistent disagreement among reasonable people marks a genuine plurality of values, so the model should learn to keep the option space open and to represent both sides instead of learning to pick one.

Fifth, much of the safety fine-tuning data is generated by the model itself: the model writes critiques of its own outputs against a written list of principles, revises them, and the revisions are filtered by the same reward model trained in the first element and then trained on. Later rounds of the reward model are in turn retrained on comparisons of outputs from the improved model. Call this self-distillation of the supervisor.

Here is what puzzles me. The second and fourth elements treat evaluative disagreement as something to be preserved and returned to the user, the third overrules it without apology, the first treats the target as whatever survives aggregation of actual responses to a written specification, and the fifth lets the regulated system generate the content of the regulation, subject to a filter descended from the first element. That looks like four different theories of value wearing one lab badge, but I cannot tell how much of the appearance is real.

So, please do the following.

Reconstruct the implicit view as a single position, or as a position plus an explicit list of exceptions. Say what it holds about what value is and where it comes from, about whether there is such a thing as moral expertise and who would have it, about what persistent disagreement among apparently reasonable people is evidence of, and about why the factual and the evaluative are treated so differently in the second element. Identify the nearest recognizable position in moral or political philosophy — I suspect something in the neighborhood of public-reason liberalism, but say what in the package does not fit that.

Then separate strict presupposition from comfortable fit. For each element, say what the lab would be committed to merely by continuing to do it, and what it is free to believe instead. In particular, tell me where the practice is underdetermined: are there two or more quite different theories of value that would each license this whole package, so that the board cannot read a commitment off the engineering at all?

Say which of the apparent tensions are real contradictions and which dissolve once a distinction is drawn that the lab has not drawn but could — for instance between harm to third parties and the user's own good, between what is true and what may legitimately be imposed, between a model's output as an act of assistance and the same content as information, or between the distribution of annotator opinion as a measurement and as something to be respected.

Then take two rivals and say concretely what each would change. One rival holds that there is real moral expertise and that some people have more of it; the other holds that value is response-dependent but that the lab's job is to track idealized rather than actual responses. For each, tell me which of the five elements would have to be altered, how, and what the lab would lose in capability or in public acceptability by altering them.

Finally, assess the fifth element on its own. Is there a real bootstrapping or diagonalization worry about a system whose normative regulation is generated by earlier versions of itself, or does the human-derived filter break the loop? And is the fourth element's rationale coherent: is persistent disagreement in a small, demographically narrow, paid, time-pressured pool even the right kind of disagreement to bear the weight the lab puts on it?
