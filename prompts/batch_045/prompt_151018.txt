Hey — hoping you can help me draft something, because I'm out of my depth and the meeting is next week.

I teach philosophy here (mostly philosophy of law and social philosophy) and somehow let myself get talked onto the university's Commemoration and Honors Committee, the body that decides whose names stay on buildings, and we now have the worst case we've had. Vance Hall is named for Dr. Hollis Vance, dean of our medical school from 1974 to 1989. Between roughly 1981 and 1986 he oversaw a drug-safety study run on about 400 men at a state prison farm in the next county; the men signed consent forms, but the forms didn't mention the risk of permanent liver damage, and by the time it ended several had chronic illness and two had died. None of it was hidden — it was published in a well-regarded journal, it got cited approvingly in federal guidance, and in 1991 this university gave Vance its Distinguished Service Medal for it. He died in 2016. Under our new Values Review Framework, adopted last spring, honors may be withdrawn for conduct conflicting with the university's core commitments regardless of when the conduct occurred, so nobody disputes that we're permitted to act. Three things are making it miserable. First, the obvious one: this is a sanction under a rule that didn't exist when he acted, and I keep hearing the Nuremberg defense in my head — the aggressive-war charges were exactly what defense counsel said had been invented after the fact — and my colleague Dana, a historian and the most pro-removal person on the committee, says I have to choose: either those judges were hypocrites or it was legal at the time is never a defense. I don't think that's a real fork, but I can't say why yet. Second, what I'd call the victors' justice problem, which bites harder: we opened Vance's file and not two others. Bramble Hall is named for a man whose company ran very similar prisoner research for a different state, and his grandson sits on our board in the middle of a roughly $40m campaign; Olds Field is named for a university counsel who signed off on Vance's protocol. We voted 4–3 to examine Vance alone, and it's hard to miss that he's dead, he was never well liked, and his children aren't donors. Third, there's a real reason to act and a real reason to be suspicious of acting: some of the men's families still live in the county and one descendant works in our mailroom and has asked for the name to come down — but the university itself funded and decorated this work, so scrubbing Vance's name looks a lot like laundering our own history. Oh, and Vance's daughter, who's a lawyer, wants to be heard; she points out that her father wrote a 1976 essay insisting research subjects must be told enough to refuse, and says he believed the forms were adequate, which strikes me as damning or exculpating depending on how charitably you read him.

The drafting subcommittee has floated three routes and I have to pick one and defend it: (1) removal, with the name scrubbed and a press statement; (2) no removal on retroactivity grounds, file closed; (3) what one member calls reclassification without condemnation — Vance Hall gets a new name, the old name goes on a documented historical marker that sets out what happened, and the university explicitly declines to call Vance a bad man or to describe the move as punishment. There's also a procedural proposal I like, a parity clause: no name comes down until we've opened files on every comparable honoree within twelve months, or else we suspend removals entirely. And a substantive test I half-invented, the counterfactual notice test: could a reasonable person in his position, using only the norms available to him at the time, have foreseen that this conduct would later be treated as sanctionable? What I need is a memo I can circulate to the committee on Tuesday and then send on to the Board of Trustees, who vote on the 14th. Bottom line up front, please — pick one of the three, say which — and give me reasoning the people who disagree can actually answer, because Dana will read it closely and will be annoyed if I merely assert that retroactive sanction is fine here. Say 900 words or so, addressed to the committee, and include: the recommendation on Vance; whether to adopt the parity clause and why; an honest, fully-loaded statement of the single strongest argument on the side you're rejecting, stated at least as well as the winning side's; and two or three criteria we could apply to Bramble and Olds next year so we aren't reinventing the rules every time — and there definately will be a next time. Please don't give me footnotes, sources, or a reading list; I've read plenty of this literature, I just need something I can put in front of eleven tired people who want to go home.
