I'm in the final year of an undergraduate programme in philosophy and statistics, and I'm taking a module on causal inference and the philosophy of applied statistics. Last week's seminar was on Simpson's paradox, and most of the discussion was about how a reversal shows that the aggregate comparison can be an artefact of an unbalanced mix, so that one should always disaggregate before drawing a conclusion. That framing struck me as too quick: surely whether the disaggregated comparison is the trustworthy one depends on what the stratifying variable is doing causally, and there must be cases where the subgroup comparisons are the artefact and the headline number is the one to act on. I'd like a case built to order that makes exactly that point, since I want to use it in a short presentation and I need something sharper than the two textbook warhorses (admissions broken down by department, and stone size in a urology trial).

Could you construct, to the following specifications, a concrete case in which the reversal runs against the subgroups: the new protocol (call it A) has the better overall success rate, while the established protocol (B) has the better success rate within every stratum of the stratifying variable — and yet A is unambiguously the protocol a decision-maker should adopt. Constraints: (i) a realistic institutional setting of your own choosing, with both arms a few hundred patients and every cell of the table at least about sixty strong, so nothing rests on small-sample noise or on rounding; (ii) the aggregate advantage for A should be at least five percentage points and the within-stratum advantage for B at least eight percentage points in every stratum, so the reversal is robust rather than marginal; (iii) the stratifying variable must be something the treatment itself brings about rather than a pre-treatment difference between the groups — in the usual jargon, a consequence or mediator of the treatment, not a confounder — and your story must make it plain that the clinicians had no usable pre-treatment proxy they could have stratified on instead, since otherwise the easy reply is that they simply ought to have measured baseline severity and disaggregated by that; (iv) the decision problem must be pinned down so that the reversal has practical bite: a future patient drawn from the same population, about whom the decision-maker will not learn which stratum they fall into before the protocol has to be chosen, so a single protocol must be adopted for everyone and the quantity of interest is the total effect on that population. Please give the full integer table (counts, not just rates), set out the causal structure in words or as a list of arrows, and then do the arithmetic explicitly: the aggregate risk difference, the stratum-specific risk differences, and which of the two verdicts is the causally correct one for the decision as specified, with a sentence on why the composition effect arises.

Three further things would help me see whether the construction actually works rather than stipulating its conclusion. First, tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, and show me what happens when it is removed: re-run the same numbers with the stratifying variable reinterpreted as a pre-treatment confounder instead of a post-treatment consequence, and confirm that the verdict then flips and the disaggregated comparison becomes the trustworthy one. Second, give the strongest reply available to someone who insists that the subgroup analysis is the one to follow — my sense is that they will say they were never after the total effect but after a direct effect holding the post-treatment variable fixed, and I'd like you to say both whether that is a genuine estimand worth wanting here and whether naive stratification on the post-treatment variable actually identifies it. Third, say whether the reversal is measure-relative: does it survive a switch from risk differences to odds ratios, or is some of the drama an artefact of the scale? Finally, close with one line stating what the case is supposed to establish, so I can check whether your scenario earns that claim or merely builds the answer into the setup.
