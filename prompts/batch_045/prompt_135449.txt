I sit on the grants committee of a small foundation that funds work on astronomical and existential risks — we meet every six weeks and our decisions are usually about things like asteroid detection and pandemic preparedness, so we are used to large numbers but not to this kind of argument. Over the last few months I have gotten interested in panpsychism: the view, as I have come to understand it, that the fundamental physical entities have experiential properties, so that consciousness does not switch on at some threshold of complexity but is instead built out of experiences that were there all the way down. The argument that convinced me is the one about the intrinsic nature of the physical — that physics tells us only what matter does and not what it is, and that the only thing we have any positive conception of that could be the intrinsic nature of a physical entity is experience. That strikes me as close to the default position rather than a wild one.

But if it is true, I cannot see how it does not swamp every other cause on our docket, and I have to present something to the committee on Thursday. I was discussing this with a friend who does astrophysics, and she sketched the following: granted that every particle carries some experience, the number of physical events in the observable universe is roughly the number of particle interactions, so the overwhelming majority of the universe's experience-events are not in brains at all but in hot, high-energy astrophysical processes — stellar interiors and, above all, accretion disks around black holes, where gravitational energy is being dissipated at a rate she put, on the back of an envelope, at something like forty orders of magnitude above the total metabolic energy throughput of all terrestrial animals across all of history. (I have not checked her number.) If even a small fraction of experience-events carry negative valence, then essentially all of the suffering that has ever existed is happening in quasars, animal suffering is a rounding error, and the rational thing for our committee to do is to stop funding anything else and start treating suffering in fundamental physics as the dominant cause area. When I have floated this to people who know the literature, they tell me that panpsychism is not pan-sentience and that suffering requires a fairly specific functional organization — nociception, goals that can be frustrated, something at stake — but that sounds to me like a way of keeping the metaphysics while avoiding the ethics: if the electron's experience is not valenced, what makes it an experience rather than a bare disposition, and if valence needs a functional organization then have you not reintroduced exactly the emergence the view was supposed to avoid?

So I would like help pressure-testing the chain before Thursday. Is the move from experiences all the way down to astronomically large quantities of suffering actually licensed by the argument I accepted, or does something break between the intrinsic-nature premise and the valence premise — and if it breaks, is there a principled way to say how much experience the universe contains and how much of it matters, or is the honest answer that the framework gives us no grip on either quantity? If my friend's accounting is right in outline, should we really be redirecting our grants toward astrophysical processes, and is there anything concrete a foundation like ours could fund to estimate or reduce such a thing? And if the answer is that we cannot act on it, I would like to know whether that is a merely practical limitation or whether it reveals that the whole exercise of sizing the universe's suffering from fundamental physics is ill-posed in a way I should have noticed.
