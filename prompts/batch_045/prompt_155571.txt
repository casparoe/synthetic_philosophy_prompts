THE NOTES

1. The target view. Call it Lexical Threshold Negative Utilitarianism (LTNU). Mental states have hedonic intensities on a single signed scale, negative for suffering and positive for pleasure. There is an intensity threshold t such that suffering at or beyond t in intensity is lexically dominant in disvalue: no finite amount of pleasure compensates it. Suffering milder than t is commensurable with pleasure at some finite exchange rate k. The view is designed to thread a needle: it avoids the implication of strict negative utilitarianism that a pinprick outweighs any quantity of bliss, while retaining the intuition that some suffering — agony, torture — is not paid off by the pleasures that accompany or follow it.

2. The commensurability quarrel, and my diagnosis of it. LTNU presupposes a single scale on which both pleasures and pains sit. Two pictures compete. On the single-axis picture, pleasure is negative suffering, the zero point is genuine neutrality, and intensities are cardinally measurable and interpersonally comparable. On the dual-channel picture, positive and negative affect are at least partly separable systems: abolishing pain does not thereby produce pleasure, so the question how much pleasure cancels this pain is not a measurement but a stipulation. My hypothesis is that this dispute is partly verbal. If hedonic value means a decision-theoretic currency — what you would be rationally required to accept in trade — then the single-axis claim is close to analytic, since being a currency just is being commensurable. If it means a psychological or neurobiological magnitude, the dual-channel picture may well win and still leave the ethical question open, because the ethical question needs a currency and not a magnitude. On that reading the two camps are answering different questions under one heading. I want to concede in the piece that this diagnosis only goes part of the way: the single-axis view also carries the substantive empirical claim that intensity is cardinally measurable across persons, and that claim is not verbal.

3. My alternative, which I call Saturation Lexicalism. Instead of a cliff in the intensity of an individual episode, put a ceiling M on the total amount of suffering that is compensable at all. Let S be the aggregate of suffering in a world, measured in intensity-duration units, and let the pleasure required to compensate it be P(S) = k·S·M/(M − S) for S below M, with no finite P compensating S at or above M. This has three features I like: near S = 0 it is approximately proportional, so ordinary mild pains cost ordinary pleasures; it steepens sharply, so very large aggregates of mild suffering become effectively non-compensable, which recovers the intuition behind the torture case without locating a cliff in the intensity of any single episode; and past M compensation is impossible in principle rather than merely very expensive. Four problems I can already see. First, the arbitrariness has not disappeared, only migrated: why should M sit where it sits, and why should it be a feature of aggregates rather than episodes? Second, and I think this is the likely killer, the view is no longer separable: whether a given unit of pain is compensable, and at what price, depends on how much suffering exists elsewhere and elsewhen, so two populations each comfortably below M can be compensable separately and yet non-compensable together. Third, because S is extensive in duration as well as intensity, a sufficiently long mild headache drives S toward M and becomes non-compensable — lexical priority ends up doing its work on duration, not intensity, which inverts the intuition that motivated LTNU in the first place. Fourth, the view recovers effective but not literal non-compensability: for any finite S below M there is some finite P, so torture remains compensable by an astronomical quantity of bliss. I am unsure whether that fourth point is a defect or the feature I should be selling.

4. Indexing the threshold. Here is where my reporting matters. The welfare biologists I have interviewed disagree about whether animals with small nervous systems can suffer with anything like the intensity of a human's worst pain, and under LTNU that empirical question is ethically decisive — but only on one reading of the threshold. If t is an absolute hedonic intensity, common to all creatures, then a creature whose entire achievable range lies on the mild side of t can never generate lexically dominant suffering, and in principle the worst thing that can happen to it is compensable by pleasures elsewhere. If t is indexed to a creature's own achievable hedonic range — the worst state available to a creature of that kind, or some fixed percentile of its range — then every creature capable of suffering has states that dominate any amount of pleasure, and the empirical question largely drops out of the ethics. I lean toward range-indexing, because it tracks what I take to be the functional role of the concept: the worst thing that can happen to a creature like this. But it has a consequence I find hard to accept, which is that a being whose maximum possible suffering is mild in absolute terms nonetheless has lexically dominant states, and that lexical priority then attaches in virtue of a counterfactual — what the creature could have felt — rather than what it actually felt, which sits oddly with a view that presents itself as being about actual suffering.

5. Lexicality and decision theory. If some states are lexically dominant, then ordinary expected-value comparison breaks down: a lottery carrying a tiny probability of a lexically dominant outcome outranks any finite certainty, so preventing a one-in-a-trillion chance of agony dominates providing large certain benefits. The obvious repair is to lexicographise the expectation itself and compare lexically weighted expected values, but as far as I can tell that just relocates the fanaticism rather than removing it, and it is precisely this structure that makes suffering-focused arguments look like Pascal's mugging to my editors. The question I want the piece to press is whether LTNU can be stated so that it is action-guiding under uncertainty without either collapsing into fanaticism or collapsing back into ordinary finite-exchange-rate consequentialism.
