I am writing a chapter of my PhD thesis in political theory on the conceptual question of what counts as corruption, and I keep running into the same dissatisfaction with the formulation that dominates both the policy literature and the philosophical one: the principal—agent definition, on which corruption is roughly the abuse of entrusted power for private gain, or the deviation of an office-holder from the formal duties of their role in pursuit of some private advantage. It seems to me to misclassify the cases that actually matter for the cross-cultural worry. A small gift pressed on a rural clinic nurse or a municipal clerk, where refusal would be a grave insult and no favour is requested or given, comes out as corruption only if one stretches 'private gain' past breaking point, yet it is precisely the sort of case that Western anti-corruption campaigns treat as a symptom. And clientelism — a councillor who steers a drainage contract or a handful of public jobs to a neighbourhood that reliably turns out for her — comes out as straightforwardly corrupt on the standard view, even though the exchange is open, expected by everyone involved, and arguably a form of political responsiveness rather than a departure from it. The view I have been developing in response I call the avowal account: an exercise of public power is corrupt, on this view, when it is governed by a consideration that the office-holder could not avow as a reason in the justification they owe for that decision — so that what matters is not who gained, nor even whether a duty was breached, but whether the operative reason survives the publicity that office imposes. (I take the anthropological point about gifts creating bonds of obligation rather than transferring prices to be what makes the gift case interesting here, and I take the 'consideration' in question to include motives that are never spoken: the donor's phone call that mentions nothing at all.)

What I would like is a careful comparison of the two accounts rather than a verdict on mine. Please set them against each other along roughly these axes, and add any axis you think I have missed: whether each needs a determinate account of the office's purpose or of the public interest that is independent of the office-holder's own avowable reasons, and what happens to it if no such account can be given; whether the avowal condition is genuinely independent of the entrusted-power formulation or merely a redescription of it, given that the duties of a role might already specify what can and cannot be avowed; how each handles the three cases above and, in particular, whether they diverge on gift-giving and on clientelism or quietly converge once the details are filled in; and whether the avowal account really escapes the charge of parochialism that is pressed against Western anti-corruption norms — since what a society will accept as an avowable reason for an official act is itself shaped by local expectations about office, obligation and friendship, my test may simply relocate the ethnocentrism one level down. I would also value your view on one downstream question: if the avowal account were right, would it support the line that anti-bribery law and corporate compliance practice try to draw between bribes and hospitality, or would it dissolve it, given that hospitality can equally be the sort of consideration that cannot be avowed?

Since this is going into a thesis chapter, please be disciplined about the epistemic status of what you say: distinguish claims that are settled or near-settled among people who work on these questions from claims that are genuinely contested, and mark clearly where you are reconstructing my own view rather than reporting anyone's. Where the two accounts' verdicts on a case depend on a contested premise — about whether offices have determinate purposes, about the nature of gift obligation, about whether the principal—agent model fits political office at all — say so explicitly rather than presenting the resulting verdict as the consensus. Tell me what would have to be true for the avowal account to be preferable to the standard one, what you take the strongest objection to be, and where you think the comparison simply runs out because the question is not settled. Please write in British English.
