I run a small reading group — four of us, all longtime readers of the rationalist blogs — and for the last month we have been arguing about a principle we had been leaning on without much scrutiny. Call it *Openness*: for every true proposition *p*, it is possible that someone knows *p*, where the someone is allowed to be a different agent, in a different possible situation, with different capacities, more time, and better instruments than we have, and where knowledge is assumed to be factive (if someone knows *p* then *p*) and to distribute over conjunction (if someone knows *p* and *q* then someone knows *p*). We liked Openness because it does real work as a razor: when a mysterian claims a fact that no possible investigator could ever get into a position to know — whether there is anything it is like to be a certain system, which way a spectrum is inverted, what the fundamental furniture is behind two empirically equivalent theories — we say that if it could not be known even in principle, we should suspect there is no fact there at all. Then one of us sat down, noticed that this is basically the shape of Fitch's 1963 result, and built the following argument, which now seems to collapse Openness into something none of us believe and, worse, to generalize into refuting the restricted versions we retreated to.

(1) Openness holds: for every true *p*, possibly someone knows *p*. (2) Some truths are in fact unknown: there is a *u* such that *u* is true and nobody knows *u* (take whether the number of grains of sand on Earth at a specified instant was even). (3) The conjunction *c* = (*u* and nobody knows *u*) is itself true. (4) By Openness applied to *c*, possibly someone knows *c*. (5) But in any possible situation in which someone knows *c*: by distribution, someone in that situation knows *u*, and someone in that situation knows that nobody knows *u*; by factivity applied within that situation, nobody in that situation knows *u*; contradiction. (6) So *c* is not possibly known; by (1) and (3) *c* is both possibly known and not possibly known, so Openness is false — and if you run the same derivation without assuming (2) you get the stronger result that Openness entails every truth is known by somebody. (7) Nothing in (4)–(5) used anything about knowledge beyond factivity and distribution, so the collapse generalizes: replace *known* with any factive, conjunction-distributing status and *everything is in principle so-and-so-able* entails *everything is actually so-and-so-ed*. (8) Two consequences make us think the argument has overreached rather than discovered something. First, take restricted Openness for mathematics: every mathematical truth is knowable by some possible ideal agent. The generalized argument would force every mathematical truth to be actually known — yet the Moorean conjunction used in (4), *φ* and nobody knows *φ*, is not a mathematical proposition, so it is not clear the mathematical principle ever has to apply to it; the argument seems to be silently assuming that whatever domain you restrict to is closed under conjoining with *and this is unknown*, which holds for empirical truths about who knows what and apparently fails for mathematical ones. Second, take writability: every truth could in principle be written down correctly by some possible agent. That strikes all of us as close to obvious — for any particular truth, some possible agent could just inscribe the right answer — yet the same reasoning yields that every truth is actually written down somewhere, and hence that some truths are, absurdly, *unwritable*.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Please name the exact step, explain why it looked innocent, and price the alternatives rather than just picking one — we would most like to keep (2), we would like to keep *something* in the neighborhood of a restricted Openness that still bites against mysterianism, and we would very much like not to end up as idealists who say that every truth is already known by somebody. In particular, please address two replies we have been circling: the suggestion that the flaw is a scope or rigidification slide, so that the second conjunct of *c* should be read as *actually* unknown, letting an agent in another possible situation know *u* while also knowing that we do not — in which case say whether that repair survives a revenge construction built on the proposition that *u* is true and not possibly known by anyone, and whether a rigidified Openness still has any force as a razor; and the suggestion that the argument is perfectly valid and the only question is which premise is least costly to abandon, including what the verificationist pays if she simply bites the bullet on step (6). Kindly write your answer in American English.
