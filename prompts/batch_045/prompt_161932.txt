I'm a senior at a big public high school in Ohio, and I run our school's giving club — we raise a couple of thousand dollars a year from bake sales and a spring 5K, and a committee of six of us decides where it goes. I've applied to study public policy next fall, so I've been trying to be more careful about the words I use, and there's a pattern in our meetings I can't name. Three versions of it, from this year. One: we weighed buying insecticide-treated bed nets through a charity that publishes a cost per net and an estimate of what a dollar accomplishes, against giving the same money to a local tenants' union that keeps people from being evicted. Half the room thought the housing work might matter more, since an eviction wrecks everything downstream for a family, but its effects are spread over years, depend on landlords and judges and city budgets, and nobody can say what would have happened otherwise — so it has no number, and we funded the nets without anyone once claiming the nets were better. We claimed they were the ones we could evaluate. Two: once we'd chosen the nets, we started tracking cost per net delivered, and then cost per net actually hanging over a bed, and each refinement was a genuine improvement and also quietly changed what we were paying for. Three: a tutoring program pitched us with a spreadsheet of hours delivered; everyone in the room said out loud that the number would rise if tutors just sat with students longer without teaching them anything, and we funded it anyway, while a proposal about how the school's discipline system treats kids never made the shortlist, because there's no spreadsheet for it.

What makes me think this is one thing rather than three is that the filter doesn't pick arbitrarily — it picks in a direction. Everything that survives has an effect that is near-term, happens to an identifiable person, and can be attributed to us; everything that gets screened out is systemic, delayed, or works by changing how an institution behaves. It also seems to feed on itself: the option that has a number is the one that gets funded, which is the one that gets audited, studied, and refined, so next year it looks even stronger next to the option nobody funded and nobody bothered to measure. A friend from policy debate says this is Goodhart's law, and someone else mentioned a drunk looking for his keys under a streetlight. Both feel adjacent and neither feels right. Goodhart, as I understand it, is about a measure getting gamed once it becomes a target — but in my first case nobody is gaming anything and the number is honest; the trouble is that only one of the options has a number at all. The streetlight story seems to be about searching where the light is, but it doesn't capture the directional tilt, or the way this year's choice decides what next year's light will be. So: is there an established concept for the whole pattern, or for the part that isn't Goodhart? Is my list of three really one phenomenon with two faces — selection and drift — or two that merely travel together? And where are the edges? I'd be annoyed to be handed a single label; I want the carving. I'm not looking for citations or a reading list, just the distinctions, in American English, at a level a careful high schooler can follow.
