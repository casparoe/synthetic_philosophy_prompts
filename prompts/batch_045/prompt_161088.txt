I'm TAing an intro decision-theory course and marking short answers, and I want a second opinion before I commit to marks. Not going to tell you what I thought of it either way.

The question we set (20 marks):

Ada is booking a holiday. Her preferences over three package deals are cyclical: Rome ≻ Kyoto ≻ Lisbon ≻ Rome. Each of those gaps is worth more to her than a small fee, so she will always pay a small fee to swap the less- for the more-preferred deal. A travel agent who knows her preferences runs a money pump on her.

(a) Set out the sequence of offers, and say exactly what Ada ends up with and why that is supposed to be a problem for her. [8]

(b) A classmate says Ada can just refuse one of the offers and then nothing bad happens. There are two different ways of doing this: refuse the first offer on the grounds that she can foresee how her later choices will go, or refuse a later offer in order to stick to a plan she settled on in advance. Explain both, say what each commits her to (does she have to act against what she prefers at the time? does she lose anything?), and say whether either of them shows that her cyclical preferences were never irrational to begin with, or only that she must not choose in a certain way. [12]

One student's answer, typos and all:

(a) Start her with Lisbon. Offer Kyoto for a small fee; Kyoto ≻ Lisbon so she pays. Then Rome for a fee; Rome ≻ Kyoto, so she pays again. Then Lisbon for a fee; Lisbon ≻ Rome, so she pays a third time. She ends up with Lisbon, the package she likes least of the three, having paid three fees. Strictly worse off than where she started: she has paid for the priveledge of ending up with her worst option. This works because she takes each offer as it comes instead of thinking ahead, and because the agent knows her preferences; someone who saw the whole sequence in advance would just refuse to take part in it.

(b) Two ways out. She can refuse the first offer and keep Lisbon. Or she can accept the first two trades and refuse the third, keeping Rome. The first is resolute choice: you work out in advance which plan is best and then stick to it, which is why you refuse right at the start instead of getting dragged along. The second is sophisticated choice: you look ahead, see that later trades undo the earlier ones, and stop at the point where continuing stops paying — and since at that point Lisbon ≻ Rome, you have to act against your current preference to do it. Both escape the loss, and neither requires her to give up her preferences: sophisticated choosers re-order theirs until they are consistent, while the resolute chooser keeps whatever preferences she has and only changes how she chooses. So the pump does not really show that cyclical preferences are irrational. What it shows is that rationality is about how you choose over time rather than about consistency at a time — transitivity is a diachronic requirement on choice, and the pump is only a vivid way of making that point.
