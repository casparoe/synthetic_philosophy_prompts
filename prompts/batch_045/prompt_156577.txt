I am leading a seventy-five-minute seminar for second-year undergraduates in a general epistemology course on higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia. They already have evidential support and defeat, but nothing about evidence concerning one's own reliability. Do not explain the topic to me; design the session.

Give the order in which the components must come and what depends on what — in particular, why it matters whether the puzzle is first framed as a conflict between two bodies of evidence or as a conflict between a judgment and a verdict on that judgment. Choose one case to carry the whole session. I have been considering a case in which the higher-order source's reliability is not independent of the first-order proposition: an automated diagnostic auditor, well calibrated on common conditions and poorly calibrated on rare ones, so that how much you should discount your own diagnosis depends on whether this case is rare, which is exactly what you were trying to determine. Say whether that case can carry the session or should come second, after a cleaner one, and what a carrying case must have and must lack — for instance, whether the higher-order source should also assert something with first-order content. Then map the misconceptions to expect — I anticipate at least: that higher-order evidence is simply one more piece of evidence about the world to be weighed against the first-order evidence; that since one can simply change one's mind there is no genuine puzzle about akrasia; and that the upshot is scepticism about one's own reasoning generally — and say at which point in the sequence each must be headed off, rather than merely that it will arise. Finally, give one closing question that separates a student who has the structure of the problem from one who has only learned to say that one should split the difference, and state what I should openly decline to cover at this level.
