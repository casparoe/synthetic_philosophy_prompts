i'm TAing a first-year seminar and want to replace the usual "compatibilism vs incompatibilism" handout with something that actually sorts people instead of letting them pick a team. Can you build me a decision tree / quiz hybrid — 7 to 9 questions, leaves capped at about a dozen — that starts from gut reactions to cases and drops someone at a named position? The leaves I need covered: hard determinism, hard incompatibilism, event-causal and agent-causal libertarianism, reasons-responsive compatibilism, hierarchical/mesh-style compatibilism, semi-compatibilism, revisionism, the "we can never have the kind of responsibility we thought we had" pessimist, and an illusionist/eliminativist leaf.

The constraints that matter to me:

- Every branch node has to be answerable by someone who has never heard the labels — concrete cases and yes/no claims, not "do you think moral responsibility requires alternative possibilities."
- Build the ordering out of the logical space, not off a list of isms: say which questions are genuinely independent of each other, which answers force which later answers, and where a combination of answers has nobody living in it. Give each empty cell one line on why it's empty (incoherent, just unattractive, or merely unexplored).
- Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really separate rather than inventing a question to fake a difference between them.

The angle I want driving the whole thing is the worth wanting bit, not just the is it free bit. So two or three nodes should ask whether the capacity a position hands you is one you'd actually want or would take if offered — not whether it qualifies as free will. Two cases I want used: (1) a designer — a neuroengineer, a state, whatever — who secretly shapes someone's decision-making over decades so they reliably do what the designer wants, while leaving all the compatibilist-friendly machinery untouched: reasons-responsive, not coerced, not compelled, endorses it on reflection; (2) a case where someone is offered a swap — keep the capacity they have, or trade it for an indeterministic one they control less but that nobody designed.

Last thing: tell me which node you expect to produce the messiest, most self-inconsistent answer patterns from actual students, and whether that messiness tracks a real fault line in the debate or just a badly phrased question of yours.
