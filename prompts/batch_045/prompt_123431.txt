I've ended up defending a version of the scale argument on insect suffering that most people I talk to think is way overconfident, and I want it actually stress-tested rather than politely summarised back to me.

The position, in two parts. (E) Evaluative: the number of insects alive at any moment is astronomically large, so even a low credence that they have valenced experience at all yields an enormous expected quantity of valenced experience; and since insect lives look cheap and short – huge broods, no parental investment, nearly everything dying young by predation, starvation or weather – then conditional on there being something it is like to be one, the valence distribution seems skewed negative rather than positive. So the expected total is huge and mostly bad. (A) Action-guiding: that is enough to make invertebrate welfare a genuine priority now, at minimum for research and for not making things worse, even before we know much.

Two things I've built into my version that I want you to take seriously as load-bearing. First, the standard move that N and the probability of sentience aren't independent – the same r-selected facts that make the numbers vast are the facts that make sentience unlikely – is fine as far as it goes, but it cuts both ways: conditional on sentience, those same facts are evidence about the *sign* of the valence, and they point down, not up. Second, the arithmetic quietly assumes valence is a continuous quantity accruing per unit time. If what matters is discrete valenced episodes per organism, and an insect lifetime contains something like a handful of them, the total deflates by orders of magnitude, and I don't think the people making this argument have noticed how much rests on that modelling choice rather than on the biology.

So: what would count against it?

- Defeaters for E and defeaters for A separately, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and – this matters most to me – the ones that kill A while leaving E standing. My guess is the strongest material is here: if insect populations sit near carrying capacity, most interventions swap *which* insects exist rather than *how many*, so the durable reduction the argument needs may simply not be on offer. I want that pressed hard. Is there a version that survives even if that replacement problem is total, say via welfare-quality rather than headcount interventions, or does the replacement problem eat those too?
- For each defeater, tell me which ones my own side should concede as genuinely counting against us, and which we'd be right to dismiss as irrelevant, verbal, or just restating a prior commitment.
- What does the position predict that its rivals don't? If it's compatable with every possible finding, say so plainly, and then tell me whether that's fatal for a quantitative argument of this shape or just what robustness looks like.
- Honest insulation check. Between 'even a tiny probability is enough given the numbers', 'we can't act yet so fund research', and 'absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence', has this thing been qualified into something that can't lose? And if it has, is that a problem here, or is it like other small-probability-enormous-stakes arguments where immunity to evidence is a feature rather than a bug?
- Whether totalism about valence is doing hidden work at this scale, and separately whether the whole framing needs some minimal diachronic unity of the subject that an animal with a three-week life and no long-term memory might not have. Also: are the unit question (organisms vs insect-years vs discrete episodes) and the replacement question genuinely separate problems, or the same problem in different clothes?
- And the bit I actually care about: give me real numbers. Your own credence that insects have valenced experience at all; conditional on that, your credence that their lives are net negative; your credence that we can durably reduce the total rather than just reshuffle it. Show the arithmetic, then tell me which single input the conclusion is most sensitive to and what kind of finding would move that input.

Commit to a verdict. Is E more likely true than not? Is A? What's the one thing that would change your mind, and which of the defeaters above do you think really bites rather than just being awkward? Don't give me a balanced tour of the literature or a list of considerations on both sides – assume I've read the usual lab results everyone cites and take a position, with reasons.
