I am trying to write down, in advance of reading any more of the literature, what I should be demanding of a population axiology given the job I actually need it to do. Please write that specification.

The job: I need an ordering over *policy options*, not over timeless population states. The options available to the institution I advise differ simultaneously in (i) the quality of life of the people who will exist on all of the options, (ii) how many people there will ever be — via birth timing, family-planning and maternal-health funding, or interventions on existential risk that move the expected size of the future by orders of magnitude — and (iii) how well any of that can be known in advance. So the thing to be ranked is a risky prospect over histories, and adequacy means adequacy for choosing under that kind of uncertainty.

Write the desiderata: which comparisons the ordering must get right, which cases it must dissolve or explain rather than dodge, which distinctions it must respect and which it may collapse, and the consideration behind each. Mark which requirements you take to be non-negotiable for this purpose and which are the preferences of a particular camp. Then the part I most want: flag any subset of the requirements you suspect is jointly unsatisfiable, sketch the argument, and say which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidate views. If two of my own requirements below are really one requirement, or are not requirements at all, say so.

Three candidates of mine, which I want kept, sharpened, or rejected as confused:

1. *Grain-invariance.* The ranking of two options must not change when the very same history is redescribed at a finer temporal or personal grain — as persons, or as person-stages, or as processes with fuzzy boundaries. If a view's verdicts swing with the bookkeeping, I want to know whether that is a feature of the view or a reductio of it.

2. *Individuation-robustness.* Consider a population of software persons where minds can split into two successors and two can merge into one, so that at a given time there may be no fact of the matter how many people there are. The axiology must still rank options in which such entities figure, and the ranking must not be a function of an arbitrary counting convention imposed on a continuum of intermediate cases.

3. *Actionability under cluelessness.* Every real option seems to have small-probability effects on enormously large far-future populations, so a theory whose verdicts are always dominated by speculative tails may be unusable for the job I have described — unless the view also tells the agent what to do when that happens. I cannot tell whether this is a genuine desideratum or an impatience of mine that a theory is entitled to ignore.

My suspicion going in is that avoiding the repugnant conclusion, honouring the step-by-step pull of mere-addition reasoning, and ranking different-number comparisons at all while staying transitive cannot all be had together, and that the costs of giving up each are not alike — but I would rather see the conflict located precisely than confirmed. Close with a scorecard of at most a few lines each for total utilitarianism, average utilitarianism, critical-level views, and person-affecting or restrictive views, and name any requirement that none of them meet.
