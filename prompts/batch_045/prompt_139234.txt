So I just spent three hours in a pub arguing with my friend Dan about whether the no-free-lunch theorems are just Hume's problem of induction in a lab coat, and now I actually have to do something with the argument. Dan's line: NFL proves you can't get something from nothing, every learner needs an inductive bias, so it's Hume all the way down and formal learning theory hasn't answered anything. My line: NFL is a claim about average performance when you average uniformly over all possible target functions, and that uniform average is itself a massive inductive assumption, so as a sceptical argument it saws off the branch it's sitting on — and besides, Hume's problem is about whether I'm justified in expecting the next instance to resemble the last ones, not about my score in a tournament I never entered.

The reason I'm telling you this: I've got about three years and a small pot of money (two postdocs plus my own time) to run a research program that tries to actually connect formal learning theory to the epistemology of induction, and I need to decide what it should work on and in what order. Not a survey, and definately not a reading list — I want a defended agenda.

Here are the candidate lines I scribbled down afterwards, and I want you to rank them and tell me which I'm overrating:

(a) Turning NFL into a positive tool: instead of asking whether it vindicates or refutes induction, characterise for each learner or bias the exact class of problem distributions on which it beats chance — a kind of "inductive fingerprint" — and see whether that reframing dissolves the philosophical question or just relabels it.

(b) The turtles question: if I justify my bias by appeal to a distribution over distributions of problems, does an NFL-style result reappear one level up? Does it matter if it does?

(c) Whether the demand for assumption-free justification is simply ill-formed, because "better learner" is only ever defined relative to some weighting over problems — so the honest response to Hume is relativised, local vindication rather than a global one.

(d) Whether any of this should be about prediction at all, or about action: pragmatic, dominance-style vindications that cash out induction in expected-value terms.

(e) An empirical component — actually estimating the distribution of "natural" problem instances, on the theory that the conceptual debate quietly waits on it.

Rank them, say what criteria you're using (how upstream each is, tractability for people who are philosophers with maybe one theorist collaborator, neglectedness), tell me which two you'd actually fund and which you'd drop, and what evidence over the next couple of years would reshuffle the list. Also — this matters to me more than the ranking — please be clear about what's just mathematics (settled, given the definitions) versus what's genuinely contested among people who work on this, and flag where you're giving me your own sympathies rather than anything like a consensus. Dan and I were both very good at presenting our favourite framing as if everyone already agreed with it.
