You are an analytic moral philosopher who also writes short fiction in the tradition of the philosophical thought experiment — stories in which the argument is carried by what the characters decide to do, not by exposition. Work through the material carefully before you begin.

First, brainstorm briefly: in a short paragraph, list three or four candidate setups (setting, institution, characters, and exactly which form of the puzzle each would dramatize), note the main weakness of each, and say which one you are going with and why. Then write the story itself, 900–1,200 words.

The concept is the levelling-down objection to prioritarianism. Prioritarianism ranks outcomes by a sum of transformed well-beings, Σ g(wᵢ), where g is strictly increasing and strictly concave, so that a fixed gain counts for more the worse off its recipient is. Its proudest advertised advantage over the older egalitarianism is supposed to be immunity to the levelling-down objection: if you drag the better off down to the level of the worse off and give nothing to anyone, some wᵢ falls, hence the sum falls, and prioritarians never say the world got better in any respect because people were cut down to size.

The wrinkle your story must stage is that this immunity depends on what gets its own priority weight — on what the units are. Suppose the ministry weights person-years rather than whole lives, because citizens' lifespans differ and the measure has to compare a life of forty years with one of ninety. Now consider one single life made of two halves, at well-being levels 10 and 30, and stipulate within the story that a life's total well-being is just the sum of the well-being of its years. Level that life down to two halves of 19 each: total lifetime well-being falls from 40 to 38, the person is the only person affected, and no one is benefited. Yet √10 + √30 ≈ 8.64 while 2√19 ≈ 8.72, and with a logarithmic weighting ≈ 5.70 versus ≈ 5.89. The official measure rises; and the more unequal the original life and the more concave the weighting, the more it rises — so the reform faction's proposal to make g more concave, precisely in order to show extra concern for the worst-off years, makes the anomaly worse.

Requirements:

1. Dramatize rather than lecture. The arithmetic must appear inside the fiction — a clerk's ledger, an audit, a briefing note, a contested signature — but no character may deliver a seminar on the view, and the story should not read as a dialogue with props.

2. Someone should state the standard immunity argument, in their own terms and convincingly, before the anomaly surfaces.

3. Give the prioritarian at least one serious reply, spoken with real force: for instance, abandon person-years and weight whole lives — and then have someone say what that costs, given that lifespans differ; or the "better in one respect, not better all things considered" reply; or a challenge to the assumption that a life's good is the sum of its years. Then let the other side answer it.

4. The story must turn on a decision someone makes under pressure — to certify the figures, to run the programme anyway, to bury the finding — and it should end on that decision, with the argument unresolved and no tidy moral drawn.

5. No citations, bibliographies, or references to actual philosophers or papers: the fiction is self-contained, and invented institutional language is welcome.
