I teach a second-year undergraduate seminar on rational choice and decision theory: 22 students, one 75-minute session, mixed majors, most of them with only an introductory logic course behind them. The next session is on ambiguity, and I want them to run into the phenomenon before I name it. Specifically, I want them to discover that they will pay more for a bet on a draw from a bag whose composition they know than for the nominally identical bet on a bag whose composition they do not know, to notice that this happens for both colours and not just one, and then to be faced with the fact that their four valuations cannot all be read as probabilities without contradicting each other. No lecture at the front: the pattern has to come out of choices they actually make, and the naming comes after the last round.

Some constraints and some things I would like tried. Materials are limited to opaque bags, coloured poker chips, index cards and a projector; no money changes hands, and payoffs need to be points toward a genuinely trivial prize, so the incentives have to be honest without being real. I do not want the standard version in which I secretly fix the composition of the ambiguous bag and they merely bet on it. I would rather the ambiguity be constructed by the participants: before any betting, each student writes down the interval of proportions they regard as genuinely unnarrowable — say, red anywhere between 20 and 80 per cent — and the class aggregates these into the range the ambiguous bag is then drawn from. My thought is that this makes the aversion attach to the spread of their own uncertainty rather than to anything about the physical bag, but I am not confident it works and I would like your view on whether it does or whether it just muddies the setup.

I also want a round in which they price, rather than merely choose between, four tickets: a bet on red from the known bag, a bet on black from the known bag, and the same two bets on the ambiguous bag — writing a number in points for each, so that whatever incoherence is there can be read straight off the four numbers afterwards rather than inferred from a pattern of forced choices. And I want two further comparisons. First, a chance to buy, with points, the removal of the ambiguity: a sample drawn from the ambiguous bag in advance and revealed to them only after they have committed, so that I can see what the reduction in uncertainty is worth to them. Second, a pair of cases in which the ambiguous bag's composition is fixed either by a randomly selected classmate who is rewarded when the bettor loses, or by an impersonal random mechanism identical in every other respect. The point of that pair is to pry apart genuine aversion to unknown probabilities from the ordinary thought that someone is out to get them; whether the two come out the same or differently, tell me what the result would show and what I should say about it.

Please give me: the rules and materials; the exact sequence of rounds with timings; how the scoring works so that the numbers they write are incentive-compatible in an honest if approximate sense; what goes on the board and when, given that nothing may be written down before the reveal that tips the structure of the argument; and a debrief that maps particular moments in the session onto particular features of the case — the reversal across the two colours, what it would take for a valuation to be additive, and what the sure-thing principle asks of them here. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, including the most likely way students will try to game the scoring. And be frank about which part of the phenomenon the activity cannot generate and which part I will simply have to state: I would rather know in advance where the game stops and the talking starts.

Respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each two or three sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
