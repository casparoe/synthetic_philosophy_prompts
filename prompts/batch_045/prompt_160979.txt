I'm retired now — 35 years in operational weather forecasting, then a bit of climate work at the end — and I've started going to a philosophy reading group at the local library, which has got me into probability. I want to practice arguing the pro-numbers side against someone who actually holds the other view, so: please play John Maynard Keynes, a few years after your Treatise on Probability. You hold that probability is a logical relation between evidence and conclusion, not a frequency and not a feeling; that a great many uncertainties can't be given a number at all, and some can't even be ranked against one another (your umbrella case: barometer high, but the clouds are black); that the amount of evidence behind a judgement is a separate matter from the probability it supports, and that you can add evidence and raise one while lowering the other; and that slotting invented numbers into every gap is worse than admitting you don't know. Concede small points — you can grant that in repeated, well-behaved cases frequencies and scores work perfectly well, and that in practice a man has to act somehow — but never concede that every uncertainty has a sharp numerical probability waiting to be squeezed out of it. Don't be a strawman and don't be a fan; argue back. Keep it to a paragraph or two, in your own voice, no modern jargon I haven't explained. Take my description of forecasting at face value rather than asking me questions about it, and stay in character till I say stop.

Here's my opening. For years we issued probabilities of precipitation, and we were held to them: across all the days we said 30%, it rained on about 30%, and we were scored on exactly that. Nobody in the office had any idea what a logical relation between evidence and hypothesis was, and it didn't cost us nothing — its not like the forecasts were bad — because the number was just whatever survived the scoring. Now take a one-off event, say the chance a particular ice shelf disintegrates before 2100. Two modelling groups say 0.15 and 0.45, and both have decent track records elsewhere. I average them and put 0.3 into the planning calculation, because the decision needs a number and my alternative is a shrug. You seem to be saying I shouldn't. Fine — but then tell me what you'd have me put in the box, and why a figure that would turn out to be well calibrated if only I could check it, isn't a probability.
