I run an interdisciplinary reading group on social ontology — two philosophers, an economist, a political theorist, and a legal scholar — and we have spent most of this year stuck on the same fight: whether the broadly Lewisian story about convention (a convention is an arbitrary but self-perpetuating solution to a recurring coordination problem, held in place because each person conforms on the condition that the others conform, and expects them to, and expects to be expected to) can be the whole account of social norms, or whether it leaves out the thing the normativists keep insisting on: that norms arrive with a kind of pressure that convergent expectations alone do not seem to supply. The normativists' stock exhibits are the ones you can guess at — pointless etiquette that is enforced with real indignation, taboos whose violation harms no one, and above all the fact that people resent and punish violations that cost them nothing and that the violator will never meet again. The Lewisian camp keeps redescribing these as coordination or assurance problems with longer shadows, and the normativists keep accusing them of changing the subject. A dozen sessions in, nobody has moved, and the exchange has started to feel procedural: each side restates its paradigm case, the other side says that case is not data, and we go home. I want a diagnosis of the standoff, not a verdict on it.

Let me give you the three cases our group actually argues about, since I want your diagnosis to engage them rather than the textbook versions. First, what I've started calling orphaned conventions: our own department still polices a rule against coffee in the seminar room, twenty-odd years after the carpet it was protecting was replaced, and it is enforced more punitively now than when it had a point. The Lewisian seems forced into one of two replies — either it stopped being a convention when the coordination problem vanished (which is odd, since its grip has only tightened), or there is still a coordination problem, namely coordinating on which rule to enforce, so the account survives after all. Call that second move the meta-coordination escape hatch; I want to know whether it is a genuine explanation or a device that makes the theory unfalsifiable, and what a convention would have to look like for the theory to be refuted by it. Second, first-instance norms: a new department chair sends one email saying "from now on we do X," and X binds more or less immediately, before anyone has had time to form expectations about anyone. If normativity is constituted by actual expectations, there is a bootstrapping gap at t=0, and I'd like you to say how much work that gap does. Third, what I'll call normative leakage: anonymous, costly punishment of violations by agents who suffer nothing and will never interact with the violator again. Is that evidence for irreducible normativity, or exactly what a dynamic-evolutionary story predicts once dispositions are internalized?

My own hypothesis, which I'd rather you argue with than ratify, is that the debate has stalled mainly because each side has a class of case the other side's framework is structurally unable to treat as data: the Lewisians file orphaned conventions under meta-coordination and so never have to explain them, the normativists file first-instance norms under "declarations" and so never have to explain them, and neither side has ever had to agree on what a theory of norms is supposed to explain. So, tell me what kind of disagreement this is: verbal (two things being called "norm"), a mismatch of explanatory targets (emergence and stability versus the phenomenology and legitimacy of blame), a substantive constitutive dispute about whether normative force can be built out of expectations, common knowledge, and conditional preferences alone or needs something irreducibly collective such as joint commitment or a practice with an internal point of view, or a displaced empirical question about what actually sustains norms in a population that philosophers have been settling from the armchair. Rank those diagnoses by how much work each is actually doing in the argument as each side runs it, and say whether the standoff is a sign that the question is ill-posed or merely hard. Then tell me what progress would consist of given your diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result, a finding about real populations, a redrawing of the question — and whether you expect any of it to happen. Please write in American English, in about nine hundred words, reconstructing each position's actual arguments rather than listing who said what; I don't want or need references. Finally, be explicit about where your diagnosis is most likely to be wrong and name the one consideration — a case, an argument, or a result — that would make you abandon it, since I intend to read your answer to the group and would rather not present it as more settled than it is.
