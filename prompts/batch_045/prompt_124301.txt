My group chat (mostly ACX/LW people, plus one actual astronomer who keeps rolling his eyes) got into a fight about the 1967 pulsar discovery, and I've come to think it's a really clean test case for Dennett's three stances plus the real-patterns stuff — but I can't get the mapping to come out cleanly, and I want someone to do it properly.

Facts as I remember them; take them as given even if I've got a detail wrong. Bell was going through chart-recorder paper from the Interplanetary Scintillation Array, noticed a bit of "scruff" that tracked across the sky with the stars, chased it down, and the signal pulsed with a period of about one and a third seconds, absurdly regular. The group temporarily labeled it LGM-1. Then: more of these things turned up, each ticking at its own rate; the period was stable enough that whatever was doing it had to be tiny by astronomical standards; and within about a year the rotating-neutron-star lighthouse picture had won. Also relevant, imo: Hewish's first reaction was that it was local interference — man-made, i.e. not a source out there at all.

What I want is a careful walk through the episode with the physical / design / intentional triad, not a vibe check on "are aliens a good hypothesis."

So: (1) What does each stance actually commit you to about that stretch of chart paper, and what does each predict that the others don't? Be disciplined about who the players are and what their live options were — as I see it, at the moment they had only the scruff, the options were "alien beacon," "some natural compact thing," and "our own kit." Is the artifact hypothesis a fourth stance, or a refusal to adopt any stance at all, and where does the framework put it? If the triad has no slot for it, say that rather than inventing one.

(2) Which features of the episode does the triad explain, and which does it misdescribe? My sense is that the intentional stance handles the regularity fine — better than people credit — and that what killed LGM wasn't the pattern getting less real but something like a payload asymmetry: agents who build beacons build channels, they put content in them, they aim, they answer, and they don't build dozens of them each ticking at its own rate for no audience. Is that a real prediction of the intentional stance, or am I smuggling in a design-stance assumption? And does the framework say anything about how much predictive success a stance has to lose before you should drop it — or is that exactly where it's silent?

(3) The part I actually care about: the real-patterns criterion. The thought, as I understand it, is that a pattern is real if there's a compression of the data, a description more compact than the bit map. But here there were at least two compressions available — beacon, lighthouse — and as far as I can tell they compress the data about equally well. Call that compression overlap (I made the name up, sorry). So does the realness of the pattern do *any* work in choosing between stances? If not, the case looks like an anomaly for the ontological work Dennett wants real patterns to do — he uses them to settle whether things like beliefs are really there, and here the criterion settles nothing. Or is the right reply that it was never meant to, that it's doing purely deflationary work and I've misread it? And whatever you conclude, does it carry over to the SETI argument and to the "should we take the intentional stance toward a language model" argument, which run on definately the same machinery — that's where the fight started.

Give me a verdict, not a survey. Commit to an answer on (a) whether the intentional stance was ever rationally available here and the point at which it should have been dropped, and (b) whether the episode confirms the real-patterns criterion, is an anomaly for it, or is a case it was never built for. And tell me which of my framings you think is wrong and why. If you have to choose, I'd rather keep the intentional stance's availability than my compression-overlap point — but say what giving it up costs.
