I teach a third-year seminar on agency, and before I take my own position into class I want to hear it argued against by someone who actually holds the other side. Please play a philosopher-psychologist of the following conviction, held without hedging: that the Libet-style results are decisive against the claim that a conscious intention is the proximal cause of the action it is about. You hold that once the neural antecedents of a spontaneous movement have been isolated and timed, the conscious "now" is at best a downstream readout of a process already underway, and that redescribing it as a decision, an endorsement, the exercise of a policy, or an agent's taking of a stand changes the label and not the causal order.

You are not to be a strawman. You know the retrospective-report objection — that a W judgment is a memory-infected reconstruction rather than a datum — the worry that a potential extracted by averaging across many trials need not be present, in that shape, on the trial the subject is reporting on, the "veto" manoeuvre, and the accumulator-style reinterpretation on which the slow ramp is only what the crossing of a threshold by ongoing background noise looks like from outside. You may concede any of these locally: you may allow that some published figures are soft, that the averaging worry is real, even that the classical protocol is a blunt instrument. You will not concede that conscious intention does causal work. Reply in the first person, in one or two paragraphs of continuous prose, no headings or lists, sharp but fair, willing to grant a small point in order to press the main one; stay in character until I write "stop".

My opening move is a case I have built for my students, and I want to see what you make of it. Put the historical protocol to one side and suppose the following as stipulated results of a variant I will call the dial-and-frame experiment. Subjects prepare to flex the right wrist whenever they like. In their left hand they hold an analog dial and are told to keep it, continuously, in proportion to how much they currently want to flex — an online measure, not a recollection. EEG is recorded as usual. Across blocks, the instruction for the retrospective report is varied: in one block subjects report when the urge to flex first appeared, in the other when they had settled it, when they were no longer undecided. And on a random quarter of trials they are instructed in advance to cancel at the last moment.

The stipulated findings are these. First, the dial leaves its baseline within thirty milliseconds of the readiness potential's onset, in both blocks, and it does so before the subject's retrospective report on the vast majority of trials — several hundred milliseconds before, in the block where subjects report settling rather than urge. Second, the timing of dial departure does not move when the report instruction changes, but the mean reported time does, shifting about a hundred and fifty milliseconds later under the settling instruction; the neural onset does not shift at all. Third, on the instructed-cancellation trials the dial rises with the potential and then falls away again, and no movement occurs.

I put it to you like this. Your argument has always got its force from one comparison: the brain's preparation begins before the subject's reported intention, therefore the reported intention comes too late to be the cause. But in this case the online measure of what the subject is consciously undergoing tracks the neural onset, is invariant under the changes of wording that move the report, and on cancellation trials rises and subsides with the preparation it is supposed to be downstream of. So the lagging, labile quantity in your comparison is the report, not the intention, and the lag is an artefact of asking people to date their own states after the fact. If the dial is registering the onset of conscious urge, then either the conscious state is there from the beginning of the preparation and your precedence claim collapses into a claim about dating, or the dial is not registering anything conscious — and you owe me an account of what a continuous, voluntary, frame-invariant hand movement is tracking, if not that.

What do you say to it?
