I'm in a graduate seminar right now where we've been working through the problem of the unity of the proposition, and while preparing for it I read an encyclopedia entry on the topic whose argument I tried to reconstruct from memory for my notes. Here is roughly what it said: Suppose you think that when you judge that *a* is to the left of *b*, the judgment is a complex made up of *a*, *b*, and the relation of being to the left of. Bradley pointed out that a mere collection of those three items no more says that *a* is to the left of *b* than it says that *b* is to the left of *a* — it says nothing at all. If you try to fix this by adding a further relation whose job is to attach the first relation to its terms, that further relation is itself just another item in the collection, so you need a third relation to attach it, and so on forever. Since the chain can never be completed, no judgment is ever genuinely about anything and nothing is strictly speaking true or false, which is why Bradley held that relations are not real and that reality must be a single undifferentiated whole. Russell's way out was his multiple-relation theory of judgment: judgment is a single many-place relation holding the mind directly to *a*, *b*, and the relation itself, so there is no proposition-as-unity left to explain. Nowadays, though, nobody loses sleep over this, because the regress only gets going if you think of a proposition as an unstructured heap; once you identify the proposition with an ordered set-theoretic tuple — or, better, with the sentence that expresses it — there is a determinate fact about the order of its parts and the problem dissolves.

The trouble is that our seminar leader has a hobbyhorse that sounds like it would just relocate all of this, and I can't tell whether it does. His view, as I understood it, is that a proposition isn't a thing built out of parts at all but a *type of act*: what unifies *a*, *b*, and the relation is that someone predicates the relation of them, and the binding is done by the predicating, not by any further item. (He also said something dismissive about "Fregean unsaturatedness," which I half-remember as the claim that a concept is incomplete in a way an object isn't, but I couldn't reconstruct why that was supposed to help.) A friend in the seminar offered a different fix over coffee: a proposition is unified by whatever fact in the world would make it true, so there is no need for a tie internal to the proposition. I'm inclined to think the encyclopedia entry is too confidence about the set-theoretic reply — it sounds like it just moves the structure somewhere else — but I don't trust my own sense of where the real disagreement lies, and I'd like to know before class.

So could you go through my reconstruction sentence by sentence and tell me which parts someone who works on this would accept, which are reasonable popularizations, and which are subtly off? In particular: is the regress really an argument for global skepticism about truth, or is it used for something more modest? Is the move from "relations are unreal" to "relations don't exist" fair to Bradley, or is a technical notion being read in an everyday way? Is the bit about a proposition being a mere heap actually the same problem as the regress, or are two different worries being run together? Is Russell's theory properly described as a solution that worked, or did it collapse for reasons of its own? And do my seminar leader's act-based view and my friend's truthmaker suggestion actually escape the regress, or do they just relocate it a level down — say, into the unity of the act, or of the fact? I'd also appreciate a plain statement of what the live positions on this look like and what each one still owes us, so I know what I'd have to give up if I wanted to take any of them seriously.
