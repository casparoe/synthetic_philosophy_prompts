I've been reading the rationalist corner of the internet for something like fifteen years, and the piece of received wisdom I've absorbed most thoroughly is the inside view / outside view distinction: when you want a probability on some project of yours, you can either build a causal model of the particular case — the plan, the people, the obstacles, the specific reasons this time is different — and read a number off that, or you can find the class of cases like this one and ask how often they turn out well, and take that frequency as your anchor. The community norm pushes hard toward the outside view, on the ground that the inside view is where overconfidence and the planning fallacy live. I've never been entirely happy with that norm as it is usually stated, because it comes as a slogan rather than as a procedure, and I now have a case where I need the procedure rather than the slogan.

The case: I'm deciding whether to help found a small independent research group — six people, a two-year runway already soft-committed, a narrowly defined technical question that the existing institutions in the field are visibly failing to work on, and a founding team with one genuinely unusual advantage, which is that three of us have already done a smaller version of exactly this and it worked. The outside view, as near as I can construct it, is grim: of the attempts at independent research organizations of roughly this shape and size that I can count, the fraction still doing work anyone cites five years later is somewhere around one in twelve. The inside view, run honestly and at length, gives me something in the range of 40 to 60 percent. That gap is the whole question.

So I've been collecting actual arbitration rules — procedures you can in principle execute, which take an inside-view estimate and an outside-view base rate as inputs and return a single number or a defined policy. I've assembled ten (some of them standard, some of them things I've seen argued in comment threads, a couple invented by a friend who forecasts professionally). Please rank them.

1. Take the base rate as your prior, and allow an inside-view consideration to move you only to the degree that the predictive weight of that kind of consideration has itself been measured against a reference class. In practice this means almost nothing moves you.

2. Logarithmic pooling: treat the two views as roughly independent sources of information and combine the probabilities by taking a geometric mean, possibly weighted toward whichever source you think has the better track record in this domain.

3. The ceiling rule: the outside view doesn't set your probability, it caps it. Absent a pre-registered, explicitly stated exception, you are not allowed to go above some fixed multiple of the base rate — say five times.

4. The ten-minute stranger: summarize the plan on one page, hand it to a well-calibrated forecaster who knows the domain but has no stake in it, and defer to whatever they say, on the theory that compression destroys the inside view's rhetorical padding while preserving its real information.

5. The pre-mortem inversion: write the detailed causal story of how the group failed five years from now, then ask whether you could have written that same story before launch, from the outside, without knowing anything special about this case. If you could, the story is not evidence about this case and gets no weight against the inside view.

6. Nearest-neighbour reference class with a size floor: build the most specific reference class that still contains at least twenty cases; if the class you want has fewer than twenty, move up a level of generality until the floor is met, and use that class's frequency.

7. Shape from the inside, magnitude from the outside: use the inside view exclusively to determine the structure of the outcome distribution — which mechanisms matter, what the failure modes are, how sensitive the outcome is to which inputs — and use the outside view exclusively to set the overall scale or base rate, on the theory that the two views fail in different ways and the inside view's failure is specifically a failure about magnitudes.

8. The transmission test: hand your inside-view case to a competent outsider who doesn't share your motivation, and have them reconstruct it as strongly as they can in their own words. Whatever strength doesn't survive that transmission is treated as affect rather than information and given zero weight; only the residue is allowed to move you off the base rate.

9. The second-order outside view: instead of applying the outside view to the object-level question, apply it to your own track record of applying the inside view. Look at the historical hit rate of your own past inside-view confidences on comparable judgments, and use that measured reliability as the weight you put on the inside view this time.

10. Pre-registered opposing reference class: before you look at any outcome, write down which reference classes you would refuse to be measured against, and the criteria for refusing them. Then let an opponent choose the class. If your refusal doesn't follow from a criterion you stated in advance, you accept theirs.

11. Coherence-checked intuitive adjustment: start from the base rate, adjust upward by however much the inside-view case feels strong, then sanity-check the result for internal coherence and against a couple of remembered examples, and stop when it feels right.

Please take this seriously and work carefully. Before you commit to an ordering, spend a few paragraphs brainstorming what this space of procedures even looks like — what a rule has to do in order to count as reliable here, what the characteristic failure modes are (laundering the inside view through a reference class chosen for the feature you already believed in; throwing away real private information in the name of humility; producing a number that is well-calibrated across cases but useless for this decision), and what reliability should even be measured in: calibration, Brier score, log score, regret, decision-relevant expected value, or something else. Then settle on your ordering. For the purposes of this answer, please be a professional philosopher of science who has also spent years doing applied probabilistic forecasting inside an organisation that kept score on its own predictions; I want both the epistemology and the knowledge of what happens to these rules when they meet a real track record.

What I want back: a score from 0 to 100 for each of the eleven, where 100 means that if you could use exactly one of these for the rest of your forecasting life on cases of this shape you would pick this one, and 0 means it is worse than flipping to the base rate and ignoring the inside view entirely. Sort them into three or four tiers as well, and say in a sentence or two what puts each one where it is and what its most likely failure looks like. Then apply your top-ranked procedure to my actual case and give me one number — the probability that the group still exists and is doing work anyone cites five years from now — with the working compressed into a couple of sentences. Two things to watch out for while you do this: don't reward a rule merely for sounding humble, and don't punish one merely for being informal if it is the thing a good forecaster would actually do.

One format constraint, please: no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italic text, and no tables — continuous prose only, the way you'd write a long email to a colleague. I do need the numbers to be recoverable, so just write them into the sentences themselves, and don't bother with a summary or closing paragraph.
