so my reading group — very online, LessWrong-adjacent, we've been crawling through Consciousness Explained about a chapter a month — had a blowup last night over the color phi bit, and I've been asked to referee and honestly cant do it.

Setup, in case you need it. In the color phi demo a red dot is flashed at one location, then a green dot at a location a bit further along, with the timing rigged so that what you actually see is a *single* dot travelling between the two that turns green partway along its path. The puzzle: the color change looks like it happens mid-trajectory, before there was any green dot in the world to account for it. Dennett's two stories are the Orwellian one, where you have a real experience and the record of it gets rewritten after the fact, and the Stalinesque one, where nothing is experienced until the brain has both dots in hand and then composes the whole moving-then-changing-color episode in one go.

The argument, roughly as it happened:

Marcus: color phi is direct evidence for the Stalinesque story. There's no moment where you consciously see red-dot-then-green-dot and then a memory edit gets applied. There's just one construction, laid down after the second flash. So the content of an experience can be settled by stuff that happens after the event the experience seems to be *of*.

Nadia: the only data anyone has here is what subjects say afterward, and both stories predict exactly the same report. All you've shown is that the content is fixed later, which I already grant. You haven't touched the timing question.

Marcus: so it's a tie broken by parsimony. Orwellian needs an extra event — a genuine experience that gets deleted and replaced — that does zero explanatory work. Same predictions, fewer moving parts, Stalinesque wins by default.

Nadia: that argument scales, and not in a good way. If you can't tell from the inside whether your experience of *anything* happened on schedule or got composed 200ms late, parsimony says everything is late and nobody ever experiences anything when it happens. Either you're happy with that, in which case it's a big claim you don't get for free, or parsimony isn't doing the work you said it was.

Marcus: I am happy with it. That's just the ordinary point that perception lags the world, and nobody thinks that's skeptical.

Nadia: ok, concrete test then. Run the sequence, then at a variable delay after the green dot, hit subjects with a mask or attention-grabbing distractor that disrupts consolidation. On my story there's an original experience plus a revision process, so breaking the revision should sometimes let the original, unrevised content back out — subjects occasionally reporting two dots, red then green, instead of one dot changing color. On your story there is no original, so disruption should only ever degrade or truncate the construction: partial trajectories, or no trajectory, never the pre-revision version.

Marcus: your own design doesn't discriminate. If the mask lands before the construction finishes, I predict a partial construction, and a partial construction of a trajectory that gets cut off early can look a lot like "just the red dot" — which is exactly what you're calling the unrevised original. Same observation, two explanations, back where we started.

Then Nadia said something I didn't follow: that maybe neither story is right, and there's no single moment at which the experience happens at all, because the content gets fixed in different places in the brain at different times — so asking whether the dot turned green before or after the second flash is like asking whether the email arrived before or after the meeting, it's the wrong kind of question. Marcus said that was Stalinesque with extra fog. They both turned to me. I had nothing.
