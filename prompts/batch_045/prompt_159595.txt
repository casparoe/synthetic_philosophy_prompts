Two members of a medical ethics reading group I sit in on — call them Nils and Marisol — spent most of last session arguing about Szasz's thesis that mental illness is not a disease but a metaphor, and afterwards they asked me to settle it. I couldn't: each of them conceded just enough at each step to make the next exchange restart somewhere else. I'd be grateful if you would adjudicate the argument move by move, and I'd be more grateful for your reasoning than for your verdict. I've numbered the moves.

(1) Nils opened with Szasz's core claim: a disease is a structural or functional abnormality of the body, of a sort that anatomy, pathology, or physiology can in principle identify, whereas what psychiatrists call mental illness is conduct, speech, and experience that a community finds disturbing, distressing, or unmanageable. On this view the term does metaphorical work: it borrows the prestige and the exemption from blame that go with being sick and applies them to what are really problems of living, moral conflict, and deviance. He added that the stakes are not academic, because the disease framing is what licenses involuntary commitment and the insanity defence, two practices he said medicine has no business running. He was careful to say this is not a denial of suffering: Szasz allowed that people suffer terribly and may want help, and insisted only that the help be contracted for rather than imposed, as with any other service offered to an adult.

(2) Marisol replied with the case she called decisive: general paresis of the insane. For decades it was a psychiatric condition — grandiosity, deterioration, paralysis, death — until the spirochete responsible was identified and it became neurosyphilis, a plain brain disease. So psychiatric categories are syndromic and provisional: clinicians pick out a cluster of symptoms before anyone knows the mechanism, and sometimes the mechanism turns up. She concluded that Szasz illegitimately generalizes from we have not yet found the lesion to there is no lesion, which is the argument from ignorance with a clinical accent.

(3) Nils said the case proves his point rather than hers. He gave the pattern a name, *the retirement problem*: whenever a condition on the psychiatric roster acquires a demonstrable bodily mechanism, it is retired into neurology or general medicine and stops counting as a mental illness. This shows, he argued, that the category has no fixed membership; at any moment it names roughly the set of distressing conditions whose bodily basis we have not yet worked out — a residual class defined by our ignorance. And a residual class cannot carry the weight his opponents put on it, because the inference from this person has a mental illness to this person has a medical condition has no content until further notice; it is a promissory note, and the promissory note is what is used to justify coercing him today.

(4) Marisol answered that syndrome-first medicine is simply how medicine works, and that Szasz is holding psychiatry to a standard internal medicine has never met. Migraine, epilepsy, and the various fevers of unknown origin were all defined clinically long before their mechanisms, and nobody concluded that they were myths. More importantly, she said, disease does not require a lesion in the first place: a disease can be a dysfunction, a part or a system not doing what it does in a normally functioning organism, and that notion applies as well to the systems that regulate fear, attention, mood, and reward as it does to a kidney. So Nils needs a second premise — that failure of a mental mechanism is not real failure — and she said he had not produced one.

(5) Nils's second premise was about anchoring. In the somatic cases, he said, the syndrome is anchored by involuntary bodily signs — a sustained temperature, an electrical storm on an electroencephalogram, an infiltrate on a film — while in psychiatry the anchoring items are conduct and reported experience, and whether those count as abnormal is settled partly by local norms about how much grief, fear, eccentricity, or religious enthusiasm is fitting. That is why, on his telling, the same phenomena can be a symptom in one setting and a vocation, a religion, or a poem in another, with nothing in the body adjudicating between those descriptions. He extended this to Marisol's dysfunction account: to say a mechanism has failed you need a specification of what it is for, and for fear, mood, or attention we have no agreed specification, so dysfunction here quietly imports a contestable ideal of how a person ought to feel and attend — which is exactly the evaluative element Szasz said was being disguised as a diagnosis.

(6) Marisol then produced what she called her strongest case: functional neurological disorder. Patients have real weakness, tremor, or blackouts; there is no lesion and no disease process; the diagnosis rests on positive clinical signs rather than on the absence of findings; these patients are not malingering; and many improve with physical rehabilitation and retraining rather than with anything resembling moral exhortation. Here, she said, is a condition that is disabling and medically treatable while occupying precisely the position Szasz's criteria would call a pseudo-disease, so if his criteria entail that it is not a genuine medical problem, so much the worse for the criteria. She added that the disputed-baseline worry in (5) overstates: medicine fixes normal function against population baselines for blood pressure and filtration rates too, and the fact that some baselines are contested shows that a field is immature, not that it is counterfeit.

(7) Nils then proposed a positive test. Call it *the export test*: something is a genuine medical condition only if a competent clinician working from the patient's body — imaging, tissue, blood, electrophysiology, involuntary signs — could identify it without knowing what the patient's community thinks about the conduct in question. He conceded that the test demotes more than psychiatry: it also demotes pain disorders, chronic fatigue, and fibromyalgia, which he was willing to call medically real but epistemically unsettled. He said that is the honest result — those are areas where medicine has a complaint and no mechanism, and pretending otherwise is what produced the confident emptiness Szasz attacked.

(8) Marisol closed by saying the export test shows only that a field is not finished, and she pressed the practical point the argument had been circling: even granting Szasz everything about the metaphysics, the conclusion would be about who may coerce whom, not about whether suffering is real, whether care should be funded, or whether people should be excused. On her reading the dispute collapses sociologically into a fight about involuntary commitment, and disease language is doing the humane work of getting people care and leniency rather than punishment. Nils's last move, which they never resolved, was that this is the one place where metaphysics and politics cannot be separated: what makes forced treatment look benign is the belief that the person labours under a disease that impairs capacity, and if that belief is false then the coercion has to be justified the way any other deprivation of liberty is justified — on the criminal-law model, with its full procedural apparatus — which is what Szasz wanted and what the disease framing has always been used to avoid.

Please go through the eight moves and grade each — clearly mistaken, partly right, or sound — saying briefly what each gets right and where each goes wrong, then give an overall verdict naming precisely which issue each of them has the better of. Say too whether any part of the disagreement is merely verbal, and what, if anything, both of them are missing. Finally, tell me where your own adjudication is most likely to fail: which single argument, case, or distinction, if it were successfully made, would lead you to a different verdict, and which of the premises you lean on is the least secure.
