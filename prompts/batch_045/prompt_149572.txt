I spent a career in experimental condensed-matter physics, mostly inelastic neutron scattering, which means I spent a career inverting data: taking a measured cross-section and asking what spectral function produced it. I retired four years ago and have since been sitting in on philosophy of science; I have no training in it, so I am sure I am reinventing things and using the wrong vocabulary. What follows are notes toward a short essay I have been writing on the underdetermination of theory by evidence. I would be obliged for comments. Please do not give me a reading list, names to look up, or citations — I can find those myself. What I cannot do by myself is have someone argue with the argument.

The notes

Working title: Two Underdeterminations. The claim I want to defend is that the thesis that theory is underdetermined by evidence is not one thesis but a family, that the members differ enormously in how much metaphysical weight they can bear, that the famous members have no instances, and that the members with instances bear almost no weight. The appearance of a single alarming thesis is produced by sliding between them.

(i) Logical underdetermination. For any finite body of evidence there are indefinitely many mutually incompatible theories each of which entails it. The construction is trivial — conjoin any theory with arbitrary claims about unobserved regions or times, or run a grue-style manoeuvre on its predicates — and precisely because it is trivial it cannot be the source of anyone's worry.

(ii) Strong empirical equivalence. Two incompatible theories agree not merely on the evidence we happen to have but on all possible evidence: no experiment, however distant or refined, could separate them. This is the only version with real metaphysical bite, and the only way I know to secure it is a wholesale redescription of the world — the five-minute hypothesis, and devices of that shape. Historians of science do not, so far as I can tell, record a single case of two working theories related in this way.

(iii) Underdetermination at the bench. Within an accepted framework, and granting the framework entirely, the data do not fix the parameters or the functional form. Two subspecies. (a) Redundancy: in a Bayesian mixture model the likelihood is invariant under permutation of component labels, so several parameter vectors fit exactly equally well, and nobody thinks the world is ambiguous — we break the symmetry by an identifiability constraint and move on. (b) Genuine non-identifiability: the inverse problem violates one of Hadamard's conditions — typically uniqueness, or continuous dependence of the solution on the data — so that a band of functions fits within error. But here the remedy is experimental design, not argument: extend the momentum-transfer range, change the temperature, measure a second observable. Everyone in the lab is a thoroughgoing realist about the framework while disagreeing about the curve, which is the first sign that this species cannot be what the debate is about.

(iv) Conventional underdetermination. What remains when the ambiguity is not curable by any amount of data of the kind available. It lives in the near-null space of the forward operator, and it is tamed by adding a penalty functional that is not in the data: Tikhonov regularization penalizes curvature and so prefers smooth solutions; maximum-entropy methods penalize distance from a default model; sometimes one imposes positivity or monotonicity. Groups with different penalties, all defensible, publish different curves, all consistent with the same measurements. And more data of the same kind does not remove the disagreement, because the new data are informative only along the directions the old data already constrained; along the others the likelihood is flat and the penalty decides everything. Here, it seems to me, is the only species of underdetermination that is both real and stubborn — and it is stubborn because of a stipulation, not because of the evidence.

(v) The bait-and-switch. The argument from underdetermination needs (ii). What it has is (iii) and (iv). It borrows its urgency from the everyday pervasiveness of the latter and its conclusion from the former.

(vi) A wrinkle, which I am not comfortable about. (iv) is not entirely comfortable for the realist either. If the fine structure of the reconstructed curve is fixed by convention, then the unobservable description we publish is partly chosen, and the norms doing the choosing — smoothness, parsimony, robustness across priors — look pragmatic rather than truth-tracking. So the anti-realist gets a local and much more interesting victory than the global one: not that unobservables are unknowable, but that the unobservable description is partly stipulated. The curious fact that then needs explaining is convergence: laboratories do converge, and they converge because the penalties encode genuine prior knowledge — sum rules, positivity, known asymptotics — and because conventions are shared. But if that prior knowledge is evidence, the ambiguity was never as deep as I claimed in (iv); and if it is not evidence, we have a principled reason to distrust the fine structure of every reconstructed curve in the literature. I do not know which horn to take.

(vii) The punchline, which is where I am least sure I am not being silly. A theory–evidence pair can be underdetermined relative to one question and not another. In multiparameter models whose parameter sensitivities span many decades, a handful of stiff combinations of parameters are pinned down tightly while dozens of sloppy directions are essentially unconstrained — and yet predictions of new observables remain sharp, because the predictions depend mostly on the stiff combinations. So what is the value of this coupling? and what will the next measurement read? have quite different evidential statuses on the same data. Underdetermination is therefore a three-place relation: theory, evidence, and inferential target. Stated as a two-place relation it is ill-posed in exactly Hadamard's sense, and it needs a regularizing term — a specification of what question the theory is meant to answer. The realist wants that term set to the whole truth about the domain; the anti-realist wants it set to something weaker. The disagreement between them, I want to say, is entirely about the choice of the regularizing term, and not at all about the evidence.

What I would like from you

1. Accuracy. Three things in particular, since I am writing about my old field from memory. First: is it right that Hadamard's three conditions are existence, uniqueness and continuous dependence on the data, and is my claim in (iv) correct that further data of the same kind does not wash out the penalty — or is the prior in fact swamped asymptotically, with only the null-space component untouched? I want the qualification stated precisely, because (iv) and (vi) lean on it. Second: I recall from partial-wave analyses of elastic scattering that at fixed energy there can be distinct phase-shift sets reproducing a differential cross-section exactly, a discrete ambiguity rather than a continuum. Is that right, and if so is it a case of (iii-b) or of (iv)? Third: is my description of stiff and sloppy directions in (vii) fair, and is sloppy the word people actually use for it?

2. Are (iii-b) and (iv) genuinely different, or is (iv) just (iii-b) plus impatience — the same ambiguity, differently described once we admit that the experiment we would need is not going to be done?

3. Is the bait-and-switch charge in (v) fair to the argument as it is actually made? I expect a defender to say that the constructed rivals of (i) and (ii) were never meant as live options but as a diagnosis: they show that whatever moves us to choose is not the evidence. Does that diagnosis survive my taxonomy, or does it depend on the slide I allege? Please state this objection at its strongest before answering it.

4. Is (vii) a real point or a trivial one — merely the observation that you cannot be underdetermined except relative to what you want to infer, dressed up in the vocabulary of inverse problems? And can the realist simply set the inferential target to the whole truth about the domain and thereby dissolve it? If that move is available, I think my essay has no ending, so I would like to know exactly what is wrong with it.

5. Which of the seven numbered passages is the weakest link, and why? I would rather be told that one of them fails than be told that the whole is interesting.

6. Does any of this travel? My examples are all cases of inference within a framework. Does the apparatus — especially (iv) and (vii) — say anything about the selection of frameworks themselves, or about the historical record of successful theories being discarded, or is it confined to the laboratory bench where I found it?

I would rather have three pages of argument than ten of survey, and I would rather be corrected than encouraged.
