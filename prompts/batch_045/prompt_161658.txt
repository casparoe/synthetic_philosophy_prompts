So I run a tiny interview podcast — LW/ACX-adjacent audience, couple thousand listeners, everyone's already read the Sequences and knows what a likelihood ratio is — and next weeks guest is a philosopher (I'll keep her anonymous here, call her "Dr. A") who's spent about a decade defending the fine-tuning argument for theism. She's not the William Lane Craig style at all: her version is explicitly Bayesian, she grants up front that the multiverse is the real rival and not some strawman, and the big move in her book is that any multiverse needs a universe-generator whose own parameters look fine-tuned too, so the anthropic explanation just pushes the tuning up a level. Her stock deflection is "that's a problem for the physics, not for the argument" — she wheels it out whenever anyone brings up priors over parameter space or how wide the life-permitting window actually is, and in print it works really well because it sounds like modesty.

I want eight to ten questions, and I dont want a single one where she just restates the argument. Things I specifically want covered:

- Her case against the multiverse assumes a generator's parameters are the sort of thing that can be "tuned". Is that assumption doing more work than the fine-tuning data is? She'll say explanations have to stop somewhere — I want a follow-up on why they should stop at God rather than at a generator, and another on whether "God is simple" is doing anything once you stop treating simplicity as a free lunch.
- The modal worry: the argument needs the constants to have been genuinely contingent, with some distribution over them; if the final theory turns out unique, there's nothing to explain. She'll probably say we should just use a broad prior reflecting our ignorance. I want a question that forces her to say whether an ignorance prior can carry confirmatory weight at all, plus a follow-up for when she says yes and that's just Bayesianism.
- A newer objection I haven't seen her address anywhere: you can't be maximally ignorant about the physics and simultaneously confident about the designer's motives. If the prior over the constants is broad because we dont know the physics, the prior over what a designer would want should be broad too, which drags P(fine-tuning | theism) back down. She'll likely answer that God's goodness constrains the motives. Follow-up: if the likelihood is really inherited from a moral premise, how much work is the fine-tuning doing, and is this secretly a moral argument wearing a physics costume?
- My own hobbyhorse, which I've been arguing about with a commenter who goes by measure_zero: the constants don't sit at the life-optimizing values and they don't sit at the anthropic edge either — the universe is mostly lethal volume, and the values look drawn conditional only on barely permitting life. That seems to cut against design and against anthropic selection alike, so it can't be a point in favor of theism. I want that put to her, with a follow-up ready for if she bites the bullet and says something like God wanted a law-governed universe that doesn't look designed (I'm pretty sure she's said something close to that).
- The one-parameter-at-a-time framing: she'll say the multi-dimensional window is even narrower. Follow up on whether holding the other constants fixed at their actual values is its own kind of this-universe chauvinism, given that compensating co-variation could widen the window a lot.
- Whether her argument needs the self-indication or the self-sampling assumption (or genuinely neither), and what happens to the argument if she has to commit to one. My listeners will notice if she tries to stay neutral here, so build that in.

Then I want one question, or a short chain, that makes "that's a problem for the physics, not for the argument" progressively harder to reach for — something about what the argument is even claiming to have established now, if its strength depends on physics nobody has. And a closing question about what actual observation would make her lower her confidence, something more specific than "if the constants turned out not to be fine-tuned".

For each question give me a line or two on the answer she's most likely to give and the follow-up keyed to that answer. And please, no "what got you interested in this?" openers.
