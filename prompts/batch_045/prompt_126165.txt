my students have completely stalled out on the problem of universals and I think it's because we keep arguing about it instead of doing anything with it. I've got a second-year metaphysics seminar, 18 students, one 80-minute slot, and the room is split in a boring way: half of them think that saying "these two marks are the same shape" commits you to something that literally recurs, half think it's just a convenient way of talking about resemblance and that no recurring thing is needed. Nobody has moved in two weeks.

So: design an activity where they hit this themselves. Small real stakes — points, a score, something they'd rather win — such that the rules force them to count, sort, or trade on sameness of shape in order to play. They should be settling questions like "one squiggle or two" because the game requires an answer, not because I asked for one.

Couple of design constraints I care about, from things I've tried and half-botched before. First, the marks should be unreadable — cards with a squiggle in a script I invent myself, or shapes off a random generator. If they can read them, the whole thing collapses into meaning and turns into a semantics discussion; I want the question to be only whether the same shape turned up twice. Second, I want looks and origin to come apart: a pair that matches because one was traced from the other, a pair that matches by accident, and one traced copy that's degraded just enough that its not obvious whether the same shape survived the copying. In my experience they go straight to "well, it depends what you mean by same" — that's fine, but I want them to notice how much work that move is doing rather than treating it as a conversation-ender.

Past that, two outcomes I'm after. One: at some point they should build, out of their own cards, a short chain where each mark shares something with the next one but the first and the last share nothing — and then have to say whether they were ever tracking a single thing at all. Two: they should leave the room still unable to describe what's on the table without saying "the same shape again", and still unable to say what that comes to. That pinch is the point. I don't want them to end up holding a position; I want them to end up feeling that the vocabulary is indispensable and uncashable at the same time.

Give me: rules, materials, a scoring scheme with no money in it, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps particular moments onto particular moves in the dispute — which moment generates the pull toward a recurring thing, which generates the pull toward "its only resemblance", and which moment is the one where "its only resemblance" starts to look like it needs a shared respect of its own. I don't want the words universal, particular, trope, type or token said out loud until the debrief. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I can already hear someone asking whether this is just a dispute about words), how to handle it when it does, and be straight about which part of the disagreement the game simply can't stage and I'll have to state myself.

Last thing: put a short note at the end saying where your design leans on a contested reading of what the realist is actually committed to, how confident you are in the design overall, and which single bit of it you're least sure will survive contact with an actual room of eighteen undergrads.
