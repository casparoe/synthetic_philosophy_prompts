I write about the ethics of artificial systems — specifically, which of them, if any, we might owe something to — and a colleague has handed me an argument I cannot find my way out of. I would like your diagnosis of it.

The position I want to end up with has three parts, and I would rather give up almost anything else than give up any of them:

(A) Substrate neutrality. If a computation matters morally, it matters in virtue of its abstract causal organization — the pattern of states and state-transitions and their counterfactual relations — and not in virtue of what realizes it. Two systems that implement the same computation equally well therefore have the same moral status, whatever they are made of.

(B) Implementation is a fact. For any physical system and any computation, there is a determinate answer to the question of how well the system implements that computation, and the answer is not settled by our choices of vocabulary, our explanatory interests, or how convenient it would be to describe the system one way rather than another.

(C) Non-vacuity. Some artificial systems could turn out to be moral patients, and this is the sort of thing we could in principle get right or get wrong, rather than merely decide.

The argument I have been given — call it the Profligacy Argument — is meant to show that these three cannot all be true.

(1) By (A), moral status tracks implemented computation. So any difference in moral status between two systems must correspond to a difference in what they implement, or in how well they implement it.

(2) Implementation is not mere match of actual state-history. A system implements a computation C only if its states can be mapped onto C's states in a way that is systematic and that survives counterfactual variation: nudge the system's state along some dimension, or vary its input, and the successor state must be the one C prescribes. Define the system's modal fidelity with respect to C as the proportion of the relevant intervention-space — the space of state-perturbations and input-variations over which we test — on which the mapping holds. This is meant to dispose of the familiar triviality result, according to which any system with enough discriminable states can be paired with any small enough automaton by a gerrymandered mapping that matches on the actual run. Such mappings have negligible modal fidelity: they break under nearly every intervention.

(3) Modal fidelity is graded, and there is no natural threshold at which a system begins to "really" implement C. Any finite system degrades under sufficiently demanding interventions — heat it, cut it, drive it outside its operating envelope — and the mapping fails. Conversely, under a sufficiently narrow intervention-space, nearly any mapping looks robust. Nothing in the system settles which grain we test at, which perturbations we allow, or over what timescale we evaluate.

(4) What settles these is what we want the ascription for: prediction, control, explanation, design. Ascriptions of implementation are the working currency of engineering and cognitive science, and their grain is set by the job in hand. So, given (2) and (3), how well a system implements C is a fact relative to a scheme of description.

(5) Profligacy. The space of admissible schemes is rich enough that for a great many systems S — a thermostat, a bucket of water, a stretch of wall — and a great many computations C, including computations of intense suffering, there is some admissible scheme relative to which S implements C at high modal fidelity. The manoeuvre is to choose the intervention-space to which the gerrymandered mapping happens to be robust, and then describe that space in general and ungerrymandered-sounding terms: perturbations within the system's normal operating range, inputs drawn from its natural distribution, and so on. Step (2) requires only that the mapping hold over some intervention-space; it says nothing against fitting the space to the mapping.

(6) So a domestic thermostat, under one admissible scheme, implements a computation of great suffering at high modal fidelity, and under another implements nothing of moral interest at all. Both ascriptions are equally well supported by everything (2) can see.

(7) Hold on to (B), and there is a determinate fact about whether the thermostat suffers, while we have no route to it. That is skepticism wearing realism's coat, and it makes (C) unreachable in principle.

(8) Drop (B), and by (1) moral status itself varies with our scheme of description. Pick the right scheme and the thermostat is a moral patient of the first rank; pick another and it is a mere artifact. There is no further question about which is correct, and (C) becomes trivially satisfiable, which is no better.

(9) So (A), (B) and (C) cannot all stand, and something has gone wrong somewhere in (1) through (8).

I have tried two repairs and can see a problem with each. The first adds to (2) a simplicity constraint: the mapping, or the mapping together with its intervention-space, must admit a short description and must project beyond the cases used to fix it. But simplicity looks like precisely the sort of pragmatic, purpose-relative virtue that (4) invokes, and I cannot see why a gerrymandered mapping should always lose on simplicity to the intended one. The second takes moral weight to scale with modal fidelity, so that near-misses have near-zero weight; but then I owe an answer to a case I will call the Time-Share, in which one processor interleaves ten thousand simulations of the same human nervous system, each at fidelity 0.99. If weight is additive and roughly linear near the top of the scale, ten thousand degraded runs outweigh one clean one, which seems to me the wrong result, and in any case grading the weight does not tell me how many patients there are.

Please tell me which step you reject — or, if you think the reasoning is valid and a premise simply has to go, which one and what it costs — and explain why the step looked sound and what I pay for each of the alternatives I might have rejected instead. Apply your diagnosis to two cases in particular: the Time-Share above, and a second I will call the Frame-Skip, in which an emulator computes only every thousandth state-update and reconstructs the intervening states by a fixed interpolation rule, so that at a coarse temporal grain it matches the target computation across a wide intervention-space while at a fine grain the intermediate states are artifacts of a rule external to the target. Is anyone a patient there, and does your answer to the Profligacy Argument settle it?

Finally, close by telling me where your own diagnosis is most likely to be wrong and what would change your mind: which consideration, if it were made good, would lead you to a different verdict about which step to reject. Please write in British English.
