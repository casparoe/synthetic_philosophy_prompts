I'm in a small reading group working through the triviality results for indicative conditionals. In our last session someone used a one-liner to wave the results off: the probability of a conditional is a conditional probability — that, he said, is all the thesis ever claimed, and the results only refute a straw man, so the substantive thesis is untouched. I don't think that's right, but I couldn't say why on the spot, because the sentence seems to me to slide between being a definition, a piece of notation, a substantive claim about what if means, and something we already know to be false.

Could you lay out, as a menu rather than as a single reading, the distinct precise theses that sentence could be expressing? I'd like them ordered from the deflationary end — where it says no more than that conditional probability is written P(B | A), or that P(B | A) is the probability the agent would have in B upon learning A — to the substantive end, where it says something about the semantic value of an indicative conditional, or about whether such a conditional expresses a proposition at all. For each reading I'd like: a one-line formal statement where notation helps; whether it is trivial, contentious, or false; whether it is a casualty of the triviality arguments or untouched by them; and whether it treats the conditional as a full member of the algebra of propositions — negatable, conjoinable, and embeddable as the antecedent or consequent of a further conditional — or instead abandons the idea that it is a truth-evaluable proposition with a probability of its own.

Two things would make the menu more useful to me than a general survey.

First, say explicitly which readings are embeddable and which are not, and whether the triviality arguments still go through once embeddability is surrendered. I'd also like to know where in the argument the assumption is doing its work: my sense is that the proof needs the relevant identity to hold not just for the agent's probability function but for that function conditionalized on arbitrary further information, and I can't yet tell which of the readings can survive restricting it in that way.

Second, a test case. Take four cells with probabilities P(A ∧ B) = 0.1, P(A ∧ ¬B) = 0.2, P(¬A ∧ B) = 0.3 and P(¬A ∧ ¬B) = 0.4, so that P(A) = 0.3, P(B) = 0.4 and P(B | A) = 1/3. For each reading on the menu, tell me what it says about the probability of if A, B, or about the acceptability of asserting it, and whether that is a commitment anyone could actually hold. Where a reading forces the probability of the conditional to be both 1/3 and 0.4, say so, and say which assumption does the forcing.

Finally: which reading is someone who brushes the results off with this one-liner almost certainly reaching for, and is that a reading he can keep while still helping himself to embedded constructions such as if it is not the case that if A, B, then C? I don't need citations or a reading list — I have the syllabus. I need the distinctions sharp enough to take into the next session.
