I run a fortnightly reading group for final-year undergrads and masters students in history and political economy — about 18 people, 90 minutes, a seminar room with movable tables, ideally no laptops. Our next session is on the "why Britain and not China?" question: the Needham-style framing, and the great divergence literature everyone seems to have an opinion about. I really don't want to run it as a normal seminar, because I know exactly what will happen: half the room says coal, colonies and institutions, the other half says contingency and that it could easily have gone the other way, and everyone leaves thinking the disagreement is about the facts. What I want them to feel in their gut, before anyone names it, is that the question might not be one question at all — that a lot of its apparent tractability is manufactured by choices buried in the framing. I've been trying to sort out what those choices are and I've got three that I keep coming back to, with my own clumsy labels. "Contrast drift": a discussion that starts by explaining a difference slides into explaining one of the two things, so factors that were present on both sides get cited as causes of the difference. "Retrospective outcome-picking": the outcome is only salient because it succeeded, so "why industrialisation?" is really "why the thing that then happened?", and re-describing the outcome slightly (first sustained growth in output per head vs. first factory-based mechanised textile production vs. fastest catch-up) changes which causes even look relevant. And what I've started calling the "twin-track assumption": treating two regions as parallel, independent runs of the same experiment when they were causally coupled — silver flows, trade, technology transfer, war — so that "why this one and not that one?" quietly compares two things that were never separate trials.

Can you design a 90-minute activity for that room that makes students run into all three without me lecturing them first? I'd like it to be a game or structured simulation with real-ish stakes and payoffs (points, chips, no money, nothing that needs printing beyond cards). Here's my seed idea, which you should feel free to throw out and replace: I hand out a pool of maybe twenty anonymised factor cards — deliberately schematic, e.g. "cheap accessible coal within 50 km of the main textile districts", "a large internal market with low internal tariffs", "wage levels high relative to the cost of energy", "access to a captive overseas market for manufactures", "a state that borrows cheaply against future tax revenue", "widespread household-level production for the market", "high-yield agriculture supporting dense population", "a merchant diaspora with long-distance credit networks" — and groups have to build the best two-sentence answer to "why Britain and not China?" using them. Then the facilitator flips each cited card to reveal whether it was in fact present in the other region too, and any card that was present on both sides costs them a point rather than earning one. My worry is that this lands as a gotcha and they feel tricked, so maybe they should pre-register, before the reveal, what fraction of the cards they expect to be shared. I'd also like a mechanism for the third problem — some kind of "linkage challenge" where a group can flip a card to "shared" by naming a plausible transmission channel between the two regions — so that the non-independence of the two cases is something they do rather than something I assert. And ideally an opening move that makes them commit, in advance of any evidence, to which region they'd have bet on in 1750, so the baseline-of-expectedness point arrives because of what they themselves staked.

What I need back is: the rules and the payoff scheme; a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes including setup and reset; what the facilitator says and, just as importantly, doesn't say; and a debrief that maps specific moments onto specific bits of the three problems, and then says what survives — because I don't want them walking out thinking "so the question is meaningless", I want them able to say what would have to be fixed for it to be answerable and what the answer would then be about. Please also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: there's always one student who's read a lot of the actual history and will try to relitigate the empirical claims, and I suspect the scoring will turn it into competitive trivia and the conceptual point will get lost. Two more constraints: I'd rather the activity didn't hinge on any contested empirical claim (I've heard it said that living standards in the Yangzi delta were comparable to England's as late as the mid-eighteenth century, but I don't want to bet the session on my having got that right, so nothing should break if it turns out to be wrong), and I'd rather it didn't just reproduce the structure-vs-contingency argument they've all seen before. Finally, be honest about which part of this the activity simply cannot reproduce and which bit I'll have to supply myself in the debrief — I'd rather know in advance than discover it halfway through.
