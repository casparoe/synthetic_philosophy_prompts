The wrong kind of reasons problem seems to me to have settled into a stalemate with a fixed shape: everyone agrees that a demon's threat is not the sort of reason that could make a saucer of mud valuable, and everyone agrees that buck-passing and fitting-attitude accounts of value need some right-kind/wrong-kind distinction if they are to survive; everything after that is proposed distinction, counter-example, revised distinction, new counter-example. One camp keeps trying to draw the line — reasons that bear on whether the attitude fits, reasons sourced in the features of the object, reasons to respond to something rather than to bring it about, reasons whose weight is inherited from the object's value — and the other concludes that no such line can be drawn in a non-question-begging way and that the theory should be narrowed instead.

I have two hypotheses, and I want them tested rather than confirmed. First: that the dispute is not really about reasons but is a proxy war over whether desiring and admiring have a correctness condition independent of the attitudes' effects — fittingness taken as primitive versus analyzed away — with talk of kinds of reasons as the vocabulary both sides use to fight it. Second: that the counter-example machine runs on a diet of threats and bribes, all of which are extrinsic to the object in the same way, and that the proposals should be tested against a case I have not seen handled, in which the demon rewards you not for desiring the saucer but for desiring everything exactly in proportion to its actual value, whatever that value turns out to be. That reason is extrinsic, so it looks wrong-kind, yet it makes your desires co-vary perfectly with value, so a buck-passing account seems to return every verdict correctly.

Given all that: is this a substantive disagreement about reasons, a verbal disagreement about what counts as a reason, or two questions — one about the analysis of value, one about the normativity of attitudes — pursued under one heading? Give me the diagnosis grounded in what each side actually argues, tell me honestly whether my proportionality case does any work or is merely a bribe in costume, and then say what would count as progress here — a distinction, a formal result, a reframing of the question — or why no progress should be expected.
