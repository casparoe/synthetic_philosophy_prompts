I'm giving a dept seminar in evolutionary biology in two weeks — it's a joint thing with the philosophy department, so the room is mixed — and I want a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defense of the view.

The thesis: Gould and Lewontin's 1979 spandrels paper is best read as a claim about evidential asymmetry, not as a claim about how common non-adaptation is in nature. The "what percentage of traits are adaptations" reading is a dead end and I'll say so. But the methodological core survives, and it bites hardest on a move that's still completely routine: inferring from "this trait does something useful now" or "knocking it out lowers fitness" to "selection built it for that job."

Sketch of what I'm actually arguing. I split the cases three ways instead of two:

- arch: the adapted core — selection for the job it's doing is the best explanation of why it's there
- spandrel: structurally entailed byproduct — the space exists because of the arches, and it couldn't be removed without redesigning them
- tenant: something that moved into a space that was going to be there anyway; the slot would have existed, in roughly the same form, if the tenant had never evolved; and once it's in residence selection maintains it, so under any synchronic test it looks exactly like an adaptation

My claim is that most of the cases people call spandrels in practice are tenants, and tenants are where the adaptationist inference does its worst work — worse than with spandrels, because for a spandrel you can at least argue from the architecture, whereas for a tenant no amount of current-function evidence tells you anything about origination. I operationalize it as a vacancy test: before you call a trait an adaptation for job J, show that the slot it occupies wouldn't have been there, or would have been there differently, if the trait hadn't been. I also need to distinguish this from exaptation (Gould and Vrba, 1982): exaptation is a claim about function-history, tenant-hood is a claim about the counterfactual availability of the slot. The target I'm applying it to is the behavioural ecologists' everyday slide from "this behaviour raises fitness in this population" to "this behaviour is an adaptation."

The room: two behavioural ecologists who do optimality modelling and cost-benefit work and think Gould was a gifted rhetorician who never measured a selection coefficient in his life; two evo-devo / developmental people who are sympathetic to constraint talk but will want my distinction to cash out in developmental mechanism; one population geneticist who wants equations and effect sizes and thinks the whole debate is unfalsifiable stamp collecting; a couple of philosophers of biology who'll want to know how this isn't just a re-run of what got sorted out about empirical vs. methodological adaptationism; and a handful of grad students who mainly want to know what changes in their work on Monday.

For each: a two-minute reply, what I should concede, and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Also tell me what I should give away on slide two before anyone asks, which single reply has to land or the whole thing dies, and — importantly — which of my three categories (arch, spandrel, tenant) is the weakest and how I should defend it rather than just hope nobody pokes at it.
