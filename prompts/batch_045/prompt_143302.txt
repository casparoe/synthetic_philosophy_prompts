I'm spending this term as a research assistant on a project that is building a searchable archive of display letterforms — we photograph and tag glyphs pulled from posters, signage and experimental faces, and before a glyph can be tagged as an instance of a letter it goes past our letterform identification panel, which is chaired by a senior type designer named Renata Voss. The panel's answers are always yes or no with a single line of justification, and in none of the rulings I have read has she ever stated a general criterion. I have been going through her archived verdicts on submissions put forward as candidates for lowercase a, and they run like this: a conventional two-storey a from a transitional serif, yes; a geometric single-storey a, yes; a single-storey a in which the stem has come away from the bowl leaving a hairline gap, yes, an a with a broken join; an extreme display a whose bowl has been triangulated and whose upper arch has straightened into a bar, yes; a textura a which the panel itself could not name when the glyph was shown alone but which everyone read correctly inside words, yes; a mirror-image single-storey a — bowl on the right, stem on the left — submitted as a specimen from a constructed script, no; a single-storey a with a left-hooking descender, no, the tail has carried it over into g; a bowl with a straight vertical stem on the right rising a full ascender above the x-height, submitted from an ordinary Latin-script face whose d already has exactly that shape, no; and a conventional a stretched to ten times its width, yes. My friend, who does philosophy and keeps returning to the old Letter Spirit thought that there is no list of features shared by every a, only a sense of the letter's spirit that emerges when a whole alphabet shares a style, says the record is explained by one hard rule: a closed or nearly closed counter plus a stem on the right that stays inside the x-height and does not descend, and nothing else matters. I think at least two other rules fit the same verdicts just as well — one keyed to what competent readers actually do with the glyph, where her textura ruling suggests she means reading inside words rather than naming in isolation, and one keyed to the glyph's job inside its own alphabet: which slot it occupies and what it contrasts with there.

The submission in front of us now is a display face called Sable Grid, built on a coarse grid under a stated generative rule: every lowercase letter is a bowl plus one attachment, and the attachments are handed out so that no two letters in the face collide. In Sable Grid the o is a bare bowl; the g is a bowl with a tail that drops below the baseline and hooks left; the d is a bowl with a stem on the right that rises past the x-height and then curves left into a small beak at the top; and the glyph submitted for the a slot is a bowl with a straight vertical stem on the right, rising a full ascender above the x-height and ending in a flat terminal — to all appearances the very shape she turned down in the earlier ruling. It occurs nowhere else in the face, so it has no rival for the slot, and the designer's reading test is in the file: fifteen readers were given a paragraph set in Sable Grid, and after the first line or two they read the a-slot glyph as an a at essentially every occurrence and the beak-topped glyph as a d; shown the two glyphs in isolation on a card, eleven of the fifteen called the a-slot glyph a d, and on the beak-topped glyph their answers scattered between d, a and nothing at all. Asked where the shape came from, the designer said it was not deformed out of an earlier a at all, it simply fell out of the grid rule — while also pointing out that you can reach it from a conventional a by lengthening the stem in three small steps, and from a conventional d in none.

So: what does Renata Voss say about the a of Sable Grid, and how confident are you? I would like you to set out the two or three rules that fit her record equally well, to say what each predicts about the glyph in the a slot and what each predicts about the beak-topped glyph sitting in the d slot, and then to give me your best guess together with the reasons for preferring it — including what she would have to give up, or quietly reinterpret, in order to rule the other way. And because we can still commission one further test submission before the panel meets, tell me which single glyph we should put in front of her to force the rules apart, something on which your candidate rules stop agreeing, and tell me what each of her possible answers to it would reveal about which rule she has really been running all along.
