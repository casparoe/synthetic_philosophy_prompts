ok so last night me and my friend got into about a three hour argument and I need to turn it into an exam question before I lose my mind.

The topic was this "lexical threshold" negative utilitarian sort of view — at least thats what I think its called, I half remember it from some forum post and dont ask me who came up with it. Roughly: mild aches, boredom, small disappointments are supposed to trade off against pleasures in the normal additive way, but once suffering crosses some intensity threshold it lexically dominates any amount of happiness, so no quantity of pleasure can ever compensate for it. Shes a straight hedonist and kept saying pleasure and pain are obviously two ends of one scale and this threshold thing is just a hack for people who cant stomach where the math takes you. I kept saying the "one scale" bit is the part doing all the work and she never actually defended it, she just kept restating it louder. We gave up around 1am, neither of us moved.

I TA a third-year seminar on well-being and I want one 90 minute in-class essay question (1200 words max) out of this. The thing I want to avoid is a question you can answer by describing the view and then listing pros and cons — I want a question where the crux is whether the view can even state its own central claim without helping itself to the commensurability its supposed to be skeptical about.

Heres the case I was sketching out on the bus, improve it or bin it if youve got something sharper. Call it "the Dimmer": you can either let one person have four seconds of agony at intensity 7.1 on a 0–10 scale where the lexical threshold sits at 7.0, or you can push a button that drops every single pain down to 6.9 but multiplies the total duration of suffering by a million (a trillion people each nursing a 6.9 headache for a thousand years, whatever, pick numbers that make the point). The threshold version seems committed to pushing the button, and to still pushing it when you make the multiplier bigger. Then the second horn: if instead the threshold is supposed to sit on something aggregated — total suffering, or suffering-per-person over a life — the million-year headaches can add up and cross it, but now you need interpersonally comparable cardinal intensities to do the adding, and once youve got a common currency for pains, what was the reason you didnt have one for pains and pleasures?

Third thing, only if it fits: she also brought up tiny probabilities, and I said if the disvalue above the threshold is lexically infinite then any nonzero risk of crossing it swamps everything and you get total paralysis, and if you fix that by putting a second threshold on probability then youve quietly reintroduced a cardinal product of probability and disvalue, i.e. a scale. If a good answer should have to deal with that too, work it in.

What I need from you:

- The question itself. One question, no subparts that can be answered separately, and it should make them take a stand: is the view self-undermining, or can it say what its saying without a common scale — and if it can, what fixes the direction of the priority, given that incommensurability is symmetric?
- A model answer, around 700 words. First sentence has to be the bottom line — the verdict on whether the view survives — with no setup or throat-clearing before it.
- A marking scheme out of 20 built on four or five must-hit moves, with the marks for each and one line on how people typically botch it. Include an explicit note on what caps an answer at a B: I want it to be something like "correctly restates the view and the case but never says which premise the threshold theorist should give up and what giving it up costs," not something a student can dodge by hedging.
- No citations, no reading list, no "see also." I dont want to chase references and neither do the students.

Just start with the question, no preamble and no "here is a question about..." line. Then the model answer, then the scheme. And make it a question she would actually have to stop and think about, shes going to read it.
