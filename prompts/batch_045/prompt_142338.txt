I'm a third-year undergraduate writing a senior thesis for a cross-listed seminar on historiography and cultural memory, and in three weeks I present the central chapter at our department's honors colloquium: twenty minutes, then a question period in front of four respondents and a room of my classmates. The chapter's argument, in brief, is this. The standard way of posing the legend question — did something like the core event happen, and how much of the surviving narrative is accurate to it? — has the wrong shape, because it makes history a property of a story's causal origins, and therefore returns the same verdict for traditions that function in utterly different ways in the lives of the communities carrying them. I want to replace it with two conditions. Call the first constitutive load: some detail of the story is presupposed by an institution, a boundary, a liturgical calendar, a land-claim, a genealogy or a legal title, such that abandoning that detail would require re-founding something rather than merely revising a belief. Call the second evidential uptake: insiders begin to treat at least some details of the story as answerable to the same kind of checking they would apply to a non-legendary past event — they date it, locate it, and argue about it in public, and the arguments are expected to be settled by evidence rather than by authority or piety. My claim is that a legend becomes history when it acquires both; that constitutive load almost always comes first and does most of the work, with evidential uptake as the tipping point; and that the crossing is itself a datable historical event rather than a verdict we render from the present. My three cases then line up in a way I find interesting and slightly unnerving. The Trojan material crosses earliest, and not with the nineteenth-century spadework at Hissarlik but in the Classical period, when Greek communities were using it to date their own foundations and Herodotus was turning his canons of inquiry on it. The Arthurian material crosses later, but it does cross, on the strength of dynastic genealogies, landscape monuments and eventually the antiquarian quarrels over where the things in it supposedly happened — note that our earliest insular source, a sixth-century monk writing a moral jeremiad, describes a siege at Badon Hill without naming Arthur, and the name only attaches in a ninth-century compilation and again in the tenth-century Welsh annals, which is exactly the kind of gap my apparatus is meant to explain rather than be defeated by. The Exodus crosses latest of the three, and by a mechanism — the festival calendar — that has nothing to do with whether anyone marched anywhere. The argument runs in three steps: verification is neither necessary nor sufficient; what changes over time is how many otherwise-independent commitments would have to be false for the story to be false; and this entanglement, not causal origin, is what the word history has been tracking all along.

The four respondents are these. First, a field archaeologist of the Bronze Age Aegean and Anatolia, evidence-first in temperament, impatient with theory, who thinks the word legend is a dustbin and will want to know what I would count as evidence and what would change my mind; I expect her to say that I have made history a fact about people's attitudes rather than about the past, and to press the point that in some of my own cases the silence of the record is informative. Second, a theorist of cultural memory and reception who works on affect, trauma and postcolonial criticism, and who will suspect I am a closet positivist: she will object that a criterion keyed to institutions ratifies whatever happened to survive, and so systematically withholds historicity from traditions whose institutions were deliberately dismantled — colonized and missionized peoples — while granting it to any well-institutionalized fabrication. Third, an analytic philosopher of history, who will ask whether my conditions are supposed to be necessary or sufficient, whether the account is circular, whether it is a semantic thesis about our word history or an epistemic thesis about what we are entitled to believe (and, if semantic, why anyone outside the colloquium should care), and who has two counterexamples ready: prehistory, where there is history and no institutions built on it, and the forged founding charter of the Donation of Constantine sort, where there is constitutive load and no history. Fourth, a historian of ancient Judaism who will point out that whatever I say is going to be heard as taking a side in the minimalist–maximalist dispute, that my account seems to imply the Exodus became history at some point in the Second Temple period, which the maximalists in the room will hear as conceding everything and the minimalists as giving away far too much, and who will ask what my criterion does with a tradition whose entire load is liturgical. A friend of mine who is writing a classics thesis on Homeric reception tells me the Trojan half is the weakest part of the chapter, so I would like that probed hardest.

What I want from you is a prep sheet, not a defense. Give me the nine or ten objections I will actually get from this panel, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, each attributed to the respondent who will raise it and phrased the way that person would phrase it — I want the archaeologist to sound like an archaeologist and the philosopher like a philosopher, not like a generic critic — and each with a reply I could deliver in about two minutes, plus a one-line note on where that reply is weakest. Then tell me: what I should concede up front, before anyone asks, in order to be taken seriously by this particular group; the single reply that has to land if the chapter is to survive the hour; a one-sentence way to dispatch the isn't-this-just-relativism question from the floor without spending five minutes of my Q&A on it; and the question I am hoping nobody asks. My own guess is that the killer is the Ossian case — the Gaelic poems Macpherson published in the 1760s as translations of ancient originals, which turned out to be substantially his own composition: they acquired enormous constitutive load almost immediately, European romantic nationalism and an entire iconography of the north, and then insiders subjected them to evidential uptake and concluded they were largely fabricated. Tell me whether I am right that this is the one to fear, whether my apparatus can absorb it, or whether something worse is waiting that I have not seen. Please don't give me a reading list or citations; I can find those myself. I want the arguments.
