I am a retired physical chemist — forty years in molecular spectroscopy, mostly on band shapes and intermolecular potentials — who has taken up philosophy of science since retiring, and I would be grateful for comments on the notes below. They are the skeleton of an essay I am writing for a small reading group; I would much rather have the argument pulled apart now than after I have written five thousand polished words of it.

Working title: Progress without truth — a regime-relative notion of truthlikeness.

1. The itch. For most of my working life I used models I knew to be false: the harmonic oscillator, the ideal gas, Debye–Hückel, the rigid rotor. Nobody in the laboratory thought the harmonic oscillator was true; everybody thought it was closer to the truth than treating a bond as a rigid rod, and closer than having no model at all. Getting better answers out of it was largely a matter of knowing where it breaks. When I began reading philosophy of science I assumed that turning this laboratory platitude into something precise was a solved bookkeeping problem. It is not, and the reasons it is not strike me as more interesting than the platitude.

2. Popper's move and its collapse, as I understand it. The definition I have in front of me works with two classes: the true consequences of a theory and its false ones. Roughly, a theory does better when its true consequences go up or its false consequences go down, with the comparison cashed out as inclusions between the two classes rather than as a number, so that the definition does not presume we can count infinities. The point of the whole apparatus was to allow that our best theories can be strictly false and yet that inquiry can still be moving toward the truth. My understanding is that this fell apart in the mid-1970s: three papers, I am told in the same issue of the same journal, showed that on that definition no false theory is closer to the truth than any other — exactly the verdict the notion was introduced to avoid — and that Popper accepted the criticism essentially as soon as it was put to him (I have read that last bit second-hand and would like to know whether it is documented or merely anecdotal). My own gloss on why it collapses: once a theory is false, both its true and its false consequences are infinite and spread across the whole of logical space, so the inclusion comparisons degenerate; and I have a half-memory that the actual argument works by disjoining the false theory with whatever you want to compare it to, so that any candidate improvement can be shown to gain truths only by losing truths. I have not been able to reconstruct this myself, and I would rather not build an essay on a half-memory.

3. My diagnosis. The collapse looks to me like the penalty for asking a question nobody in science ever asks: how close is this theory to the truth about everything? We never deploy a theory everywhere. We deploy it in a regime — low velocities, dilute solutions, weak fields, distances below the mean free path — and against a limited set of questions we actually care about. So: index truthlikeness to what I am calling an inquiry frame, by which I mean a finite partition of the possibilities over some domain together with a scope restriction, and score a theory only on the questions in the partition, only within the regime. Crudely: answers got right minus answers got wrong, inside the frame. As I read it, the impossibility argument needs the whole of logical space in order to run; cut the space down to a frame and it should not get off the ground.

4. The first thing wrong with that. We do not know which answers are right — that is the whole difficulty — so in practice I have to score against a benchmark, which will be the best theory currently on offer. But then the measure has stopped being about the truth and started being about agreement with our present best guess, and I am not sure I have done anything except relabel empirical adequacy. I would like to know whether that slide is avoidable or whether any usable notion of closeness has to be epistemic in this way.

5. Refinement robustness. Second objection: verdicts depend on how finely the questions are posed, and a coarse partition can flatter a bad theory. My fix is to demand dominance: one theory improves on another relative to a frame only if it does at least as well on every admissible refinement of the partition and better on some. I suspect this is so demanding that almost nothing ever comes out closer to anything else, which would be a new route to the same pessimistic conclusion. Is there a weaker clause that still blocks the gerrymandering — dominance only over refinements down to some grain, perhaps, or only over refinements that respect the regime — or is the demandingness the price of the whole approach?

6. The historical case that matters, and the clause I am least sure about. Newtonian gravitation and general relativity do not share a question set in any obvious sense: one asks what force acts on a body, the other what the curvature is. My bridge is to count the older theory as recovered as a limiting case of the newer one — weak fields, low velocities — and to add a second requirement, that in the regime where the two theories diverge the newer one's novel answers are borne out. Progress, on this account, is limit-recovery plus differential novel success. I cannot tell whether the first clause is doing real work or is only a bow in the direction of the correspondence principle, and I cannot tell whether the two clauses can come apart.

7. The case that worries me. Prout's hypothesis — that atomic weights are integer multiples of that of hydrogen — is false, and yet the discovery of isotopes and of the proton makes it look prescient in a way that, say, phlogiston does not. Limit-recovery gives me nothing here; Prout is not a limiting case of anything. What I want to say is that the hypothesis was right about a structural feature, discreteness and a common building block, while wrong about the magnitudes, and I have no way to say that which is not simply hindsight. Is there a principled distinction to be drawn between the Prout case and the phlogiston case, or is the difference only that we now possess a theory that redeems one and not the other?

8. Where I suspect this leaves me. If the notion has to be indexed to a frame, a regime and a benchmark, it is a long way from the plain claim that science is getting closer to the truth, and it may turn out to be the view that what survives theory change is structure rather than content — I think that position has a name — wearing extra apparatus. I would rather be told that now.

What I would like from you, in whatever order serves the argument:

- Accuracy first. Correct anything wrong in section 2, and give me a reconstruction of the impossibility argument if you can produce one; if you cannot, say so rather than sketching something plausible.
- Whether restricting to a frame genuinely blocks an argument of that kind, or whether an analogue of it reappears inside the frame. Work it out rather than asserting it.
- Whether my move is a variant of something already in this area. A name in a sentence is enough; I do not need a bibliography and I can find the literature myself once I know the term. But if you are not reasonably confident that a named approach or result exists, say you are unsure instead of supplying one — an invented reference is worse for me than none.
- The strongest objection you can mount, and the strongest counterexample: a pair of historical theories where my account gives a verdict you take to be clearly wrong.
- A blunt verdict on whether this is worth five thousand words, with reasons. Do not be kind about it.
- Throughout, please keep separate what is settled background, what is contested among people who actually work on this, and what is your own conjecture; do not present a contested reading as the consensus, and mark the places where you are extrapolating. Assume competence in physical science and none in philosophy; I do not need a primer, and I would rather have three hard paragraphs on sections 3 through 7 than a survey.
