I'm in a small reading group on population ethics — we spent last week on the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism, the bit where adding up welfare across people is supposed to miss something important about claims, boundaries, or about who exactly has a complaint. I'm fairly convinced by it in the abstract, but I got stuck when I tried to find it out in the wild, so I went poking at law, which I basically know nothing about, and now I have a puzzle I can't sort out.

Here's the pattern as I understand it (correct me if I've got it wrong). In ordinary civil litigation everything is per person: each plaintiff has to show their own injury to have standing, the verdict form has a seperate line for each of them, and damages get paid to each individually. More strikingly, a defendant can't reduce what they owe by showing that the activity which hurt these plaintiffs made thousands of other people better off — my impression is that "the plant also created jobs and cheap goods" is simply not a defense to compensating the person it poisoned, and I half-remember some rule about not being allowed to add together different people's claims to reach a jurisdictional threshhold. But the same legal system, when an agency writes a regulation, does the opposite: it tots up monetized benefits and costs across millions of unidentified people, uses a value-of-a-statistical-life number, and lets diffuse losses to some fall on the wrong side of the ledger so long as the total comes out positive. And with pure risk — exposure that raises everyone's chances before anyone is identifiable — tort mostly shrugs, while the regulatory side is entirely comfortable.

Two extra bits I want to throw in, because they're what actually broke my brain. First, a proposal someone floated in the group: a "ledger defense," where a defendant in a mass-exposure case could put in evidence about the net welfare effect of their conduct across everyone affected and have compensatory damages reduced accordingly, on the grounds that this is exactly what the regulatory state already does and it's inconsistent to refuse it in court. Second, the reverse move: let lots of tiny scattered harms be bundled so that together they clear a seriousness threshold none of them could clear alone — an injury without an injured person, if you like.

So: what does the refusal to net presuppose? Is it the separateness of persons objection proper, or can corrective justice, deterrence, administrability, or just the bilateral structure of a trial get you the same practice with no metaphysics of persons at all? Which features of the practice strictly require an anti-aggregation view and which merely sit well with one — and are the per-person bit and the pure-risk bit even consistent with each other? If we swapped in the aggregative view wholesale, what exactly would have to change at trial: the standing rule, the verdict form, who gets paid, what counts as an injury? And is there a version of the ledger defense a utilitarian should reject, or is refusing it just an indirect-consequentialist rule in disguise?

Please be explicit about confidence: flag which parts of your reconstruction you're actually sure about versus inferring from the general shape of the law, and keep apart what the practice needs from what it merely tolerates. And if the whole thing rests on a mistake — if I've muddled two unrelated doctrines, or tort law isn't nearly as non-aggregative as I've made out, or the regulatory side doesn't aggregate the way I think — say so plainly instead of writing around it; I'd much rather be told the puzzle is fake than get a tidy answer to it.
