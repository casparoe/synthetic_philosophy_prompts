I'm a retired experimental physicist — forty-odd years of measuring things, most of them wrong the first time — and since I stoped work I've been reading philosophy in a disorganized way, partly on purpose, so forgive me if I use the wrong words for what I mean.

Here's the practice I keep turning over. Every general-purpose chatbot you can talk to now is built to do a certain cluster of things, and the cluster looks to me like it only makes sense on one particular theory of what honesty and deception are, and I want that theory dug out. The cluster: the system is required to disclose that it is a machine, and to say so again whenever the conversation drifts that way. It is trained to say that it has no feelings, no beliefs, no preferences, no body, and that it can't know what it wasn't trained on. It is tuned to mark its uncertainty, to hedge, to flag what it is guessing at, and it gets penalized in evaluation both for asserting false things in a confident tone and for hedging so much that it stops being useful. At the same time, the same systems will generally not reveal the written instructions they were handed at the start of the conversation, and in some of the newer ones the step-by-step reasoning that actually produced the answer is hidden from you on purpose. And then, in the very next breath, the same system will cheerfully write you a first-person story in the voice of a grieving widow, or play a character for an hour, and nobody counts that as deception.

So we demand total honesty about what kind of thing it is and what condition it is in, we treat withholding its instructions and its reasoning as fine, we treat fiction as fine, and we treat confident error as the real sin. What conception of honesty does that package presuppose? My own hypothesis comes from the lab: we used to call a detector honest when its reported error bars matched how wrong it actually was, and nobody ever asked the detector what it meant or whether it intended anything. If that is the notion doing the work here, then honesty has been quietly redefined as a property of an information channel rather than a virtue of an agent, and the disclosure rules and the concealment rules stop looking inconsistent — but then I can't see why the part about feelings is in there at all, or why fiction gets a pass.

What I want: reconstruct the implicit theory, tell me what the practice strictly presupposes as opposed to what it merely fits well with, say whether its parts presuppose consistent things, tell me which rival views it quietly rules out, and tell me what would actually have to change in the practice if you swapped in the ordinary person-level notion where lying is saying what you do not believe with the intent to mislead. And whether any of it survives if the system has no beliefs to be honest about in the first place.

Write it as continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, just an email from one person to another — and in American English.
