I just had about a two-hour argument with my roommate about insect suffering and I think I lost it, so I want to see the thing done properly by people who've actually thought it through. Write me a dialogue.

Please just stipulate all of the background below — I don't want quibbles about the numbers, and I don't want anyone citing studies, naming philosophers, or dropping footnotes, since I can't check any of that anyway:

- There are something like 10^18 insects alive at any one moment, and the overwhelming majority die young: enormous numbers of offspring, almost none of whom make it to adulthood.
- Insects have nociceptors, they can learn to avoid things, and they'll sometimes pay a cost to get away from something bad — but nobody agrees whether any of that adds up to actually feeling anything, i.e. to having valenced (pleasant or unpleasant) experience.
- Both sides put the probability that a typical insect has valenced experience somewhere between 5% and 40%, and both are happy to reason in expected-value terms in ordinary cases.

On those numbers, the expected quantity of insect suffering is astronomical and it swamps basically everything else we might spend effort on. Call that the scale argument. My roommate granted every bit of that and then said the argument eats itself, and I couldn't answer her. Her move, roughly: if the stakes really are that big, then the expected value of *finding out* whether insects are sentient is bigger than the expected value of acting on your current guess. Fine, you say — so we fund research instead. But then the same reasoning applies one level up: the expected value of figuring out which research would actually settle the question beats the expected value of just doing the research. And so on. Every time the scale argument tells you to do something, it tells you louder to first buy information about whether that thing is worth doing, so it never licenses any action at all — only an endless regress of meta-inquiry. Which means it isn't an argument for doing anything about insects; it's an argument for reading papers about arguments for doing things about insects. She also threw in that once you let huge numbers times non-tiny probabilities drive everything, you get a domination cascade — bacteria are vastly more numerous still, and any place you draw the line looks arbitrary.

The dialogue: two people, call them Sam (defending the scale argument, roughly my side) and Nadia (the regress-and-dominance skeptic, roughly hers). Requirements:

- Six or so turns each, short turns, in normal human speech — hedging, half-finished sentences, the occasional concrete example with made-up numbers. Not two essays with names on them.
- Genuine back-and-forth: each of them has to actually answer the last thing the other said, not pivot to their prepared speech. Neither is a strawman and neither folds.
- Give Sam real resources beyond "shut up and multiply." For instance: that information isn't free-floating, because acting generates data (pilots, land-use changes, small interventions are themselves experiments), so "act" and "learn" aren't cleanly separable and the regress has to bottom out somewhere; that the regress is a general feature of any decision problem with option value and isn't special to insects; that "spend the next decade on research instead of interventions" is itself an action, a cheap one, and not obviously a reductio; and that the insect/bacteria cutoff isn't arbitrary, because for insects we have some behavioural and physiological proxy to hang a credence on, whereas for bacteria there's nothing, so the number there isn't a probability but a shrug.
- Give Nadia real replies to those, not just a restatement. For instance: that "learning by doing" quietly re-imports the intervention you were supposed to be justifying; that the insect/bacteria difference is one of degree, so it's still a line drawn on a continuum, and proxies like nociception are evidence of detection, not of valence; and, most importantly, that the upshot of the regress isn't "do nothing" but "the scale argument can't be the thing that tells you what to do" — she should say what she'd put in its place, something like acting only on considerations that are robust across the whole 5–40% range (don't cause insect deaths gratuitously where avoiding them is nearly free), which is a lot weaker than "insect welfare should dominate your portfolio."
- Let each of them concede at least one real point, and let the concession change what they say next.
- It shouldn't end in agreement or a tidy synthesis. End with each of them stating, as precisely as they can, the one thing they'd have to see in order to change their mind — and the disagreement should be sharper at the end than at the beginning, not blurrier.
