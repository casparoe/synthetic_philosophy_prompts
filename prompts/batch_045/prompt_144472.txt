Bit of a strange one, but this stopped being a hobby argument for me about six months ago. I build catastrophe models at a reinsurer — the kind where you generate a synthetic catalogue of, say, 100,000 simulated years and then quote your clients a "1-in-250-year loss." Our CFO asked me point blank whether that number is a fact about the world or just our opinion dressed up in a suit, and then a regulator asked roughly the same thing. A colleague on my team is a hardline subjective Bayesian who says there's no such thing as chance, there's only credence, and that our whole output is just our credences with extra steps. I think he's wrong but I've been losing the argument, and writing down my actual position has made me realise I'm not sure it all fits together. Also relevant: a few weeks ago I noticed something about my own models that I can't unsee — the catalogue is finite, 100,000 years, and for the model that *is* the whole history of chancy events. There's no longer run than the catalogue.

So here is roughly where I've landed. I'd like to know whether it hangs together:

1. Chance is real. There's a fact of the matter about the chance of an event that isn't just a fact about what anyone believes or should believe.
2. But chance isn't extra furniture in the world: no primitive causal powers, no propensities as extra ingredients. What makes it true that the chance is such-and-such is just how events actually turn out, in total — chances are whatever the most informative and most economical overall summary of the actual pattern of outcomes says they are.
3. Credence should defer to chance. If you learn the chance of A is x and you have no inadmissible information, your credence in A ought to be x. That's the whole reason I care about (1) at all.
4. Beyond mere coherence, the only norm on credence is: update by conditionalising. I'm suspicious of any further substantive requirement, and I don't think there's some deep extra rule about "fitting" the world.
5. Anything about the past or the present is automatically admissible; the only inadmissible information is information about the future that doesn't come to you via the chances.
6. Chance is compatible with determinism. Even if the underlying physics is deterministic, it can still be true that this coin's chance of heads is 1/2 — think of the statistical-mechanics flavour of example.
7. Two people with exactly the same evidence can rationally come to different credences about the same event, and neither is making a mistake.
8. Two case verdicts I'm attached to: the coin was flipped an hour ago and is sitting under a cup and nobody has looked, so 1/2 is the right credence — and I want to say that's still right even if determinism is true. And I want to say it's genuinely 1-in-250 for my house flooding this year, even though my house doesn't get 250 tries.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture, and I don't care who said what — please don't send me to any papers or reading lists, I want the argument itself. Go through the set and tell me which pairs actually conflict, which only look like they conflict, and for each real conflict what would have to give and how much it would cost me. Rank them by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, and don't assume I know where the problem is, because I genuinely don't. If there's one revision that cleans up the most, tell me which, and tell me what it costs.

Format, please, in this order:

- Bottom line — three sentences, no hedging.
- Real conflicts, worst first — around 400 words. Rank them, and for each say exactly which of (1)–(8) has to be modified or dropped and why the alternatives are worse.
- Not actually a conflict — around 200 words. The pairs that look bad but I'm fine on, and why.
- Cheapest single repair — around 150 words. One change, and its price.
- What I say to the CFO — around 80 words.
