Write an essay that takes a definite side in the dispute between internalist and externalist accounts of reasons for action, as that dispute was framed by Bernard Williams's distinction between internal and external reason statements.

Begin by stating both positions fairly and in your own terms. The internalist holds, roughly, that an agent A has a reason to do some action only if A could be brought to do it by a sound deliberative route from A's actual motivational set: reasons must, in some sense, be capable of gripping the agent they are reasons for. The externalist denies this, holding that at least some reason statements are true of an agent regardless of what would move them; on the externalist reading, the much-discussed example of a young man from a military family who is repelled by the martial life his relatives take for granted can correctly be said to have a reason to serve even if nothing in his current motivations could reach that conclusion by reasoning, exhortation, or imagination. Do not spend more than a short opening section on this background; the essay should be judged on what it does with the material below, which is designed to push past the standard dialectic.

Part one: the Conservatory case. Anya is a forty-year-old actuary. She has no taste for music, has sampled it on a number of occasions and remained indifferent, has no desire to acquire a taste for it, and faces no social, professional, or personal pressure that bears on the question. Her motivational set does include a standing, moderately strong desire to have what she calls a rich inner life; but she believes, wrongly, that music has nothing to contribute to that, having concluded from her earlier exposure that it is pleasant noise at best. As it happens, if she were to complete a structured six-month listening course, she would reliably come to care about music intensely and would thereafter have powerful internal reasons to keep listening, to buy recordings, and to reorganise parts of her life around concerts. She has no current reason from her motivational set to enrol in such a course, and she would find the first weeks of it tedious. Does Anya now have a reason to enrol?

Distinguish two internalist readings and use them to structure your answer. Call the first step-wise internalism: a sound deliberative route makes a reason statement true only if every step along the route is itself one the agent's motivational set at that point in the route supports taking. Call the second route-availability internalism: a reason statement is true if some sound deliberative route runs from the agent's motivational set to the action, even where the route passes through intermediate states the agent has no current reason to bring about, and even where the first step is unsupported. Step-wise internalism seems to yield that Anya has no reason to enrol; route-availability internalism seems to yield that she does. Say which is correct and why, and do not resolve the case by declaring it merely verbal without first explaining what would settle it.

Part two: the indexing problem. Internalist analyses quantify over the agent's motivational set, but over which one? Consider a variant. Twenty years from now, and as a downstream consequence of a career change she does have good current reason to make, Anya's motivational set will include a deep love of music. She currently attends loud concerts only to please a friend she values, and could preserve her future hearing cheaply by wearing ear protection, which she finds slightly uncomfortable. Under a strictly synchronic indexing, she has no reason now to protect her hearing for the sake of future musical goods, since no route from her present motivational set reaches that action through those goods; under a diachronic or lifetime indexing, she plainly does. Synchronic indexing appears to permit agents to destroy their own future reasons with impunity, and diachronic indexing appears to attribute to people reasons that have no grip on them at the time of action. Choose an indexing, defend it against the objection just sketched, and say explicitly what it implies about the truth value of reason statements uttered about someone whose motivations change abruptly.

Part three: the collapse worry and the cost budget. Route-availability internalism combined with generous diachronic indexing seems able to recover almost any externalist verdict, since for most agents some sufficiently long and elaborate route runs from what they care about now to almost any action you like. Step-wise synchronic internalism avoids this but appears to deny verdicts that ordinary normative thought treats as secure. Your essay must therefore do more than pick a label: it must specify a deliberative-cost budget, that is, a principled account of how much deliberative work, information-gathering, imaginative exercise, or experience-acquisition an agent may reasonably be required to perform before a route stops counting as available and the associated reason lapses. Say how large the budget is, what sets it, whether it varies between agents, and what it rules out. Then address the collapse question head on: given the budget you have defended, is the internalism/externalism dispute a substantive disagreement about reasons, or has the correct version of internalism become extensionally equivalent to a moderate externalism in a way that makes the original debate hollow?

Conclude by committing to one of the following, and justifying it: step-wise internalism; route-availability internalism with a specified cost budget; a hybrid that is internalist about some domain of reasons and externalist about another, with the boundary drawn and defended; or full externalism. Whichever you choose, include a section giving the single strongest objection to your own verdict and answering it, and a closing statement of what would have to be shown for your verdict to be false.

Constraints: argue from the cases and distinctions supplied here rather than from authorities; do not cite, quote, or attribute particular texts, and do not appeal to what some theorist has said in support of a premise. The essay should stand on its own reasoning. Aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,600 words, in a formal register, and end on a clear verdict rather than a survey of options.
