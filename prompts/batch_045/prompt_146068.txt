I am a retired experimental physicist — forty years in semiconductor characterisation — who has lately been sitting in on a philosophy reading group, and I have agreed to take next month's session: forty-five minutes, a flip chart, an audience much like me, science-trained but philosophically untrained and inclined to dismiss the whole thing as verbal trickery. I do not need Goodman's new riddle explained to me; I need a plan for explaining it.

Tell me what must come first so that the new riddle is not heard as Hume's old one, and how to make vivid that Goodman is granting induction and asking a further question about which of the hypotheses fitting our evidence is projectible. Then the carrying example: should it be the emeralds, or one of my own? I have drafted two — specimens counted as dulbrit if ductile when tested before our June instrument recalibration and brittle when tested after, and rock samples counted as gron if granite north of the equator and basalt south of it, the second meant to block the reply that time-indexing is doing all the work. Which should bear the weight, and what must such an example contain and conspicuously lack? Give me also the misconceptions in the order this audience will produce them — that grue is a gerrymandered disjunction and therefore not a property, that the natural-kind predicates of physics settle projectibility for us, that a simplicity prior or Occam's razor dissolves it, that this is just scepticism about the future — and say, for each, the point in the sequence at which to head it off and which one I should let stand unanswered for a while. Finally: one closing question whose answer would distinguish real understanding from polite comprehension, and an explicit statement of what — the circularity in entrenchment, the grue/bleen translation argument, Bayesian formalisations — I should name and decline to pursue.
