I'm in a graduate seminar on philosophy of religion, and we've spent the last two weeks on karma. The live dispute in the room has been about whether karma should be read as a claim about moral desert — a person's suffering is not merely caused by their earlier acts but owed to them, merited by them, the thing that it is fitting they undergo — or as a bare causal law with no moral operator inside it, on which suffering is the downstream consequence of action the way a headache is the consequence of dehydration. Call these the desert reading and the causal reading. I began attracted to the desert reading, on the grounds that the causal reading leaves karma too thin to do the religious and explanatory work it is always asked to do. But I have since built an argument that seems to take the desert reading somewhere nobody in the seminar wants to go, and I cannot locate the bad step. To block the easiest exit, please grant me one stipulation: we are considering agents who have fully reliable karmic discernment, who know of any suffering they encounter that it is merited and in what degree. So no premise can be dodged by pointing out that in real life we are never in a position to know what anyone deserves.

(1) On the desert reading, S's suffering is morally fitting: it is not merely that S's earlier act brought it about, but that this is what an act of that kind merited.
(2) If S's suffering is merited in that sense, then there is a pro tanto reason for S to undergo it, a reason that would be absent if the suffering were undeserved. (Without this, "desert" has been emptied of content and we have quietly returned to the causal reading.)
(3) Reasons of this kind are agent-neutral. What makes it a reason is the value of the state of affairs — that a desert claim gets satisfied — and not who is in a position to satisfy it; so it counts equally for anyone who can realize it or refrain from frustrating it.
(4) In the karmic case nothing restricts who may act on it. Karma is not, by hypothesis, handed down by any court or official, so there is no question here of standing, authorization, or an appointed punisher.
(5) Nor is there anything else in the case to weigh against it. I have stipulated that the suffering is known to be merited, and "she deserves it" is precisely the sort of consideration that answers the question of why I should not step in.
(6) Therefore I have a pro tanto reason not to relieve, and arguably to assist or bring about, the suffering of anyone at all, since on the desert reading all suffering is merited. The perfect karmic accountant would be required, pro tanto, to let every pain run its course.
(7) That cannot be right. It makes compassion pro tanto wrong, makes the relief of suffering something that needs an excuse rather than something there is reason to do, and turns the tradition's own ideal of the compassionate being who takes on the suffering of others into the height of immorality.

What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Name the step you reject, explain what that step was borrowing its plausibility from when it looked unobjectionable, and then price the alternatives, including the ones I cannot bring myself to accept. If forced, I would rather keep (1) and (2) than keep (6). The fix I attempted is to say that the reason in (2) is a reason only for the sufferer: desert gives S a reason to accept, and gives other agents no reason at all. A classmate called that ad hoc and I could not explain why she was wrong, so I would like to know whether it is, and what a principled version of that restriction would have to look like. I am also worried that the argument proves too much: run the same reasoning on any retributivist theory of punishment and it seems to convict retributivism of licensing private punishment by anyone, which I take to be a reductio of the argument rather than of retributivism. Is the standard retributivist escape — that desert is a necessary but not sufficient condition, and that inflicting suffering additionally requires an institution with standing — available to a karma theorist who has no institution to point to? And is there a separate problem in the move from (3) to (6), given that karma is standardly held to be inexorable: if nothing I do can alter whether the merited suffering occurs, is there any content left in my supposed reason, and can the desert reading guide action at all if it cannot?
