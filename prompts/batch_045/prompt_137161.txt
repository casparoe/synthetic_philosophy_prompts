I have just joined a lab that runs Libet-paradigm experiments on the timing of voluntary action, and my supervisor and I keep talking past each other: she wants to know at what point in the brain the decision to move happens, and after reading Dennett on the Cartesian theater and the multiple drafts model I have started to think the question is not well posed. Before I say something foolish in a lab meeting, I would like the case mapped properly.

Analyze the Libet experiments and the decades of argument they set off as a case study in the Cartesian theater versus the multiple drafts model. The setup, as I understand it: subjects watch a rotating spot, flex a wrist whenever the urge strikes, and report where the spot was when they first felt like moving; a slow negative potential begins building several hundred milliseconds before the movement, while reported urges land roughly two hundred milliseconds before it; Libet took this to threaten free will and argued that what survives is a conscious veto in the last sliver of time before the muscle fires.

I want more than a restatement. Give me the mapping: what, on the Cartesian theater picture, the experiment is even asking, and what a positive result would establish; what the multiple drafts model says is going on when a subject reports the position of the spot, and whether the time at which a subject became conscious of intending is a quantity the model can dispense with or one it has to reconstruct; where Dennett's handling of the case does genuine work and where it begins to look like stipulation; and how the later objections land, in particular the one I keep running into, that the pre-movement potential is not a decision signal at all but the accumulation of ongoing neural noise toward a threshold, so that its onset marks nothing at all.

Then tell me what the episode does to the theory. Does the long failure to settle whether consciousness of intention comes before or after the neural build-up confirm that the question was ill-posed all along, or does it show that the multiple drafts model buys its elegance by explaining away something that is perfectly real to the person acting? And if the question really is ill-posed, what should my supervisor be measuring instead?
