I have a friend who has been reading about Kelly betting and geometric rationality, and he talked me into an argument that ends somewhere I refuse to go. I would like a diagnosis of the argument rather than a reading list, so: no references needed, just tell me which step breaks and what it costs to break it.

The setup, so we are using the same numbers. Every round you face the same gamble: with probability 0.6 the amount you stake is doubled, with probability 0.4 the stake is lost. You may stake any fraction f of current wealth; whatever you do not stake is untouched, and wealth compounds. Two claims about this setup I take to be uncontroversial:

(i) Maximizing expected wealth says stake everything every round. Expected wealth then grows at the fastest rate available, 1.2 per round, while actual wealth goes to zero with probability 1.

(ii) Maximizing the expected log of the growth factor gives f = 0.2. A bettor who does that eventually overtakes and stays ahead of any other fixed-fraction bettor with probability approaching 1.

His argument:

1. Claim (i) is not a description of a risky policy that some agents happen to like. It is a reductio of expected value as a guide here. The reason expected value goes wrong is that the expectation of terminal wealth is carried almost entirely by paths whose total probability goes to zero, paths on which you win far more than 60 percent of the time. The paths that actually occur lie in a set whose probability approaches 1, on which the win frequency is 60 percent and the realized growth rate is the average of the per-round log returns.

2. So the quantity to optimize is not the expectation of wealth but the growth rate along the path you will actually live through, that is, along a typical path: one whose properties hold with probability approaching 1.

3. General principle: a choice is to be assessed by what happens on typical paths. Branches of vanishingly small probability are not to be permitted to drive the decision merely because the magnitude attached to them is enormous. Expected value is a bad guide in exactly this way: it lets such branches drive everything.

4. Nothing in step 3 depends on repetition. Repetition is only what makes the typical set easy to pick out and its probability close to 1. The underlying point concerns probability versus magnitude, and that point holds in a single trial as much as in a long sequence. In a single trial the analogue of what happens on the paths that actually occur is what happens in the more probable branch. This, he adds, is why compounding quantities generally should be optimized geometrically.

5. Hence for a one-off decision, rank options by the outcome of their more probable branch: maximize the median outcome, and treat the less probable branch as noise, however large or small it is. For two-branch cases the median just is the modal branch.

6. He embraces the consequences. A: offered once, at no cost and no risk, either a 40 percent chance at ten dollars or a 40 percent chance at ten billion dollars, you should be indifferent between them, and indifferent between each and declining, since in all three cases the more probable branch is that nothing happens. B: offered once either a 51 percent chance at one dollar or a 49 percent chance at a million dollars, you should take the dollar. C: you should decline a one-in-a-million chance at a fortune in exchange for a negligible certain cost, and by the same token decline to pay a negligible certain cost to remove a one-in-a-million chance of losing everything you own, however much that is.

7. I cannot accept 6. The indifference in A, the ranking in B, and the refusal in C all seem wrong to me, and C bothers me most: refusing cheap protection against a catastrophic long shot is precisely the kind of error the growth-rate reasoning was supposed to save us from.

8. A further worry, which is why I suspect step 5 rather than step 3, though I cannot make it precise: if maximizing the median is the right rule per decision, it does not appear to be the right rule for a life made of many decisions. With a thousand independent decisions each of which lands in its own typical branch with probability 0.999, the probability that they all do so is only about 37 percent. A rule justified by a law of large numbers that refuses to compose with itself looks like it was derived for the wrong kind of object.

9. So either step 3 is false, or the move from 3 to 5 fails, or the Kelly apparatus rests on something much narrower than I had assumed.

Here is what I want to keep, if I can: both (i) and (ii), and the view that expected utility with a reasonable utility function is the right rule for one-off decisions. Is keeping all of that coherent, or am I just trying to have it both ways?

Concretely: which step do you reject, and why did that step look harmless going in? If you reject the move from 3 to 5, what exactly is it about repetition that does the work there, that is missing in the single case: is it the law of large numbers, the fact that in a long sequence the rare branches are still sampled at their true frequency and so still contribute to the realized growth rate, the fact that the typical set has probability near 1 rather than being a single branch, or something else? If instead you reject 3, do I owe an account of why (i) is a genuine objection to expected value rather than a description of what a risk-neutral agent knowingly chooses? And either way, what is the price: what do I have to give up about the normative status of Kelly, about the critique of expected value, or about the scope of geometric rationality?
