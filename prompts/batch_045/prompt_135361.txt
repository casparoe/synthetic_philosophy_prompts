Below is the question I set and one student's answer to it. Give it a mark out of 20, and then give me the actual margin comments you'd write: go through it part by part, say which claims are right and why, redo any arithmetic you think is wrong and show me your numbers, flag anything that's confused or doesn't follow, and say exactly where you'd dock points and how many. I'd much rather have precise, checkable remarks than a general impression of whether the answer is good.

THE QUESTION (20 points total)

A variant on Sleeping Beauty I'll call "Two-Shift Beauty". On Sunday Beauty is read the protocol in full and then drugged. The experimenters toss a fair coin. Then:

- Heads: Beauty is woken on Monday and again on Wednesday.
- Tails: Beauty is woken on Monday, Tuesday and Wednesday.

Every waking is followed by an amnesia drug, so at each waking she cannot tell whether she has been woken earlier in the experiment and she cannot tell what day it is. She knows the protocol and that the coin is fair. Two positions: call a "simple halfer" someone who keeps credence in heads at 1/2 on waking and, within each branch, spreads credence evenly over that branch's wakings (so P(Monday | heads) = 1/2 and P(Wednesday | tails) = 1/3). Call a "thirder" someone who gives equal credence to every waking she cannot distinguish from her current one.

(a) [5] On waking, what should Beauty's credence be that the coin landed heads? Give the simple-halfer number and the thirder number, showing your working.

(b) [7] At every waking she is offered a bet. Version 1: she pays $x and is paid $1 immediately if the coin landed heads, nothing otherwise. Version 2: she pays $x at each waking and is paid $1 once, at the end of the experiment, if the coin landed heads. Work out the fair price x for each version. In the original Sleeping Beauty case (heads: one waking; tails: two wakings) both versions come out at 1/3 — explain why they come apart here, and say what that tells you about the "betting argument" for thirding.

(c) [4] Suppose that at a waking she is reliably told that the day is Wednesday. What is her credence in heads then, for the simple halfer and for the thirder?

(d) [4] In two or three sentences: does anything in (b) or (c) settle the halfer/thirder dispute, and if not, what is the dispute actually about?

THE STUDENT'S ANSWER

(a) Simple halfer: 1/2. Within the heads branch there are two wakings, so H-Mon and H-Wed get 1/4 each; within the tails branch there are three, so T-Mon, T-Tue and T-Wed get 1/6 each. P(heads) = 1/4 + 1/4 = 1/2. The reason is that Beauty knew on Sunday that she would be woken at least once, so nothing she learns at a waking is relevant to the coin, and the chance of heads is 1/2.

Thirder: 2/5. There are five centered possiblities she can't tell apart — H-Mon, H-Wed, T-Mon, T-Tue, T-Wed — so each gets 1/5, and P(heads) = 1/5 + 1/5 = 2/5. Frequency check: heads trials produce 2 wakings and tails trials 3, so per trial the expected number of heads-wakings is (1/2)(2) = 1 and the expected total number of wakings is (1/2)(2) + (1/2)(3) = 2.5, giving 1/2.5 = 2/5. Both views agree on all the uncentred facts: fair coin, protocol as described.

(b) Version 1: per trial she places 2.5 bets on average and is paid $1 at every heads-waking, and there is on average 1 heads-waking per trial, so 2.5x = 1 and x = 2/5. Version 2: she still places 2.5 bets per trial but collects only once, and only in heads trials, so expected collection is 1/2; 2.5x = 1/2 and x = 1/5. In the original case version 1 gives 1.5x = 0.5, x = 1/3, and version 2 gives the same 1.5x = 0.5, x = 1/3. They coincide there because in the original case a heads trial contains exactly one waking, so the expected number of heads-wakings per trial (1/2) equals the probability of heads (1/2). Here a heads trial contains two wakings, so the expected number of heads-wakings is 1, which is twice P(heads), and the two prices differ by a factor of two.

So the fair price of a dollar-on-heads at a waking is 1/5, and since a credence in heads just is the fair price of a bet that pays $1 if heads lands, 1/5 is Beauty's credence: the 1/2 is the chance and the 2/5 is the frequency, and neither of them is her credence. This means the betting argument for thirding fails in this variant, since it delivers 1/5 rather than 2/5.

(c) Simple halfer: P(heads | Wed) = (1/4)/(1/4 + 1/6) = 3/5. Thirder: H-Wed and T-Wed get 1/5 each, so P(heads | Wed) = (1/5)/(1/5 + 1/5) = 1/2.

(d) Nothing in (b) or (c) settles it, because the two sides don't disagree about any uncentred fact — both accept that the coin is fair, that the protocol is as described, and that Beauty's credence in heads on Sunday is 1/2. The halfer holds that Beauty gains no evidence relevant to the coin at a waking, since she already knew on Sunday that she would be woken. The thirder therefore has to say that waking up gives Beauty new evidence about the coin: that she is now in a waking, which is twice as likely if the coin landed tails. So the crux is whether the self-locating information that one is now awake counts as evidence about how the coin landed.
