I am assembling the closing packet for the last session of an undergraduate seminar I teach on early modern political thought. The session is on Pascal's handful of fragments about justice and force, and I would rather end on something other than a bullet list of positions. Background, in case it is useful: those fragments argue that justice without force is impotent and force without justice tyrannous, that justice is endlessly disputed whereas force is recognized at once and without dispute, and that, being unable to make what is just strong, we have made what is strong just — with the apparatus of office (the robes, the ermine, the room in which judging happens) doing the work of dressing force in justice's vocabulary. I also want the non-cynical half of the reading preserved: Pascal is not simply reporting that might makes right. Because genuine justice is unavailable to us here, established arrangements have to be treated and obeyed as just, and it is dangerous to tell people that the laws are not just, since they obey them only because they take them to be just. A friend in constitutional law pointed out to me that a pantoum is a conservative form in the literal sense — it cannot get anything said without carrying forward two lines it already said — which is exactly why I want one.

So: a pantoum of four quatrains, rhymed abab, in which lines 2 and 4 of each stanza become lines 1 and 3 of the next, and the final line is the poem's opening line repeated. The interlacing should carry the argument rather than decorate it. Each inherited line should be a maxim that changes owner as it changes context, readable as a claim about justice in one stanza and as a claim about force in the next, so that the borrowing of lines enacts the borrowing of authority; and the return of the last line to the first should be felt as the circle closing, since on this view there is no standpoint outside force and its legitimations from which force could be audited. Keep the diction clipped and antithetical in the manner of a maxim, contemporary rather than pseudo-archaic, and let the repeated lines shift meaning through their new neighbours rather than through any change in their own wording.

Two things to avoid. First, no debunking sneer: the poem should not sound like someone discovering that power is corrupt, and the final repetition should land as a settling — the closed circle is, on the reading I want, also the thing that keeps the peace. Second, no religiosity and no name-dropping: the word Pascal and the titles of his works should not appear in the poem. Give me the poem alone, with no prose preface, and finish with a single sentence on its own line beginning with the word Summary: that names the line at which force takes justice's place and says in one sentence what the closing return shows.
