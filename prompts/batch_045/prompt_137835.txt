I have recently taken over operational management of a small facility that rears black soldier fly larvae for processing into animal feed, and one of the first decisions to land on my desk is how our larvae — on the order of ten million per week — should be killed before drying. Until a few months ago I had never given insect welfare a moment's thought. I have since encountered what is usually called the scale argument: the idea that even a quite low probability that insects have valenced experiences, multiplied by the enormous numbers of insects that human activity affects, can generate an expected moral cost that rivals or exceeds that of practices we already treat as serious. Taken at face value, it seems to imply that my choice of killing method may be among the more consequential decisions I will ever make, which is not a conclusion I trust.

Before I accept or dismiss that, I want to flag three features of my situation that I suspect make the bare expected-value framing misdescribe what is actually at stake.

First, the uncertainty does not look independent across individuals. Whether my larvae matter is, as far as I can tell, one global question about insect neurobiology and the functional role of their nociceptive responses, not ten million separate coin flips. I therefore cannot lean on any law of large numbers and expect some stable fraction of the vast total to turn out to matter; the bet looks all-or-nothing. I would like to know what follows from that structure. In particular, does it tell against doing expected-value arithmetic over headcounts at all, and does it instead favor treating my situation as a problem of buying information — so that the most defensible use of resources is to help resolve the underlying question rather than to change how the animals die?

Second, I am unsure what is supposed to be multiplied by what. If the thing that matters is experience over time, then headcount is the wrong currency and the relevant magnitude is closer to the number and intensity of candidate experience-seconds, which makes a method that kills in milliseconds and a method that kills over hours differ by far more than the headcount framing suggests. It also matters that most larvae in a system like mine die of other causes before harvest, and that one might count organisms, or candidate neural systems, or something else.

Third, the headcount is not fixed by nature but by my decision. A slower or costlier method raises unit costs, which changes how many larvae we bring into existence and how much demand is met by other producers or by crop-based feeds instead.

What, if anything, are the most important implications of the scale argument for a decision like mine — for the killing method, for the scale of production, for whether to fund inquiry into the underlying question, or for whether to set the matter aside as a case of cluelessness? And what is the strongest case that it has no practical implications here at all?
