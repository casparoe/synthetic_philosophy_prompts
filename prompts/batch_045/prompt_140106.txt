I have to write a guidance note for a clinical ethics committee that is being asked to approve a consent form for a corpus callosotomy. The sticking point is how the operation's effect should be described to the person signing it: as dividing one subject of experience into two, as leaving one subject whose experience has become in some way fragmented, or as leaving the number of subjects genuinely unsettled. Before I write anything about what the evidence actually shows, I want the specification. So: what would a theory of the unity of consciousness have to deliver in order to be good enough for that decision?

Give me the desiderata list — the phenomena it must account for, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must respect, the findings it must not contradict — with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and each requirement marked either non-negotiable or a preference of one camp over another.

Some things I want covered, though add what I've missed:

- The question how many subjects there are and the question whether a subject's experience at a time is unified are different questions. Keep them apart, say what would settle each, and tell me which one the committee actually needs an answer to.
- Whether unity is synchronic or diachronic or both, whether it is transitive, and whether it admits of degrees. Give a case that would discriminate.
- The staged-section case. Suppose the commissure were sectioned in stages over several weeks under local anaesthesia, with the patient asked each day what has changed. What should a theory predict the patient can report midway through, and what should we conclude if the patient reports no change at all — that unity is not a phenomenal matter, or that first-person report is not a reliable instrument here? Either way, the theory should tell me what to ask.
- The long-project case. Much is made of inter-manual conflict, but suppose instead we had evidence, after full section, that the two streams each sustained a different ranking of the patient's long-term projects and acted on it where it could — different verdicts on where to live, whom to trust, whether to continue with a hazardous treatment. Is that one subject with conflicting aims or two sources of consent? Which requirement on your list settles it, and is that requirement one we should have been confident of in advance?
- Consistency with the cases that make the naive "one hemisphere, one subject" story untenable: people who have had a hemisphere removed, or who were born without a callosum, and who do not present as fragmentary; the temporary unilateral disconnection produced when one hemisphere is anaesthetised for cortical mapping; and the widely repeated observation that patients after full commissurotomy do not, on the whole, behave like two agents sharing a body.
- Whether a verdict must be determinate in every case, or whether the theory may return "vague" or "no fact of the matter" — and what it costs a consent form if it may.
- Whether unity must make a difference to what it is like, or whether it can be cashed out in terms of access, agency, or one experience's being a proper part of another.

Then flag any subset of the desiderata you think cannot be jointly satisfied — that is the part I most want — and name the single requirement that excludes the largest share of candidate theories. Finally, a short scorecard: three or four lines each on the co-consciousness account, the mereological or subsumptive account, the view that unity is really availability to a single workspace or attentional system, and the view that there is no substantial unity to be had.
