My friend Miriam and I got into a huge argument last night after a conference dinner and I'm still chewing on it. She grew up in a small town in Bavaria, did her PhD in Boston and now teaches here, so she's heard every version of this from both sides. It started where it always starts: in Germany pedestrians wait at a red light at three in the morning on a totally empty street, and Miriam says she still does it and doesn't feel stupid about it. I said that's just rule fetishism: the point of the light is to stop cars from hitting people, there are no cars, so the reason the rule exists has run out, and what's left is you doing it because a rule says so. And morality is about acting for the reasons that actually apply, not for the bare fact that somebody wrote something down — so, I said, German-style rule-following isn't really a moral posture at all, it's a reflex wearing a moral costume, and a culture that trains people to treat the rule's existence as the reason has trained out the one thing that would stop them if the rules turned evil. I even went there and said it's the same mechanism that produces the superior orders defense at a trial, and that the American habit of asking whether the rule actually makes sense *here* is why you get civil disobedience and whistleblowers on our side of the line.

She pushed back in a way I didn't have an answer to. She said I was confusing "the rule is my only reason" with "the rule is an extra reason on top," and that once a rule is in place and publicly known it does work nobody designed it to do: people plan around it, it becomes the thing you point at when two people disagree, it gives the person with less power somewhere to stand, and it means nobody has to re-litigate the whole question from scratch every single time. She had a name for it, the rule's "public residue" — the effect a rule has just by existing and being known, apart from whatever it was invented for. Her claim was that the light keeps its public residue even at 3am, because what makes it usable as a public standard at all is that its coverage is unconditional, and because the moment you let people audit a rule's purpose case by case you've handed everyone a self-serving exception they'll be very good at rationalising. I sort of hear that for contracts or property lines, but a traffic light at 3am with nobody around really does look like zero public residue to me. She also said the US isn't less rule-governed, just governed by unwritten rules that are harder to argue with, which felt like a dodge at the time but I've been turning it over since, and at the end she said I should figure out whether I'm objecting to rules or to codified, state-backed rules specifically. Honestly I can't tell.

So what I want to know is which premise of mine is doing all the work and whether there's a version of the argument that survives once you take it away. Is the 3am case really the fetishism case, or am I just helping myself to the claim that a rule's point is exhausted by whatever it was originally for? Is the Eichmann-style escalation a cheap shot or does it point at something real, and if it's cheap, what exactly is the difference between treating a rule's existence as a reason and treating an order's existence as a reason? And is "which culture has the better of it" even a well-posed question, or is the honest version just asking which failure mode you'd rather live with — the society that keeps bad rules because they're rules, or the one where everyone quietly renegotiates them and the renegotiation tracks who has the most confidence? Please don't give me a reading list, I want the argument itself pulled apart and I want to know which bits I'd have to give up, because I'd of thought this was obvious an hour ago and now I'm not sure I even know what I was claiming.
