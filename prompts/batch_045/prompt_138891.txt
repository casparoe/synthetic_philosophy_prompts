I'm in a small reading group that just worked its way through Arrow's theorem — the four-ish conditions (unrestricted domain, Pareto, independence of irrelevant alternatives, non-dictatorship) and the result that nothing satisfies all of them at once — and instead of moving on I've been obsessing over actual ranked-choice voting, the eliminate-the-last-place-candidate-and-redistribute kind that Maine, Alaska, New York City primaries and Australia's lower house now use for real elections. So here's what I want: a reconstruction of what this practice presupposes about what an election is *for* and about what it even means to talk about the will of the voters, written for someone who has no library access and can't look anything up.

The specific things I keep tripping over, in no particular order. (1) The reform pitch is basically that ranked choice ends spoilers — that a candidate who can't win shouldn't change who does — but that sounds like independence of irrelevant alternatives, and ranked choice notoriously violates it; whether a no-hope candidate is on the ballot can flip the winner. So is the reform case committed to something its own method breaks, or is the anti-spoiler complaint actually a weaker, more local thing about vote-splitting under plurality specifically? I'd like you to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and to say where it's just underdetermined and several theories would licence the same rules. (2) It's sold as producing majority winners, but the majority in question is *still standing after everyone else has been eliminated*, not *beats every rival one-on-one*, and those can come apart: ranked choice can dump the candidate who'd beat both finalists head to head, and in Alaska's 2022 special election the one Republican who looked like the stronger general-election candidate got knocked out ahead of the other one. What conception of collective preference does that presuppose, and is it a coherent one? (3) The ballot asks for one ranking up front and then reuses it after candidates have dropped out, which seems to presuppose that my ranking is stable and menu-independent — that my second choice in a ten-way field is the same as my second choice once it's down to three. Is that a real commitment of the practice or just a practical convenience? (4) And relatedly: two-round runoffs like France's let voters change their minds between rounds, which Arrow's framework doesn't even have room for, since it takes preference orderings as fixed inputs. Is that an evasion of the theorem or a genuinely different picture of elections as preference-forming rather than preference-aggregating?

Then, the part I care about most: if we took the pairwise-majority idea seriously instead — elect the candidate who beats everyone head to head — what would actually have to change in how elections are run and talked about, what do we do when there is no such candidate, and is there any reading on which the specific pattern of violations ranked choice has is a feature rather than a bug? I'd rather get one argument followed all the way down than a survey of options, and please don't point me at papers or reading lists — just reason it out.
