I chair the serious-incident review panel at a secure forensic psychiatric hospital. After every violent or threatening incident on a ward we decide, at the next morning's case meeting, whether to respond to the patient as a moral agent — blame, demands for an apology, an expectation that he answer for what was done — or to take what Strawson, in his 1962 paper, calls the objective attitude: treat the episode as something to be managed, adjust medication, rewrite the risk plan, go no further. We currently do this by feel, and the same incident comes out differently depending on who is in the chair.

I want a written procedure my panel can run in about fifteen minutes with a file in front of us, written for staff with no philosophy training, with the chair signing off at the end. It should be operationalized out of Strawson's own account — the reactive attitudes, the participant versus the objective attitude — rather than out of clinical intuition dressed up as theory. Three things it has to get right:

First, it must separate early and explicitly the two ways Strawson describes withdrawing from the participant attitude: the plea that this action was not really the agent's (he didn't know, he was pushed, he mistook the situation), which leaves our general attitude toward him intact, and the wholesale disqualification of the agent (he is only a child, he is in the grip of a psychosis, his mind has been systematically perverted), which invites the objective attitude. These are the cases the literature calls excuses and exemptions, and we collapse them constantly, which is why our decisions look arbitrary to the patients.

Second, it needs one step for whether the reactive response is apt and a separate step for whether expressing it is a good idea. Our team has a standing slogan — take responsibility, don't blame — and we use it to avoid saying anything that sounds like blame to someone who is ill. I suspect we have drifted into never asking whether the resentment the staff actually feel is warranted, and into presenting the objective stance as neutral when it plainly is not.

Third, it must say when the objective attitude is required, when merely permissible, and when adopting it is a failure — including the case where we drift into it because blame is uncomfortable or because managing someone is cheaper than answering to him.

Then run it on three incidents: a patient with borderline personality disorder who, after her leave was cancelled, smashed a window and cut herself and the next day said she did it to make us listen; a patient in an acute psychotic episode who struck a nurse he believed was poisoning him; and a patient with no diagnosis who threatened a weaker patient for his cigarettes. Give me the honest result in each, including the one where the procedure tells us to do something my team will resist.

Close with a note on where the procedure is weakest: which step depends on the shakiest reading of Strawson, which distinction gets flattened in translation to a checklist, and what we would have to find in our own case records for you to rewrite or drop it.
