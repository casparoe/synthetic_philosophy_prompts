I have no academic training in philosophy, but I have been thinking on my own about Dennett's reply to Searle on original versus derived intentionality. The case that keeps returning to me is Dennett's two-bitser: a coin-sorting gadget that can tell a genuine quarter from a fake and signal twenty-five cents, even though it has no understanding of what money is, so its signal seems to be about a quarter only because we users interpret it. On Searle's side, the gadget's intentionality is merely derived, while ours is original; Dennett's reply is that our own intentionality may be derived too, from evolution, culture, and the history of our own development, so the contrast may not be a difference in kind.

I host a small independent reading group, and for our last session on this topic I would like to close with a sonnet of fourteen lines. Use the octave to build the naive picture of the two-bitser sorting coins and signaling twenty-five cents, its apparent aboutness supplied entirely by us. Make the volta the moment the reader realizes the hard question is not about the gadget but about us: why think our own thoughts are not just a longer chain of derived meaning? Let the closing couplet say exactly what has to go if Dennett is right: the idea of original, self-standing intentionality. It should be accurate enough that a philosopher of mind would recognize the argument rather than merely see the terms name-dropped.

Put the sonnet first, then a section titled Commentary of about 150 words explaining how the octave, volta, and closing couplet each map onto the disagreement between Searle and Dennett.
