Hey — I'm an NP at a residential opioid-use-disorder program, and I keep getting pulled into arguments I don't have a framework for. The thing that set this off: a friend in a philosophy grad seminar described a position to me (I think the label was "tranquilism") which says that the only thing that matters for someone's welfare is the absence of craving and the absence of suffering — so getting what you want is good, if it's good at all, only because it quiets a craving you already had, and pleasure, joy, meaning, achievement on top of that add nothing on their own. When I said that out loud at lunch everyone laughed, but I've noticed it weirdly matches what our team actually ends up caring about in practice: we discount the patients who report feeling great while still chasing, and we worry most about the ones who are calm and blank. I also had a genuinely unsettling thought, which is that on this view a person with no cravings at all who just sits there is doing maximally well, and I can't tell if that's a feature of the view or a reason to throw it out.

Here's the practical setup. We run a weekly case conference, roughly 20 minutes per patient, about a dozen patients a week, and the output is a one-line recommendation (hold the dose, taper, add a medication, refer to counseling, move to discharge planning) that gets signed off later by our supervising psychiatrist, who isn't in the room. The stakes are real — dose changes and discharge dates — and the information is terrible: self-report, a couple of screeners, sometimes a family member on speakerphone, no way to measure anything precisely. Right now we mix retention and urine-screen numbers, how the patient says they feel, whether they hit their treatment-plan goals, and plain vibes, and two people read the same chart and come out opposite ways. So: turn tranquilism into a procedure my team could actually run in that meeting. I want an ordered set of questions, what counts as a good-enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with "no change" and where it has to stop and hand the decision over to the patient as a question about values rather than about welfare. It has to be usable by people who've never heard the theory. Tell me which distinctions from the view have to survive the simplification and which ones we can safely drop — my own guess is that the ones we can't drop are: craving versus ordinary desire or preference; craving someone is having right now versus craving that will hit them the moment they walk out the door; suffering versus pain or discomfort; cravings the person endorses having and maybe even enjoys versus ones they hate; and whether we're allowed to count cravings we predict for the future. I'd also like you to name whatever I'm missing, especially anything about whether improvement has to be "authentic" in some sense or whether any route to zero craving counts the same.

Please run the finished procedure on three cases so I can see whether it actually behaves. One: patient stable on methadone, reports no cravings at all, but says life is completely flat, nothing feels worth doing, no friends left, and she wants to taper because she wants to "feel something again." Two: patient with intense, frequent cravings who describes the whole cycle as the thing that makes life worth living, refuses agonist therapy, isn't suffering much by her own account, and is only here because a court sent her. Three: the case I think is the real test — someone whose cravings vanished after we started a medication that also flattened her into apathy and blunted everything else. At least one of those should come out with an answer I don't like, and I'd rather you say so plainly than smooth it over. Then be honest about what the procedure can't do: whether it can aggregate over time in any principled way (a good week followed by a crash versus steady mediocrity), whether it can rank two patients against each other when we only have one slot, and where the theory just gives out and we're back to guessing. No citations or reading list, please — I'll fill those in myself if this ever turns into anything; I want the reasoning and the procedure, not the bibliography.
