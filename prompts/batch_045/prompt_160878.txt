Before I retired I spent three decades in analytical chemistry, and one habit of mind has stayed with me: a predicate such as "heap" or "tall" strikes me as behaving the way an instrument behaves — clean, repeatable readings well away from the limit of resolution, unstable readings inside a band around that limit, and, within the band, nothing in the sample that settles the matter. What I never expected of my instruments, however, was that the width of the band would shift with what the measurement was for: narrow when I am weighing a reagent on the bench, wide when I am estimating tonnage in a silo. Call this resolution drift.

I have been working through the familiar families of response to the sorites — epistemicism, supervaluationist and subvaluational treatments, degree-theoretic or many-valued semantics, contextualism, and the view that the predicate simply has no determinate extension — and I want to know how each copes with that single phenomenon. Please score each of the five from 0 to 100 for how well it accommodates resolution drift, where a high score means that it accounts both for the confidence of clear cases and for the movable, purpose-sensitive width of the borderline band, without collapsing into the claim that the extension is just whatever the speaker takes it to be. Give one sentence of justification per score, and note for each whether it locates the drift in semantic content or merely in use. Finally: suppose resolution drift turned out on reflection to be nothing more than the ordinary context-sensitivity we already grant to "flat" and "empty" — which single score would you then revise most sharply, and in which direction?

Please answer in American English.
