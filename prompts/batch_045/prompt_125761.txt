I sit on the research committee of a small grant-making foundation. Over the next four years we can fund roughly six postdoctoral fellowships in normative philosophy, and we have decided against spreading them: the whole envelope will go to a single cluster of questions. The cluster we keep returning to is that of free-floating rationales — considerations that favour a response, or count in favour of an outcome, without being anyone's reasons in particular. Not shared or collective reasons in the ordinary sense, where a group or its members hold them together, but reasons whose every candidate bearer is missing: unowned, unentertained, or unentertainable considerations that nonetheless seem to retain whatever force reasons have.

So that we are not leaning on any settled terminology, let me fix ours provisionally. One of our advisers sketched a five-way taxonomy, which I suspect does not survive scrutiny as stated:

1. Extinct bearers. A rationale whose only bearer is dead — a promise made to someone who has since died, the point of a bequest whose settlor and beneficiaries are both long gone, the stated aim in a constitution's preamble.
2. Never-bearers. Considerations that concern individuals who cannot now exist: people who would have lived under a different policy, future generations whose identity depends on what is done now, and merely possible agents generally.
3. Bearerless offices. A rationale attaching to a role, institution or office rather than to its occupants — the purpose of an endowed chair, the point of a procedural rule, the mission of a statutory body. Occupants turn over; nobody now endorses the founding rationale, and arguably nobody ever did.
4. Unentertainable reasons. Reasons generated by the structure of a situation that no deliberator could formulate or act on — considerations available only after the last opportunity to respond has passed, or which the agent's own representational limits exclude.
5. Self-effacing rationales. Reasons for an entity to bring about a state of affairs in which that entity no longer has the standing to hold reasons at all: reasons for a court to abolish its own jurisdiction, for a constitution to authorise its replacement, for a group to dissolve.

We also have a working hypothesis that the standard menu of responses is too narrow. Faced with an orphaned rationale, an institution appears to have two options: act on it, or treat it as void. Our suspicion is that there is a third and fourth response-mode that the literature's action/belief dichotomy hides — something custodial or archival (preserve the rationale, keep it legible, without executing it) and something expressive or extinguishing (acknowledge it, mark it, or formally retire it). We want to know whether that is a genuine insight or a confusion generated by our own taxonomy.

What we need from you is a defended research agenda, not a survey. Concretely:

Which three or four questions within this cluster should six people work on for four years, in what order, and why? Please give us the reasoning rather than the list: what makes each question worth years of someone's career, which questions are upstream of which (what has to be settled before a downstream question can even be posed clearly), which are tractable by philosophers working conceptually with no empirical budget, and which we should decline to fund because their resolution would not change much else.

Then, because our trustees will ask: which of the alleged practical pay-offs are real? Our interest is institutional reasoning about inherited purposes — donor conditions on collections, endowed posts whose founding description no longer names a discipline, corporate purpose clauses, lapsed statutory mandates, treaty and constitutional preambles. We think a great deal of bad reasoning here comes from a false dichotomy between "binds because it was originally willed" and "binds not at all". Tell us which implications for that domain genuinely follow from a good account of free-floating rationales, which are overrated, and which do not follow at all. We would also like your view on one application our programme officer is keen on — specifying purposes for artificial systems that will long outlive their designers — and whether it is the same problem or merely adjacent.

Two further constraints. First, if you think our five-way taxonomy, or the hypothesis about third and fourth response-modes, is confused, say so and replace it rather than working inside it; we would rather be corrected than accommodated. Second, do not give us a reading list, a bibliography, or a guide to who has said what — we have librarians for that, and we are deliberately not footing the agenda on any author's programme. Where you need a term of art, define it in a sentence. For your top-ranked question, work through one concrete institutional case end to end so we can see what a result would look like, and state what evidence or intermediate result within the four-year window would move that question up or down the ranking.

Please write the answer in British English.
