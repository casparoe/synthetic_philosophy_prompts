I help run a small private group that plays one-shot games with real money attached to them, mostly as a way of testing decision-theoretic ideas on ourselves. Twenty of us are about to play a game of our own devising, and two members got into an argument in the group chat about what to do in it. They asked me to referee; I couldn't, and I'd be grateful for a careful adjudication.

The rules: each of the twenty players privately submits a claim, any whole number of dollars from $0 to $1,000,000. The claims are opened together. If they sum to $1,000,000 or less, every player is paid exactly what they claimed. If they sum to more than $1,000,000, nobody is paid anything. No communication, no repetition, no side payments, no enforcement; whatever is left unclaimed goes back to the donor who funded the pot. (One member said this is just the two-player demand game from bargaining theory with more players. I don't know that literature and the label doesn't matter.) What matters is that the game was deliberately built as an easier cousin of the Platonia dilemma — the scenario in which twenty people are each offered a billion dollars on the condition that they be the only one to claim it, with no communication and no sharing of the prize afterwards — and that the argument was about how much that changes.

The exchange, lightly trimmed but otherwise as it happened:

(1) Hana: Claim exactly $50,000. The superrational test is not "what is my best reply to whatever the others do" but "which policy, adopted by everyone because everyone reasons as I do, leaves me best off?" If all twenty of us claim c, the pot survives just in case 20c ≤ $1,000,000, so any c up to $50,000 is universally adoptable and the best such c is $50,000, paying everyone $50,000 with certainty. Note how much better this is than Platonia, where the best symmetric policy is a randomized one — each player claims the billion with probability 1/20 — and even then the probability that exactly one person claims it is about 0.377, so roughly 62% of the time the money simply evaporates and each player's expectation is under $19M against an equal share of $50M. Our game has no such leakage: superrationality here pays out the entire pot.

(2) Dmitri: $50,000 is a knife-edge. It pays only if the other nineteen claims sum to $950,000 or less, and pays nothing if they sum to $950,001. What I should maximize is c × Pr(others' sum ≤ $1,000,000 − c), and my uncertainty about that sum is not a smooth bell curve centred on $950,000 — it is lumpy, because people pick round numbers. Say fifteen of the others claim about $50,000, three claim $60,000 or $70,000, and one claims $20,000: that is about $965,000, and my $50,000 does not fit while $35,000 does. Whenever the others' total has its mass just above the line, claiming the fair share is worth zero in the most likely worlds and shading well below it is worth real money. Prudence, not symmetry, should set my number.

(3) Hana: Two problems. First, the lump you are insuring against is not a fixed object; it is the output of nineteen deliberations of the same kind as yours. If shading is the sensible policy, it is the sensible policy for everyone, and if everyone shades to $40,000 the total is $800,000, everyone is paid $40,000, and we are no safer than before but $10,000 poorer each. A rule that is dominated once universalized cannot be what a theory recommends whose whole point is that identical reasoners converge on the same rule. Second, the insurance premium here buys nothing: the dollars I give up by shading are not transferred to the worlds where I would otherwise bust, they revert to the donor. Going from $50,000 to $40,000 surrenders a fifth of what I collect in the good worlds, so it pays only if it raises the chance of a payout by more than a quarter. That requires the risk to sit in a narrow band just above $950,000, which is exactly the shape you asserted rather than argued for.

(4) Dmitri: The universalization test is the right test for twenty ideal reasoners who know one another to be ideal reasoners, and we are not that. Hofstadter actually ran a version of this on the readers of his column — the Luring Lottery — and by his own account it was a fiasco: readers piled in, the prize was swamped, restraint was almost entirely absent. I don't remember the rules well enough to lean on them, and if entrants could submit more than once the analogy is imperfect. But the lesson survives: a rule that tells you to assume others will land where you land will reliably cost you money in a mixed population, and superrationality says nothing about how to move when you know the population is mixed. And note that my claim and yours are causally independent — nothing I write on my slip makes anyone else claim less. If I think there is a real chance two or three people will claim $150,000, my restraint buys precisely nothing; it just lowers my own number.

(5) Hana: On causation: if that objection were sound it would also sink Platonia, whose entire point is that the causal-independence objection is the trap. Superrationality does not say my slip influences yours; it says the reasoning that produces my slip is the reasoning that produces yours, so the question to ask is which policy it is best to be running. On the mixed population: agreed that it matters, but the two games differ in a way that helps me. In Platonia, claiming is very nearly dominant — if the others will be silent you win a billion by claiming, and if they will not, you lose nothing by claiming — so a superrational player facing likely claimers really is torn. In our game over-claiming is not dominant: if the other nineteen claims land anywhere between $850,000 and $1,000,000, my claiming $250,000 destroys my own $50,000 along with everyone else's. So a few greedies do not make restraint pointless. Restraint is pointless only if there are enough greedies to bust the pot whatever I do, and in that case my claim is worthless either way and my choice is a free one. The case that matters — a couple of greedies, everyone else moderate — is exactly the case where universalized restraint still pays.

(6) Dmitri: One last thing, which I think breaks the $50,000 answer even on its own terms. The host has just told us he cannot confirm that all twenty invitees are in: there will be 19, 20 or 21 players, roughly equally likely, and we won't know which until the claims are counted. If everyone follows your rule and claims $50,000, then in the twenty-player case we land exactly on $1,000,000 and are paid, but with twenty-one players we are at $1,050,000 and every one of us gets nothing. A symmetric rule that is safe in all three cases has to use $1,000,000/21, which is $47,619 and not a round number — and if there turn out to be only nineteen of us, that rule leaves something like $95,000 sitting unclaimed that nobody can have. So even granting you the whole superrational framework, the answer is indexed to the worst case, not to fairness, and it throws away money in the good case.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each of the six moves, say whether it is clearly right, partly right, or clearly mistaken, and why. Then say who is more right overall, and what — if anything — both of them are missing. I have to submit a claim myself: if you think there is a single right number for the slip, give it, and say what it depends on.
