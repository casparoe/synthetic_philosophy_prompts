I want to state as precisely as I can what a heritability estimate does and does not license, and I would like the principle — or the small set of competing principles — that yields the verdicts I already trust, together with an honest statement of which of those verdicts it forces me to give up. All of the following cases are stipulated.

1. Village A: an adequate and nearly uniform diet; heritability of height ≈ 0.8. Ada is 12 cm taller than Bea. I am willing to say that Ada is taller largely because of her genes.
2. Village B: the same gene pool as A but chronic undernutrition, mean height 8 cm lower; within B the heritability is also ≈ 0.8. I refuse to say that the A–B gap is genetic, and I feel this refusal is not in tension with (1), though I cannot say why.
3. Population C: everyone is treated alike except for a few children who receive a supplement, and the supplement accounts for all the variance in the trait. The reported heritability is ≈ 0. I say the differences are not genetic.
4. Population D: two genotypes, one doing better under cool conditions and the other under warm ones, with genotypes and temperatures spread evenly; the genotype main effect is ≈ 0 and the interaction term is counted apart, so the published heritability is ≈ 0. I still want to say that genes matter here — arguably more than in (3), even though the headline number is the same.
5. Disorder E: children with genotype g develop it on an ordinary diet and do not on a modified one; within a shared-diet population heritability ≈ 1. I call the disorder genetic and also call it preventable, and I do not think those two claims conflict.

My tentative principle is this: a heritability estimate licenses causal claims only about differences between individuals within the population and the range of environments actually studied, and licenses nothing about differences between group means, nothing about what produces a trait in an individual as opposed to what makes individuals differ from one another, and nothing about immutability. It seems to handle (2), (3) and (5), but I cannot see how it handles (4), and in (1) it appears to give me less than I want.

Please test that principle against the five cases and repair it. Give the exact wording of each clause and the case that motivates it; state what the repaired principle implies about a case I have not listed — say, a population with the same gene pool as A but far more variable diets, workloads and illnesses, where heritability ≈ 0.4: is the claim "Caleb is tall because of his genes" weaker there than in village A, weaker by how much, or simply unsupported? And tell me plainly which of my verdicts no coherent principle can accommodate.
