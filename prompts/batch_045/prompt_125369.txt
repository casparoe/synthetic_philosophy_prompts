I'm retired — thirty-odd years as a process chemist, mostly catalysts — and a couple of years ago I drifted into a philosophy discussion group that meets in the back room of a pub near me. Half of it goes over my head, but theres one thing we keep circling and I'd like a proper answer to it rather than a reading list, which I've been handed more than enough of already.

The thing I keep noticing is how the Holocaust gets handled as a public practice rather than as a historical topic. Schools teach it as a compulsory subject, there's an annual day of remembrance, and in several European countries denying it is a criminal offence while denying other mass killings, as far as I can tell, isn't. The rhetoric around all of this is thoroughly universalist: never again, it concerns everybody, it could happen anywhere, it tells us something about ourselves and not just about the people who did it. But there's a second norm running right alongside, and it's the one I can't fit with the first. Compare it sideways — to the slave trade, to a colonial famine, to another twentieth-century mass killing — and you get accused of relativising it, of implying a moral equivalence, of taking something away from it; a woman in our group did roughly this and the room went cold. Compare it upward, to something abstract and modern — factory production, railway timetables, filing systems, bureacratic division of labour, the rational organisation of an industrial process — and nobody resents it at all, it's treated as the deepest available insight. Someone lent me a sociologist's book making exactly that last move and everyone thought it was profound. Ranking atrocities by death toll is treated as a sort of category error too, which I find telling.

So: what does this package of practices actually presuppose? I'd like the implicit theory reconstructed, not named and sympathised with. It looks to me as if at least three things are in play and might not fit together: a universal lesson being drawn from an event that's been placed outside every comparison class (but doesn't generalising require a class?); an assumption that comparing two things along some dimension means putting them on a level in every respect; and a resistance to counting bodies, which suggests the wrongness isn't meant to be aggregative at all but has to do with the means — the paperwork, and the paperwork's rationality. Tell me which of these the practice strictly has to presuppose, which it merely sits well with, and where it's underdetermined so that several quite different views would license the same practice. My own rough distinction between sideways and upward comparisons may be doing more work than it deserves — say so if it is. And what would concretely have to change in the teaching, the memorials and the law if we dropped the uniqueness bit and ran the whole thing comparatively? Under 500 words if you can manage it.
