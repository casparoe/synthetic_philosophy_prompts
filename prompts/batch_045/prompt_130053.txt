i have no philosophy background and got into this by accident, through real life rather than reading. A few years ago my mum was in hospice and I kept noticing how the staff talked about what they were doing. They could give her morphine at doses that everyone in the room knew might bring her death a bit closer, and the way they justified it was: we are aiming at the pain, not at her dying — the dying is something we can see coming and accept as a side effect. But when my brother asked, basically, why not just end it now if we all know where this is going, they said they couldn't do that, and it didn't sound like they were only talking about the law. Same nurses, same patient, same likely outcome, and the whole moral difference was supposed to sit somewhere in what they were aiming at. (What also bugged me: in the last bit they sedated her into unconsciousness until she died, and there the relief from suffering and the dying seem to be the same thing happening under two descriptions, not two separate effects.)

Since then I've poked around online about trolley problems and the doctrine of double effect, and now I have a bunch of things I can't settle.

What does a rule like the hospice one actually presuppose about what intending is, as opposed to merely foreseeing? Is that distinction load-bearing for the practice, or is it just the story the staff tell to make themselves comfortable — would the practice really have to change if we dropped it and went by the risk being imposed, or by what the patient asked for? And is it even the same distinction that's doing the work in the trolley cases, the switch case versus pushing the guy off the footbridge? Maybe what people are reacting to there is something cruder, like touching him or using his body, and the intended/foreseen thing is what philosophers bolted on afterwards.

Two things I made up on my own that I want tested. First, my disappointment test: you intend a foreseen outcome if you'd be disappointed, or feel you'd failed, if by some fluke it didn't happen. So the nurse who'd be relieved if the patient somehow didn't die isn't intending death, and the one who'd feel she hadn't managed is, whatever she says out loud. Does the practice need something like this, or does it need a thicker notion of intention that a person can't settle just by what she avows, or even by what she'd feel? Where does my test go wrong?

Second, my subcontractor case: you can't reach the switch yourself, but you can phone the signal box. The man there will flip it, and he'll do it because he personally wants the guy on the side track dead. You know this, and you don't care about the guy either way — you just want the five saved. Is phoning him OK? If double effect says the very same flip is fine for you and not fine for him, then it looks like it's a feature of what's in an agent's head rather than of the action, and I want to know what a rule like the hospice one would have to look like if it took that seriously, e.g. whether a nurse's private reasons could make an otherwise identical dose wrong.

Please actually commit to answers rather than laying out the options and leaving me to pick. Give me a definite verdict on whether the intended/foreseen distinction is doing real work in both the hospice rule and the trolley judgments, or only in one of them, and a definite verdict on whether my disappointment test is on the right track or definately broken — with your reasons. Where you're extrapolating past anything that's actually settled, say so in a sentence, and don't point me at books or papers, I just want the argument.
