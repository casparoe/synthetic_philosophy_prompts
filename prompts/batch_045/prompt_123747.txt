Malcolm's argument that dreams are not experiences — that a sleeping person cannot satisfy the criteria for undergoing anything, and that the sentence 'I dreamt that such-and-such' is not a report of something that happened to me in the night but a telling whose grammar tempts us into demanding an answer where none can be given — and Dennett's reply that there is an ordinary empirical question here, to be settled by asking what sequence of internal events would best explain the reports, have been facing each other for decades without, as far as I can tell, anyone changing sides. The natural thought is that this is a standoff about sleep physiology that the discovery of REM sleep, the fact that dream reports can also be elicited outside it, and the prospect of decoding neural activity into something report-like should have dissolved; my hypothesis is that none of that even touches the disagreement, because the two sides operate different tests for what counts as an experience at all — Malcolm something like a public-criteria test, Dennett something like a best-explanation test — so that no datum would be treated as decisive by both. Is that right, or is the impasse better described as verbal, a quarrel about the word 'experience', or as a disguised question about the determinacy of the facts rather than about their existence? Please work the diagnosis through this case: a lucid dreamer agrees in advance to make an agreed signal at a fixed clock time, makes it, and on waking places the corresponding action at a different point in the dream's remembered order, and cannot be brought to say which placement is correct. What does each side say about that case, and what, given your diagnosis, would count as progress — a distinction, a kind of evidence we do not have, a different way of putting the question — or should we expect none? I want a diagnosis with reasons rather than a survey of the literature, and I am not asking you to adjudicate who wins.
