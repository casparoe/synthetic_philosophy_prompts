I'm teaching a graduate seminar on the emotions this term, and I want the first session to settle what a theory of emotion is even for before we open any of the candidates. So write me the specification: the requirements an adequate theory of what emotions are would have to meet, each with the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and a verdict on which are non-negotiable and which are only preferences of one camp.

Fix the purpose this way. I want a theory good enough to underwrite the kind of emotion ascription institutions make: a clinician saying that a patient's fear is disproportionate to her situation, a court treating an anger as culpable or as mitigating, a grief that does or does not fit its loss, a system that labels a face afraid. The theory has to earn the right to call an emotion correct or incorrect, and to say whose emotion it is.

The cases I want handled, at a minimum: the recalcitrant emotion, where someone judges the situation harmless and is frightened anyway; emotions whose object seems to be an absence, a non-occurrence, or a bare chance rather than an event, such as relief at a cancellation, fear of a one-in-ten-thousand outcome, or the resentment that attaches to a long pattern of small slights rather than to any one of them; emotions that seem essentially spread over time, with no instant at which they are wholly present, like suspense, anticipation, or grief; emotions addressed to someone rather than merely about them, where having the emotion seems itself to make a claim, as with resentment, gratitude, or an anger that demands acknowledgement; and emotions in subjects who lack the relevant concepts, an infant's distress at separation or an animal's fear of a learned cue, if you think those belong. Two further constraints the list should confront rather than assume: that a theory be able to say what makes something one emotion rather than two, and how emotion is to be marked off from mood, from sentiment, from sheer bodily feeling, and from wanting; and that the best-known results of affective science apparently give us no distinctive autonomic or neural signature per emotion category, so any requirement that quietly implies one should be flagged as doing so.

What I most want is the sorting. Which of these demands are genuine, and which are a prejudice of the perceptualists, the judgmentalists, or the feeling theorists that the rest of us need not accept? Is some subset of them unsatisfiable together, since if so that is itself the result I care about? And which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidates? Close with no more than a paragraph each on how the judgment view, the perceptual or attitudinal view, and the feeling view come out against the list. Write the whole thing as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, because I'll be reading it aloud at the start of the session.
