I convene a small reading group of machine-learning engineers at my company: about a dozen people, seventy-five minutes, at the end of the workday, no homework and no grades, so anything that feels like an assignment loses the room. Their preparation is a graduate machine-learning course plus several years of practice; none has formal logic beyond what reading code requires, and none has encountered the literature on advanced AI agents. I want to spend one session on embedded agency — the cluster of difficulties that arise for a reasoner that is itself a physical process inside the system it reasons about, as opposed to a designer reasoning about a system from the outside. I want them to leave able to say why this is one problem and not a list of engineering annoyances. Do not explain embedded agency to me; plan how I should explain it to them.

The plan should contain the following.

First, the order in which the components must arrive, and for each, what it depends on having already been established. I have a provisional three-way division that I would like you either to use or to replace with a better one: situatedness, the fact that the agent has no view from outside and must learn its model of the world from data gathered inside it; self-inclusion, the fact that the agent is among the objects its world-model has to contain; and self-consumption, the fact that the agent's own reasoning is a physical event whose outcome changes what happens next, so the reasoning cannot be a detached mirror of a fixed target. If this is the wrong cut, say so and give me the right one, with the dependency structure drawn explicitly.

Second, the single example that will carry the whole seventy-five minutes, together with an account of what such an example must have and must lack. My candidate is a small prediction scenario: a system is asked to predict, one minute ahead, whether a lamp will be lit, where the lamp is wired so as to be lit exactly when the system predicts darkness, and the system is scored on accuracy. I am drawn to it because no deterministic predictor has a fixed point here, and because randomising over predictions does not obviously help, since the lamp's behaviour depends on the distribution the predictor emits rather than on a single sampled output. Tell me whether this is the right example to hang the session on, or whether another does the same work better. In particular I want a case in which the agent's uncertainty about its own future output is load-bearing — the environment responds to what the agent actually does — but in which nothing turns on computational limits, chaos, or an inability to simulate the physics, and in which every candidate algorithm fails, so that no one can leave blaming the difficulty on the world's being large or on a poor choice of method.

Third, the misconception map. I expect at least these: that this is simply Gödel's theorem or the halting problem under a new name; that the remedy is to give the agent uncertainty about its own source code, after which it is merely uncertain and can apply standard probabilistic reasoning; that the agent can simulate a copy of itself in a sandbox and read off the answer; that the problem vanishes provided the objective never mentions the agent; and that this is the same thesis as the one about cognition being embodied or situated in the phenomenological tradition. Add the ones I have missed. For each, state the point in the sequence at which it must be defused, and whether defusing it early would spoil material that comes later. I would rather a misconception be permitted to surface and then be dismantled at the right moment than be pre-empted in a way that makes the later material feel like repetition.

Fourth, what I should refuse to explain at this level, and say aloud that I am refusing. I assume the proof of Löb's theorem and the internals of the proposed formal fixes belong on that side of the line, but I want your view on where the line should fall for an audience that will happily follow an induction over a program's execution trace and will not follow a modal fixed-point argument.

Fifth, one question for the last five minutes whose answer tells me whether the idea landed rather than whether the session was enjoyed. My instinct is to present a case in which the agent does have a clean input-output channel and a fixed external objective and ask whether the difficulty survives, but I am not confident that a correct answer to that question distinguishes understanding from mimicry. If it does not, propose one that does.

The only resources are a whiteboard and the seventy-five minutes; no readings, and no formulas beyond what fits on the board without derivation.
