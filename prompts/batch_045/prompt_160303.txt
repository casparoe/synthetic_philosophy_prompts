I'm a junior at a public high school, and I run the math club. Over winter break a friend and I put together a small program that writes fully worked solutions to the counting-and-probability problems we practice on, and about twenty underclassmen now use it as a coach. Maya and I have been arguing about how to tell whether a solution it produces is actually any good, and the argument has started to feel like it is about something more general than our club. I would be grateful for your help with it.

Maya's position is that we should check the final answer against the answer key and leave it at that. Her reasons, as I understand them: checking outcomes is nearly free, so we can vet a hundred problems in an afternoon; checking every line would require someone at least as good at combinatorics as the program, and if we had such a person we would not need the program in the first place; and grading by outcome is the only way we can use it on problems none of us could solve ourselves, since we do not have to be able to solve a problem in order to check its answer.

My worry is that the reasoning is the product. The underclassmen are not paying for the number at the end; they are reading the steps in order to learn how to count. Last week the program solved a round-table seating problem by dividing by twelve to account for rotations and reflections -- the problem does not identify reflections -- and then made a second, unrelated counting error in its case breakdown that happened to bring the total back to the number printed in the answer key. Anyone who read that solution learned two bogus moves. Nobody noticed, because the only thing we check is the last line, and the last line was right.

Maya has a genuine reply to this, which is why we are stuck. If we reward solutions for having individually approved steps, she says, the program will drift toward steps that are unimpeachable and useless: restating the problem, naming the variables, announcing that it will now count the remaining cases. A cautious step is far easier to approve than a bold one, and she has already caught it padding. She also points out that the problems the club president writes herself come with no answer key at all, so on those my method is the only one available and hers says nothing.

Her newest proposal is that we have a second program read each line of the first one's solution and flag the first line it thinks is wrong. I said that is circular, since we would be trusting the kind of thing we are trying to check. She said checking is not the same as producing: it is much easier to recognize a bad step than to come up with a good one, so a weaker checker can still be worth running, and running it is cheap enough to do on everything. I do not have a reply to that, and I also do not trust it.

What we have to work with: an answer key for roughly two thirds of the problem set, about forty minutes a week from a graduate-student volunteer who is good at checking counting arguments but says she cannot reliably do it in under two minutes per solution, and unlimited cheap access to the second-program idea.

I would like advice on how to think about this, and something like a rule for what we should actually do. Please structure your reply in these sections:

1. "What we are actually arguing about" (~150 words). Give the general shape of the problem in plain terms. If there is a standard name for the distinction Maya and I are drawing, tell me what it is, but please do not assume I know any of the literature around it.
2. "Where Maya is right" (~120 words).
3. "Where I am right" (~120 words).
4. "What neither of us has seen" (~150 words). Please include here whether the second-program scheme really is circular or not, and what exactly it would and would not catch.
5. "What to do on Monday" (~150 words). A concrete procedure that fits the three resources I listed, and an honest statement of what that procedure gives up.
6. "How confident you are" (~100 words). Where your advice rests on something firm, and where you are guessing or extrapolating.

One last thing: if the way I have framed this is confused -- if the trade-off I think I am facing is not the one I am actually facing, or if there is no principled answer here and nothing but judgment calls -- please say so plainly instead of being polite about it. I would much rather be corrected than agreed with.
