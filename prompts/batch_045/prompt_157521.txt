ok so a guy in my department has this argument that species taxa aren't real -- that "species" is a label we slap on for convenience, more like "constellation" or "weed" than like "tiger" or "gold". I think the conclusion is nuts, but every step looked fine when he said it. Here's me reconstructing it as carefully as I can:

(1) A species taxon is real only if species membership is fully determinate: for any two organisms there's a fact of the matter whether they're conspecific, and it isn't just a function of what question we happen to be asking.

(2) Working biologist's actually use several different species concepts, and all of them do real work: the interbreeding / reproductive-isolation one, the ecological-niche one, the phylogenetic "smallest diagnosable branch on the tree" one, the morphological-and-genetic-cluster one.

(3) These aren't merely different tests for one underlying thing. On real organisms they cut across each other. Two populations that interbreed freely in the contact zone can still be separate branches genealogically; asexual rotifers and bacteria swapping genes sideways aren't covered by the interbreeding concept at all; ring species come out as one species or two depending on where you close the loop; two lineages can sit in the same niche without exchanging genes. So we get genuinely rival partitions of the same animals and plants.

(4) No one of these partitions is more "natural" than the others. Each tracks a real causal process -- gene flow, selection, ancestry -- and each is the right tool for some legitimate research question.

(5) If several incompatible partitions of the same organisms are all equally natural, then there's no fact of the matter about which organisms belong to which species taxon.

(6) So, given (1), species taxa aren't real.

Here's why I think it proves too much. Run the same argument on genes or on organisms. There are several working concepts of a gene -- the Mendelian one, the molecular-sequence one, the developmental one -- and they carve up the genome differently, and each is the right thing to use for some question. What counts as *one* organism is contested too: a lichen, a siphonophore colony, an animal plus the microbiome it can't digest without. If (1)-(6) were sound, genes and organisms would be conventions as well, which is silly. And people do real science with species *counts* -- extinction rates, biodiversity comparisons, endangered-species law. It can't all be bookkeeping.

I want to keep (2) and (3), because the messiness of actual practice seems undeniable, and I'd really like to keep species taxa real while I'm at it. So I guess I have to break the chain at (1) or at (5), and I can't tell which -- or whether both are quietly doing something sneaky. Somebody also told me that the species-are-individuals-not-classes move dissolves this whole thing, but I don't see how it helps.

Which step is broken, and why did it look fine? And what does the repair cost me: do I end up committed to lots of real cross-cutting species taxa, or to exactly one concept telling me what species really are with the rest demoted to mere tools, or to something I haven't thought of? Price the options for me.
