I'm assembling cases for a seminar on whether a theory of rationality can be indirectly self-defeating — whether it can require an agent to be, by its own lights, irrational. Parfit's hitchhiker is the standard illustration: a benefit up front, a cost to be paid later, no way to bind yourself, and a counterparty who is only a fallible reader of character. Every version I have tried lets the agent escape by an easy route, so I want a new case built to order under constraints that close those routes.

Take the theory under discussion to be a self-interested theory of rationality, S, on which an act is rational exactly if it maximises the agent's own well-being. Construct an ordinary-life case — one paragraph, nothing science-fictional, no contracts or courts, no repeat-play reputation, no drugs or neurotechnology, no near-perfect predictor — in which each of the following holds, and in which none of them holds merely because you stipulated it:

1. At the later node, performing costs the agent more than any further benefit she could get from it, and S's verdict there is unambiguous: performing is irrational.
2. Ex ante, rationality on S's own terms recommends becoming the sort of person who performs, so S recommends acquiring a state that will later move the agent to act against the balance of reasons as S then measures them.
3. The acquired state contains no false belief and no self-deception: at the later node the agent sizes the situation up correctly, judges that she has most reason not to perform, and performs anyway. What she acquires is therefore a non-reason-responsive disposition — akrasia engineered in advance — and not a mistake about how the world is; and it must not be the kind of state she can dismantle by a further rational decision at that node.
4. The counterparty can tell, by ordinary human judgment and without being misled about what the agent is really like, that she has this disposition, and can rely on it. Nothing in the case turns on the counterparty being fooled.

Then show that the case does what I asked. Say what the sophisticated chooser does at each node and why, state what the ex ante choice is and why it is the rational one by S's own standard, and then say what a resolute-choice or planning view — the view on which rationality consists in holding to a plan adopted earlier rather than in re-deciding at each node — says about the same case, and whether it gets the agent to perform without any irrationality at all. I also want the honest audit: which single feature of the case is carrying the weight, what the case degenerates into if that feature is removed, and how someone who holds that the hitchhiker needs no irrationality would attack it — the place where the attack actually bites, not a straw version of it.

Response in five titled sections, in this order: The case (~150 words); Why the easy routes are closed (~100 words); What each view says (~250 words); What is doing the work (~120 words); The strongest objection (~120 words).
