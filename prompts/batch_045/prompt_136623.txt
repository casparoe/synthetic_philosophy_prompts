I work in the policy unit of a transport regulator. We are drafting guidance on civil liability for injuries caused by vehicles operated at a high level of automation, and one submission we received argues that the entire debate about responsibility gaps is a pseudo-problem. I want that claim tested rather than asserted, so I would like an audit of the analogy it rests on.

Our worked example is this. An operator runs a fleet of roughly two hundred autonomous delivery vans in a defined operational design domain. The driving policy was pretrained by a large vendor, then fine-tuned on the operator's own fleet data by a third-party contractor under a service agreement that bars the operator from inspecting or altering the weights. After deployment the policy is updated nightly by a pipeline that distills the interventions of remote safety-drivers. Remote supervisors each monitor twenty to thirty vehicles at once and can issue only coarse commands: hold, pull over, resume. The operator's routes are shaped by client contracts that penalise missed delivery windows. On the incident in question, a van mounted a kerb and seriously injured a pedestrian. The forensic picture is that no component malfunctioned; the behaviour emerged from the interaction of the vendor's pretraining, the contractor's fine-tuning, the post-deployment update pipeline, and the scheduling pressure; nothing anybody did fell below any existing standard of care; the specific failure was not foreseeable by any of the parties; and a competent human driver in the same situation would very likely not have avoided it either.

The submission's argument, in substance: this is structurally identical to what employment law has handled for a century. A hospital is answerable when a resident makes a judgment call it could not have foreseen, and the law's willingness to attach liability there has never depended on locating a fault in the ordinary moral sense. An autonomous fleet is simply a subordinate made of silicon; the interesting questions are only about which defendant to point at, and no new conceptual or institutional machinery is needed.

What I want from you:

First, the mapping, set out explicitly as a correspondence table. Principal, subordinate, the contract of employment, the scope or course of employment, training and instruction, supervision, the employee's own actionable fault, vicarious as opposed to primary liability, the employer's insurance and its incentive to take care, professional licensing and discipline: for each, say what plays that role in the fleet case, and mark every element for which the target side has nothing at all. Those blanks are the part I most want.

Second, which of the disanalogies are load-bearing for the conclusion and which are idle. Please address at least: that in employment the employee is a second, directly liable wrongdoer whose fault the employer's liability derives from, whereas here there may be no primary wrongdoer for liability to be derivative of; that liability deters by acting on an agent who can anticipate sanctions and has interests of its own, which a trained policy cannot do; that the fault apparatus in the source domain is gradable and rests on the availability of the agent's reasons, reconstructible in a way a policy's determinants are not; that delegation to an employee is revocable and refusable, while the operator can only issue coarse commands to a system that cannot police its own domain boundaries; that the causal contribution is dispersed across a supply chain and across post-deployment learning the operator is contractually barred from inspecting, so there may be no single principal who chose and controls the agent; and, centrally, whether the submission's verdict that there is no gap is a claim about allocating liability being used to settle a claim about attributing responsibility.

Third, whether the conclusion survives. Does it hold as stated, or only in the weakened form that losses can be allocated to someone without anyone being at fault? If it survives only on the condition that we move from derivative, fault-anchored liability to something like primary enterprise or strict liability, say so plainly, since that is precisely the change the submission said was unnecessary. If the analogy was carrying the conclusion, identify the point at which it does the work.

Finally, if there is a better source domain for the same job, propose it and give the improved mapping: the firm itself as an artificial person whose intentions are attributed rather than found, products liability and design defect, the keeper of a dangerous animal, or something else. Say what it licenses that the employment framing does not, and what it costs.

I am not asking for legal citations or a survey of the literature; I need the structure of the comparison and what it can bear.
