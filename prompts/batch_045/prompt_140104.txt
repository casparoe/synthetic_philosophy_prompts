Hi — I need a teaching plan here, not the explanation itself.

Context: I'm a philosophy professor (political philosophy mostly) and a friend who runs a youth citizens' assembly has talked me into doing 45 minutes on a Saturday morning with about twenty-five 16-to-18-year-olds who are then going to spend the rest of the weekend drawing new state-legislative maps with some redistricting-software volunteers. She wants the boundary problem in their heads before they start moving lines around, on the grounds that otherwise they'll treat redistricting as pure geometry. My worry is the opposite failure: they'll hear the word boundary and immediately think district lines, gerrymandering, packing and cracking — when the question I care about is who the people are in the first place. So: give me the plan for the explanation. What has to come first and what depends on what — do I have to get them to feel the circularity (the people can't decide who the people are without already being constituted) before any principle like all-affected-interests means anything to them, or does that paradox land as a cheap gotcha at this age? And what's the one carrying case?

The case I keep coming back to is one I made up, and I want you to tell me whether it works or what it needs: Cedar Flats is an upstream town of about 500 year-round residents plus a few hundred seasonal cabin owners who pay property tax there; below it sits a town of 12,000 whose drinking water runs off the wetland the upstream town is voting on draining for a housing development; and the state, which owns part of the land and would send someone to enforce whatever gets decided, has six million people. Somebody says it's the 500, somebody says the 12,000, somebody says all six million pay for the state agency, somebody says the herons. I like it because affectedness, coercion and contribution come apart, but I can't tell whether it's too crowded, or whether it quietly rigs the answer toward everyone affected. Tell me what the case has to have and what it has to lack — my instinct is that it must not turn on citizens vs. non-citizens, because that instantly converts into an immigration argument and we lose the conceptual point, but you tell me.

Two more things. First, I've been toying in my own notes with splitting the one question into three — who belongs, who gets a hearing, who actually casts the vote — and I can't decide if naming that distinction helps them or is one more piece of machinery for 45 minutes. Second, the misconceptions I'm braced for, in no order: the district-lines conflation; "just count everyone affected" (which sounds obviously right until it goes global, and then they'll think the upshot is world government and that that's fine); "there's a legal answer, just look it up" (legal authority vs. legitimation); residence as an invisible default; and confusing all this with whether 16-year-olds should vote, which is the franchise question and not the membership question. I want you to place each one at a specific point in the sequence rather than listing them, and to add the ones I've missed — especially anything a group of teenagers who've just been handed mapping software will generate that I won't see coming.

Give me, in this order and roughly these lengths: Sequence (~250 words, numbered, with the dependency made explicit); The carrying case (~150 words — verdict on Cedar Flats, what it must have and lack, or a replacement); Misconception map (~300 words, each one pinned to where in the sequence it gets defused); The check (~80 words, one question for the last five minutes, plus what a confident-but-wrong answer sounds like); What I'll skip and say so (~80 words, what I should openly refuse to settle at this level — I assume I shouldn't try to adjudicate between the all-affected and the coercion principles, or touch secession, or open the does-democracy-have-any-value can of worms, but tell me if I'm wrong); Where I'd change my mind (~120 words, what in your plan is most likely to break in the actual room, which argument or consideration would make you reorder it, and what I'd see in the first five minutes that should make me switch to a different opening).
