I spent most of my career in condensed matter theory, and since retiring I have been auditing philosophy seminars. For a paper on indirect normativity I reached for a piece of my old toolkit, and I have ended up with an argument whose conclusion I do not believe and whose fault I cannot locate. I would be grateful for a diagnosis.

The target is the family of accounts on which what an agent objectively ought to do is fixed by what an idealized version of that agent — fully informed, fully rational — would want her to do, or would advise her to do, in her actual circumstances. I take this family to be the natural home of indirect normativity: the criterion of rightness is one thing, the procedure by which a creature like me approximates it is another, and there is no guarantee that the best procedure aims directly at the criterion.

Against it I ran the following.

(1) On the idealized-deliberation account, A ought to φ if and only if the fully informed and fully rational A* would want A to φ, in A's actual circumstances.
(2) Full rationality includes instrumental rationality: if A* knows of an available procedure that is more likely to arrive at the true answer than any alternative, and knows this, then A* adopts it.
(3) For a finite agent, deliberation is a search over a space of candidate actions, guided by how good each candidate looks and governed by a stopping rule; deciding what to do is, in the first instance, deciding which search to run.
(4) A fact about search: any procedure that accepts a candidate only when it looks better than the one currently held comes to rest at a local optimum rather than the global one, whenever the landscape is rugged. The procedures that do best on rugged landscapes are ones that, especially early in the search, sometimes accept a candidate that looks worse — with an acceptance probability that falls off both with how much worse the candidate looks and with how much of the search is left to run. This is the idea behind simulated annealing, which I used for years.
(5) Human deliberation is finite, and the landscape of candidate actions is rugged. Options that are locally attractive but globally poor are common, and the route from one good option to a better one often runs through an intervening option that is worse than both.
(6) So the procedure most likely to arrive at the true answer is one that includes sanctioned departures from local improvement, and by (2) A* must adopt it: an agent who would never take a downhill step is knowingly choosing a procedure that stalls.
(7) But a procedure that sometimes delivers the worse option is a procedure that sometimes delivers a less than fully rational verdict; so A* must sometimes be less than fully rational.
(8) A* is, by hypothesis, fully rational. So A* is impossible: no agent satisfies the condition in (1).
(9) Nothing satisfies that condition, so nothing is what anyone objectively ought to do.
(10) And the argument does not trade on any special feature of the ideal-advisor version. Substitute full-information desires, idealized endorsement, or any account that reads an ought off the deliverances of a hypothetical idealized deliberation, and steps (2) through (8) appear to go through unchanged. So the whole family collapses into normative nihilism.

I do not accept (9), and I do not believe the family collapses, since views in it are held by people who are much better at this than I am. I am also reluctant to surrender (4), which is close to a theorem, or (5), which strikes me as well confirmed by the cases.

So my question is: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it commit me to? I keep circling two places without being able to settle between them, and I suspect both may be wrong. The first is the idealization itself — perhaps full rationality was always meant to include unbounded computational capacity, in which case (3) and (4) describe a creature the account was never about, though then I do not see what grip the account has on agents like us. The second is the move from (6) to (7), where I may have conflated a procedure's being the rational one to adopt with its every output being a rational verdict.

If the fault is in the second, I would like to know what that distinction is worth. Can an account that needs it still deliver a determinate ought for a particular agent on a particular occasion, or does the annealing point show that indirect normativity must be more indirect than its defenders ordinarily claim — the idealization fixing the criterion while the procedure that tracks it is, at the level of individual steps, partly irrational? And if so, is there anything left of the thought that the criterion is independent of the procedure?
