You are a formal epistemologist who has also spent years designing and running classroom games — betting markets, signaling games, Dutch-book exercises — for philosophy students who are comfortable with Bayes' theorem but impatient with notation. Before you write anything, think carefully and show me the thinking: brainstorm three or four candidate skeletons (a trading round with private signals, a bookmaking round, an iterated public-announcement-and-revision round, an ex-ante choice made behind a veil of ignorance about which informational role you will occupy), pick the one in which the common prior assumption is something the students *violate* rather than something they are told about, and then build only that one out in full. Tell me in two or three sentences which skeleton you chose and why you rejected the others.

The setting: I teach an upper-level seminar on formal epistemology and rational choice — 16 students, one 80-minute session, late in the term, in a room with movable chairs, a whiteboard, a projector and index cards. They have done conditionalization and are at ease with updating on private evidence; they have deliberately been given nothing on Aumann-style agreement results or on common priors, and I want to keep it that way until the debrief. No real money is permitted and I would rather avoid stakes altogether: a leaderboard and one coffee voucher is the most I can offer, so the scoring has to generate the pressure on its own. What I want them to walk out having *felt* is this: that the common prior assumption is not the empirical (and obviously false) claim that reasonable people start out with the same opinions, but a counterfactual claim that all residual differences in belief trace to differences in information, so that it is equivalent to the injunction that you would have held the other person's posterior had you been handed their evidence rather than yours; that this assumption is what does the work in the results saying that agents with common knowledge of each other's posteriors cannot rationally persist in disagreement and cannot rationally trade; and that when it fails, betting becomes not merely permissible but in a sense required — which is why the assumption matters so much in the epistemology of deep disagreement.

Four devices are non-negotiable, because they are the reason I am commissioning this rather than adapting something off the shelf. First, a blind prior escrow: before anyone learns what informational role they will have, each student writes down and seals a personal prior for the hidden state; one sealed number is then drawn at random and imposed as the house prior for the trading and settling rounds, with the rest kept for later comparison. Second, a belief-plan or signal-swap requirement: before the state is revealed, every student must fill in a complete table saying what they would believe if they had received each of the possible signals instead of the one they got, so that the facilitator and, at debrief, the class can check whether everyone's tables are conditionalizations of one single distribution. Third, a bookmaker or mediator role: one or two students are withdrawn from the market, licensed to post odds and take bets from everybody else, and scored in a way that rewards exploiting inconsistency between the numbers other students post, so that failure of the assumption shows up as arbitrage rather than as a theorem. Fourth, a sectarian round late in the session: half the room is privately given a story that legitimately moves their prior — the urn they are all drawing from was itself drawn from one of two factories with different base rates, say — after which bets are visibly accepted and both sides can be fully rational in accepting them, setting up the question whether ordinary deep moral, religious or political disagreement looks more like the factory case or more like the signal case.

Give me rules, materials, a payoff scheme precise enough to run without arithmetic arguments, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps particular moments in the session onto particular parts of the idea — including the moment at which a student who traded realizes what they thereby conceded about their own prior. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: students treating the whole thing as a puzzle about the urn rather than about each other, one confident student's numbers anchoring the room, a timid bookmaker, the veil-of-ignorance round collapsing because someone works out which role they have been assigned, and the standing confusion between being given the same prior and having the same information. A colleague in the economics department says that any game with a shared prior built into the setup is cheating, since the instructor stipulates the prior and of course the students then share it; address her objection explicitly in the design. Finally, be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce and what I will simply have to say myself: the full iterated hierarchy of common knowledge, since I can only run a few rounds of public announcement; the limit structure of the agreement result; and above all the fact that the assumption is a claim about a counterfactual situation, which no amount of role-play can put a student into, however well the game substitutes an actual ex-ante choice for it.
