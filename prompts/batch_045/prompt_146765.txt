For the past eighteen months I've been reporting a long magazine piece on how institutions put a number on suffering — the shadow prices regulators attach to a statistical injury, QALY-style league tables in health systems, the compensation formulas assembled after industrial disasters — and somewhere in the middle of that reporting I drifted from a shrug to a settled position on the torture-versus-dust-specks question. I now accept the aggregationist answer: for any aversive experience, however trivial, and any severe one, there is some finite number of instances of the trivial one that is collectively worse than a single instance of the severe one; the recoil people feel at the usual formulation is an artifact of our inability to represent the enormous quantities the case is standardly stated with; and once you grant a finite exchange rate between a second of mild irritation and a second of agony, everything else is bookkeeping and the only live question is the empirical one of what the rate is. I hold this in the interpersonal form (a vast number of people each getting one speck) and not merely the intrapersonal form (one person getting all of them), and I take the two to stand or fall together, on the grounds that the boundaries between persons are not doing any independent moral work here. I'd like to state this in print, which is why I want the case against it mapped before I do.

What would count against it, and how much? I'm not asking for a literature survey or for names and papers — I've read enough of the background, and I want the structure of my exposure. Some candidates I've collected, which you should feel free to discard, repair, or supplement with better ones: the lexical-threshold response (that some harms belong to a category that no quantity of a lesser category can reach, and that thresholds need not have a principled location to be real); the fanaticism link (that if tiny harms aggregate without limit, then tiny probabilities of catastrophic outcomes must dominate every ordinary consideration, and that whatever move blocks that will also block me); an argument a philosopher friend made over dinner that I couldn't evaluate — that a minute of torture is itself a dense bundle of micro-aversive moments, so if severity is only ever a matter of how many, no threshold can be stable, and my view and the threshold view may not be genuinely different views; and a wrinkle from my own reporting, namely that the dust-specks comparison is a comparison between total states of the world, whereas every decision I actually care about is a comparison between differences that one agent's choice makes, and the torture verdict may not transfer between the two framings. I'd also add a feasible-magnitude variant I put to my newsletter readers: roughly eight billion people each getting one speck per day for a century, on the order of 10^14 specks, versus one person tortured for a year — a number the mind can almost hold. They split, and I genuinely don't know whether that split is evidence, or noise, or something my view has already ruled out in advance by labelling the intuition an artifact. That last point worries me most: I want to know whether I have insulated the position rather than argued for it, and whether insulation of that kind is legitimate for a view like this one or is itself the refutation.

For what it's worth, I am least moved by intuitions about stylised cases, including my own, and most moved by structural arguments: a result showing that my view collapses into one I independently reject, or that it cannot state a distinction I need in my reporting — severity as a difference in kind, say, or the concentration of harm in a single life.
