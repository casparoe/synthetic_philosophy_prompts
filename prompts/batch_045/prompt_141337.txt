I'm writing a novel that follows four narrators from the same family, each getting a section set roughly twenty years apart, and I keep leaning on generational shorthand in the drafting — the Silent Generation grandmother who won't throw anything away, the Boomer uncle, the Millennial sister, the Gen Z kid. My writing group is split: one of them says the whole framework is reification of a journalistic convenience, and I've found myself defending it, semi-seriously. The version I actually hold is that generational cohorts are real kinds, though not natural kinds — more like the sort of thing whose reality consists in doing genuine causal and explanatory work. Roughly: a cohort is a set of people whose formative years exposed them to the same small bundle of historically dated conditions (a war, a crash, a technology arriving when they were a particular age), and that shared exposure leaves a trace stable enough that the label predicts real stuff — how someone argues, what counts as an insult to them, how they raise kids, what they're nostalgic for — better than raw age or income or class does. I also think the labels feed back: once people sort themselves under one, they start behaving more like the stereotype, which makes the kind real in a second, loopier way.

So: what would count against that? I don't want the standard "it's all a continuum, where do you draw the line" complaint, I can handle that one. I want defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to separate what a defender of my view would concede counts against it from what that defender would just wave off. Some things I'm chewing on and want folded in: the granularity worry — if cohorts are real, why is the natural width about eighteen years rather than five or forty; the portability worry — does the same kind survive being exported to countries whose formative conditions were totally different in the same years; what I've started calling backward cohort adoption, where people born well after a cohort's window (raised on older siblings' media, say) end up being described by, and describing themselves with, the earlier label; and the inverted cusper, someone born dead center of a window whose formative conditions were the exact mirror image of the ones supposed to define it. I'd also like you to tell me what my view predicts that the "it's just a heuristic" rival doesn't, and whether the view has quietly been qualified into unfalsifiability — if every failed prediction gets absorbed as "well, cohorts were never meant to be that precise," is that a problem for a claim of this particular kind? For what it's worth, the thing I'd personally find most persuasive either way is whether the labels do measurable work on people who acquired them rather than being born into them.
