I run a one-person business and I need a specialist job done — the kind of work only a handful of people can do, and the one I want is in another country, works through a small professional community, and charges about two months of my income. There is no realistic way for either of us to sue the other. He has agreed to do the work first and be paid on delivery, which means that on the day the finished job lands in my inbox I will already have everything I wanted, and he will have nothing but my word. I have tried to think about this calmly and I don't like where it goes: at the moment of paying, paying costs me the fee and not paying costs me, as far as I can tell, a bad reference in a community where maybe a third of my future clients would ever hear about it. I don't want that to be the whole story — I would rather be the sort of person who is trusted in dealings like this, and I think I mind that for reasons that aren't only about the money — but I also don't want to kid myself that wanting it makes it true.

As I see it I have four options, and I'd like you to take them seriously rather than talk me out of the framing. First, simply pay when the time comes, on the ground that I already am the sort of person who pays. Second, deliberately set about making myself that sort of person: tell several people whose good opinion I value exactly what arrangement I've made, or spend time imagining in detail what it would be like to be the man who took the work and vanished, or otherwise manage my own future motives — while knowing perfectly well that I'm doing it in order to get myself to do something that, when the moment arrives, I will have no self-interested reason to do. Third, remove the choice rather than change the person: transfer part of the fee now, park the rest with a mutual acquaintance, or set up an automatic payment on delivery that I would have to take an active step to cancel. Fourth, hand the money now to a friend who is pathologically scrupulous about debts, with instructions to release it when the work arrives, and let her conscience do the work that mine apparently won't.

My brother-in-law, who took two philosophy classes and has been insufferable ever since, says this is just some famous thought experiment and the answer is obvious, but he refuses to say which one or why. So: what should I actually do, and what is the argument? I'd like the question of what it is rational for me to do at the moment of payment kept sharply separate from the question of what it is rational for me to do now, and I'd like to know whether those two answers can be made to fit together or whether the point is precisely that they don't. Three things bother me in particular. Option two seems to work only if I somehow forget that I engineered it, and I obviously haven't forgotten — so is there something incoherent about installing a disposition while knowing you installed it for a reason, and does the same worry apply to believing something because it would be useful to believe it? Options three and four look like they might just relocate the same difficulty one step sideways — in option four, for instance, I would then have an interest in talking my scrupulous friend out of paying, and she would have an interest in keeping the money if the job fell through — and I want to know whether any of these devices genuinely closes the gap or merely moves it. And then there is what he thinks: he is nobody's fool, he may well know that people in my position reason exactly this way, and he will see it if I start setting up escrow — so what should a shrewd counterparty infer from each of my four options, and should the answer change what I choose? If it helps, tell me what the canonical version of this setup is and what maps onto what in my case, and whether the fact that a theory of rationality might advise me to make myself irrational counts for or against that theory.
