EXAM QUESTION. In A Treatise on Probability Keynes separates the probability of an argument from its weight. Roughly: the probability is the degree of rational belief the premises warrant in the conclusion, which turns on the balance of favourable over unfavourable evidence; the weight turns on how much relevant evidence the argument draws on, so that new evidence can increase the weight while leaving the probability the same (or even lowering it). Modern decision theory offers at least three framings for "not knowing the probabilities": (i) Knight's unmeasurable uncertainty, where no usable probability distribution over outcomes is available at all; (ii) imprecise or interval-valued probabilities, where you work with a set of probability functions and get lower and upper bounds instead of a single number; (iii) ordinary subjective Bayesianism with one precise prior, where uncertainty about the probabilities is handled by robustness checks across priors. (a) Explain why Keynes's own treatment is not straightforwardly any of these three, then say which is closest to what he was doing and why. (b) Ramsey's essay on truth and probability is usually read as having killed the logical-relation view. Does that critique tell against the weight notion specifically, or only against the claim that there are objective logical probability relations? Answer in short prose; cite no sources.

STUDENT ANSWER. Keynes's Treatise defends the logical interpretation: probability is a relation between a body of evidence and a conclusion, the degree of rational belief the premises warrant — not a frequency, and not a state of mind. Crucially, probabilities for Keynes are not always numerically measurable; some are only ordinally comparable and some are not comparable at all, as in his umbrella case, where it is neither more likely than not, nor less likely than not, nor exactly even, and he thinks it is then fine to let caprice decide. This is why probability theory is not for him a branch of statistics, and why he spends so long on the principle of indifference, which he criticises for manufacturing numbers where there aren't any, and why he allows direct judgement to rank probabilities in cases where no number is forthcoming.

The weight of an argument is the other half of the picture. Weight is best understood as the agent's confidence in her own probability judgement: a kind of second-order probability that the first-order judgement is right, which rises as the body of evidence behind it grows. That is why new evidence always raises the weight even when it leaves the probability unchanged — you have become more sure of what you were already claiming. And the practical upshot follows directly: since a high weight means the judgement is more likely to be true, an agent choosing between two courses of action should prefer the one supported by the argument of greater weight even when its probability is somewhat lower. This is also, I think, what Knight meant by uncertainty: for Knight the problem is that the evidence is too thin to pin down a distribution, which is just Keynes's point that the weight is low. Both are saying the same thing, namely that you don't have enough information, and "Knightian uncertainty" is basically the 1921 name for low Keynesian weight.

Ramsey is best read as a friendly amendment rather than a refutation. Since the warranted degree of belief varies with what the agent knows, Keynes was already a subjectivist in substance, with the objective logical relation doing no real work; Ramsey's complaint was really that Keynes hadn't followed through and gone the whole way to subjective degrees of belief constrained only by coherence. So the parts of Ramsey people quote against Keynes — measuring belief by betting behaviour, the Dutch-book style argument for coherence — are an extension of the Treatise rather than an attack on it.

Of the three framings, (ii) is closest. Keynes's refusal to attach a number just means the probability is interval-valued, i.e. a set of probability functions with lower and upper bounds, which is more or less what the modern imprecise-probability literature calls a credal set; that literature is largely a rediscovery of the Treatise with tidier notation. (i) is too strong, since Keynes never denies that probability relations exist, and (iii) is too simple, since it insists on a single number. The connection to the later economics is that once you see the weight is low in the cases that matter, long-run expectation has to rest on convention rather than calculation, which is what he argues in the General Theory.
