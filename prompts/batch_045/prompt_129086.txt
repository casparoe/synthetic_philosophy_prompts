I am a novelist, and I have written myself into a corner. The book is set in a provincial museum: a conservator, mid-career, discovers during routine imaging that the museum's most loved painting — a small Dutch interior the town has printed on its tea-towels for eighty years — is not by the master it is catalogued under but a forgery made in the 1920s by a woman whose name appears in the ledger and nowhere else. She has to decide whether to say so. I gave the case for silence to another character, the forger's granddaughter, who is herself a restorer, and the trouble is that I cannot write the reply: every version I draft makes the conservator sound like a snob, or like someone changing the subject. Here is the argument as the granddaughter puts it. (1) The only way an object can matter to a person is by making some difference to what that person can perceive, do, or think — a difference detectable in principle. Call this the detectability constraint: a difference that no possible examination could reveal is not a value-making difference. (2) Stipulate, for the sake of the argument, that the 1920s canvas and the painting it copied are and will remain indistinguishable in every physical respect — pigments from the same shop, the same craquelure, and the instruments of the next two centuries will not separate them either. I need that stipulation: without it the answer is merely to wait for better machines, and I want the argument to be harder than that. (3) So the discovery destroys nothing of value: the thing on the wall affords every experience, and supports every thought and every action, that it did the day before. (4) What holds for the painting holds for anything whose specialness consists in where it came from — the letter, the battlefield, the wedding ring, the first edition, the very pen. (5) So provenance never matters, and neither does history: the value of a thing is exhausted by its present, detectable profile. (6) That is absurd. On (5) my conservator's relation to her dead father is worth no more than an indistinguishable simulation of it, and the town loses nothing if the real picture is burned at midnight and the copy hung in its place before morning. (7) Therefore (1) is false, and undetectable differences of origin are value-making after all.

Two further things make the knot worse. First, a friend in my writing group says (3) is simply false, because once the discovery is made public the experience of standing in front of the picture is not the same experience; the granddaughter replies that on that reading the loss is caused by the disclosure rather than by the forgery, and the honest conservator becomes the vandal who burns the tea-towels herself — an outcome my plot cannot survive and my characters would not forgive me for. Second, I cannot tell what to do with degrees: the museum's picture is in fact about a third nineteenth-century restoration, so if provenance is what matters, it matters in a way that comes in shades, and I do not know whether that gives the argument a threshold problem, a sorites problem, or neither. One more case, which I invented and am rather fond of: a fire in an abandoned workshop throws pigment onto a primed panel and produces, by pure chance, a panel indistinguishable from the Dutch interior. Nobody made it and nobody passed it off as anything, so it is not a forgery; but it is not by the master either, and it has the worst provenance imaginable while being identical to look at. If (7) is right, is it worth the full thing? If I have to choose, I would rather keep (1) than (6) — I do not want the novel to rest on a spooky metaphysics in which value floats entirely free of anything anyone could ever detect — but the forger cannot win either. And whatever the conservator says has to be sayable out loud, without jargon, in a museum office at midnight, by a woman who has never read a word of philosophy. So: no reading lists, no citations, no bibliography. I have no library and I am not going to footnote a novel.

What I need from you is not a verdict of valid or invalid but a diagnosis: which step or transition should be rejected, why that step looked safe while I was writing it, and what it costs me elsewhere in the argument if I reject there rather than one step earlier or later. Please give me a table with one row per step or transition you want to flag (group steps freely if you think nothing happens at one of them), with exactly these columns: Step — the number, or a transition written as 3→4, or the move from (6) and (1) to (7); Verdict — one of sound as stated, valid but premise false, equivocation, scope or quantifier shift, modal slide, acceptable but doing less work than it appears; Diagnosis — one sentence on what precisely goes wrong, or on why the step looked fine if you are keeping it; Cost — one sentence on what I must accept, or must be able to explain, if I reject here instead of at the neighbouring step; Line for the conservator — one sentence of jargon-free dialogue she could actually deliver in that office, or the word none if the step has no speakable version. After the table, give me a single JSON object with these fields: step_rejected (string); why_it_looked_fine (string, at most 40 words); biggest_remaining_cost (string, at most 40 words); disclosure_paradox (string, at most 40 words — is the loss caused by the discovery or by the forgery?); provenance_is (one of scalar, all-or-nothing, neither, followed by at most 25 words of explanation); chance_panel_value (string, at most 40 words — what the fire-made panel is worth on your diagnosis); forger_replies (string, at most 40 words — the strongest thing my restorer says next, after the conservator's best line).
