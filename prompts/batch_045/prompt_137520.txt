I'm a retired stuctural biologist — forty years of watching models get less wrong — and I've started sitting in on a philosophy of science reading group, wich is how I ended up constructing this thing over breakfast. It has to be broken somewhere, because the conclusion is silly, but I can't locate the bad step.

(1) Whether theory B is closer to the truth than theory A should be a matter of what B gets right and what B gets wrong.
(2) What a theory gets right is the true statements it entails; what it gets wrong is the false statements it entails. So B is closer than A iff B's true consequences outrun A's while B's false consequences don't outrun A's.
(3) But take any false theory T. For every true statement h, T entails "T or h", so T has infinitely many true consequences. And for every false statement g, T entails "T or g", so it has infinitely many false ones as well. Both columns are infinite for every false theory, so the comparison in (2) never comes out: every false theory ties with every other.
(4) So no false theory is closer to the truth than any other false theory — flat earth and slightly-squashed-sphere earth are equally far off, and so is Newtonian mechanics next to Aristotelian.
(5) That's absurd. I spent a career watching theories get closer, and "slightly squashed sphere" plainly is closer than "flat".

I want to keep (5) whatever happens. I'd also like to keep (1) — I don't like the idea that closeness to the truth is just a tally of entailed truths — but I'll give it up if that's the cheapest fix. Which step do you reject, and why did it look all right when I wrote it down? My own guess is that (3) is an artifact of counting junk disjunctions that nobody would count as something the theory "gets right"; but once I throw those out I don't know which consequences I'm allowed to count, and I suspect the answer will depend on which list of questions I decide are the relevant ones. If that dependence is the real price, tell me whether there's any way of cashing out "closer to the truth" that doesn't depend on such a choice, and what it has to pay instead.
