I'm teaching a unit on conventional obligation next term and I keep going back and forth on it, so here are my gut verdicts — I'd like the principle they add up to, if there is one.

Visitor from a no-tipping country leaves nothing in a US restaurant: basically fine, I don't think she's wronged anyone. Same visitor shoving in with everyone else to board a bus where the scrum is the norm: fine. An American who leaves a fat tip in a country where tipping isn't the practice: not fine — mildly obnoxious, even though nobody is harmed in any obvious way. Local queue-jumper: clearly not fine. Local crossing against the light on an empty street with no one watching: fine, and fine for the visitor too. Someone who flies to the same city every month for years and never picks up the local queuing norm: not fine, and worse than a two-day tourist doing exactly the same thing. And the one I can't settle: a delivery app that flashes a 20% default at you — I feel more obliged than I did with the old optional tip jar, and I can't tell whether the app is making a request or quietly rewriting the convention.

The thing I've been toying with is a draw-down condition: you owe conformity in proportion to how much you're taking out of the local cooperative scheme that runs on that convention, so tourists and passers-through owe almost nothing. But it doesn't explain why exiting a convention is free while importing one isn't, or why the empty-street jaywalk feels different from the queue jump.

So: one principle or several? Which of my verdicts does it force me to drop, and what does it say about the expat of ten years who still won't tip, and about a convention most locals actually resent and are trying to get rid of — is complying with it a way of respecting them or of propping up something they want dismantled?
