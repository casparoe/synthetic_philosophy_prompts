Six weeks ago my father died, and I became the fifth person in my family to hold the tenancy of the same hill farm on the Welsh borders; my daughter was born in the same fortnight. I now have to decide whether to place most of the land under a ninety-nine-year conservation covenant, which would bind my descendants and effectively end the farm as a working holding, or to sell part of it and give the proceeds to charities working on problems that are urgent now. A friend with a taste for anthropic reasoning has told me that I should expect both the tenancy and the human race to end sooner than I had assumed, on doomsday-argument grounds: I should treat my position in any series of which I am a member as a random draw from that series, and my position is an early one.

I have read enough to know that this argument has a large literature, and that the two sides usually end up trading assumptions at each other. I would like better than that. Please write a dialogue between two philosophers who disagree about it and who are equally good: Dr Nia Protheroe, who holds that the reasoning does give me genuine information and should move me, and Dr Alasdair Kemp, who holds that it gives me nothing I can act on, for a reason he has to work for rather than because the conclusion strikes him as absurd. Set it at the kitchen table on the evening of the funeral, with my great-great-grandfather's deed and the covenant papers between them; I am there but say little, and they address me from time to time.

They should get properly into the following, rather than gesture at them.

First, the deed. I discovered last week that it contains a genuine lottery: at each handover the next tenant is chosen by lot from among all the founder's descendants then living, and the farm passes on until someone drawn declines it. The pool has grown from a handful of people to forty-odd. Does a lottery like that give me any reason to expect the sequence of tenants to be short? My own thought is that it randomises who holds the farm at a given time, not when in the farm's history I turn up, and I want them to fight about whether that distinction is fatal to the inference or merely cosmetic, and about what sort of mechanism would be needed to make the inference go through.

Second, the arrival-time version of the reasoning: an outsider who happens upon an ongoing process at a random moment and infers its remaining duration from how far through it appears to be, the sort of thing tried out on how long a wall would stand or how long a theatrical run would last, where it seems to work tolerably well. Does its success support the version about me? One of them should press that the arrival-time version needs only a random time of observation whereas the other needs something about who I am; the other should say why the two might nevertheless stand or fall together.

Third, what exactly to conditionalise on: whether the datum is the thin one, that I find myself at roughly such-and-such a position among humans, or the thick one, which includes that I am alive in 2026 and know roughly how many people are alive now and how many lived before. The argument's strength seems to depend on this choice, so I want each of them to propose a principled rule for which centred facts to conditionalise on, and to explain why the rule does not simply smuggle in the conclusion. Handle the reference-class question here rather than waving at it: humans, or all observers, or observer-moments, or in the farm's case the founder's descendants, and why the answer changes.

Fourth, what follows for the decision even if the shift is real. They should take seriously that the covenant's value depends on there being a world in which it is honoured for a century, that the alternative is spending now, that my own choice may slightly affect how many people exist later, and that ordinary uncertainty about institutions, markets and climates may swamp any anthropic shift. If they disagree about its size, let them say how large it would have to be to matter, and why.

They should each concede at least one substantial point, name the consideration that would most move them, and end by identifying one thing they agree I should do regardless. Do not settle anything by naming authorities or by appealing to what is usually said; argue it out. Where a probability matters, state it plainly rather than dressing it in notation. Aim for roughly 1,400 to 1,800 words, in British English throughout.
