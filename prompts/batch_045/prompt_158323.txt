So I got talked into running a two-hour session at a writers' centre next month — selfhood for novelists, twelve adults, no philosophy background, two of them will arrive already saying "the self is an illusion, man", and one who thinks brain scans settled all this in the mid-2000s. The hook I want to use is that Dennett-ish idea of the self as a center of narrative gravity (center of gravity being a posit that isn't a extra bit of stuff, but is still the thing you have to calculate with). I more or less get it, I don't need it explained to me again. What I need is a plan for explaining it, to that room, in that order.

Specifically:

1. What has to come first and why — what depends on what. My instinct is that the center-of-gravity bit has to land as "an abstraction you can't touch that still does real work" before the word narrative enters the room, otherwise they hear "the self is a story" and I've lost them for the rest of the session. Tell me if I've got that backwards, and what the actual dependency is. Also: the gravity half of the metaphor is the part I can't cash out — why gravity and not, say, summary or digest? I need that before I can say it out loud.

2. The one example that carries the whole thing, and what such an example must have and must lack. I want it fictional and I'd rather it be something I can point at than a thought experiment. Candidate from my current draft: a woman who has kept a diary for thirty years that she never rereads, and in which she writes down things that didn't happen; everyone who knows her describes her, independently, as the most honest person they know; she has never once acted on anything in the diary. I like it because the private told narrative, the public narrative, and the behavior pull in different directions. But I'm worried it smuggles in sincerity as a separate question, or that it invites "well then there are three selves" — which I don't think the view says. Fix it or replace it, and say what the replacement has to have.

3. The misconceptions, in the order they'll form, and the point in the sequence where I should kill each one. The ones I expect: "so the self isn't real"; "so the self is just the story you tell"; "so you can edit yourself into a different person by retelling"; "so a character is real iff their story is consistent". I'm sure I'm missing the one that actually bites. Novelists have an occupational version of this — something about the inner life being the real thing and the page being its shadow — and I don't know how to defuse it without telling them their craft is metaphysics.

4. One exercise for the end that shows me it landed rather than that they're nodding. Ideally something they do on their own manuscripts in five minutes.

5. What I should flat out refuse to get into at this level and say aloud that I'm refusing. I'm guessing the free will / moral responsibility tail and anything involving split brains, but you tell me.

Last thing, and it's really the reason I said yes to this: I want to use it on my own book. There's a passage where my narrator is unreliable about her own motives in a way that isn't quite lying, and I want to know whether "center of narrative gravity" gives me anything there or whether it just relabels what I already do. If the honest answer is that the idea offers a novelist nothing they don't already have, say that too — but say it after the plan. And don't recommend me any reading, I definately won't do it.
