Two political theorists who have spent a year talking past each other — one arguing that "the will of the people" names something real that exists prior to, and can therefore stand in judgment on, any given set of institutions; the other arguing that it names nothing more than what a legitimately constituted procedure outputs, and that appeals to it outside such procedures are exercises in ventriloquism — have agreed to close out their dispute as an adversarial collaboration. Your task is to write the joint document they should produce.

Work with the following vocabulary, which they have already negotiated. Both accept four candidate readings of "the will of the people" as the space of options: (1) the *aggregative* reading — whatever a specified voting rule, applied to a specified demos, produces; (2) the *deliberative* reading — what that same demos would endorse under conditions of adequate information and mutual justification; (3) the *ascriptive* reading — the will of a bounded group identified by belonging ("the real people," as against outsiders and their domestic allies); and (4) the *acclamatory* reading — the will a leader discerns and the crowd ratifies by acclamation, prior to and independently of any counting rule. Both also accept three named difficulties: *mandate underdetermination* (an electoral victory underdetermines which of a platform's commitments, and which version of each, was authorized); the *spokesperson's regress* (any claim to speak for the people must itself be authorized by some procedure, whose authority is in turn contestable); and the *boundary problem* (every voting rule presupposes a demos, but no voting rule can legitimately pick its own demos).

Produce the document in this format.

**1. Agreed claims.** A numbered list of 8–10 claims both can sign. These must be load-bearing rather than platitudinous: "both sides care about democracy" is worthless; "an electoral plurality does not, by itself, settle which version of a mandate was endorsed" is acceptable if you then say what follows from it. Include at least one claim about what each side concedes the other is right to worry about.

**2. Verbal versus substantive.** A short section identifying which parts of their dispute dissolve under disambiguation of "the people," "democratic," or "legitimate," and which survive. Say explicitly if you think a crux is only apparently substantive.

**3. A disagreements table.** Columns: (a) the crux; (b) the populist camp's own formulation, worded as that camp would word it; (c) the proceduralist camp's own formulation, worded as that camp would word it; (d) a case or consideration both agree bears on the crux; (e) the populist's advance prediction about that case; (f) the proceduralist's advance prediction; (g) what each side would count as evidence against itself. Each side must be able to read column (b) or (c) and say "yes, that is my position, fairly stated."

Use these four cases as the bearing evidence, one per crux: a party winning a plurality on a commitment that a constitutional supermajority then blocks, with a later referendum on that same commitment passing 51–49 at 64% turnout; a movement party in office implementing a reform opposed by a large majority of its own voters while insisting that surveys register preference, not will; a secession referendum in a wealthy region, where what is at stake is which demos gets to decide; and an unelected technocratic body sustaining, over a decade, a policy that stable majorities have consistently opposed.

**4. Unsettled remainder.** One paragraph, jointly signed, on what the collaboration did not resolve and why — distinguishing "we lacked the evidence" from "we could not agree on what would count as evidence."

Aim for 1,200–1,600 words. The test of the draft is whether both parties could actually sign it: no resolution may be smuggled in by describing one side's view in the other side's pejorative vocabulary, and no agreed claim may be one that only sounds consensual. It is fine, and indeed expected, for the document to end with the disagreement standing, provided it is now exact.
