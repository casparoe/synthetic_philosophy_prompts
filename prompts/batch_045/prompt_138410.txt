I take the minutes for our city's Names and Monuments Panel — the little advisory body that gets dragged in every time somebody wants a statue moved or a school renamed — and I've started noticing that we vote one way and then justify it a different way each time. I'd like to know what rule we're actually running before next month's meeting, because we've got a genuinely nasty one coming up. Here's the record so far. We voted to remove the statue of Josiah Creel, the cotton magnate who paid for the infirmary and the free library, to the museum store, leaving the plinth empty; his money came from slave-picked cotton and the city's Caribbean and African communities had campaigned for years. We voted to keep the statue of Dr Marianne Voss, who more or less invented municipal sanitation here and cut typhoid deaths dramatically, and who also spent two decades campaigning for the compulsory sterilization of people with learning disabilities and feeding the campaign that produced the country's sterilization law; we kept it and bolted on a second plaque, and the minutes say her public health work is separable from the campaigning and that nobody living in this city carries the mark of that side of her career the way people still carry Creel's. We voted to rename Calloway Primary, named after the city's first director of education, who built the school system and also designed its segregated special schools and wrote the manual that fed into the same sterilization law; it's now named after a local nurse, and his name went to a plaque in the archive. And we kept the memorial to the men of the city who died in the frontier wars, on the grounds that it names no one in particular and honours conscripts rather than the cause, though we did pay for a second inscription naming the dead on the other side. One thing worth flagging: we have never yet renamed anything purely because a historian dug up something about a figure nobody in the city had heard of.

The new one: a primary school whose old building is being replaced wants to name the new one after Wilf Sowerby, headmaster there from 1948 to 1971, who rebuilt the school after the war and is remembered warmly by hundreds of living former pupils, several of whom are on the fundraising committee. A local historian has just established that before he came here Sowerby was a district officer in the colonial administration in Kenya during the Mau Mau emergency, and that he signed the detention orders and ran one of the camps where detainees were badly abused. Nobody in the city knew any of this, but the city does have a Kenyan community, including a couple of families whose grandparents were detained, and their association has written to ask the school not to use the name; the alumni, for their part, feel like they're being asked to erase a man who was kind to them. So: what are the two or three rules that fit all four of our previous votes equally well, and what does each of them say about Sowerby? Give me your best guess at which way we'll actually go and why you'd pick that rule over the others, tell me which of our four precedents is the closest analogue and which one we'll probably reach for dishonestly, and then hand me the one hypothetical case you'd want us to decide next that would tell the rules apart. And if you think our precedents genuinely don't settle it either way, say so plainly and tell me what principle we'd be committing ourselves to with each of the options on the table — naming it, naming it with a permanent counter-inscription, naming it after the pupils instead, or something else.
