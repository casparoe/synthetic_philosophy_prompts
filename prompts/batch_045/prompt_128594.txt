I am a sixth-form student working on an extended project, and there is a distinction I keep circling around without being able to pin down. I have been reading about Wittgenstein's remarks on certainty, and in particular about his idea of hinge propositions: the propositions we do not check, do not seriously doubt, and which must stay put for our practices of checking, doubting, and giving grounds to be possible at all. I would like you to compare this with the older idea I have met in epistemology of basic or foundational beliefs: beliefs that are justified without being justified by any further belief, and which bear the weight of the rest of what we claim to know.

On the face of it, the two look like the same thought in different words, and I would like to know whether that is really so. To help me get traction, I have invented a distinction of my own and I would like to see how well it survives. Some things we never doubt seem to be things we simply grow into, without anyone ever teaching us and without our being able to say how we came by them: that there are other people with minds, that objects do not vanish when unobserved, that the ground will not turn to liquid. Call these inherited hinges. Other things we never doubt are ones we were explicitly taught, and for which some authority or source could in principle be produced: that I was born in a particular town on a particular date, that the bus to school goes where the timetable says it goes, that the sum of the angles of a triangle is what my maths teacher said it was. Call these tutored hinges.

My questions are these. Is my distinction any good, or does it cut across the grain of what Wittgenstein is doing? Do inherited and tutored hinges fail in the same way when they are genuinely challenged — say, when someone is raised inside a form of life with very different assumptions, or when an expert tells me something I was taught is simply false? Relatedly, is a tutored hinge really a hinge at all, or is it just an ordinary well-supported empirical belief that has gone hard with disuse, so that my category is really a case of misclassifying ordinary knowledge? And should hinge propositions be understood as beliefs at all, or as something more like rules, framework assumptions, or ways of going on that only wear the grammatical clothing of a belief?

Please do not merely describe the two pictures side by side. Come down on the question of whether hinge epistemology is a genuine alternative to foundationalism or only a relabelling of it, and say what exactly the difference amounts to. Use concrete everyday examples liberally — my project is short on those — and pitch your answer at a careful sixth-former who is willing to work but has no specialist training.
