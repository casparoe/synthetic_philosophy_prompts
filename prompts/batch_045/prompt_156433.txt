I am drafting the chapter on the intent element for a graduate handbook on atrocity crimes, and a colleague who prosecuted at the international tribunals has been arguing me into a corner I cannot get out of. His claim is that the special-intent requirement makes the category of genocide unworkable, and that once you see why, the category itself should go. I have reconstructed his argument as charitably as I can below. Every step looks defensible to me; the conclusion strikes me as intolerable, and when I try to generalize the reasoning it seems to destroy far more than he wants. I need you to find the defective step.

The argument:

(1) What makes genocide distinctive, if anything does, is that the group as such is the object of the attack. The wrong is not merely that many individuals were killed or harmed but that a collective form of life is being undone; the victim is targeted as a member, not as an individual.

(2) This was Lemkin's own idea. He coined the term in the early 1940s and described the object of the crime as the undoing of a group's political and social institutions, its culture, language, national feeling, religion and economic existence — the replacement of the victim group's pattern of life with the perpetrator's. The drafters of the 1948 Convention narrowed this: five enumerated acts (including deliberately inflicting conditions of life calculated to bring about the group's physical destruction, and forcibly transferring its children to another group), four protected groups (national, ethnical, racial, religious — political groups were left out), and the single animating requirement of intent to destroy the group in whole or in part, which the International Court of Justice read in 2007 as requiring intent to destroy at least a substantial part.

(3) Intent is a mental state. Nobody observes it, cabinets do not minute a purpose of destruction, and perpetrators almost always believe they are acting defensively. So tribunals must and do infer it circumstantially: from the scale and systematicity of the conduct, from the deliberate selection of victims by group membership, from the targeting of the group's leadership, intelligentsia, religious sites and cultural symbols, from the destruction of its economic base, from the language used by officials, and from the surrounding context.

(4) But then consider what (3) concedes. The evidentially available content of intent to destroy is exhausted by the pattern of acts plus context. There is no behavioural residue — nothing left over, once the acts and circumstances are described — that distinguishes aiming at a group's destruction from merely foreseeing it, or from aiming at something else (defeating an insurgency, emptying a territory, assimilating a population) with destruction as the instrument.

(5) Apply this to a case I invented to test it. Call it the Vardena program. A state facing an insurgency concentrated in a highland minority region seals the region, destroys the terraces, seed stores and irrigation the group's agriculture depends on, and offers free transport and housing in lowland cities to anyone who registers as a member of the majority nationality and accepts majority-language schooling. No one is shot. Deaths from malnutrition and disease run into the tens of thousands. The cabinet records show the program was adopted to end the insurgency permanently and to cut the cost of administering a distinct nationality; they contain lines like the Vardeni question will be solved without a single shot, and notes that a killing campaign was rejected because it would invite foreign intervention. Within twelve years the highland region is empty of its historic population and the language has no fluent speakers under thirty. Every marker in (3) is present except lethal, group-selective violence. So if (4) is right, the Vardena program is genocide regardless of what any minister actually purposed — and regardless of whether the stated aim of ending the insurgency was sincere.

(6) Generalize. If the charge can be sustained on the pattern alone, then any sustained campaign of forced assimilation, deportation, or economic strangulation that foreseeably dissolves a protected group is genocide. And since the protected groups are constituted by ascription rather than by anything their members do, the category has no limiting principle beyond size, organization and causal route. Genocide then just means: coercive state policy that foreseeably dissolves a protected group.

(7) That is not a distinct wrong. It asserts nothing that the underlying crimes against humanity and the destruction of a people's collective life do not already assert; the peculiar stigma attached to the word — the crime-of-crimes rhetoric — is then a pure surplus with no element of conduct behind it. And it is a surplus that does work: it triggers duties to prevent and punish, it forecloses compromise, it licenses intervention and it settles historical argument. A legal category that adds nothing to the conduct but adds enormous consequence is a rhetorical device with a penal code attached. So the charge should be abandoned: prosecute the acts, and let the political vocabulary go.

(8) Here is my check on the argument, and the reason I think it is too strong. Run it on any ulterior-intent offence. Burglary is trespassory entry with intent to steal; terrorism is violence with intent to compel a government or intimidate a population; treason is levying war with intent to overthrow. In each, the differentiating intent is equally unobservable, equally inferred from the pattern of conduct, and equally without behavioural residue beyond the conduct itself. By this reasoning each collapses into its base offence, and we would have to conclude that burglary is just trespass and terrorism just murder — which nobody believes, and which my colleague does not believe either. So either the reasoning breaks somewhere between (3) and (7), or it proves considerably more than its conclusion.

Two things I would like to keep if I can, and I would rather pay almost anywhere else: (a) that there is a genuine difference — moral and legally cognizable — between trying to destroy a group and trying to break its capacity to fight, or to move it out, or to absorb it; and (b) that liability should not turn on recovering a minister's actual purpose, because a campaign engineered to be deniable should not enjoy immunity precisely because it was engineered to be deniable.

What I want from you: name the step where the argument fails and say precisely why it looked sound — in particular, if the failure is at (4), draw the distinction I have failed to draw between the modest claim that intent may be inferred from a pattern and whatever stronger claim (5) needs, and say whether the standard tribunals actually use (that the inference of intent be the only reasonable one available on the evidence) escapes the argument or merely relocates it. Say whether the reform usually proposed — replacing purpose with the perpetrator's knowledge that destruction of the group is virtually certain — does any better, or whether it just makes (5) and (6) explicit. Then price the alternatives: for each step you might reject instead of the one you reject, what you lose. And give me a verdict on the Vardena program under the corrected argument, with the reason it comes out that way.
