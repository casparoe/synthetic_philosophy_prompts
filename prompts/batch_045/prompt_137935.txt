I'm in a philosophy of mind reading group and we spent last week on pain, and I've come away holding a view I can't shake but also can't figure out how to test. Here it is: pain has at least two separable dimensions, the sensory-discriminative one (the stinging, burning, aching quality, plus its location and intensity) and the affective-motivational one (the it's-awful-and-I-want-it-to-stop part). My claim is that everything that makes pain bad — the thing that gives you a reason to avoid it, the thing that makes it a paradigm intrinsic bad — sits entirely in the second dimension, and that the sensory quality is bad only derivatively, because in normal people it reliably triggers the affect. The evidence I lean on hardest is pain asymbolia: patients who, as I understand the cases, still report a full-intensity, well-localised pain when you pinch or burn them, and can tell you it's sharp rather than dull, but say it doesn't bother them at all, and who sometimes grin or laugh while being stimulated. If that's right, then a pure pain sensation, all the sting and none of the horror, would be a state with nothing bad about it, and that seems to me to settle where the badness lives.

So tell me what would count against it, and please make it sharper than what I got at the reading group. I dont want the lazy version where someone just says the patients reports are unreliable; I want that turned into a specific hypothesis about what the lesion is actually doing to them, and into a claim about what observation would distinguish that hypothesis from mine. Two cases I keep chewing on, and you should take them on directly. First, a stipulation: imagine a follow-up study where asymbolic patients are given a button that lowers the intensity of a painful stimulus but costs them something mildly annoying, say a burst of static through the headphones — and they press it just as often as controls do, all the while insisting they don't mind the pain one bit. Does that show the sensory dimension was carrying badness all along, or is there a reading on which my view survives it? Second, the mirror case, which I've never seen anyone discuss: a hypothetical patient with the opposite dissociation, affect with the sensory quality stripped out, so they report a horrible diffuse dread located nowhere, with no stinging or burning character at all. My guess is that if such a state is still awful that's a point for me, and if subjects find it neutral that's real trouble, but tell me if I've got the direction backwards.

What I'd personally find most moving is behavioural or economic evidence rather than introspective report, since report is precisely what's in dispute — so weight your ranking accordingly. You're a philosopher of mind who works on pain and valence specifically, so no hedging into generalities about consciousness.
