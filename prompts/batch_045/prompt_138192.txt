Okay, so here's roughly where I've landed on free will after years of lurking on blogs about this, and I want someone to tell me how exposed it actually is.

The position: being free, to whatever extent anyone is free, just is having a control policy with a lot of counterfactual depth. Your behavior tracks a large number of distinct features of the situation you're actually in, and you have abort-and-reconsider routines that fire when the environment comes apart from what the policy was implicitly assuming. Sphexishness is just the name for the failure mode: a policy that keeps executing after its enabling conditions have been yanked out from under it, like the digger wasp that redoes the entire prey-dragging routine because someone nudged the cricket a few inches while it was inside inspecting the nest. Nothing else is needed. No indeterminism, no agent causation, no categorical "could have done otherwise" that isn't already cashed out in the counterfactual-support of the policy. Flexibility, in a fairly specific sense, is the whole story, and the only real question about any agent is how much of it there is and of what kind.

Give me a map of what would count against this. And be disciplined about it: I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to separate the ones a committed defender of the view would actually concede from the ones they'd just shrug off. I can generate the shrugs myself ("but it's deterministic", "but then you can't do otherwise in the deep sense") and I dont need those.

Where I think the pressure actually is:

1. Cheap variability. Raw behavioral flexibility is easy to get. A weathervane, a theremin, a random-number generator all produce wildly different outputs across situations while tracking none of the variables that matter. So the view has to be about tracking the *right* variables, not about output entropy. Fine — but is there a non-question-begging way to say which variables are the right ones, or does "the right ones" quietly smuggle in an intentional homunculus? If it does, that's not a shrug.

2. Meta-sphexishness. The wasp's reset-and-retry loop is itself completely rigid, but observed at the level of behavior it manufactures apparently flexible, information-sensitive-looking behavior on demand. So it looks like any operational test of "is this thing genuinely tracking its situation" can be passed by a system running a fixed meta-routine, or by a big enough lookup table. Is there a test left that isn't defeated in principle by that, and if there isn't, is that fatal or just a limit on how the view could ever be checked?

3. The sphexometer problem. Suppose we do operationalize it: count of distinct input features the policy is sensitive to, whether it aborts on mismatch, depth of the reconsideration stack, that sort of thing. On most of those metrics a current large language model scores extremely high — it responds to far more features of its situation than I do, revises on new information, aborts and restarts. My hunch is that a lot of why people dislike this view is just that they dont want the model to come out freer than the human. I'm personally inclined to bite that bullet and say the metrics are picking up something real. Is that bite defensible, or is it the view changing the subject?

4. Human data that reads as sphexish: the Good Samaritan in-a-hurry setup, priming effects, hypnotic suggestion, the ego-depletion replication mess. Does any of that count against the view, or only against the extra assumption that humans score high on it? And is the "then humans are less free than you thought" move a legitimate consequence the view should embrace, or the tell that it's been insulated?

5. The one I'd personally find most persuasive, if it works: whether the view can still do the work free will is supposed to do. Blame, desert, the reactive attitudes, the difference between someone who couldn't help it and someone who could. If flexibility comes apart from that — if, say, compulsive checking or a hyper-context-sensitive addiction scores high on flexibility and low on responsibility — that's trouble in a way no amount of "you've merely redefined freedom" settles. And conversely, if the view quietly dissolves the retributive stuff, say that plainly instead of papering over it.

6. Also worth addressing: the empirical basis for the central example is shakier than the rhetoric. Fred Keijzer has argued the wasp does adapt after enough iterations, and that repeated inspection of a disturbed nest may be adaptive rather than stupid. Does that touch the view at all, given that I'm using sphexishness as a limiting case rather than a claim about wasps? Or does the view secretly need the anecdote to be true to do its intuitive work?

Finally, the honest bit: I keep finding that I can absorb any counterexample by saying "that's just more sphexishness than we assumed", which is suspicious in the way a gerrymandered position is suspicious. Tell me straight whether this thing has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and then tell me whether that's even a problem for a view of this kind. It's supposed to be a claim about which differences make a difference, not an empirical prediction, so maybe insulation is fine here — or maybe that's exactly the diagnostic.

Skip citations and reading lists, I've read the usual stuff. Just argue, and tell me where I'm most likely to lose.
