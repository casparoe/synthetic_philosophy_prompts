I am reading a recent popular book about how scientific reclassification changes what our words mean — there is a chapter on whales, fish, and the shifting boundaries of natural kinds — and it contains what it presents as a sympathetic but critical account of Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism". I cannot tell how much of it to trust. The chapter says, more or less, the following:

Quine's target was the positivist picture on which every true statement has two components, a linguistic one and a factual one, so that some statements — the analytic ones — are true by meaning alone, with the factual component set to zero. He then asks what "true by meaning alone" amounts to, and shows that each of the answers on offer goes in a circle. A bachelor is an unmarried man, but ask what makes "bachelor" and "unmarried man" synonymous and you are told: they can be substituted for one another without change of truth value. Press on that and you are told: they can be substituted necessarily, that is, in every possible world — and "necessarily" turns out to mean nothing more than "analytically". Meaning, synonymy, definition, necessity: four names for one unexplained notion. The conclusion is therefore negative: there are no analytic truths, only statements we are, for the time being, extremely unwilling to give up. That is the first dogma dispatched. The second is reductionism, the idea that each statement can be checked against experience on its own; against it Quine sets holism, the view that our statements meet experience as a body, so that in principle even the laws of logic are revisable. What survives is genuinely useful, though: analyticity was never a special kind of truth, just a sociological fact about which sentences a community has stopped arguing about. Better still, the notion can be rescued by restating it epistemically. Call a statement analytic when a competent speaker who understands it can see that it is true without going and looking. "All vixens are female" qualifies; "all swans are white" does not. Notice the consequence, which the argument of this book depends on: analyticity is not timeless. "Whales are mammals" was not analytic in 1750 and is analytic now, because once the taxonomists had settled the question, the sentence became one you can know simply by knowing what the words mean. Analyticity is a status a sentence acquires when the inquiry around it goes quiet.

Three things about this leave me uneasy. The first is the chapter's rescue: I am inclined to think it misses the point, since Quine's complaint was precisely that nobody can say what "in virtue of meaning" comes to, and "seeable as true just by understanding the words" looks like the same idea in different words — but I read the paper before I read the book, so that may just be loyalty to my first source. The second is the whale case: it strikes me that the interesting option is that "whales are mammals" is necessary but knowable only a posteriori, not that its meaning quietly changed somewhere around the eighteenth century, and that the chapter needs the second option for its own purposes. The third is the framing of the dogmas, about which a friend has said two things I cannot adjudicate: that Quine's own view is that the two dogmas are at root one dogma rather than two independent targets, and that what the paper undermines is not the distinction itself but the epistemological work the logical positivists wanted the distinction to do.

I would be grateful for a clause-by-clause audit rather than a general verdict. Please tell me which claims about Quine you would accept as stated, which are standard simplifications that a specialist would wince at but which do no real damage, which are substantive misreadings, and which are things Quine would explicitly deny — and, where a claim is wrong, what the accurate version of it would be. In particular:

Does the chapter's epistemic restatement of analyticity escape the circularity Quine alleges, or does the circle reappear one step later? If it fails, say exactly where; if it can be repaired, say what would have to be added, and whether what remains is still able to do the work analyticity was supposed to do.

What does the whale case actually show? Argue for whichever of the two readings you favour, and set out plainly what each one costs — in particular whether the chapter's reading commits it to saying that a reclassification changes the meaning or the reference of "whale", and what that would predict about what eighteenth-century naturalists were talking about.

What verdicts does the chapter's criterion deliver on cases it has not considered, and do those verdicts look right to you: "water is H₂O"; "every vixen is a fox"; a long and complicated tautology that no competent speaker could verify by inspection alone; "I am now conscious".

Finally, the chapter closes by concluding that, since analyticity is merely a sociological status, no philosophy that leans on conceptual analysis can get off the ground. Does anything like that follow from the parts of the chapter that are correct? Or is there a defensible position in between the positivist picture the chapter describes and the scepticism it ends with?

Please write in British English, and assume that I know the broad outlines of the period but not the secondary literature, so do not lean on the names of commentators without explaining what their view is.
