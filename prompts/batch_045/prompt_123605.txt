I'm a science journalist and I'm finally getting around to a piece on the Knobe effect — you know the thing, people will say an executive who couldn't care less about the environment intentionally damaged it when his profit program happens to wreck it, but won't say he intentionally helped it when the very same program happens to clean it up. For a few weeks now I've been running my own knockoff versions past a friend of mine (philosophy PhD, dropped out, now does compliance work for a hospital system — he's the most consistent person I know to test this on) and recording two things each time: whether the agent did the side effect intentionally, and a 0–10 blame-or-credit rating. His track record so far, where in every vignette the agent knows the side effect will happen and says out loud that they don't care about it either way: program that will harm the environment → intentional, blame 7; identical program that will help the environment → not intentional, credit 0; scheduling software that cuts nurses' overtime pay → intentional, blame 6; bike lane that lowers kids' asthma rates → not intentional, credit 0; cheaper disinfectant that leaves a smell everyone in the lab hates → intentional, but blame 1 ("technically yeah, but I'm not worked up about it"). In every single one, including the good side effects, he also said the agent did it knowingly.

The case I actually want to spring on him next, and I'd rather go in with a hypothesis: a transit director is ordered by her board to cut the late-night bus route. She knows with near-certainty that one night-shift home health aide will lose her job because there's no other way for her to get to her clients, she fights the cut for weeks, proposes two alternatives that get voted down, and tells a reporter afterwards that it's the last thing in the world she wanted. The aide does lose the job. So: will he say she intentionally cost the aide her job? Will he say knowingly? And which rule is he implicitly running — something like "bad foreseen side effects get called intentional and good ones don't", or "intentional is just the word we use to mark someone as a fitting target of blame", or something about the agent's own attitude, like whether she treated the effect as a reason against acting or merely as a cost she regretted paying? I'm less interested in which answer is philosophically correct than in which of those rules fits his record so far, where they come apart on this case, where his weird blame-1 verdict on the disinfectant guy fits in, and what the one further vignette should be to separate whichever rules survive — I've got room for exactly one more in the draft.

Also, whatever rule he's running, does it have anything interesting to say about a version where the thing making the decision isn't a person but a model or an allocation algorithm, or is that a different question entirely that I should leave to the philosopher I'm interviewing on Monday? My editor wants a line about juries and corporate intent and I'm not sure the analogy is doing any work, but its due Friday and I'd definately rather not publish something that a grad student can take apart in a single tweet.
