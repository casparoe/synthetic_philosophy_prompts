I am a PhD student in philosophy working on early modern moral psychology, and I have been asked to lead a reading-group session on Leibniz's ethics for a mixed audience of graduate students in philosophy and intellectual history. While preparing it I read a recent trade book on Leibniz by a well-regarded historian of early modern philosophy, and I cannot tell how much of its account to trust. The chapter on his practical philosophy summarizes the position roughly like this:

The key to Leibniz's ethics is that he separates the level of justification from the level of action. Goodness is not a matter of will: not even God makes a thing good by commanding it, and his command is right because the thing already is good. What goodness consists in is the greatest quantity of perfection, which, where created persons are concerned, simply is happiness — Leibniz gives us the greatest-happiness formula a century before Hutcheson, and Bentham only later detached it from its theological housing. God surveyed the possible worlds and actualized the one with the largest surplus of happiness over misery, and that is all that calling this the best of all possible worlds amounts to. We cannot run that calculation ourselves, so God issues three rules of natural justice instead — harm no one, render to each his own, live honourably — which function as rules of thumb calibrated to the balance of happiness in ordinary cases. Because they are only proxies, the wise agent will set them aside whenever he can see that breaking them yields more happiness; that is what is meant by calling justice the charity of the wise: charity supplies the end, and wisdom supplies the knowledge of when the rules stop tracking it. The trouble is that the metaphysics undoes the ethics. If monads have no causal commerce with one another, then no one can really promote anyone else's happiness, and an ethics of beneficence describes a relation that cannot obtain. What survives is theodicy: suffering is the price of a larger total good, and Leibniz's own definition of love as taking pleasure in another's happiness shows that his benevolence is really a refined self-love. Since he also holds that the world is so ordered that virtue is in the end rewarded with happiness, the good man is simply the man who has correctly calculated that virtue pays.

Two things here strike me as probably wrong, but I would not know how to argue for either. I have always understood the three grades of natural justice as ascending standpoints an agent can occupy — abstaining from injury, giving others what is theirs, and then a universal beneficence ordered by the understanding of perfection — rather than as decision-procedures issued in place of a calculation; and I cannot see why the denial of causal interaction between monads should make benevolence impossible, since Leibniz does not seem to think moral relations are causal ones. But the author is a professional and writes with great confidence, and I may well be wrong in both places.

What I need is an accuracy audit I can act on. Please work through the passage claim by claim and sort it into what a Leibniz specialist would accept without demur, what they would wince at as a simplification or a tendentious but defensible framing, and what is straightforwardly mistaken. In particular, I would like you to say:

whether the book's organizing thesis — that Leibniz is a two-level consequentialist avant la lettre, with God as the global maximizer and human agents as rule-followers using the precepts of natural justice as proxies for a calculation they cannot perform — illuminates or distorts the material it is meant to organize, and where precisely the distortion sets in;

whether identifying the good with aggregate happiness is legitimate for Leibniz, given that his official criterion is perfection, and what work is being done by the distinctions among metaphysical, physical, and moral goodness that the summary erases;

whether reading justice as the charity of the wise as a licence to override the precepts in favour of aggregate gain is compatible with wisdom's being a constituent of justice rather than an escape hatch from it;

whether the monadological objection — no causal interaction, therefore no agent can benefit another — is a genuine difficulty for Leibniz's ethics of beneficence, a confusion about the kind of dependence at issue, or some of each, and how a defender of Leibniz would answer it;

whether the closing claim that Leibniz collapses morality into calculated self-interest gets the relation between virtue and happiness right, or whether it mistakes a providential guarantee for both a motive and an empirical prediction;

and finally, whether the two conclusions the book draws downstream — that Leibniz rather than Hutcheson is the true source of the greatest-happiness formula, and that his ethics is best described as theodicy with a moral psychology bolted on — follow from a corrected version of the passage or only from the misreadings in it.

I should say that I am not asking for citations, a bibliography, or chapter-and-verse references: I have the primary texts to hand and can check the passages myself, and I would much rather you flagged where a judgment turns on a textual point I should verify than tried to reconstruct the text from memory. What I need is the conceptual audit — which distinctions the summary runs together, which modest theses it overstates, and which standing objection it presents as a refutation. If it is helpful, close with a single paragraph I could put in front of the reading group as the corrected account.
