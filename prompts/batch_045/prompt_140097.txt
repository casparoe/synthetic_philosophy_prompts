I write about science and ideas for a national newspaper, and once a year I'm asked to give a forty-five-minute masterclass to the incoming cohort of our science-journalism fellowship: twelve people, all of them already covering climate in some form, none of them historians, and most of them quietly convinced that anything operating on a timescale longer than a funding cycle is "background" and belongs in the fourth paragraph, after the news peg. This year I want to spend the session on Braudel's distinction between the longue durée, the conjuncture, and the event — not as historiography, but as a working instrument for deciding what goes in the first sentence of a story.

Please work as both a historian of ideas and a veteran editor of explanatory journalism. Think the session through carefully before you answer, and begin by sketching three possible orderings of the material; keep one, and tell me in two sentences each what is wrong with the other two. Then give me the plan, not the lecture: I can write the prose myself.

What I need from you:

1. The dependency structure. What has to be established in the room before the distinction can do any work at all? My instinct is that nothing lands until they grant that a causal claim has a timescale — that "the cause" of a thing is not one item at one moment — and that the news peg systematically selects for the shortest available one. Tell me whether that is the right first move, whether something has to come before it, and what each later piece borrows from the earlier ones.

2. The carrying example. One case, worked through at all three tiers, that I can put on the board and return to four times during the session. It must contain a real, datable event, because these are reporters; a slow geographical or climatic constraint that genuinely constrains rather than merely sets the scene; and a middle tier — a price cycle, a policy regime, a generation of infrastructure — which is the one they will overlook. Do not reach for Hurricane Katrina or the Dust Bowl, and tell me specifically what each of those gets wrong as a teaching case: my suspicion is that both are now so thoroughly pre-digested that the fellows will already have the structural story in their heads and will feel confirmed rather than caught out.

3. Two devices of my own that I want pressure-tested. The first is a timescale ledger: for every causal claim in a piece, write the number of years over which that cause does its work, then look at the distribution — in my experience it collapses into a spike below five years. The second is what I call the dateline fallacy: the assumption that the moment something became visible to a correspondent is the moment it began. Tell me which of these is doing real work and which is decoration, and at what point in the session each should appear.

4. The misconception map, with a position in the sequence for each. The ones I expect: that longue durée means geography is destiny; that a slow cause can be directly attributed to a single event (one fellow will say some particular flood was caused by climate change, and I want to know where that error lives — is it a timescale error, an attribution error, or something else); that structure is just a box-out bolted onto the news; that the oldest cause is automatically the deepest one; and that structural explanation dissolves accountability, which is the one that will actually make them angry, because holding people to account is what they think the job is. For each: the point at which to head it off, and whether to head it off pre-emptively or let it surface first and then break it.

On that last one there is a constraint. The obvious move against "Braudel didn't care about individuals" is to point at what Braudel himself wrote, and I'm not willing to do that: I can't verify a title or a date from a lectern, and I don't want the session resting on my recollection of a bibliography. Give me a way to defuse it that works on the concept alone.

5. The check. Something I can run in the last five minutes that distinguishes a fellow who can now see the three tiers from one who has merely learned the vocabulary. My current idea is to have them take a story they filed in the last month and rewrite the nut graf so that the slowest-moving cause appears first, then tell me what the rewrite cost them. Tell me whether that tests what I think it tests, and what a wrong answer would look like.

6. What to refuse. Name two things I should openly decline to get into at this level — I'm thinking of the question whether structural explanations are causal at all, and of anything requiring me to adjudicate between schools of historiography — and give me the single sentence I should say instead, so the refusal doesn't read as evasion.

Finally: I want one sentence left on the board for the whole forty-five minutes, the through-line of the session, in a form a fellow could repeat to an editor the next morning. Give me that sentence and place it inside \answer{...} as the last line of your reply. Nothing should follow it.
