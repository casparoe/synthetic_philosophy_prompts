I've been chewing on the wrong kind of reasons problem for a couple of years now — mostly on my own, occasionally with one friend who thinks the whole thing is a pseudo-problem — and I keep going in circles. I don't want another verdict on who's right. I want a diagnosis of why nothing ever moves.

Here's the shape of it as I see it. The fitting attitude style of analysis says, roughly, that for a thing to be valuable is for there to be reason to favor it. Then comes the standard counterexample: a demon threatens to destroy everything you care about unless you admire him. Now you have a reason to admire the demon, but the demon isn't remotely admirable, so the analysis seems to hand out value where there is none. The moves in response are all familiar: restrict the reasons that count to the right kind, where the right kind are supposed to be the ones having to do with the object rather than with what admiring it does for you; split object-given from state-given reasons and keep only the first; say instead that value is what makes attitudes fitting and refuse to cash fittingness back out into reasons; push the buck-passing in the other direction; insist the demon only gives you a reason to get yourself into a state of admiration, not a reason for admiring him. And then somebody produces a new variant and we do it again.

My working hypothesis is that the phrase wrong kind of reason is doing at least three seperate jobs that nobody bothers to pull apart: an epistemic job, marking which reasons are evidence of the object's value; an explanatory or metaphysical job, fixing which direction the explanation runs between value and the reasons there are to hold attitudes; and a taxonomic job about which attitude is even the target — admiring, approving, cherishing, judging good, something else. If that's right, then the two sides aren't fighting about one thing, and every new counterexample is aimed at a different job, which would explain why the cases keep failing to land against what the other side actually thinks.

To make the question concrete, here's a variant I made up that I can't get the standard machinery to sort cleanly. Call it the Cultivation Demon. He doesn't threaten you and he doesn't bribe you. He tells you, truthfully, that if you take up a genuinely mediocre poem and work at it until you honestly admire it — no faking, you actually have to learn to see what's there — you will become the sort of person who notices value in things generally, and that this is a real non-instrumental improvement in you, not just a pleasant side effect. Compare the ordinary claim that you have a reason to listen to difficult music because it trains your ear. This reason isn't a threat or a payoff, and it isn't about the poem's poetic properties, but it isn't purely about the state of your admiring either, since what it tracks is a change in you. So: right kind or wrong kind? Does it bear on the poem's value even derivatively? And whatever your favored account says here, does the answer turn on anything to do with value, or only on how that account happens to be regimented?

Second thing I want you to take up: if you can build structurally identical demon cases for belief, for intention, and for hope — cases where there's a reason for the attitude that has nothing to do with what the attitude is answerable to — then maybe this isn't a debate in value theory at all and has just been misfiled for a long time.

So: is this a verbal dispute, a substantive one, several different questions sharing a label, or a badly posed question? Argue for your diagnosis using what each side actually commits to, not by listing positions. Then tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result, a reframing of the question — and tell me honestly if you think nothing would and why. No citations or reading lists, I just want the diagnosis itself. Please write in American English.
