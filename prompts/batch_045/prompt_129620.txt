I retired four years ago from a lab job — thirty-odd years calibrating satellite radiometers, which mostly means I have strong feelings about whether a number means what it says — and since then I've been going to a philosophy reading group and, less voluntarily, sitting on the committee at our regional hospital that signs off on the predictive models the clinical departments want to buy. I've now said yes and no to enough of these that I think I have views, but I cant tell you what the views are. Most of what I've read on this subject is slogans, so please don't give me a tour of the literature; give me a rule, say where it bites, and say which of my answers it can't rescue.

The verdicts, as best I can reconstruct them.

1. A deterioration model last year. Two groups, and the bad outcome genuinely happens more often in one of them — 40% versus 15%. The vendor's score was honest in both groups: a 0.3 meant 30% for everybody, which I checked myself on a holdout sample, and I liked that a lot. But at the alert threshold the model missed 20% of the true deteriorations in the high-rate group and 45% in the other. I said no. Missing sick people at twice the rate in one group is the thing I can't sign off on.

2. This spring the same vendor came back with the miss rates and false-alarm rates equalized across the two groups; they got there partly by using a different cut for each group, 0.45 for one and 0.30 for the other. I said no to that too. Now the flag goes with something like a 70% risk in one group and a 40% risk in the other, and a number in the middle of the range doesn't mean the same thing depending on who it's attached to. Separately, the business of two different cut-offs for two kinds of patient struck me as a *different* kind of wrong from the numbers complaint, but I can't say what the difference is and I'm not sure I trust the feeling. I'd like to know whether that's really one objection or two.

3. A post-operative complication model where the "group" was age. It flags far more seventy-year-olds than forty-year-olds, and seventy-year-olds really do have more complications. I said: that isn't a fairness problem, it's a prediction, go ahead.

4. A model for who gets assigned a care manager, trained on past cost and past utilization. It never saw the group variable, and it was well calibrated by every test I could think of. But one group has had worse access to primary care for decades, so at the same level of need they run up less utilization, so they get flagged less. I said no — the instrument is measuring access and calling it illness. I spent my career throwing out instruments that did that.

5. One where the errors were worse in one group for the boring reason that their records are thinner — fewer prior labs per patient, so the model is less certain about everybody. That one I wanted to call a data-quality failure rather than an unfairness, and I approved it with conditions. I'm not sure the distinction survives contact with the people it happens to, but I do think it's a distinction, and I want a rule that has room for it.

6. And one constraint that isn't a verdict on a case: I don't trust any fix that's been tuned to make the numbers come out right for the two groups we happened to print on the form. I've watched too many people massage an analysis until a comparison looked clean. So whatever rule we land on, I want it to keep behaving when somebody slices the population along a line nobody listed — patients whose first language isn't English, people who live alone, whatever it turns out to be. A rule that only works for the groups we named is curve-fitting, as far as I'm concerned.

So: is there one principle that gets all six, or nearly all of them? State it precisely enough that I could go check it, and tell me which case is doing the work for each clause. Then tell me which verdict has to go, and which one you'd overturn if it were up to you — I'd rather be told I'm wrong about one thing than be handed a rule with six exceptions bolted onto it.

And the case I have no verdict on, which is next month's: a tool that doesn't produce a probability or a score at all, only a ranking of patients for a scarce resource with a fixed number of slots. The group difference will show up as who lands in the top of the list — say the low-rate group is about a third of the patients and about a fifth of the top fifty names. Nothing in my calibration instinct seems to apply to a bare ranking, and yet I expect to have an opinion when I see it. What does your rule say there, and does it matter that slots are all-or-nothing in a way that a score isn't?

Finally: does my constraint 6 actually favor one candidate rule over the others, or does it cut against all of them equally? And what does the rule you like predict for some case I haven't listed — pick one yourself, and tell me which further case would best separate your rule from whatever came second.
