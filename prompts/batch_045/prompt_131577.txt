I commission and edit evolutionary-explanation pieces for a popular-science magazine — roughly one "why do humans or other animals have trait X?" feature a month — and I want a triage procedure my editors can run on any proposed adaptive explanation before we buy it. I want the steelman version of the Gould–Lewontin spandrels critique, not the version that mostly licenses us to feel smarter than whoever pitched the story, and not a blanket skepticism that would make the section impossible to fill.

Concretely: an ordered checklist of questions, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, a point at which the procedure stops with a no, and the points where it hands the question back to a human because it is editorial or value judgment rather than evidence. My editors are generalists with at most an undergraduate biology course, they have about twenty minutes per pitch, and I sign off on the outcome, so the procedure has to be runnable without a specialist in the room and say plainly when it cannot be.

The distinctions I most want preserved rather than flattened by the simplification: current utility versus historical selection for that effect — a trait can be useful now without having been built for that use — and, among the non-adaptive explanations, structural byproduct with no current function versus byproduct later co-opted for a function versus a trait that made sense in an ancestral environment and is now mismatched or locked in by lineage history. I also want the procedure to include what I call the accessibility check: is there some variant near the actual trait that would perform the alleged function better and that the lineage could plausibly have reached? If such a variant exists and is absent, the adaptive story owes us an explanation, and I want the procedure to say what would discharge that debt.

Then run it on three pitches: one arguing that the human taste for spicy food is an antimicrobial adaptation; one arguing that music is a byproduct of other cognitive machinery with no adaptive story of its own; and one that I expect to be uncomfortable, since my house line is that our debunkings are better evidenced than the adaptationist claims they mock, and I suspect a symmetric procedure will say they are often not. Show me where that suspicion is correct and where it is not, and be honest about what the procedure cannot settle even in principle.
