The question:

Let K be "it is known (by someone) that" and let the diamond be metaphysical possibility. The knowability principle is KP: for every proposition A, if A is true then it is possible that A is known (A → ◇KA).

(a) Set out Fitch's argument against KP. Be explicit about which premises about knowledge it uses, and about exactly what the conclusion is.

(b) Now define a proposition as transparent iff it contains no epistemic vocabulary — no occurrence of K and no occurrence of "knowable" — and consider the restricted principle RKP: every true transparent proposition is knowable. Does the argument in (a) refute RKP? Whether or not it does, is the restriction doing honest work, or is it just gerrymandered to dodge this one derivation?

(c) Suppose two further things: (i) the language contains, for every opaque truth, a transparent surrogate — a transparent proposition necessarily equivalent to it; and (ii) knowledge is closed under known necessary equivalence: if someone knows N, knows that N ↔ B, and N entails B, then they know B. With (i) and (ii) in place, does the paradox return for RKP? If it does, say precisely which extra commitment is carrying the weight; if it doesn't, say what else would have to be added, and whether the restriction in (b) is still worth anything to someone who wanted knowability in the first place.

The student's answer:

(a) Take a truth A that nobody knows, so A ∧ ¬KA. KP applies to every proposition, so it applies to this one: ◇K(A ∧ ¬KA). But nothing can know that conjunction. Knowledge is factive, and knowing that A is not known gives that A is not knowable — K¬KA implies ¬◇KA. The other conjunct gives KA, and hence ◇KA. Contradiction. So □¬K(A ∧ ¬KA). The proposition is unknowable because it is Moorean: to know it you would have to know A while avowing that A is not known, which is pragmatically self-defeating in the way that saying "it is raining and I do not believe it is" is. Since KP was supposed to hold for every proposition and it yields ¬(A ∧ ¬KA), i.e. A → KA for arbitrary A — every truth is known — KP is refuted. The premises about knowledge the argument uses are factivity and distribution over conjunction, i.e. logical omniscience. This last premise is the weak link: a finite agent can know a conjunction without having bothered to unpack both conjuncts, so knowledge is not closed. The argument therefore only shows that knowability is incompatible with closure, and since no sensible account ofknowledge for finite agents grants closure anyway, the paradox has little force.

(b) RKP restricts the principle to transparent propositions, ones with no epistemic vocabulary in them. The only instance of the principle that Fitch's argument needs is the one for A ∧ ¬KA, and that proposition mentions knowledge, so it is not transparent and RKP says nothing about it; so RKP survives. And the restriction is not ad hoc. The anti-realist's claim was always about states of affairs in the world being in principle manifestable to us — that is a claim about ordinary non-epistemic propositions — not about claims concerning who knows what. So the restriction just makes explicit what was meant all along.

(c) With surrogates the paradox comes straight back. Let A be an unknown truth. The opaque proposition A ∧ ¬KA has, by (i), a transparent surrogate N necessarily equivalent to it. N is true, so RKP applies to it and gives ◇KN. By closure under known necessary equivalence, whoever knows N thereby knows A ∧ ¬KA, so ◇K(A ∧ ¬KA) — the same contradiction as in (a). So RKP is refuted as well, the restriction does no work at all, and the anti-realist has to give up the knowability principle outright.
