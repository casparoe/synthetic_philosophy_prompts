Last night my friend and I got about two hours into an argument about whether culture is ever really a cause, and we gave up arguing about the literature and started betting on what Weber would say if he could read it. I want you to settle the bet. Here are the two cases. Case one: imagine the cleanest natural experiment you could ask for — a sixteenth-century settlement hands the religion of a region to whoever rules it, so two otherwise identical regions on either side of a border end up Protestant and Catholic by decree, and four centuries later the Protestant side is measurably richer, and richer in a way that runs through literacy and savings rates rather than through any doctrine people still believe. My friend says Weber would have killed for that design and would treat it as his thesis confirmed with better tools. I say he'd think it misses the thing he was after, because the causal claim he cared about was about a meaning-complex — work as a calling, a conduct of life adopted for its own sake — and no coefficient on a religion dummy gets you that. Case two, which is the one I actually care about: today the calling-attitude has definately jumped its religious tracks. The people who most obviously live it now are startup founders, the productivity/quantified-self crowd, and the earn-to-give people, none of whom have any theology, and inside most rich countries the old Protestant–Catholic income gap has basically vanished. Would Weber still say culture is causing something there? What does he do with a spirit of capitalism that has been completely detached from the religious ideas that formed it — does it stop being a cause, become a different kind of cause, or does the framing fall apart? He was writing long before any modern causal-inference toolkit existed, so I want you to reason from what his method actually was: ideal types, interpretive understanding of meaning, elective affinity rather than one-way causation, and the claim that cultural sciences are about significance rather than laws. Don't just summarise the thesis back at me — take a side on both cases, say which piece of his machinery forces the answer, and tell me which question he would refuse to answer at all. Finish with a single line: Verdict: [one sentence, the core of what he'd say].
