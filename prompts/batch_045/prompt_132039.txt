I sit on the acquisitions committee of a small contemporary-art museum, and last month we had to vote on a suite of large-scale images produced by a generative model. We scored the works blind and then again after being told how they were made; the second round went very differently, and the committee has not recovered from the argument. I would rather think this through with Hume's essay on the standard of taste than with our untutored intuitions, so I would like you to reconstruct what he would say.

Reason strictly from the qualities he demands of a true judge — delicacy of sentiment joined to good sense, sharpened by practice, exercised through comparison with other works, and purged of prejudice — and give me his likely verdict on three questions. First: can a generative image be a proper object of taste at all, given that no one intended the particular features we admire in it, and that what we respond to is partly the style of a body of human-made work the model was trained on? Second: is the drop in our scores once we learned the method the kind of prejudice his criteria exclude, or is knowledge of how a thing was made the sort of thing a true judge ought to keep? Third, and most important: his account seems to presuppose that judges have access to a common body of works to compare against, whereas most people now encounter images through individually tailored feeds, so that no two judges share a comparison class — could the standard of taste survive that, or would he have to say taste has become irreducibly local?

Mark clearly where you are extending him rather than reporting him, and where you think he would simply have no resources for the question. Please argue from the criteria themselves rather than from secondary literature: I want the reasoning, not a reading list, and I have no interest in whose article to look up next.
