hey — so I don't have any academic background, I just listen to a bunch of podcasts and read blog posts about AI stuff in my spare time, and there's one comparison I keep running into that I can't tell is doing real work or just sounding smart.

The version I keep hearing goes roughly: an aligned AI system should be neutral between different conceptions of the good life, in the same way a liberal state is supposed to be. It shouldn't take a side on whether you should be a devout muslim, a hardcore effective altruist, a tradwife, a marxist, whatever — it should let people live by their own values while enforcing some thin set of rules that stop them from running into each other. People then use this to conclude that models should refuse to take positions on contested moral or political questions, should give you the steelman of each side instead, should treat "the values to be served" as just whatever the user's values happen to be, and that any other design is the developers' politics sneaking in the back door. So the answer to "whose values" is supposed to be: nobody's in particular, it's a referee.

I want to know how far that actually holds. Can you lay out the correspondence — what plays the role of citizens, territory, coercion, voting, courts, rights, public justification, exit — and then where it breaks? And specifically whether the "never take sides" conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole time.

Some of the places I get stuck, in no order:

People say the reason a liberal state has to be neutral is that everyone is stuck under one government with no way out, so it has to justify itself to all of them. With a model you can just close the tab and use a different one. Does that kill the analogy, or does it only mean the analogy bites for whatever model everyone ends up actually using in practice?

A state also has to *give reasons* — justify its rules to the people who live under them. A model doesn't reason at anyone, it just produces output. Does the whole legitimation story transfer, or is that the part that quietly drops out?

And then the people affected by what a model does aren't only the people talking to it. If a model makes it trivially easy for everyone to write a lease, or a threatening letter, or a fake medical abstract, that shifts what everyone can do, including people who never touch it. There's no citizenship and no territory but there sure are third parties. What plays "citizen" in the analogy then — everyone affected? That seems like it makes the system look way more like a state than the people using the analogy want.

Also, neutrality needs a line between "conceptions of the good," where you're supposed to be neutral, and "basic rights," where you're not allowed to be. Whoever draws that line has already picked a side. For a model that line is in the training data and the system prompt. Does that mean "neutrality" is just a nice name for one faction's settlement winning?

Here's the fix I've started kicking around and I definately don't trust it: I've been separating *neutrality of address* — how even-handed the model is to whoever is currently talking to it — from *neutrality of outcome*, i.e. the fact that no matter how it's set up, the model shifts what's cheap and easy for everyone downstream, and there seems to be no neutral setting for that. Is that distinction real or am I just renaming the problem? My worry is the two collapse into each other, since the output *is* the thing that changes what people can do.

There's also a rival comparison I hear: it's not a state at all, it's more like a butler or a lawyer — you hired it, it should do what you want, end of story. Where does that one fail, and is there a better analogy than either?

If it helps to be concrete: someone asks the model to help them draft campaign ads for a ballot measure on abortion, or asks "should I leave my wife." What does the neutral-state story say it should do in each, and does that answer still look right to you once the mapping is explicit?

Please don't just point me at papers — I've got no way to get at them and I want the argument anyway, not a reading list. And I'd rather you tell me which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion and which are just noise, because right now they all feel equally damning and that probably means I'm missing something.
