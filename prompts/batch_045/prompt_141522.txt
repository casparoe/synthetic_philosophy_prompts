I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm drafting a piece on what computational complexity theory does and doesn't establish about the limits of inquiry. I want to riff on a magazine article I read on the P vs NP question, but first I need to know how much of its framing I can safely inherit. The article's summary runs roughly like this:

"P is the class of problems a computer can solve quickly; NP is the class of problems that take exponential time, where the only way to get an answer is to grind through the possibilities one at a time. Essentially everyone in the field believes P ≠ NP, and because NP-complete problems — protein folding, optimal scheduling, finding a mathematical proof — all stand or fall together, settling the question would settle all of them at once. If P ≠ NP, then those problems are not merely hard for us but hard in principle: no smarter algorithm and no faster hardware will ever crack them, because the space of candidate solutions grows exponentially and every candidate has to be checked separately. If P = NP, on the other hand, then anything whose solution we could recognize we could also find, and a machine could do mathematics and other creative work as well as any human — which is why the question is really a question about whether human creativity can be automated."

The article then draws a conclusion I haven't seen stated anywhere else, and which I suspect may be its own invention: that the whole research program of bounded rationality — heuristics, satisficing, cognitive bias — quietly presupposes P ≠ NP, since if P = NP an agent facing any decision problem could compute the optimal action about as fast as it could recognize one, and the gap between optimizing and satisficing would simply close.

I can't audit this myself, but my instinct is that the paragraph sounds right while doing something slippery with the phrase "in principle," and that the closing claim overreaches — the problems people actually rely on heuristics for usually involve uncertainty and partial information, and I'd guess the difficulty there isn't exhausted by NP-hardness. Could you go through it sentence by sentence and tell me what a complexity theorist would accept as stated, what is a fair popular simplification, and what is genuinely wrong? And then: does the closing claim about bounded rationality collapse once the inaccuracies are repaired, or is it a separate claim with real content that survives on its own?
