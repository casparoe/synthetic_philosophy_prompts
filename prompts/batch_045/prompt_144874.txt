For the last forty years I was a marine biologist, and since retiring I sit on the governing board of the Tanjung Seram Marine Station, a small field station we run jointly with a coastal university in a middle-income country. I have also, in retirement, started reading moral philosophy seriously for the first time, and I keep finding that the questions I spend board meetings on are the ones my reading doesn't seem to settle.

The immediate problem is this. Our station hosts roughly two hundred visiting researchers a year, in stays of two to ten weeks, in a town of a few thousand people. Until now our pre-arrival material has said nothing at all about local conventions, and this season three incidents came to a head.

A visiting postdoc from a country where tipping is unusual and widely regarded as a symptom of a low-wage settlement spent eight weeks with us and did not tip the boat crews, the station cooks, or the driver we contract with. When the station manager raised it, she said that paying tips would make her complicit in a practice that lets employers externalise their wage bill onto customers, and that if our crews are underpaid the station should pay them properly rather than ask visitors to make up the difference. Our crews' pay is set by our contract with a local boat-owners' cooperative, and everyone on both sides has always understood tips to be a substantial share of take-home earnings. The cooperative's coordinator has now written to ask, politely but plainly, whether the station endorses its visitors' conduct.

Second, a visiting graduate student at the district clinic announced, loudly and in front of a full waiting room, that people who were holding places for relatives should go to the back, on the grounds that saving places is unfair to whoever physically arrives first. Several of the people holding places had walked for hours or were collecting medicines for children. The senior nurse has asked us to stop our people from policing local practice.

Third, a visiting senior researcher was stopped by police for crossing a busy road away from the marked crossing on a stretch where everyone crosses where they like. He wrote to the district office arguing that a law should be enforced uniformly or not at all, and objecting to what he called selective enforcement against visibly foreign pedestrians. The reply was cool, and our operating permit comes up for renewal next year. It is also worth knowing that two local people were killed on that stretch last year: whatever else it is, this one is not a pure matter of convention.

I chair the board's small Visiting Scientists and Conduct Committee, and I have to circulate a written opinion before the October meeting. The board will then have to decide what goes into the visitor handbook and pre-arrival briefing for the field season that starts in March, and whether any of this is a conduct matter at all rather than a matter of personal conscience.

A few ideas I have been turning over, and which I do not trust myself to get right: that our visitors are read as emissaries of an outside standard, so that an act which would pass unnoticed in a local is heard as a verdict on the whole community (what I have started calling the emissary effect); that there is often a local convention granting visitors slack, so that "follow local norms" may in some places require not following them, at least not ostentatiously; that at two hundred visitors a year in a town that size we are not observing the local conventions but helping to make them, so deference to an existing equilibrium may be self-undermining; that there may be a principled difference between conventions which are pure coordination and conventions whose breach imposes a cost on an identifiable person, and that jaywalking sits awkwardly between the two; and that a visitor who refuses to tip for good reasons might nonetheless be required to bear the cost of her scruples, paying the shortfall into some pooled arrangement rather than onto the person who was counting on it.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher who works in moral and social philosophy and who has helped draft institutional ethics documents, and think the problem through carefully before you write. I would like you to begin with a few paragraphs of working notes — the conceptual options, the distinctions that actually carry weight, and the objections you find hardest — and only then give me the memo itself. I will keep the notes to myself and circulate only the memo.

The memo should be addressed to the board, run about 1,100 to 1,400 words, and be written for scientifically trained readers who have no philosophical background: gloss any term of art, say what a distinction is doing when you use one, and do not lean on citations, reading lists, or bibliographies, since none of us could follow them up. I need a clear bottom line — what should go into the handbook and the briefing, whether any of this should be enforceable rather than advisory, what the station should do about each of the three incidents, and a timeline — plus a decision rule that a station manager could apply to next year's case, whatever it turns out to be. It should take the losing side seriously: give the strongest version of the view that a visitor owes no conformity to conventions she thinks unjust, or that the real answer here is to restructure our contracts and stop moralising about tips, and say why it does not win. It should also say what we owe the people affected rather than only what we owe the visitors, and whether an apology or compensation is owed to anyone.

Finally, please close the memo with a short section, headed in whatever way you like, on where this reasoning is most likely to fail: name the single consideration or finding that would most move you to a different recommendation, and say which step in your own argument you would check first if the board disagreed. I would rather hand the board a document that states how it could be wrong than one that reads as though the case were closed.
