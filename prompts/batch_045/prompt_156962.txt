I'm in a small reading group — half ML-theory people, half philosophy — and we got into an arguement I can't settle. My friend's line is that the no-free-lunch theorems are just Hume's problem of induction written in summation notation: they say you can't get something for nothing, you have to bring assumptions about the structure of the world to the table, and nothing a priori tells you which assumptions to bring, so the theorems add nothing a careful Humean didn't already know and are basically decorative. I half believe that, but I also think there might be something sharper in them than "you need priors," namely the conservation-law reading: that whatever you gain on the cases you'll actually meet is exactly paid for on cases you won't, so it's a quantitative identity rather than a sceptical shrug. I want to test which of us is closer on something concrete instead of going in circles, and I definately don't want another tour of the formal statement.

So: take the 1997 Kasparov–Deep Blue rematch and analyze it through the lens of the no-free-lunch theorems together with the problem of induction. Facts so you don't need to look anything up (exact figures don't matter to me, ballpark is fine): Kasparov beat the machine 4–2 in the 1996 match and lost the 1997 rematch 3½–2½ after the machine had been substantially upgraded; it searched somewhere in the tens-to-hundreds of millions of positions per second while Kasparov examined orders of magnitude fewer, but his evaluation of them was far better targeted; the evaluation function was hand-built with heavy grandmaster input rather than learned from data; and the final game collapsed quickly. There were also allegations that Deep Blue was retuned between games on the basis of games Kasparov had just played, and that humans were in the loop somewhere — I am not asking you to adjudicate any of that, I'm interested in it only as a structural possibility: what does it do to the analysis that one side's inductive bias could be revised overnight against fresh evidence while the other side's couldn't, and while the other side couldn't see the logs?

What I want is the mapping done carefully and pedantically. What plays the role of the algorithm, of the problem, of the ensemble of problems, and of the evidence, given that chess is one fixed game rather than a distribution over games — does that make the theorems vacuous here, and if so what would a non-vacuous result about a single fixed target even have to look like? Where does the framework predict or explain something I couldn't have got without it: e.g. about the tradeoff between raw search depth and the shape of the evaluation function, or about why the thing that worked was search plus a bias encoding specifically human chess knowledge while Kasparov's own bias was fitted to human play and then met something fitted to the same corpus but deployed differently? And be honest about where it's silent — I assume it has nothing useful to say about minimax and adversarial search, and nothing about the fact that one of the two things being induced about was watching the other. That last part is what I keep coming back to: the failure mode here doesn't feel like "the future failed to resemble the past," it feels like "the target read my extrapolation and took the branch I ranked second." So: is there any way to get either the NFL framework or the ordinary Humean setup to say something about a target that adapts to your predictions — does the uniform-over-problems framing, or the off-training-set version, have the vocabulary for it, or do you have to move to something like a game against an agent that best-responds, and does the theorem then just evaporate? Then run it back the other way: does the episode confirm the theorems, is it an anomaly for them, or is it a case they were never built for — and does any of this give me ammunition against my friend's "just Hume with subscripts" line, or does it hand the argument to him? Skip the citations and the paper titles, I've read the standard material and I want the case analysis, not the bibliography.
