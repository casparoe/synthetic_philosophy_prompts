I am a retired earth-system modeller — four decades spent building and running global circulation models of the atmosphere and ocean — and since retiring I have been reading my way into philosophy of mind and artificial intelligence. I keep encountering the frame problem described as one of the deep problems in the field, and I cannot tell whether that reputation is earned. I would like you to settle the question for me by working carefully through one concrete historical episode rather than by surveying a literature. Please give me no references, reading lists, or citations: I have no way to chase them, and I do not want a map of who said what. I want the reasoning itself, and I want any term of art you use defined as you go.

The episode. In the mid-1980s Steve Hanks and Drew McDermott, then at Yale, published a small scenario now known as the Yale shooting problem; the best-known write-up is their 1987 paper in *Artificial Intelligence* on nonmonotonic logic and temporal projection, and the scenario itself first appeared in a 1985 paper that later received a classic-paper award. Since I cannot assume you have the details to hand, here they are: Fred, later identified as a turkey, is alive at the start; the gun is unloaded; at one time point the gun is loaded, at a later one nothing happens, and at a later one still the gun is fired at Fred. Intuitively Fred dies. The formal systems then on offer, however, did not entail this. A direct formalization, which states the initial condition of each time-varying property and then states only the effects of each action, is equally consistent with Fred dying and with Fred surviving; and the standard remedy of the period — stipulate that as little changes as possible, and that changes occur as late as possible — admits a model in which the gun mysteriously becomes unloaded during the idle interval and Fred survives, a model that is no less "minimal" than the intended one.

What I want from you is an analysis of that episode through the lens of the frame problem, run in both directions.

First, the mapping. Lay out the case in the vocabulary of the problem: which quantities vary, which are supposed to persist, what the events are, and what has to be assumed for the intended conclusion to follow. Then locate the failure precisely. Tell me whether it lies in the policy of minimizing change, in the assumption that an action's effects can be listed independently of a general rule for updating the state, or in something about the logical language itself — and show me why the mysterious-unloading model is not excluded by the apparatus, rather than merely asserting that it is not.

Second, and this is the crux: tell me what the episode shows about the frame problem as a philosophical thesis. Distinguish, at least, three readings — a defect of one family of formalisms; a general problem about the computational tractability of working out what is relevant; and an epistemological problem about which of an agent's beliefs must be revised when it acts. Technical solutions to the formal version were found within a few years, by way of successor-state axioms in the situation calculus and by Sandewall's device of occlusion, or permission to change. Say whether those solutions close the second and third readings as well, or only the first. I ask because philosophical writers — Fodor most prominently — recruited the frame problem to argue that "central" cognition is isotropic and Quinean and therefore not computationally tractable in the way modular input systems are. If the formal problem was solved, is that argument resting on a difficulty that turned out to be parochial, and is the inference from the parochial difficulty to the philosophical conclusion fair, or is it a bait-and-switch?

Third, the challenge from my own trade. In a circulation model nobody writes frame axioms. We carry a finite-dimensional state vector forward in time with discretized equations; what is held fixed is baked into the discretization and, above all, into the parameterizations for unresolved processes — convection, cloud microphysics, boundary-layer turbulence, radiative transfer. Relevance is not inferred, it is simulated. Does the evident success of that strategy show that the frame problem is an artifact of trying to do temporal projection by inference from action descriptions, and hence a problem about a style of representation rather than about the world or about minds? Or does the problem merely relocate: the closure assumptions inside a parameterization are tuned against present-day observations, and they can fail in a climate the model has never seen, so that the model exhibits a failure of just the shape the frame problem describes — a background assumption that nothing flagged as needing revision, discovered only by running the model out of sample? Tell me which of these is right, or how they should be weighed, and be explicit about the disanalogy as well as the analogy: a circulation model is not an agent, and its state vector is not a set of beliefs.

Fourth, say plainly where the frame problem is silent about this episode — in particular whether it tells us anything at all about how a real cognitive system settles what is relevant — and name the extra assumptions you would have to import to make it speak.

Finally, on your own verdict: mark your confidence briefly on each load-bearing claim; identify the single step in your argument you think most likely to fail; and tell me what would change your mind — what architecture, theorem, or empirical result would convince you that relevance is computable in general, or conversely that no physically realized system can do what the frame problem, on your reading, demands of it.

About 1,200 to 1,500 words, in measured prose, with notation only where it earns its place. You need not quote anyone; if a slogan helps, put it in your own words.
