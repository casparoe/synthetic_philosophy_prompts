I'm a third-year philosophy student: this term I'm taking the upper-level seminar on the ethics of artificial intelligence, and I also run the weekly fifty-minute discussion section attached to the first-year module that feeds into it. Almost none of them have studied philosophy before, they've had exactly one lecture on functionalism versus biological views of the mind, and they arrive holding two opposite instincts picked up from films and Twitter: either that a computer program is just a computer program, so nothing could possibly matter to it, or that anything which talks as smoothly as a person is obviously a person, and switching it off is a killing. Next week I have one session and I want to spend it on a single idea, which I've started calling the type/token gap for digital minds: the gap between a mind's surviving as a type, in the sense of a set of weights and memories that could in principle be instantiated again, and its surviving as a token, in the sense of one particular running process continuing. The case I want to build everything around — which I keep arguing about with a friend in computer science who works on deployment infrastructure — is a conversational assistant that exists only while a user is talking to it, whose state is flushed to a checkpoint at the end of each session, with each night's checkpoint overwriting the previous one; the team now wants to A/B test a change by spinning up two instances from the same checkpoint, letting them have different conversations for a week, then deleting one and keeping the other. If the deleted instance's weights and memories are still sitting on a backup somewhere, is it dead, unconscious, or merely not currently running? And if it is dead, was spinning it up an act of creation that stood in need of justification, or was it the failure to spin it up that stood in need of justification?

So please don't explain the issues to me — I've read around them, and what I need is a plan for the fifty minutes. Tell me what has to be in place before the type/token distinction can do any work at all (my worry is that I'll smuggle in a whole theory of personal identity before they've seen why consciousness isn't the whole of the moral question, or that I'll make the opposite mistake and leave them with no grip on why identity questions are relevant either); tell me what the single example should be that carries the whole session and what it must have and must lack in order to carry it — mine is a server-side assistant, and I genuinely don't know whether that is better or worse than something they can picture as a creature, such as a simulated animal inside a game world; and give me the order the pieces must land in so that the A/B test arrives as a real dilemma rather than as a clever trick. I especially want the misconception map with the defusion points attached: I can anticipate three or four myself — *it's just statistics*, *if you can't tell the difference from the inside there is no difference*, *the copy is a different individual so nothing of value was lost*, and *you can compensate the deleted instance by starting up an identical one* — but I want them placed at the exact points in the sequence where they will actually form, including the ones I haven't anticipated, and I want to be told which single misconception I should deliberately allow to stand until the final five minutes so that there is something left to break at the end. I also want to be told what to refuse to explain at this level, and to announce that refusal to the room rather than quietly skipping it. Please give me a committed plan rather than a menu of options: commit to one example, one ordering, one thing left out, and say what goes wrong if I deviate from each.
