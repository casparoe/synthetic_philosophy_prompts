I have been working through a three-way classification that gets used in cultural sociology and cultural psychology: honor cultures, in which standing is a possession that can be taken and must be defended by self-help because an unpunished slight invites further predation; dignity cultures, in which each person's worth is treated as intrinsic rather than competed for, disputes are handed over to a neutral authority, and retaliation marks the retaliator rather than repairing the retaliated; and victimhood cultures, in which moral status is claimed by displaying injury and appealing to an audience or an administrator. Proponents present these as internally coherent equilibria rather than as rungs on a ladder of moral progress, which leaves open — and in my reading mostly avoids — the question of whether any one of them is morally superior to the others.

Rather than argue that question directly, I want to approach it through an institution. Consider the American no-duty-to-retreat rule. Florida's self-defense statute says that a person justified in using deadly force does not have a duty to retreat and has the right to stand his or her ground if the person using or threatening to use the deadly force is not engaged in a criminal activity and is in a place where he or she has a right to be. Similar rules now obtain in a large share of U.S. jurisdictions; elsewhere a failure to retreat is at least treated as evidence that the force used was not really necessary. The rule seems to sit badly with the rest of a legal order that treats self-help as the disease rather than the cure: the same system criminalizes retaliation, private punishment, and the settling of scores, insists that grievances be routed through courts and police, and in negligence asks only whether conduct was reasonable rather than whether it was honorable. It also does not do the work one would expect of honor logic, since it still asks whether the defender's belief was reasonable — the individual is not made judge of his own standing.

Here is the conjecture I want tested. All three cultures, on my reading, differ less in how much they care about the person's worth than in which third party an appeal is addressed to: honor appeals to a peer public that must be shown the slight, dignity appeals to a neutral state, victimhood appeals to an administrative audience that allocates sympathy and remedy. If that is right, then a rule authorizing deadly force with no retreat and no third party at all is not a survival of honor culture but a gap in the scheme, and the interesting question is what occupies it.

So: what does the no-duty-to-retreat rule presuppose — about the person threatened, about what submission to a threat costs him, and about what the law is for — such that it makes sense? Distinguish what the rule strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably, and say whether an honor rationale (standing is a real interest the state must protect), a dignity rationale (retreat is itself dangerous, and the state cannot reliably reconstruct who started the encounter, so the innocent party should not bear the cost of the aggressor's choice), and a victimhood rationale (the threatened person is owed protection afterward, not permission now) can each generate it, or whether only some of them can. Say what the reasonableness condition does to the honor reading, and whether the rule is better understood as an honor-culture fossil, as something a dignity culture needs for its own reasons, or as a fourth thing the three-culture scheme has no name for. Finally, tell me whether an audit of this kind can do any work in ranking the three cultures morally, or whether it only shows that the scheme was never in the business of ranking them.

Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion.
