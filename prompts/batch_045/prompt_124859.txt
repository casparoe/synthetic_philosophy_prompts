I'm in a graduate seminar on self-knowledge this quarter and I need to hand in a two-page proposal next week for the term paper — about 5,000 to 6,000 words, due at the end of a ten-week quarter with no option to extend — which we'll then workshop with the professor. I'd like the proposal to be on the anti-luminosity argument and on what that argument is supposed to show about the KK principle. We spent Tuesday's session on Williamson's Knowledge and Its Limits, and I came away convinced that the argument works but unclear about what exactly it is an argument against, so I'd rather write on that than on one of the standard replies.

Here is where I've got to in my notes. Call a condition luminous if, in every case in which it obtains, the subject is in a position to know that it obtains; suppose for reductio that some non-trivial condition is luminous; run a sorites series from a clear case where it obtains to a clear case where it does not, with neighbouring cases indiscriminable to the subject; luminosity says she can know the condition obtains in the last case where it does; but a margin-for-error requirement on knowledge says you cannot know something by a method that would return the same verdict in a nearby case where it is false; so she cannot know it there, contradicting luminosity. Then the connection to KK: if knowing that p were a luminous condition, the KK principle would follow, so the anti-luminosity result is meant to undercut KK. My worry is that the argument seems to lean on two things the KK debate does not obviously have to grant. First, "is in a position to know" looks like it is doing two different jobs: in the definition of luminosity it reads like a static, evidential matter — the subject's evidence settles the question — while the margin-for-error premise only bites if it is a dynamic matter — the subject exercising some method that could equally have returned the same verdict in a nearby case — and I cannot see that the static reading supports the step to "cannot know." Relatedly, Williamson identifies a subject's evidence with what she knows, and I cannot work out whether that strengthens the argument, because the subject's evidence would then differ across the chain and the method is not really held fixed, or whether it makes it circular. Second, the argument only gets going for conditions that admit the relevant gradual structure, and I want to know whether that really is every non-trivial condition or only those with a certain kind of boundary; I have been writing "graded" versus "switch" conditions in my notes, but I am not attached to those labels at all.
