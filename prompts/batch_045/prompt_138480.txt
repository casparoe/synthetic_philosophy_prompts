hey — i sit on my city's parks, environment and animal welfare advisory subcommittee (six of us; council rubber-stamps whatever we vote, so the decision is really ours) and I have to circulate a written opinion before the meeting next Tuesday. Can you draft it?

The situation: we have one pot of money for the year, about $180k, for what our enabling bylaw calls "animal welfare within the city" — the bylaw doesn't say which animals, or whether animals that don't exist yet count, and I've decided that ambiguity is where this whole thing actually lives. Right now about $40k of the city's pigeon budget goes to a contractor who lethally traps feral pigeons in the downtown core twice a year, and has for years. Animal people have been picketing us about it. Four options are on the table:

- keep the trapping contract as is (it renews automatically on 1 July unless we vote to let it lapse, so timing matters)
- switch to contraceptive bait at the regular feeding sites and let the trapping lapse
- spend the whole pot retrofitting glass and ledges on three city buildings to stop window collisions, which mostly kills native migratory songbirds — a few hundred a year by the birding group's own count — and does nothing at all for pigeons
- give $45k to a volunteer pigeon rescue for a part-time vet tech and a loft, and keep trapping with the rest

Dana, another member, says option three is straight speciesism: we'd be spending to save pretty native birds while the downtown pigeons go on being trapped, and those pigeons are the ones we actually have a history with. Her version of that: rock doves came over with settlers, we built the ledges that stand in for cliffs, weve been feeding them for two centuries, so if any animal has a claim on this city it's these ones, not the warblers that were here first and never asked us for anything. She also points out that pigeons are the animals everyone feels licensed to be cruel to, and that this is exactly why our bylaw language is dangerous.

The bit I can't get straight myself and want the memo to lay out rather than paper over: contraceptive bait doesn't make any existing pigeon better off. It stops pigeons from coming into existence. So on one way of thinking — the point is that whoever exists should have a decent life — it doesn't help a single bird, and we'd be spending the whole pot on a benefit to nobody, while the rescue option does help identifiable birds. On another way — what matters is how much suffering there is, and a pigeon that never exists isn't being deprived of anything — it's the only option that reduces suffering at scale, and the rescue is feel-good trivia by comparison. The memo has to pick one reading, say out loud that it's picking it, and say why, because the bylaw won't do it for us.

Also: at the last meeting someone said fertility control is "making a population disappear" and used a word I won't repeat. I don't want that waved away and I don't want it indulged. There's something real in the thought that you can harm a group without harming any particular member of it, and I want two or three sentences on what the memo thinks about that.

What I want from you: a memo addressed to five people who aren't philosophers, that goes into the public record and the minutes, so no seminar vocabulary, but that doesn't dodge anything either. No citations or suggested reading please — I can't check anything online right now and the committee would ignore them anyway. Roughly 1000–1100 words, in these sections and this order:

Bottom line (~120 words) — one paragraph: the recommendation, what happens to the trapping contract, and the date it bites.
What the philosophy actually turns on (~250 words) — the population-level versus individual-level question above, explained so a non-philosopher gets it, then which way the memo takes it and why.
Why this and not the other three (~300 words).
Dana's speciesism charge at its strongest (~200 words) — she should read it and feel taken seriously, including the "they only live here because of us" part; then either why it still doesn't carry the decision, or, if you think it does carry it, say so and change the bottom line to match.
The things I can't answer (~150 words) — include the group-harm worry above and the hydra worry from our listserv, which is that making downtown better for pigeons just means more pigeons and more suffering in five years.
What would make me change my mind at the twelve-month re-vote (~150 words).
