I've been reading about Kelly betting and geometric rationality for years -- the sequences, the ergodicity posts, the usual arguments -- and I thought I had the practical upshot internalized: size bets to maximize expected log wealth, never risk ruin, shrink when your probability estimates are shaky. Then I tried to apply it to a real decision, got a number I can't take seriously, and on working backwards through the reasoning I can't locate the step where I should get off. Here is the argument as I now have it.

(1) The normative core: for a repeatable favorable gamble, choose the stake that maximizes the expected growth rate, i.e. maximize E[log W] over the fraction f of wealth risked. The case for this is the dominance result -- the Kelly bettor's wealth exceeds any other fixed-fraction bettor's by a factor that grows without bound, almost surely -- plus the ergodicity point that the ensemble average E[W] is not what a single agent experiences over time. I accept this, and I'd like to keep it.

(2) Myopia: because logs add across independent rounds, the rule is myopic. Today's stake depends only on today's wealth and today's odds; the number and quality of your remaining opportunities don't enter.

(3) Composition: the W in E[log W] has to be everything fungible that is actually at stake. Consumption is what ultimately matters, and future labor income buys consumption as surely as savings do, so W is your whole balance sheet: liquid capital plus the present value of future earnings. Reading W as "what is in my brokerage account" would be like reading it as "what is in my left pocket."

(4) Now the numbers. Take a 28-year-old with $40k invested and, say, $2M in discounted after-tax future earnings, so W is about $2.04M. (a) For a broad equity index at 7% real expected excess return and 15% annualized volatility, the continuous-approximation Kelly fraction is (mu)/(sigma^2), about 3.1 -- an exposure near $6.3M, roughly 160x the liquid capital, financed by borrowing against wages that cannot in fact be pledged. (b) For a one-shot angel ticket with a 60% chance of 10x on the amount staked and a 40% chance of zero, f* = (pb - q)/b with b = 9, so (0.6 x 9 - 0.4)/9 = 5/9, about 0.56 -- a stake of roughly $1.1M, also borrowed against future paychecks.

(5) Both verdicts are absurd, and not only because the leverage is unobtainable. Human capital is neither collateralizable nor rebalanceable, job loss correlates with the very crashes that would trigger a margin call, and the guarantee in (1) is asymptotic over repeated similar bets while a career contains very few of any given decision. Worse, the framework turns on itself: for bets of this shape the growth-rate curve falls faster on the overbetting side than on the underbetting side, roughly twice the Kelly fraction is where expected growth goes to zero, and my confidence in the 60% in (4b) cannot distinguish f* = 0.56 from f* = 1.12. So the rule's own error analysis tells me to distrust the number the rule just produced.

(6) Therefore (1), (2) and (3) cannot all hold as I have applied them. I want to keep (1) and (2) -- the first is close to a theorem and the second is algebra -- so (3) is the premise to drop, and W should be the pool I can actually and repeatedly re-stake, not my lifetime balance sheet.

The trouble is that dropping (3) doesn't settle anything, for three reasons.

First, on the narrow reading the recommendation is hostage to a boundary that nothing in the theory selects and that institutions move around: whether debts are dischargeable, whether a broker will margin you, whether next month's paycheck counts as capital. Cross a jurisdiction and your optimal stake changes. And for someone whose wealth is entirely non-tradable -- a student with no savings and only future earnings -- the narrow reading says stake nothing on anything, which leaves geometric rationality silent about career, education, migration, and whether to have children, i.e. about exactly the decisions where "don't risk ruin" seemed to matter most.

Second, on the broad reading I get (4).

Third, and this is the part I can't place: even the narrow reading licenses near-total exposure whenever the loss probability times the odds is small. A 99.9% chance of 1000x gives f* of about 0.999 -- stake 99.9% of the pool and retain 0.1%. That is not ruin in the logarithm's books, but it is ruin in every sense I care about; and given essentially any realistic floor above about 0.1% of wealth, the floor binds and the rule says to stake everything down to it. So the line between reckless and optimal is drawn by how far down the floor happens to sit, which the theory treats as a detail.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?

Is the culprit (3), and if so what is the principled account of what belongs in W? If the answer is "the capital you can repeatedly re-bet," does that make geometric rationality a theory of bankrolls rather than a theory of lives?

Or is the flaw earlier -- a scope shift from a claim about rules (the Kelly rule dominates other fixed-fraction rules asymptotically, almost surely, over repeated similar bets) to a claim about acts (maximize E[log W] at each decision)? If that is where it breaks, what exactly does the asymptotic theorem license on an ordinary Tuesday, and does it license anything at all for a decision I will make once?

Or is the flaw in (2): does the arrival of future income, the option to stop, or the fact that real opportunities are not independent break the myopia that lets a long-run rule be applied case by case?

And what should I make of the self-undermining point in (5)? If the rule's own sensitivity analysis says to shrink away from the rule's own output, is fractional Kelly a repair or an admission that maximizing E[log W] was never the right target?

I am least attached to (3) and most attached to (1). Please don't simply substitute a different utility function without saying what selects it, and don't tell me the numbers in (4) are unrealistic -- I know they are; that is the point. What I want is the diagnosis, the price of each alternative, and a straight answer to the last question: after the repair, is there any version of geometric rationality that has real bite for the large one-shot decisions of a life, or is its domain of competence limited to casinos, portfolios, and other places where the same multiplicative bet genuinely repeats?
