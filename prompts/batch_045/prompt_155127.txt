I'm giving a single two-hour evening seminar to about a dozen working software engineers: fluent in code and comfortable with Bayes' rule, but with no computability theory and no information theory. The arc has to run from Bayesian updating to Solomonoff induction and end with what Occam's razor can and cannot be made to mean. Please don't write the lecture; plan it.

Three things I want decided. First, the order in which the components have to arrive and what each depends on: in particular where the self-delimiting (prefix-free) coding point must go relative to the definition of Kolmogorov complexity, and why deferring it produces a specific, predictable failure; and where to place the point that the razor falls out of the prior rather than being fed into it as an assumption.

Second, the one worked example that carries the whole arc. My instinct is continuation of a short binary sequence, but tell me what the example must have and must lack: should it be long enough that the shortest description isn't brute-force enumeration, and should it visibly look patterned?

Third, the misconceptions this audience in particular will form — I'm expecting "simpler hypotheses are more likely to be true", "the shortest program is the explanation", and "so just use gzip" — and, for each, the point in the sequence where it should be pre-empted rather than corrected after the fact.

Finally, a check. At the end I want to put in front of them a vendor's claim that a compression-based predictor is "optimal in the Solomonoff sense", and ask one question that separates people who have grasped uncomputability and reference-machine-dependence-up-to-a-constant from people who took away "shorter is better". And tell me what I should openly decline to cover in two hours.
