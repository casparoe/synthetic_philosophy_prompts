Hey — I'm stuck on this university committee on historical accountability (I got drafted because I was the only one in the philosophy dept who didn't have an excuse ready), and we keep getting asked to issue apologies for things the institution did decades ago. We meet once a month, we get about six weeks per case, there's no budget for archival work beyond whatever packet the provost's office hands us, and anything we draft has to clear general counsel — no wording that reads like an admission of legal liability — before the president signs it. So the product is basically one page, under 400 words, with no hedging so heavy that it stops being an apology at all. I teach a course on collective responsibility, so I'm not coming at this cold: I know the usual moves about standing, about the gap between being sorry that something happened and apologizing for it, about who if anyone can recieve on behalf of the dead, about how an apology can quietly become a way of closing the subject rather than opening it. What I need from you is something we can actually run: a decision procedure with the questions in order, what would count as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure just stops with a no, and where it hands the decision back to us because it's a values call and not a factual one.

The distinctions I've been scribbling in my notes and can't get straight — use them if they're useful, and tell me plainly if they're a mess: failures of standing (nobody left who can speak for the wrongdoer, or the "same" institution has changed so much over the years that it's arguably a different entity by now); failures of uptake (no one left who can receive the thing, or the descendants are split, some wanting it and some saying the whole exercise is theatre — a friend in the history dept thinks that second part is always true); and what I've started calling failures of fit, where the apology re-enacts the wrong it's apologizing for, like a paternalistic wrong apologized for in a paternalistic voice. And then the case that actually keeps me up: the current university is clearly an heir — it still holds the buildings, the endowment income, the prestige — but nobody alive did any of it, so its a beneficiary and not an agent. Does that matter, and in which direction? Related: when an apology with no material change attached is worse than saying nothing, I have a view but the committee is split down the middle, so flag that as one of the places where we just have to decide.

Can you also run the procedure on three cases so I can watch it work, and be honest where it gives me an answer I won't like: (1) the med school's teaching collection still contains tissue samples taken in the 1950s from patients in a segregated ward, without consent, and the descendants' association wants an apology and the samples back; (2) there's a demand to apologize for and rename an endowed chair funded by a man who made his money in a trade that was perfectly legal at the time but that the university now officially condemns — and his family is threatening to pull the money if we do anything; (3) a small self-appointed group of descendants is demanding we apologize for something quite specific, while a much larger and more representative descendant organization says don't, that the framing is wrong and an apology would do more harm than good. At the end, tell me where the procedure will misfire and what it simply can't tell us — e.g. whether anyone will actually believe the damn thing once we publish it.
