My wife and I are expecting our second child in the spring, which is what got me reading the pronatalist literature in the first place, and I also sit on the grants committee of a small local foundation, which is where the question stopped being academic for me. A friend who is unsympathetic to pronatalism sent me an argument he calls the escalator, and I have not been able to find the loose joint. He sets it out like this.

(1) Mere addition. If the welfare of everyone already in a population is left unchanged, then adding one more person whose life is worth living cannot make the outcome worse. He glosses worth living in the ordinary sense: the life contains more that is good for the person living it than bad.

(2) And if the added life is comfortably worth living, adding it makes the outcome better. This is the only premise I actually need for my own purposes.

(3) Continuity. There is no lowest level of positive welfare: for any life worth living, there is a worse life that is still worth living. So there are lives that are only barely worth living.

(4) Levelling. Take two outcomes containing exactly the same people with exactly the same total welfare, one distributed very unequally and one distributed equally, with the badly-off gaining and the well-off losing. The more equal outcome is better: equality has some weight of its own, and improving the position of the worse-off counts even when the better-off lose by the same total amount.

(5) Transitivity. Betterness is transitive. Moreover, a chain of steps each of which is no worse than the one before yields an end that is no worse than the beginning, and better than the beginning if any link in the chain is strictly better.

(6) No ceiling. For any finite number, there could be a population of that size, all of them at some fixed positive level of welfare.

(7) The derivation. Fix any population A: n people, all at a very high level H. By (3) pick a level e that is barely worth living; by (6) let m be enormous. Let A+ be A together with m further people at level e. Adding them one at a time, each addition leaves everyone else unchanged, so by (1) and (5) A+ is no worse than A. Now let Z be those same n + m people with A+'s total welfare shared out equally, so that everyone is at level (nH + me)/(n + m); for large enough m this is as close to e as you like. By (4), Z is better than A+. So by (5), Z is better than A.

(8) Hence: for any population, however excellent the lives in it, there is a vastly larger population of lives barely worth living that is better than it — and in that larger population nobody is better off than anyone in the smaller one, while nearly everyone in it is worse off than everyone in the smaller one. My friend says the conclusion has a name in the literature and that I should not be distracted by it.

(9) The part I care about. Nothing in (1) through (6) mentions total welfare, or averaging, or any particular theory. Each premise, he says, is something that anyone who thinks the existence of an additional worthwhile life can make an outcome better in even the smallest degree is already committed to. So the way out is not to give up one theory among others; it is to give up (2), and with it the axiological case for pronatalism altogether.

(10) Applied to my committee, the upshot is that there is nothing to deliberate about. Any finite improvement in the lives of children who already exist is outweighed by adding enough further worthwhile lives, so the programme that yields the largest number of additional lives per dollar wins, however thin those lives are — and wins by more, the thinner they are.

I cannot accept (8), and I cannot accept (10). But I also do not want to pay (9)'s price: I would like to keep (2), and I would like to keep the thought that the committee's decision is a genuine one. My friend adds that every exit has a mirror-image absurdity — block the escalator with a threshold on how low a worthwhile life can be, or on how much sheer number can compensate for quality, and you get the reverse result, that two ecstatic people are a better outcome than any number of merely decent lives — and that this symmetry is the real lesson.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would be grateful if you could also address four things specifically. (a) Does the argument really need (4), or is there a shorter route to (8) that manages without it, and does that change which premise I should give up? (b) Is (5) doing more work than it appears? I have a vague sense that no worse than is being treated as at least as good, and that those can come apart. (c) Is keeping (2) while rejecting (8) genuinely available to me, or does it only look available until the details are filled in? (d) Is my friend's symmetry claim right — is the reverse absurdity really as bad as the original one, or is one horn cheaper than the other? Please price the alternatives rather than simply listing them, and tell me what I am committing myself to if I dig in on (2).
