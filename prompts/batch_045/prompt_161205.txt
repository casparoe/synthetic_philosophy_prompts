I'm a PhD student in history and philosophy of the life sciences, and I'm closing the last session of our graduate reading group on adaptationism. The discussion kept circling rather than settling, and I'd rather end with something that stages the circling than with a summary that pretends it resolved. So I'd like a poem.

Please write a villanelle — nineteen lines, two refrains, the usual rotation, both refrains returning in the closing quatrain — built on the Gould–Lewontin critique of the adaptationist programme. Let the form do the argumentative work: the two refrains should be the two voices that kept talking past each other all term.

- Refrain A1, in the adaptationist's register — brisk, instrumental, faintly impatient: a demand of the form *what is this for?*, short enough to say in one breath.
- Refrain A2, in the anatomist's or architect's register — flat, descriptive: an answer to the effect that some features are not for anything, that they are what is left over where two constraints meet.

Set the poem inside a building that has been converted to a new use — a church become a library, a factory become flats, a station built inside a ruin: your choice — and let the building carry the classification. Three kinds of feature should be visible in it: things built for what they now do; things built for one purpose and now doing another; and things that are purely residual, the awkward leftovers where a structure meets a constraint it did not choose. Keep the second and third apart: co-option is not the same as byproduct, and a poem that collapses them has missed the point.

What I want the closing quatrain to arrive at — and this is the bit my own notes keep returning to — is a fourth case: the leftover that later takes load. A feature that began as a byproduct and subsequently became indispensable, so that the demand in A1 now has a true answer that is nevertheless not the explanation of the feature's origin. The refrains meeting in the final stanza should show that they were never rivals: one asks about current adaptive value, the other about how the thing came to be, and a single feature can answer both.

Two accuracy constraints, since this will be read aloud to people who know the literature. First, the critique targets a *method* — atomizing the organism into independently optimizable parts and supplying a story where no alternative hypothesis is tested — not the claim that nothing is adaptive; don't let the poem imply that adaptationists deny byproducts, or that the critic holds traits are useless. Second, don't let the ending convert the critique into its own mirror-image Panglossianism, in which everything turns out to be a leftover. No naming of Gould or Lewontin, no glosses, and no use of *spandrel* as a badge: if the word appears it should earn its place as the thing itself.

Give me the villanelle first, without preamble; then a single section headed "Notes on the choices", roughly 300 words, on how the refrains map onto the two questions, which features in the building do which work, and where the load-bearing leftover enters.
