I am writing a survey chapter on the semantics and pragmatics of 'if' for a graduate handbook, and I want its spine to be a classificatory instrument rather than a catalogue of theories: a decision tree that takes a reader from answers they can give about concrete cases to the position they already occupy, without ever asking them to choose a label. I would be grateful if you would build one.

The angle I want is this. The chapter's organising question is whether the contrast between the indicative and the subjunctive conditional — 'if the growth is malignant' versus 'if the growth were malignant' — is a difference in content or only a difference in what the speaker is taking for granted. I do not want that question settled silently by the choice of theories on display; I want it settled at a node. So please construct the tree from the logical space rather than from the list of names: set out the diagnostic questions and the answer-combinations they permit, and only then locate the actual positions within that space, marking the combinations that no one occupies.

Constraints on the questions. Each node should be a question about a case or a claim, phrased so that a student with first-order logic and elementary probability can answer it without knowing any of the views; no node may ask the reader to name a principle, a thesis or a theorist, and no node may simply ask whether conditionals are truth-functional. Seven to nine branch questions, twelve to eighteen leaves.

The cases I want carried by the tree, in roughly this form:

1. The settling case. On Monday, with the test result not yet in, a clinician says 'If the growth is malignant, we operate on Friday.' On Wednesday it is found benign; on Friday she says 'If the growth had been malignant, we would have operated.' Does the Monday utterance have truth conditions that survive the settling of its antecedent, and if so are they those of the Friday subjunctive, those of the material conditional (now trivially true), or neither?
2. The antecedent you rule out. A detective is as certain as she can be that the butler spent the evening in Leeds, yet says without apparent incoherence 'If the butler was at the manor, someone let him in by the east door.' Is that utterance true, false, true but unassertable, or does it force a repair of what is being taken for granted? I want this node to separate selection restricted to a context set from selection over all possibilities.
3. Import and export. 'If I take the 9.05, then if the 9.05 is cancelled I shall drive' seems true: the ways the 9.05 gets cancelled are ways in which I hear in advance and take the car. 'If I take the 9.05 and the 9.05 is cancelled, I shall drive' seems false: in those worlds I am on the platform, having left the car at home. Does the account equate the two, and if it distinguishes them, does it still license the step from the first, together with 'I am taking the 9.05', to 'if the 9.05 is cancelled, I shall drive'?
4. Strengthening and sequence. 'If the cuttings had been watered they would have rooted; if they had been watered and the greenhouse had frozen they would not have rooted.' Does the account validate strengthening, and does the order in which the two sentences are produced matter to it — the reverse order seems to qualify or retract rather than to continue?
5. Chance under known chances. A source has a one-half chance of emitting in any given minute; you know it did not emit in the minute just past; you also know that had it emitted, the animal would have died with chance one-half, the mechanism misfiring half the time. What credence should you now have in 'if the source had emitted, the animal would have died', and what does your answer show about whether credence in a conditional is conditional credence?
6. Indefinite antecedents. 'If a patient carries the allele and is given the standard dose, she develops the rash' seems to be about every such patient, and not equivalent to a claim about some particular one. Does the account need quantification over cases with the indefinite bound by it, or can it reach the reading by scoping an existential?
7. Counterpossibles. 'If 17 had been divisible by 4, the checksum would have failed.' True, false, both, or neither, and on what in the account does that turn?
8. Backtracking. The 8.10 is late because a signal failed. 'If the signal had not failed, the 8.10 would have reached York on time' is fine; 'if the 8.10 had reached York on time, the signal would not have failed' is strained. Does the account's treatment of the second differ in kind from its treatment of the first, or only in degree, and can one ordering of possibilities deliver both verdicts without stipulation?

If you think any one of these is a poor diagnostic, say so in a line and replace it with a better one; at most two replacements.

The leaf set must include at least one member of each of the following families, with further leaves of your own where the space allows: selection over all possibilities; selection restricted to a context set, with and without a fallback for antecedents the context excludes; the restrictor view on which the if-clause restricts a modal base that mood and context supply; restricted strict-conditional views; material-implication-plus-assertability views; views on which conditionals have no truth conditions and are to be understood through the Ramsey test; expressivist views on which a conditional expresses planning or acceptance rather than belief; three-valued or conditional-event views; interventionist or structural-equation views; and treatments of counterpossibles that avoid vacuity. Each leaf should carry its usual name, or 'unnamed' if it has none, one sentence on the position's most awkward commitment, and half a line on which of my cases it handles worst.

Where two leaves cannot be separated by your questions, say so plainly instead of adding a question to manufacture a distinction, and say what would separate them. For each unoccupied combination of answers, give a one-line diagnosis: incoherent, merely unattractive, or unexplored.

Please commit. Fix the order of the questions and justify each placement in a clause; where a case is ambiguous between a semantic and a pragmatic reading, decide which you are treating it as and say which; where you have to stipulate something to make a question well defined — what counts as the same conditional across a change of context, for instance — state the stipulation rather than gesturing at it; and end with one sentence saying which leaf you would stand at yourself.

Write in British English, using single quotation marks for mentioned expressions. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations: named positions are welcome, references are not.
