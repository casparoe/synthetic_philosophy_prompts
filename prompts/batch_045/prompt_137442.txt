Im running a 90-minute session on practical rationality for about 18 undergrads, mixed crowd — some econ, some philosophy, a couple who freeze up the moment a formula shows up. Nobody there has heard of Kelly betting or the geometric-rationality/ergodicity framing, and I dont want them to hear those words until the debrief.

Design me a game they actually play, where they run into the fact that "take every bet with positive expected value" and "grow as fast as possible" come apart, and feel the pull of the second rather than getting told it. Things I'd like built in: (1) they commit once to a fixed fraction of their current bankroll, then just flip a biased coin or roll for ~20 rounds against a multiplier table so the arithmetic doesn't eat the session; (2) half the room plays as one person playing all 20 rounds in a row, the other half as a "pool" — each member plays one round per round and they split the total equally every time — so the two halves end up genuinely disagreeing about whether the bet was any good; (3) partway through I secretly change the bias without telling them, so they discover on their own that the fraction that was right for the first half is punishing in the second, and start shading down by themselves.

Then the debrief: map specific moments onto the shift from arithmetic to geometric averaging, onto why its growth rate rather than expected value that matters for the person playing sequentially, and onto why you'd bet less than the "optimal" fraction when you aren't sure of your edge. Include what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — my money is on someone betting 100%, someone betting 0%, someone announcing "thats just a utility function" in minute ten, the pooled group being so noisy that the comparison dies, and the bookkeeping grinding to a halt — and how to handle each. Finally, be honest about what the game cant show them: the places where the growth-rate argument quietly assumes multiplicativity, known probabilities, infinite divisibility, all wealth reinvested, and a horizon long enough for the asymptotics to bite — so that a one-shot bet, or a bet near the end of the sequence, collapses back toward expected value. Those bits I'll have to say out loud, and I want to know exactly what to say.
