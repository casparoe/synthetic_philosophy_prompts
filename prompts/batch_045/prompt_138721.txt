I work on electricity market design, did a couple of years at a climate think tank before that, so my reflex for anything that looks like an unpriced harm is basically: price it, set the price at marginal social damage, let the market find the efficient level, done.

In AI policy circles I keep running into the same move. Somebody says catastrophic AI risk is just like carbon: each lab emits a bit of risk into a shared atmosphere, no lab bears the full cost of what it emits, so it is a textbook global externality, and the fix is a tax or a liability rule that makes them eat the cost. The cost of the safety work itself, the slowdown, the compute you spend on tests instead of training, the thing people around here call the alignment tax, gets slotted in as the abatement cost you pay to reduce your emission. Then comes the payoff: we don't need a pause or a ban or any of that, we need the right price, and the efficient amount of risk isn't zero, same way the efficient amount of carbon isn't zero. And when you point out that whoever pays the tax loses the race to whoever doesn't, they say that's an enforcement problem, not a problem with the framing. I cant tell whether I'm being conned by a nice mapping.

So audit it properly. First the correspondence table: what plays the role of emissions, of the atmosphere, of the damage function, of assimilative capacity, of the discount rate, of the regulator, and who exactly the bystanders are, because I suspect it matters a lot whether the people getting harmed are also the people emitting. Then where it breaks, and which breaks are load bearing for that conclusion and which are cosmetic.

Things that bug me, tell me if they're real:

- With carbon the polluter mostly survives what it emits. Here the party creating the risk very often gets destroyed right along with everyone else, so the harm is partly internalized already, except the internalization arrives too late to change the competitive pressure that produced it, and it doesn't scale with how careful you were.
- Marginal damage. Setting a tax needs a damage curve you can estimate, and estimating one needs repeated observations, including partial ones. You get one shot at a civilizational outcome and no near misses to fit a slope to. And the curve probably isn't smooth anyway: below some capability level nothing happens, past it something happens once and there's no second data point. A tax with no slope to set it to isn't a tax.
- The externality people actually worry about day to day isn't firm versus bystander, it's firm versus firm: if I slow down and you don't, I lose the race and probably still die. That feels structurally closer to an arms race or a multi-player prisoner's dilemma than to pollution, and those have a different shape of fix.
- Sign flips. More capability is sometimes the risk-reducing move, better control methods, defensive or monitoring capacity, so the emission sometimes cools the planet. Does the externality framing survive an input whose sign depends on who else has it?

Does the efficient nonzero risk conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? And if carbon is the wrong picture, what's the closest one that fits: fire codes, nuclear nonproliferation, structural engineering safety factors, banking capital requirements, something else? I'd rather have a mapping that tells me which knobs exist than a definiteness I borrowed from a different problem.
