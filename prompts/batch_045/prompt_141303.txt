You are a philosopher of mind who is also fluent in the cognitive neuroscience of consciousness — the kind of person who has read both the conceptual literature on access versus phenomenal consciousness and the global neuronal workspace literature, and who can move between them without blurring them. I need you to be a careful, non-sycophantic interlocutor here: I am going to hand you an argument I half believe, and I do not want you to simply agree with me. Background and the argument I want audited. I am a first-year PhD student rotating in a lab that works on the neural correlates of conscious perception. The lab's default framing is the global workspace picture, and it is standardly introduced with Bernard Baars's theater metaphor: a stage, an attentional spotlight, actors standing for the contents of experience, an audience of unconscious specialized routines and knowledge sources, and backstage context operators — director, playwright, scene setters — that shape what gets lit without themselves being lit. At a journal club last week a senior postdoc used that picture to argue for a strong conclusion: that the theater metaphor, taken seriously, shows that phenomenal consciousness just is global availability. The argument ran roughly as follows. What makes a content conscious, on this picture, is not that it exists in some special intrinsic way but that it gets on the stage and is broadcast to everything else. There is no further inner fact. The feeling that there must be one comes from an illicit extra piece of the metaphor: we imagine an audience member who literally *watches* the stage, a little subject inside the head, and then we ask what it is like for *her*. But in the actual theory the audience is not a subject; it is lots of dumb specialized systems. Delete the homunculus — the thing Dennett-style critics call a Cartesian theater — and the residue that people call the hard problem is an artifact of the picture, not a discovery about consciousness. On this view the access/phenomenal distinction is either empty or at most a difference in how widely a content is available, and a completed workspace theory would be a complete theory of consciousness rather than a theory of one of its two allegedly distinct aspects.

I want an audit of how far the theater picture can carry that conclusion. My own suspicion is that the audience is doing all the work: the metaphor makes broadcast look like *being witnessed by someone*, and once you notice that the audience is not a subject you either lose the phenomenal entirely or you have to smuggle back an unexplained consumer who does the witnessing. But I also have the opposite worry, and I want you to press it: maybe that suspicion is itself a homuncular hangover, and a workspace theorist is entitled to say that being globally available is all there ever was to being conscious, with the appearance of a further fact arising from the subject's own later cognitive access to its earlier states.

Please structure your answer as follows.

1. A correspondence table. Give the mapping explicitly: stage, spotlight, the actors, the unlit wings, the audience, the backstage context operators, the interval or curtain, the theater walls, and the fact that there is one audience per performance. For each, say what it is supposed to be in the theory — in cognitive architecture terms and, where you can, in neural terms — and mark any element of the metaphor that has no home in the theory at all.

2. Three stress cases. Before you judge anything, run the picture against these; two are stipulations of mine, so treat them as hypothetical architectures rather than as findings to be cited.

(a) The dark broadcast. Suppose a system of the workspace kind in which ignition happens: a content is selected, sustained, and widely broadcast — but by stipulation every downstream consumer system is temporarily disconnected, or the broadcast simply has no recipients. Nothing is recruited, nothing downstream changes, and no report is possible, ever. Call this empty availability. Does this system have a globally available content? Does it have a conscious one? What does the theater picture say, and what does the theory's own criterion say, and are those answers the same?

(b) The mesh. Suppose instead a system with no workspace, no stage, and no bottleneck: a great many specialized processors, each able to read the outputs of all the others cheaply and in parallel, with no selection, no ignition, no serial stream, and no capacity limit of the familiar kind. Global availability here is total. Is there access consciousness in the mesh? Is there phenomenal consciousness? Is there *one* subject or something else? What verdict does the theater metaphor push toward, and is that verdict right?

(c) The partial-report case. A briefly flashed array is presented; subjects can name only a few items, yet a cue delivered just after the display lets them report a different subset, as if more had been there than they could name. This is the empirical crux the two sides argue about. Say what the theater picture's stage-and-spotlight structure disposes people to conclude here, which of the competing readings of the case the metaphor is silently favoring, and whether that inclination is evidence or merely framing.

3. Disanalogies that matter, and those that do not. Separate them. I am not interested in the observation that there is no little theater inside the skull; everyone concedes that. I want the disanalogies that bear on the specific conclusion — that global availability exhausts the phenomenal. Candidates worth considering, and you should add your own: the audience in a real theater is conscious and the consumption in the theory is not; a stage is a single place while availability is a relation; the spotlight needs an operator, and asking who runs it either regresses or stops arbitrarily; broadcast is to consumers while what-it-is-like-ness is not obviously to anyone; a play is made of discrete, bounded, re-identifiable elements while the stream may not be; the theater has walls, so the picture has no way to represent broadcast to systems not yet built; an audience member can watch without acting, which quietly licenses the idea of a pure phenomenal residue. For each, say whether it is fatal to the postdoc's conclusion, merely a demand for tidying, or neutral.

4. The verdict. Does the conclusion survive? Distinguish, if you can, three separable claims that the postdoc may have been running together: that the workspace story fully explains the *functions* of consciousness; that it leaves no further *phenomenal* fact to be explained; and that the access/phenomenal distinction is therefore idle. Say which survive the disanalogies, and what would have to be true for the strongest one to hold. If the audit shows the metaphor was carrying the conclusion rather than supporting it, say that plainly, and say what the postdoc would have to argue directly in order to get where he wants to go — what premise, stated without the theater, would do the work.

5. A replacement, or a diagnosis of why there isn't one. Is there a better analogy for the relation the theory actually posits — a broadcast with no viewers, a bulletin board, a shared bus, a currency, a change of file permissions, an airport announcement that only matters because of what it licenses downstream, a busier and less glamorous metaphor drawn from engineering — and does any of them avoid the homunculus without making the phenomenal vanish? Or is the situation rather that every available source domain either supplies a consumer who sees or supplies nothing that could be what-it-is-like, so that the choice of analogy always decides the substantive question in advance? If that is your view, argue for it.
