I'm in an upper-level seminar on legal epistemology this term and I keep going in circles about something, so I figured I'd ask people who think about disagreement for a living. Here's the bit of court practice I can't get straight — I'm not a lawyer, so just take my description of the doctrine as given even if I've mangled the details. Federal courts of appeals sit in panels of three, and a later panel isn't allowed to overrule an earlier panel of the same court even when all three judges are convinced the earlier decision was wrong; they have to apply it anyway and can only grumble in a concurrence or ask the full court to rehear the case. So three judges with the benefit of hindsight and the earlier panel's reasoning sitting right in front of them lose to three judges who happened to decide it first. But down at the district court level one judge's ruling binds nobody: a second district judge can read the first one's opinion, disagree, and go the other way, and nobody treats that as improper. And then there's qualified immunity — when someone sues an officer for violating a constitutional right, the officer gets off unless the right was clearly established at the time, and as I understand it one of the standard ways of showing it wasn't is to point at a split among the circuits. Judges elsewhere disagreeing is treated as a reason the officer couldn't have known. So the same system that won't let its own judges register disagreement with each other lets *other* judges' disagreement wipe out a plaintiff's remedy, and does that even when the plaintiff has a precedent squarely in his favor in his own circuit.

What I want to know is what view of peer disagreement this whole package presupposes, if it presupposes one at all. My seminar leader says none of it is epistemology — precedent is just settlement, like picking a side of the road, and the rule about panels is about hierarchy and error costs, not about what to believe when someone competent disagrees with you. I sort of think that can't be the whole story, because the qualified immunity bit plainly does treat disagreement as *evidence* of something, just aimed at the parties instead of at the judges. So: reconstruct the implicit theory for me, including the part where disagreement seems to count against knowledge or faultlessness for everyone except the judges themselves. Be honest about which pieces of the practice really follow from that theory and which are just compatible with it — my guess is the prior-panel rule can be justified from about five directions and commits nobody to anything about disagreement, while the immunity rule is doing something much stranger. Is the package coherent, or is it quietly relying on two different notions of what a peer is?

Then the part I'm actually stuck on. My friend in the seminar is a hardline conciliationist and says the whole thing is indefensible and that a consistent court system would look completely different — maybe no published dissents at all, maybe panels pooling their confidence with every other panel that has touched the question, maybe a rule where a later unanimous panel outvotes an earlier one on the grounds that it has strictly more evidence. He's also floated counting heads across circuits, so that a position signed by eighteen judges beats one signed by six. I've been toying with my own version: scrap the binary clearly-established test and make the damages scale with how many judges across the country have signed onto each side of the question, a disagreement-weighted remedy. I can't tell whether that's conciliationism applied properly or a total misuse of it, and whether a conciliationist is actually *committed* to liking it. Would they be? And what would a steadfastness-friendly version of all this look like institutionally — does anything have to change, or is steadfastness already the practice and only the immunity doctrine out of step?
