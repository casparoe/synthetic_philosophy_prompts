I'm rewriting the section on fundamentality for the lecture notes of my third-year seminar on metaphysical structure, and I keep finding that my own explanation stalls exactly where I want it to be sharpest. My students are comfortable with grounding as a primitive relation of non-causal, metaphysical dependence, and they are comfortable with the picture of reality as layered: a bottom level that is not grounded in anything, and then successive levels of items that obtain or exist in virtue of the ones below. What they cannot tell me is what fundamentality itself is supposed to be, and I have come to think the standard characterization does less work than the layered picture requires. So I would like you to explain, at a level I could hand to a strong graduate student with only minor editing, why fundamentality is almost always characterized negatively — as ungroundedness, as having no ground, as not obtaining in virtue of anything else — and what exactly goes wrong if one stops there.

The wrinkle I want worked through is that the negative notion and the architectural notion can come apart, and I want the cases made explicit rather than gestured at. The labels I have been using are these: call an item *inert-fundamental* if nothing grounds it; call a class of items a *base* if everything outside the class is ultimately grounded in members of it, so that the class does the generative work the building metaphor promises. Given that, an item that is grounded in nothing and grounds nothing else — an isolated item in a disconnected region of reality, if you want a picture — is inert-fundamental but belongs to no base; a structure containing two entirely disconnected grounding networks, each with its own bottom, has bases only in a domain-relative sense; and if grounding chains can descend forever, there may be no inert-fundamental items at all even though everything is grounded, so that the layered picture has no bottom rather than a different bottom. Please say clearly which of the two notions, if either, is doing the real explanatory work when philosophers and physicists argue about what the world is ultimately like, and whether the notion at play when someone calls a theory or a level of description fundamental is either of them or a third thing.

The part I most want you to get right is what rules the pathologies out. Transitivity and asymmetry alone do not guarantee minimal elements; the analogous move in set theory is an extra axiom excluding infinite membership descent, and I want to know whether the metaphysics is helping itself to something similar and, if so, whether that is a further substantive posit about dependence or merely part of what we already mean by it. I know the familiar anti-regress arguments — that grounding is a species of explanation and explanations cannot regress without end, or that a dependence chain with no terminus leaves nothing genuinely built — but I am unconvinced that they distinguish well-foundedness from the mere presence of a bottom, and I would like you to press on that distinction rather than report the arguments neutrally. It would also help to know whether the two notions coincide under some natural additional assumption, in which case the disagreement collapses into a verbal one, and where you would expect reasonable people to be genuinely disagreeing rather than talking past each other. Write it as continuous prose with the distinctions clearly labelled rather than as a list, aim for something in the range of a thousand words, and skip any literature review or citations; I will supply those myself.
