Hey — I'm in a small interdisciplinary seminar this term on economics and epistemology (me, two philosophy grad students, and an econ PhD student who is basically slumming it), and we've decided to try to turn it into something real: a grant application for something like eighteen months of funding for three people, on some version of the question "when a market puts a number on something, does anyone thereby know anything, and if so who, and by what route?" The background as I have it: the agreement-theorem and no-trade family of results says roughly that if traders share a common prior, each is rational in a way that's common knowledge, and the trade itself is voluntary and common knowledge, then nobody should be able to profit purely from informational advantage — so any trade that actually happens needs some other explanation, like differing risk exposure, liquidity needs, differences in taste, or a broken assumption somewhere. And then there's the mirror-image puzzle about informationally efficient markets: if prices already reflected everyone's private information, nobody would pay to gather it, so the information can't fully be in the price after all. Meanwhile, obviously, gigantic volumes of trade happen every day, and an awful lot of very smart people talk as if that volume is itself a knowledge-producing process.

What I want from you is definately not a survey of the literature and not a reading list — I want a defended research agenda. Concretely: three people, roughly eighteen months; one of us is strong on formal epistemology and probability, one on decision theory and rational choice, one on political philosophy; nobody has econometrics, we have no data access and no chance of getting ethics approval in time, so anything requiring new empirical work is off the table except in the loose sense of reinterpreting studies somebody else already ran. Given that, which open questions in this area should we actually spend the time on, and in what order? Make the criteria explicit and argue about the trade-offs between them: importance, tractability with the kit we have, how neglected a question is, and — the one we care most about — how many other questions depend on its answer, i.e. what's upstream of what. Also tell us what kind of result in the next year and a half would move a given question up or down the list, and which questions you'd deliberately leave alone because their answer wouldn't change much downstream even if we nailed it.

Two half-baked ideas of ours that I want you to either rank seriously or kill, because we keep going in circles. (1) The oracle gap: cases where a price is reliably accurate but no participant, and probably no outside observer, could reconstruct from public information why it's accurate. One of us wants to treat a price as testimony from an anonymous speaker and then import the epistemology of testimony — when is deference to that speaker knowledge-conducive rather than merely reliable-belief-producing, what counts as a defeater, does it matter that the speaker has no intention to communicate? Is that framing the most promising line here, or does it basically misdescribe what a price does? (2) Participation sensitivity: a prospective measure of how far a price would move if you deleted a specified class of traders — say the best-informed decile, or everyone trading for liquidity rather than for information. The hope is that this lets you distinguish "this market's accuracy is knowledge-driven" from "this market's accuracy is noise plus selection effects" without having to run a single regression. Does having that notion on the table change what's worth working on, or is it a dead end?

Last thing: everyone who writes about this stuff draws big conclusions from it, and I want to know which survive. Rank these — or throw them out and rank your own — by how much they genuinely follow from what's actually established: that markets forecast better than any individual expert; that we should settle ends by voting and delegate factual questions to betting markets; that prices can substitute for democratic deliberation rather than merely inform it; that restrictions on insider trading are epistemically self-defeating; that the bare existence of a liquid market is evidence that its prices mean something. Say which are overrated and which simply don't follow at all. Write in prose with a clearly ranked list somewhere in there, and then end with one machine-readable line: \answer{the single question you'd have us work on first, in twelve words or fewer}.
