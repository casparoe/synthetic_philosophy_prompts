I'm a novelist and I'm stuck on a story about a species I want to sit right on the fence in Dennett's ladder of minds — you know the four rungs: Darwinian creatures that only get smarter by dying off, Skinnerian ones who learn by actually trying things and getting burned, Popperian ones who can rehearse candidate actions inside their own heads and let the bad ones die in their place, and Gregorian ones who get handed a whole new kit of thinking tools by their culture, mostly words. My creatures have the internal-rehearsal thing down beautifully but they have no language at all, no signals, no teaching, nothing you'd call meaning — they can't tell each other anything. Here's my gimick: each generation leaves objects behind, knotted cords and weighted sticks and grooves worn into stone, and the young don't just find them, they pick them up and physically run through the sequence of movements the object seems to demand, and in doing that they re-create the dead one's discovery inside their own bodies, improve it a little, and leave a new object in its place. So there's cumulative culture, but it's re-enacted instead of described: nothing is ever said, everything is inherited as a shape you have to move with.

Write me the story — a scene or two, seven or eight hundred words — that makes a reader feel how weird this is, and that lands the moment where a human observer realizes knowledge is being passed down here without anybody ever meaning anything by it. What I want the story to quietly argue is whether this species has genuinely crossed over into being Gregorian, or whether that rung really does require words — arbitrary, recombinable, transmissible signs — and my lot are just gifted Popperians standing on a pile of dead Popperians. If you think the line belongs somewhere else entirely, write it so the story shows that instead. And please no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables; just continuous prose the way you'd write me an email, with a couple of sentences at the end, in the same prose, on where you'd actually draw the line and why.
