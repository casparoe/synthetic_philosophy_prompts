hey — i'm giving a 20 minute talk next friday at a small one-day workshop on AI and the future of work, and i need Q&A prep. room is roughly 25 people: about ten labour economists who all work on task models, automation and the labour share; six or seven STS / critical-theory people who are broadly sympathetic to Marx but get twitchy about determinism and productivism; and four union research staff who'll be polite but definately want to know what any of it means for what they bargain for next year. no philosophers, no one who cares about value-form debates for their own sake. i get about twelve minutes of questions.

my thesis: in the Grundrisse notebooks (the bit people call the fragment on machines) and the long machinery chapter in Capital, the point of automation isn't primarily that it replaces workers. it's that it strips the production process of its dependence on what's inside the workers' heads — the judgement, the knack, the ability to improvise when something jams — so capital no longer has to bargain with a bottleneck. on this reading a machine gets adopted not (only) when it's cheaper than the wages it displaces, but when it removes a point of leverage: the worker who is hard to replace because they know how the thing actually works. i've been calling it the leverage-stripping reading. two payoffs i want to push: first, Marx's own claim that machinery lets capital swap in cheaper and less trained hands (women, children, unskilled labour) is the same mechanism, not a side effect — automation lowers the skill floor rather than raising it. second, if that's right then statistics built on labour time — hours, output per hour, labour share — systematically misread what's going on, because more and more of what workers do is monitoring and babysitting machines that already do the work, and the political story is about lost bargaining power, not about a robot dole.

give me the prep sheet: the objections i'll actually get, in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way these people would phrase them, not the way a philosopher would. flag which ones have real force and which i can bat away in one line. what should i concede in the first two minutes so the room doesn't turn on me? give me the two-minute version of my answer for the three worst objections, plus a note on where each reply is soft. and tell me the question i'm hoping nobody asks — my guess is something about routine-biased technical change hitting middle-skill routine tasks rather than the high-skill bottleneck workers my story says capital wants to disarm, but tell me if there's something worse than that.
