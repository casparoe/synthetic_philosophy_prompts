I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on epistemology and decision theory — 16 students, one 75-minute session, all of them comfortable with conditional probability and about a third of them having taken a decision-theory or game-theory course — and I want to spend a single session on the Sleeping Beauty problem and self-locating belief. I do not want to lecture: what I want is an activity in which the students run into the disagreement themselves, making real guesses under real if small stakes, and only in the final ten minutes discover that the two answers have names and a literature. The two things they should come away having felt, rather than been told, are the pull of the thought that most awakenings subjectively like this one lie in the two-awakening branch and so this one probably does too, and the pull of the opposed thought that nothing about the coin has changed for the person who wakes up, who knew in advance that she would be woken, and whose confidence in heads therefore should not budge. If possible I would also like them to end up suspecting that these two thoughts may not be answers to the same question. So please give me one runnable activity: the rules, the materials (I have index cards, coins, boxes to draw from, coloured chips and a bag of chocolate, and a projector — nothing that needs preparing in advance), the roles, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes, and the payoff scheme. Assume the whole thing has to run off what is in the room, with nothing set up between rounds, and that no student should be a passive observer for more than about ten minutes.

Two design problems I want you to solve rather than dodge. First, we cannot actually put anyone to sleep or erase a memory, so the amnesia has to be simulated: I have considered spacing a given student's awakenings across the session with unrelated filler tasks in between, so that she genuinely does not know whether this is her first summons, but I do not know whether that is worth the disruption or whether a drawn slip plus an as-if instruction is good enough — tell me which and why. Second, I want the class to discover that the number they arrive at depends on the scoring rule and not only on what they believe about the coin. My proposed device is to run the same simulation twice: once scoring every awakening, so that always guessing tails is right about two-thirds of the time, and once announcing in advance that at the end of the round exactly one of the awakenings that occurred will be drawn uniformly at random and only that one scored. Under the second regime, by my calculation, every guessing policy does equally well, because being the awakening that gets scored is itself evidence favouring heads by a factor of two, which exactly cancels the surplus of tails awakenings, so that halfer and thirder alike end up indifferent between the two guesses. Check that calculation, tell me whether the contrast between the regimes really exposes the sampling assumption or is a distraction, and tell me what order to run them in.

Then give me the debrief: how to map particular moments and particular numbers from the session onto the two positions, what to say when a sharp student points out that drawing awakening-slips from a box silently assumes the very thing the thirder claims (that we may sample observer-moments uniformly), and how to handle the student who concludes that the whole exercise is only about what we choose to count. I also want an honest account of what the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself; my own guess is that no classroom simulation can reproduce the first-personal fact that it is the same subject in both awakenings, but tell me if you think that is not the deepest limitation. Last year I tried a version with a bag of slips and it collapsed into a twenty-minute argument about the bag, so tell me what will predictably go wrong on the first run and how to head it off. If you think either a stretch case in which one branch has ten awakenings, or a student bookie who sets odds and whose takings depend on how many bets there are, would earn its place in the 75 minutes, say where it goes and what it adds; if not, cut it. Do not hand me a menu of alternative designs: commit to one, defend its choices, and flag the decisions you are least sure about and would want to pilot before trusting them.
