You are a moral and political philosopher who works on the nature and value of privacy — equally at home with control-based accounts, access-based accounts, the view that privacy is about information flowing appropriately relative to a social context, and the reductionist suspicion that privacy is nothing over and above interests in bodily dignity, safety, and autonomy. Think carefully. Before you commit to an answer, spend a few lines brainstorming the live candidate accounts of what privacy is and why it matters, then do the work below.

I have just joined the patient and family advisory council of the hospital where my father is being treated, and I want to understand what our own monitoring policy already assumes, because we are about to be asked to approve an extension of it. The existing policy has three parts that sit oddly together. First, in the rooms of patients flagged as fall risk, a ceiling-mounted depth sensor runs continuously, day and night: no images, no audio, only a stick-figure skeleton derived from body position. Those skeletal traces are kept for thirty days and then deleted; clinicians may replay them only to review a fall or near-fall; and the vendor is contractually barred from any other use, including training its own models. Second, the hospital prints each patient's full name and room number on a whiteboard inside the room, legible to anyone passing an open door, and has patients state their name and date of birth aloud in a shared waiting room before every appointment. Third, it will not tell a telephone caller — even a spouse — whether a named person is currently a patient there, unless the patient has listed them as a contact.

So continuous machine observation of a half-dressed, often sleeping body is permitted; casual disclosure of identity and location to strangers in the building is permitted; remote disclosure of precisely that same fact is treated as a violation. Our ethics committee has begun defending this by appeal to a distinction between being witnessed and being archived: they say the right to privacy is at bottom a right against being recorded and kept, not against being perceived, and that a stick figure no one will ever look at, and which is deleted in a month, hardly counts as a record at all.

Reconstruct the implicit theory of privacy's nature and value that this package presupposes, and say which rival accounts it quietly rules out. Distinguish what the policy strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and be honest about where the practice is underdetermined and several theories would license it. Does the witnessed/archived distinction do the work the committee thinks it does, and is the thirty-day deletion window the load-bearing part of the policy or cosmetic? Then: the vendor wants to add a family-facing app showing a live stick figure of the patient plus fall alerts, with patient opt-out except after a fall, when the care team always gets the replay. What would each candidate theory say about that, and what would have to change in the policy if the hospital swapped its implicit view for a control-based one, for a contextual one, or for reductionism?
