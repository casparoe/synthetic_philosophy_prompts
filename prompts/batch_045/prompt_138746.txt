Last night me and my friend Ravi had a genuinely four-hour argument in a curry place about whether a synthetic minimal cell — y'know, one of those projects where the whole genome is written chemically and booted up inside a stripped-out recipient — has parts with functions. He's a physiolgist, so for him the heart pumps blood because that's what it contributes to the circulatory system, full stop, and the same goes for whatever the synthetic cell's equivalent bit is. I kept saying no: function has to do with history, with what the thing was selected for or what it's there *because* of, and a part that got assembled last Tuesday can't have a function any more than a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a frog that a lightning strike happened to assemble, whose heart would pump away without being *for* anything. Then he said: fine, then tell me what your theory is *for*. And I honestly couldn't. So I want to back up a level.

Act as a professional philosopher of biology who's actually spent time with the function literature, think it through carefully before you commit to anything (do your brainstorming first — I'd rather watch you change your mind mid-answer than hedge evenly), and give me the specification: what any account of biological function would have to deliver to count as adequate. Fix the purpose as *adequate for telling working biologists what they're committing to when they write "the function of X is to..."* in four settings — molecular biology and genomics, physiology and ecology, evolutionary biology, and medicine. I have a suspicion those four aren't all doing the same thing with the word and that no single theory serves all of them; if that turns out to be the result of the exercise, say so plainly.

Here's the raw material I want the criteria to earn their keep on (add your own cases, but hit these): (1) the heart — pumping blood vs. making a thumping noise vs. holding the pericardium open vs. keeping the chest cavity warm; any account that lets all of those in has a problem, so tell me which are supposed to be excluded and why. (2) No-history items: the chance duplicate of the frog, the synthetic cell, a gene that arose de novo in a lab population last month. (3) The ur-case: whatever the first contractile vessel was, there was no selection for pumping before there was pumping, so did the very first heart have a function? (4) Feathers: selected for insulation, later co-opted for flight — what's their function now, and does your answer change if the insulation use has since vanished? (5) A lab-evolved enzyme from a directed-evolution campaign, selected hard for one reaction, that also catalyzes a second reaction nobody ever selected for: is the second activity a function, a side effect, an accident? (6) Selfish genetic elements and meiotic-drive alleles that bias their own transmission while lowering the fertility of the organism carrying them — function at gene level vs. at organism level. (7) Malfunction: a heart with a septal defect, and separately a trait present from birth that has literally never worked even once. (8) The medical use: dysfunction is supposed to amount to more than statistical rarity and more than "we disapprove of it," and the harmful-dysfunction style of analysis of mental disorder leans on exactly that — can an account of function be *required* to carry that weight, or is that an illegitimate burden to place on it? (9) Metagenomics: annotating "function" onto genes from organisms nobody has ever cultured or described, where there isn't even an agreed containing system to analyze — what are those people actually claiming? (10) Artifacts: carburetors, thermostats, a drone's rotor — is covering them a desideratum, or is a principled organism/artifact asymmetry a point in a theory's favor? (11) What function statements are *supposed* to do for us: support counterfactuals about what would happen if the part failed, license extrapolations from a sampled individual to unobserved members of the class, and explain the presence of the trait rather than merely redescribe it.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it, and a verdict on whether it's non-negotiable, a reasonable preference, or just a prejudice of one camp (I suspect "function must be historical" and "function must be ahistorical and system-relative" both land in that third bucket when they're stated as requirements rather than as consequences of other commitments).
