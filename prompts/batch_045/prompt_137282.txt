You are a moral philosopher who works on the ethics of communication — assertion, deceptive implicature, and what speakers owe hearers. I want a designed case, not a commentary, so please treat this as a construction task and check your construction against every constraint before answering. Work carefully: before you settle on anything, spend a few lines listing three or four candidate mechanisms you considered and why you rejected them.

Background. It is widely held that lying — asserting what you believe to be false — is pro tanto morally worse than merely misleading: committing only to propositions you take to be true while arranging things so that your hearer arrives at a false conclusion by way of conversational implicature, framing, or selective truth. Call this the asymmetry view.

What the case is for. I am assembling a set of minimal-pair cases to find out whether any moral difference between lying and misleading survives once the speaker's aim, the hearer's takeaway, the relationship, and the downstream consequences are all held fixed. That fixes what counts as cheating here: a pair that gets its verdict by writing the verdict into the scenario, or by letting one route differ from the other in some consequence or collateral norm, is useless to me.

The task. Construct a matched pair in which one and the same speaker, facing one and the same decision at one and the same moment, can get one and the same hearer to believe one and the same false proposition q by either of two routes:

- Route A, the lie: the speaker asserts something they believe to be false, and q is what is said or follows trivially from what is said.
- Route B, mere misleading: every proposition the speaker asserts or commits to is one they believe to be true, and q is conveyed only by implicature, framing, or selection.

The pair must come out with Route B the morally worse thing to do.

Constraints on the pair.

1. Realistic setting: a clinic, a laboratory, a workplace, a family, a school, a tenancy or insurance matter. No murderers at the door, no espionage or undercover work, no trolleys, and no case where the speaker is permitted or required to deceive.
2. Genuine minimal pair: same speaker, same hearer, same relationship and the norms it carries, same intended false belief q, same subsequent action by the hearer, same material consequences. Route B may not exceed Route A in harm caused, in number of false beliefs produced, in premeditation or effort, or by adding a breach of some independent norm (a promise, a professional rule, a confidence) that Route A does not involve.
3. The hearer is an ordinary reasonable person: the implicature in B is one such a hearer would draw, and the assertion in A is one such a hearer would take at face value. Neither route may work because the hearer is unusually gullible or unusually suspicious.
4. In both routes the speaker intends that the hearer end up believing q — not merely foresees it, and is not indifferent to it.
5. For each route, state what the speaker asserts, why it is false in A and true in B, and why q is said in A but only implicated in B. Check this rather than assuming it: if your Route A turns out on inspection to be merely misleading, the pair has failed.

The hard part. The extra wrongness of B may not be generated by any of the four rationales usually offered:

(i) Deniability — the misleader protects their reputation or evades accountability, since they can later disown the content.
(ii) Self-regarding evasion — the misleader dodges the inner cost of insincerity, and that evasion is itself a vice, a hypocrisy, or evidence of worse character.
(iii) Conscription of the hearer — the misleader makes the hearer perform the inferential work, thereby corrupting, exploiting, or implicating the hearer in their own deception.
(iv) Greater craft — B is worse because it is cleverer, more laborious, or more calculated.

A case may involve deniability or effort incidentally; what it cannot do is rest its verdict on them. If the only levers you can find are these four, say so plainly and explain what that shows. I would much rather have an honest failure report than a case that smuggles one of them back in.

Then, for the case you have built:

6. Name, in one sentence, the single feature of the two routes that does the work — coin a term if you need one. Promising territory is the epistemic structure of what the hearer is left holding and what it would cost to undo: how localized the falsehood is, how much of the shared record the hearer must re-audit once the truth emerges, how the burden of detection is distributed, what the deception does to the hearer's future reliance on this speaker. But find your own lever if you can.
7. State the general moral principle, in one or two sentences, from which the verdicts follow, and show the verdicts falling out of that principle together with the facts of the case. The test of a non-stipulated case: someone who accepted your principle but had not been told your verdicts would have to predict them.
8. Two tests. Removal: neutralize the working feature while leaving the rest of the pair intact, and say whether the verdicts converge. Transplant: move the working feature into the other route and say whether the verdict reverses. If the feature cannot be transplanted, explain why and say what that reveals.
9. Disambiguate the verdict across three dimensions — pro tanto wrongness of the act, blameworthiness of the agent, and the degree to which the hearer is wronged as opposed to merely harmed. These can come apart. Give the direction of the comparison on each and flag any on which the reversal fails or is uncertain.
10. Two objectors. First, the asymmetry theorist: give their strongest reply in its best form (most likely that you have smuggled in a difference orthogonal to the lying/misleading distinction, or described two acts that differ in some third respect), and answer it. Second, the skeptic who holds that the distinction between lying and misleading has no moral significance at all: what do they say about your pair, and does the pair hand them anything?
