I'm in a cross-listed philosophy and cognitive science reading group this term, and last week we got stuck on whether a large language model that does well in an extended Turing-style interrogation understands anything of what it says. One of the graduate instructors ended the discussion with a line I keep hearing around the department in one form or another: competence without comprehension is how mind works all the way down, because Darwin showed that apparent design can arrive before any designer and Turing showed that a mechanism can compute something without grasping what it computes. I want to know what that sentence could actually be asserting, since it felt decisive in the room and I suspect it was decisive only because it was ambiguous. Please give me a menu of the distinct precise theses it could be expressing, ordered from the near-platitudinous to the radical, and say for each whether it is trivial, defensible but contested, or false. The readings I have in mind but can't keep straight include: the claim that an agent can be reliably good at something while being unable to articulate why; the Darwinian claim about provenance, that a blind filter of variation and selective retention can manufacture competence with no foresight and therefore no comprehension anywhere in its causal history; the Turing-flavored claim about computation, that there is no fact of the matter about what a mechanism is about apart from some interpreter's assignment, so comprehension talk is irreducibly a stance we take; a stronger claim that comprehension is always downstream of competence and never does any independent explanatory work; and the outright eliminativist claim that there is no comprehension anywhere, only competence in various degrees of organization. For each, tell me whether it would actually license the move my instructor made, which was to treat questions about machine understanding as badly posed rather than merely hard to answer.

To force the issue, I've been trying out a case of my own, and I'd like each reading tested against it. System A is a hand-written chess program whose authors can state, for every term in its evaluation function, what that term is for and why its weight was set where it was. System B is a network trained by self-play alone, producing comparable play, with no such account available to anyone. System C is made by taking System A and running it through an aggressive compilation and distillation pipeline that provably preserves its move-by-move behavior while destroying any recoverable mapping from its internal states onto the authors' original rationale: the play is indistinguishable from A's, but no one, including the authors, can now say why it prefers the moves it prefers. Call this the erasure test. If competence can survive the erasure of every recoverable reason while behavior is held fixed, that seems to tell against some readings of the slogan and for others, and I want to know exactly which. I'd also like you to take on a wrinkle I haven't seen handled. Darwin's inversion concerns a filter with no foresight, and Turing's concerns rule-following that is indifferent to substrate and to interpretation, but the process that actually produced the systems we were arguing about is neither of those: it is optimization against an objective fixed in advance by engineers, which has a little foresight baked into it but apparently none at the point where the competence is acquired. Is that a third kind of inversion, or does it collapse into one of the other two, and does the answer change which readings of the slogan are still available?

I'm writing a short response paper claiming that the one-liner does equivocation work in exactly this setting, so please commit rather than survey. Tell me which single reading the instructor was almost certainly relying on given how it was deployed, whether that reading survives the erasure test and the wrinkle about objective-driven optimization, and whether it supports treating the machine-understanding question as ill-posed or only as unsolved. Give me your verdict on each reading in a sentence or two, with the reason, and then close with a short note on where your own verdict is weakest — the one premise you'd expect a sympathetic critic in that room to push back on hardest. No bibliography or citations; I only want the argument.
