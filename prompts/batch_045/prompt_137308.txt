I have no formal training in philosophy. For a couple of years now I have been reading translated early Buddhist texts and Mahāyāna sutras on my own and arguing about them with a handful of people on a small forum. The argument that never gets settled is whether Buddhist ethics is really a form of consequentialism. Both sides quote passages at each other, and I would rather have something that sorts people by what they actually think about cases than by which label they prefer.

Please build me a taxonomy of the positions as a tree in which every branch is labeled by a claim a reader can accept or reject without already knowing the name of any view. The distinctions I care about include at least these: whether the precepts are rules or summaries of what usually reduces suffering; whether the rightness of an act is settled by the agent's intention at the moment of acting or by what happens downstream of it; whether the outcome that does the work is the agent's own karmic fruit or the welfare of everyone affected; whether concern is impartial across all sentient beings or permits partiality toward family, teacher or community; whether the ending of suffering is the only thing good in itself; and whether, for someone who has uprooted greed, hatred and delusion, there is any longer a question "what should I do?" that has a correct answer.

The tree should be able to place, and where necessary discriminate between: the reading on which right action is just the maximization of the cessation of suffering impartially conceived; the reading on which karma makes the framework a kind of long-run prudence about one's own mind; the reading on which the real subject is character and the precepts describe what a liberated person would not do; and the Mahāyāna reading on which compassion licenses breaking a precept in a particular case. Give each leaf a short name and one sentence on its most awkward commitment; six to eight leaves is plenty. Please do not attach scholars' names, titles or textual citations to the leaves — I want the positions, not the secondary literature.

It must handle these cases: the bodhisattva who kills the murderous ship's captain to save everyone aboard; a gift whose karmic fruit is said to vary with the virtue of the recipient; stepping on an insect by accident, with no hostile intention; and one case of my own that I would like included because I think it does work the other three do not — a lone arhat living in an abandoned monastery long after everyone else has died, who does not kill the ants in the kitchen and does not take what is not given, although nothing there is owned, no one is watching, and no one is affected. Is she doing anything morally assessable, and if so, in virtue of what?

Two further things. Where a combination of answers is simply unoccupied, mark the empty cell and say in a line whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just untried. And where two leaves differ only verbally, say so instead of manufacturing a distinction. My own suspicion, which I would like the tree to be able to test rather than flatter, is that the impartial suffering-minimizing reading and the character-based reading agree on nearly every case and come apart mainly over the empty monastery.
