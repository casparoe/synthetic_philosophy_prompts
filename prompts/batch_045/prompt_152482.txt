I'm seventeen, and on Saturday I'm giving the toast at my grandfather's ninetieth birthday party — roughly sixty people in a VFW hall: his three children, his ninety-two-year-old sister, the men he worked with for thirty-nine years as an elevator mechanic, the apprentice he trained who now runs the company, my cousins, and a neighbor he has been arguing with for a quarter of a century. I've been asked because I'm the grandchild who argues with him best. He will be sitting four feet away with his hearing aid in, and he has already told me how he intends to judge what I say.

His rule, repeated to me my whole life, is this: if you could have told the same story just as easily had things turned out the other way, then you haven't explained anything, you've only narrated after the fact. He applies it to machinery — three contractors had theories about the parish hall elevator, and he rode it for an hour with the panel off and found that a new skylight was throwing afternoon sun onto the door sensor, which is why it only ever stalled after lunch — and he applies it to people. His standing complaint about funerals is that the dead get described in words that would have fitted almost anybody, words that let you account for the entire person while committing you to nothing at all. What he wants instead, and what he says he'll be grading me on, is the shortest description that genuinely narrows him down: something a stranger could use to guess what he'll do next.

I've read a little about Occam's razor, Kolmogorov complexity and Solomonoff induction, and my understanding is that the best account of a body of data is the shortest program that generates it, and that a hypothesis flexible enough to fit anything you might have seen explains nothing. Please write the toast so that this idea is load-bearing rather than mentioned: no philosophy lecture to a room of mechanics and cousins, and please don't call him a simple man — he'd hate the word, and it collapses the whole thing into a pun on his own rule. The hard part, as I see it, is that a description can fail in two opposite ways: it can be so generic that it compresses nothing, or it can compress him into one true, flattening phrase that lands as a roast in front of his sister. I want the oddly specific detail that actually predicts him.

Three more constraints. His sister is devout and would be wounded if the toast quietly implied that simplicity rules out anything beyond this life; the neighbor believes the moon landing was staged, and the speech must neither pick that fight nor condescend to him. And the man he trained will notice if you get a piece of machinery wrong, so keep any technical detail plausible. Beyond the skylight story, invent one or two memories if you need them, but nothing saintly.

Aim for 600–700 words, four to five minutes spoken, and let it land as love rather than as an argument I won. If I've misunderstood the idea, correct it inside the toast rather than in a note. Please begin with the toast itself — its first sentence — with no heading, no preface, and nothing appended afterward.
