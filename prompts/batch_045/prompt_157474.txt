In discussions of Hume on money and trade, people often invoke his image of money as oil for the wheels of trade: money is not itself one of the goods that commerce produces, but a facilitator that lets the real wheels—production, exchange, and industry—turn more smoothly. The image is used to license a strong conclusion: a country's wealth cannot consist in its stock of money, mercantilist attempts to hoard precious metal are therefore futile, and expanding the money supply cannot permanently increase real output.

I want to audit that analogy rather than repeat it. Please lay out the mapping explicitly (a correspondence table is fine): what plays the role of the wheels, the oil, the engine's output, friction, and the speed at which the oil circulates. Then test the mapping against three complications from Hume's own argument. First, Hume also holds that a gradual increase in money can stimulate production during the interval before prices fully adjust, which makes additional money look less like extra oil and more like a temporary stimulant. Second, in a commodity-money setting money is itself a traded good, so it is partly one of the wheels as well as the oil. Third, the work done by a given stock of money depends on how quickly it circulates, whereas the oil image seems to make quantity the only variable.

Apply the audit to a test case. A small trading economy experiences either (a) a slow, broad increase in spendable balances from growing export sales, or (b) a sudden one-time influx of new money that goes first to a narrow group of asset holders. For each case, say whether the oil analogy or the interval-stimulation complication better predicts what happens to prices, industry, and the eventual inflow or outflow of money.

Conclude by separating what the analogy can carry from what it cannot. Does it by itself establish Hume's anti-mercantilist conclusion that money is not wealth, or does that conclusion depend on further argument the metaphor cannot supply? If a better analogy captures Hume's actual position—perhaps a standard of measurement, a claim ticket, or something else—identify it and explain why.
