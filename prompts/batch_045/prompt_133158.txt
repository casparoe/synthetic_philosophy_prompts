Hey — I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background at all (I've picked up a bit of probability from ML-adjacent stuff and that's about it), and I got into an argument over lunch with two coworkers that I still don't feel like I understand the shape of.

We were doing one of those back-of-the-envelope risk estimates for a gnarly database migration: we broke "nothing goes badly wrong" into maybe nine stages — the schema freeze actually holds, no silent replication lag, the rollback path still works, whoever's on call can find the runbook, and so on — gave each one a hand-waved probability, and multiplied. Someone said "yeah, just assume independence," we did, and we got a number. Then someone else pointed out these stages obviously aren't independent: a bunch of them fail for the same upstream reason (the team is rushed, one person wrote all of it, the same bad deploy window covers several of them), and that multiplying had probably made us way overconfident. Everyone agreed with that. What we couldn't agree about is what the independence assumption was doing there in the first place.

So: what is a person actually committing to when they presume independence in a heuristic argument like this? Is it a claim about the world, a claim about my evidence, or more like a bookkeeping convention — and how would you formalize "I have no evidence of any dependence," which seems to be the real content of the presumption but which I can't see the mathematical object behind? The bit I keep tripping on is this: people tell me independence is measure-zero-ish, that if you put a smooth prior over all joint distributions over these nine events, the exactly-independent ones get probability zero, so the presumption can't just be "my uncertainty is a distribution over joint distributions." But it also can't be nothing, because the assumption clearly does work and changes the answer. Is the right picture that independence is one special low-parameter hypothesis you hand separate prior mass to (a simplicity/complexity prior thing), or that it falls out of a maximum-entropy-style rule — among distributions compatible with what I know, take the least committal one? Those feel like they'd come apart in some cases and I definatly can't see where.

Also, and this is what I actually care about: I've started calling something "independence debt" and I want to know whether it's a real phenomenon or just me pattern-matching. It looks to me like the damage from a bogus independence presumption grows superlinearly in the number of factors — if every pair of my nine events has a small positive correlation I failed to model, my product is off by something like (1+eps) to the power of the number of pairs, not the number of factors, so going from three factors to nine isn't 3x worse, it's much worse; whereas if the unmodeled correlations are random in sign they partly cancel. Is that right, and does it argue for a discipline about how you carve the outcome up in the first place? My friend said we should re-cut the stages so each one is conditionally independent given some shared upstream causes in a DAG rather than assuming flat-out independence, and that feels obviously better but I can't say why it's better, what you give up by doing it, or how you'd know when you've carved far enough. I'd rather get the conceptual shape of all this than a formula dump — I can do the algebra, I just don't know what any of these moves mean. Please write the answer in American English.
