I'm a visiting graduate student sitting in on a seminar on agency this term, and instead of tracking the readings I've been trying to find out what I myself think, so I've been writing down my gut verdict on a list of cases before each meeting and then arguing about them afterwards with a friend in the department. Here is where I've landed. A man who murders his business partner after three weeks of calm deliberation, for the money: responsible, and resentment seems entirely fitting. The same man, except that an undiagnosed tumour was pressing on his frontal lobe and he had become wildly impulsive: not responsible, and resentment seems out of place. A man whose values are covertly rewritten by a team of neuroscientists while he sleeps, so that a month later he kills for reasons the team installed: not responsible. A man raised from infancy by violent, sadistic parents, whose character was formed by that upbringing and who kills in a rage at forty: responsible, but distinctly less so than the first man, and my resentment feels partly out of place. A woman with exactly that upbringing who then spent fifteen years deliberately and successfully rebuilding her own character, and who kills in a rage: fully responsible, and, oddly, I want to say more responsible than the first man, not merely equal to him. A person who has been through ten successive rounds of deliberate self-revision, each round's standards having been arrived at in the round before: more responsible than someone who has been through one, but I feel the sequence should converge rather than keep rising, and I can't say where. The manipulated man from the third case, twenty years on, told the whole story, who examines what was installed, wholeheartedly reaffirms it, and kills for exactly those reasons: not responsible, though this is the verdict I am least sure of. And a variant on him: an agent in whom both an installed desire and a desire he cultivated himself are present and each would have been sufficient, who kills: responsible, or at least mostly so — which I realise sits badly with the third case, since the installed desire is doing the same work in both.

My friend says my verdicts are all in the service of one idea: that an agent is responsible for an action to the degree that the features of her character which produced it were authored by her, where authoring a feature means having brought it about through a choice that was itself hers in the same sense. Call that fractional authorship. He then says Galen Strawson's basic argument is precisely the argument that nobody ever authors anything in that sense, because you must already have a character to make any such choice, so my scale never gets off zero, and if I want to keep my verdicts on the self-revisers I have to give up the first case, while if I want to keep the first case I have to give up the manipulation cases. I don't want to believe that, but I can't see where the argument fails.

So: state the principle you think my verdicts are actually tracking, or two or three competing candidates if there's no unique one — precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case myself, and with each clause keyed to the case that forces it. Then give me its verdicts, with numbers if it's scalar, on three cases I haven't listed: an agent grown to adulthood in a laboratory with his entire character specified in advance and then released, who kills; an agent whose installed values happen to fit his native temperament so well that without the intervention he would very likely have arrived at the same values anyway, so that the team merely accelerated him; and an agent with no manipulation and no abuse behind him, whose character was shaped by ten years of ordinary unchosen experience — jobs, bereavements, friendships — who kills. Then tell me honestly which of my seven verdicts your principle cannot accommodate and which one you'd overturn, whether the scalar version is stable or quietly collapses to zero for everyone, and whether a threshold version is better and, if so, what could set the threshold. Please don't send me to papers or reading lists; I want the principle worked out, not a map of the literature. Keep the whole thing under six hundred words.
