I'm in my last year of high school and I host my school's science podcast: one episode a month, about forty minutes, recorded in a converted storage closet, listened to mostly by other students and by two physics teachers who are kind enough not to tell me when I've gotten something wrong. Next month's guest is Dr. Hallie Morse, a statistical physicist who works on the thermodynamics of information. From the abstract and the notes she sent me, her position is a minority one inside the standard story about Maxwell's demon, and I would like the interview to press on it rather than let her narrate it.

Here is her view as I understand it. The familiar exorcism runs: the demon sorts fast molecules from slow ones using information about them, and the second law survives because the demon's memory fills up, and resetting one bit costs at least kT ln 2, which pays for the work the demon extracted. Dr. Morse agrees the demon never wins. She thinks that story gets the accounting wrong. On her version, erasure is not the essential step: a demon with enough memory need never erase anything. What is essential is that the memory register begins in a prepared standard state, uncorrelated with the gas. Producing those blank, low-entropy bits is itself thermodynamic work, and she argues that a demon handed an unlimited supply of fresh memory threatens the second law no more than a demon handed a charged battery: the prepared memory is a fuel, not a bookkeeping trick, and the bill is paid upstream at preparation rather than downstream at erasure. She also insists the disagreement is not merely verbal, and says it shows up in the numbers for a single run, where fluctuation relations let the demon extract more than the average bound for a while.

Please draft eight to ten questions, in an order that lets the episode build, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. Constraints: nothing that lets her simply restate the setup from scratch, and nothing that depends on her having read a particular paper or on my having read it. The questions should be answerable out of the physics itself. I would like at least one question on each of the following, though I am not confident I have any of them right, so change them if they misfire.

One: reversible measurement. The standard reply to locating the cost in acquisition is that a measurement can in principle be carried out reversibly and without dissipation, so the bill has to arrive at erasure. If that is right, has she merely moved the same cost once more rather than moved it to where it actually belongs?

Two: the demon that never erases. If the demon keeps every record forever, her story says the cost lies in the fresh bits it was given. Then what happens in the limit of an unbounded memory? Is the second law saved only because the demon is finite, and is that an acceptable thing for the second law to depend on?

Three: whether the numbers close. Her account still has to reproduce the usual bound on average work extracted per bit. Ask her to place the preparation cost as a term next to the information term, and ask whether the two are exactly equal or only related by an inequality, and how the answer changes if the preparation is done reversibly rather than wastefully.

Four: single runs versus averages. On her account, is the constraint on one run the same as the constraint on the average? Does her framing predict anything different from the standard one about how large the fluctuations can get, and could that difference be measured?

Five: the case with no measurement in it. Suppose the correlation the demon exploits is not produced by anything that looks like a measurement. Two systems simply become correlated by interacting, and some mechanism turns that correlation into work, with no register, no stored record, and no erasure step. Where, on her account, is the cost? And is such a device even an information engine on her view, or does her account only cover demons that deliberately measure?

Six: coherent records. If the demon stores its record in a quantum-coherent superposition and uncomputes it reversibly instead of erasing it, can it put off paying indefinitely? Ask what breaks, and when.

Seven: fuel or relabeling. Press the claim that prepared memory is a fuel. Is that different in any testable way from saying the demon is simply running off a low-entropy resource? If it is not, has the information-theoretic part of her story done any work at all?

Eight: what would count against her. What experimental result would show her version to be wrong rather than differently phrased, ideally something a group could actually run on a colloidal particle or a single molecule?

She also has a habit of answering objections by saying that where one puts the cost is a matter of convention. If you can, build one question that makes that reply progressively harder to reach for, and tell me which question it is.

Finally, since I am not in a position to check any of this: for each question, say in a line or two how confident you are that the anticipated answer really is the one she will give, and why. Then single out the question where you are least confident, where her answer may go somewhere you did not anticipate, and tell me what I should do on air if the follow-up I prepared does not fit what she actually says.
