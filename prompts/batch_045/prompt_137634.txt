I am an evolutionary biologist working on phenotypic plasticity and niche construction. I use *innate* only loosely, as shorthand for a reaction norm that is robust across the range of environments a lineage actually encounters, and I am sympathetic to the developmental-systems critique of the nativist/empiricist binary. Of Chinese philosophy I know only the one-sentence version: Mencius held that human nature is good, Xunzi that it is bad. I would like the actual disagreement about *xing* reconstructed in the vocabulary I work in, and then told what that vocabulary could not hold.

The case I want it tested on — some colleagues and I have been using it, and I suspect it is a bad test — call it bare rearing: a cohort of children supplied with adequate nutrition, shelter and medical care, but no enculturation of any kind, no language modelled for them, no ritual, no instruction, no example of anyone doing anything evaluatively. Asked what such a cohort would be like at twenty, the two positions appear to make different predictions. I want to know whether they really do, and whether the difference in prediction is the interesting part of their disagreement.

So:

1. Restate Mencius's position — the incipient tendencies, the spontaneous reactions, the growth and extension of what is already there — and Xunzi's — *xing* as what is so of itself without learning or deliberate effort, disorder as its unaided outcome, goodness as *wei*, the acquired product of conscious exertion — in my terms: reaction norm, species-typical outcome, canalization, sensitive period, scaffolding, developmental niche, the inherited resources that do part of the constructing, proximal versus ultimate explanation, and which of the four explanatory questions each position is actually answering. For each pairing, say whether it is an honest translation or a forced fit, and why.

2. Give me the residue: the claims with no counterpart in that vocabulary. I expect at least three candidates and want your assessment of each: the premise both thinkers share that *xing* is the same in the sage and in the worst person, which suggests the dispute was never about typical outcomes at all; the fact that the predicate under dispute, *good*, is not a measure of functioning or of fitness; and Xunzi's *wei*, which seems to mean at once artificial and deliberate doing, so that the axis he is supposedly located on has nowhere to put him. Add whatever further items you think matter more than these.

3. Then the verdict: does the residue mark a genuine limit of a developmental-systems vocabulary, something it cannot in principle say, or does it show that the classical dispute ran together questions my framework would separate, such that once separated some of it dissolves? Where the original is the unclear one, say that; where my framework is, say that instead.

Please paraphrase rather than quote: I have no way of checking a translation, and a single wrong line would do more damage than a vague one.
