hey — I'm on the editorial board of a mid-sized psych journal (we put out maybe 60 papers a year, we're not Nature or anything) and I've been asked to draft the decision memo on a submission that has us split basically down the middle. Our call is friday. Can you write it? Long-ish is fine, but please skip the citations and any suggested reading — the board won't read them and I need something we can vote on, not a literature review.

The paper: a pre-registered multi-lab replication of a "presentiment" style experiment. Participants get wired up for skin conductance and heart rate, and then a computer shows them an image that's randomly either emotionally arousing or calm. The claim is that their physiological arousal shifts a second or two *before* the image is drawn — the body apparently reacting to which image is about to come up. Around 2,400 participants across nine labs, analysis pre-registered, effect small but not microscopic, p somewhere around .0004, plus a pile of robustness checks. Our two methods reviewers couldn't find a flaw, and also can't say how it could possibly work. Two things make it messier: it was recieved as an ordinary submission rather than a registered report, so there was in principle room to wander through the analysis; and one reviewer noticed the randomisation seeds were generated on a fixed daily schedule, which is probably harmless but nobody can actually show that it is.

Where the board is:

- Three of us say reject. Their argument (they call it methodological naturalism) is that a journal needs a standing rule about which kinds of explanation are even on the table, information flowing backwards in time isn't one of them, so no p-value is going to matter. They also think our name ends up in press releases we don't control, and, more honestly, that with a prior this low you'd need evidence a lot stronger than .0004 to move anyone.
- Three of us say publish. Their argument is that we sent it to reviewers, the reviewers cleared it, and "we reject findings whose explanation we dislike" is just bias in a lab coat. One of them keeps bringing up Turing: the Turing of the 1950 Mind paper apparently thought the statistical case for telepathy was too strong to simply wave away, and his response wasn't to refuse to look at it but to redesign his test so telepathy couldn't leak into the results — house the contenders somewhere telepathy can't get through instead of banning the topic. Their version of the lesson: when you can't explain an anomaly, build a better cage, don't close the door.

The third thing nobody has developed yet, and which I kind of want the memo to take a position on, is an idea one member floated: publish the paper, but only as part of a package. The authors would pre-specify a follow-up study whose job isn't to see whether the effect happens again, but to shut the loopholes — designed so the plausible mundane stories (the seeding thing, hidden flexibility, experimenter expectancy, whatever) would show up if they were true, and so that a null result would be genuinely informative to the authors themselves. The journal commits in advance to publishing that second paper either way. And the same packaging rule would apply to any future submission the board flags as anomalous, including ones that are merely unfashionable rather than spooky.

What I need: a memo with an actual recommendation — accept, reject, or accept on conditions like those — with a timeline the authors could live with and that we could defend in public if a journalist asks. If there's a better version of the packaging idea than the one I sketched, use it. Also, I think there's a difference between "is this effect real" and "does our imprimatur add credibility the evidence hasn't earned", and the memo should keep those apart somewhere. And give the losing side the strongest version of its case, not a strawman, because those people are going to read this and I'd rather they argued with the real thing.
