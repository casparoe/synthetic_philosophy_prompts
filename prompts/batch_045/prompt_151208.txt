Hi — I edit an illustrated non-fiction history series for 9 to 12 year olds. Small imprint, me and two freelancers, and I end up ruling on maybe a dozen dodgy claims a month, some from authors in proposal, some from a freelancer fact-checking a chapter and not knowing what to do with it. Things like: a megalithic site that couldn't have been built without help from visitors from somewhere else; a suggestion that a big stretch of ancient history and a big stretch of medieval history are the same events counted twice; the lost civilisation that a Greek philosopher wrote about and that everyone keeps trying to find.

Constraints: I'm not a specialist in any of these periods, no paywalled journals, and roughly an hour per chapter, one sitting, decision has to be defensible to a teacher who writes in angry. So I need a written procedure my freelancers can run on a single claim, not on a whole manuscript, that spits out one of a small number of verdicts (cut it / rewrite it / keep it as history / keep it but framed some particular way), with clear stop points where it just says no, and clear hand-offs to me where the question is really about values rather than evidence.

The bits I keep tripping over, and want built in:

The standard filter moves are useless to me. "Extraordinary claims need extraordinary evidence" only renames the decision I'm already stuck on. And the Atlantis-shaped case pushes the other way, which is worse: it actually has an ancient written source behind it, a named author, which is more than we've got for plenty of stuff we state flatly as fact. So the procedure can't turn on whether there are sources.

I want it testing the practice, not the truth of the claim. Something arrived at honestly that later turned out wrong is not the same animal as something that can't lose. Here's my half-baked idea, use it or replace it with something better: before you bother checking whether the claim has been refuted, ask what the person making it would accept as a refutation, and then check whether anything of that kind has already happened without the claim being dropped. In my experience these things never get abandoned, they get relocated — the date shifts, the site shifts, the missing records turn out to have been deliberately destroyed — and that relocation is what I actually want caught, not any individual factual error.

It has to be capable of an awkward verdict on claims I already believe. Please run it on at least one thing that's in every school textbook, because a procedure that can't in principle return "this is fringe" for a mainstream claim is just a rubber stamp for consensus, and authors will use that against us the moment they notice.

And don't give me a hedge-and-move-on option as a free pass. When we write "some people believe" for a ten-year-old, they read it as adults quietly agreeing. So tell me what the real options are and don't leave me a category that's just a way of not deciding.

Then run it on these three:
1. A chapter arguing that the builders of a famous megalithic site must have had help from off-world visitors, because no one has shown how the stones were moved.
2. A spread claiming that a chunk of ancient history and a chunk of medieval history are the same events double-counted, and that anyone who denies it is protecting their own timeline.
3. One I've got a soft spot for and want the honest answer on: a claim from a community's own oral tradition about where a battle was fought and who won it, which contradicts the written record, and which I'd like to print partly because the written record was made by the side that won.

Be straight about where the procedure misfires and what it can't do for me.

British English please, under 700 words total: numbered steps with the stop points marked, then the three applications, then the honest note. No recieved wisdom, no "it depends", and no citations — I don't need any.
