I convene a small ethics-and-design reading group at a game studio, and our last two sessions deadlocked, because both positions in the room are being carried by the same analogy and nobody could say how much weight it actually bears.

The case that prompted it: we are sunsetting one shard of an older persistent world — a few thousand accounts, some of them eighteen years old, with houses, guild halls, and a small number of items whose drop rates players spent years chasing. Our legal team's memo says the EULA gives us full discretion and we owe nothing, on the grounds that these things are just data, and the comparison it reaches for is Monopoly money: worthless outside the game, valuable at all only because the player agreed to pretend.

A group of our designers, and a fair number of players, reply with the same comparison inverted. Paper money is also just paper, and these days mostly just a row in a bank's database; its reality and its value are entirely a matter of convention, backed by an institution that controls supply and punishes counterfeiting. So if your bank balance is real, my vault is real; if a central bank can be said to take something from you by inflating it away, we take something when we delete a shard. Deletion is therefore a taking, and compensation, a notice period, or some form of export is owed. They also point out that this is broadly the line taken in recent philosophy of virtual reality — Chalmers's *Reality+* (2022) argues that virtual reality is genuine reality and its objects real digital objects — though none of us is a philosopher and we could not tell whether that helps them or not.

So I want the analogy audited rather than asserted. Please give me:

1. **The correspondence table**, as the players' version needs it: in the monetary case, what plays the role of the note or coin, the mint, the central bank, the legal-tender rule, counterfeiting, inflation, devaluation, a bank run, a sovereign default, and the option of holding value outside the banking system; and what plays each of those roles in a persistent virtual world.

2. **The disanalogies, sorted** into the ones that matter for the conclusion and the ones that are merely vivid. Two I want addressed directly: money is overwhelmingly fungible, while the items players actually care about are not; and money circulates outside the institution that issues it and survives that institution's collapse, whereas a virtual item can exist only inside the single institution that constitutes it. Connected to the second point, the operator of a shard is at once mint, central bank, legislature, police force and physical universe, and can unilaterally (a) delete an item, (b) duplicate it for everyone, or (c) close the world. For each of (a) through (c), say what the monetary counterpart would be and whether the mapping is close enough to do any argumentative work.

3. **What the analogy quietly imports.** My worry is that "real in the way money is real" is doing its work by importing an exchange-value model of what matters, so that an item is real because it has a price — which would make the items with no market value unreal, and harms that cost the player nothing in market terms no harms at all. That seems to give the wrong verdict in a second case we also have to rule on: sustained harassment and unwanted sexualised contact directed at avatars, and the deliberate destruction of builds with no resale value.

4. **An extension to harms.** Here is a sorting device I have been using and want stress-tested. Call an object real to some degree along four axes: *efficacy* (it makes a difference to what happens in the world), *recognition* (the community's constitutive rules treat claims over it as valid), *portability* (its worth can be carried out of the institution that made it), and *uptake* (it figures in the subject's experience, including through an avatar they have, over years, incorporated into their body schema). My impression is that the money analogy secures reality on the first three axes and distorts or ignores the fourth. Is that right? And if it is, what analogy should carry the harm cases instead — the avatar as prosthesis, the world as rented or shared space, the world as a language whose utterances take effect, the world as a game whose constitutive rules generate a genuine standing to complain? For each, say what conclusion it would license and what it would smuggle in.

5. **Whether the players' conclusion survives.** If the audit shows virtual objects are real but in a way that is radically institution-dependent — real, and yet incapable of existing outside the institution that made them — what follows for what we owe? Distinguish the candidate remedies: cash compensation, a notice period, a consultative voice in the decision, or technical export of the item out of the world. Say which of these the analogy supports, which it undercuts, and which need a separate argument entirely. If your honest verdict is that the analogy cannot carry any of it, say that, and tell me what would have to be argued directly instead.
