I convene a large first-year course on practical reasoning, and twice a term my teaching assistants and I have to grade short policy papers in which the argument almost always turns on a historical analogy: this is another Munich, this is Vietnam again, this is the Thucydides trap playing out. We mark them inconsistently, and I would like a procedure instead.

Please give me a decision procedure a marker can run in about ten minutes per paper, with no training in philosophy or history, to determine whether a given analogy is doing argumentative work or only lending a conclusion an air of inevitability. I want ordered tests, each with a statement of what would count as a pass and what as a fail, and a clear account of where the procedure stops and hands the remainder to judgment.

The cases it must handle: the student who supplies no features beyond the name; the lesson stated so that no outcome could disconfirm it; the analogy the historical actors were themselves invoking, so that the comparison is part of the causal story rather than a neutral observation of it; and the case both sides cite for opposite conclusions. I also want a place for the level-of-abstraction problem, since any two episodes match under some description, and for a test I have found useful in my own reading — an invariance test, on which an analogy earns its conclusion only if the outcome would survive changes in the features the analogy does not mention.

Then apply the procedure to three papers on my desk: one arguing that a trade concession to a rising power is Munich; one arguing that a limited military intervention is justified because Vietnam taught us to avoid quagmires; one invoking the Thucydides trap to argue that a great-power war is structural and policy therefore cannot matter. The Vietnam argument is one I make myself in lecture; if the procedure fails it, say so plainly. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot tell me — where a good paper will still be marked down and a weak one still pass.
