I have been reading the decision-theory material on the rationalist blogs for a long time, and I still cannot state the principle I am implicitly using when I answer these puzzles. So I have started writing down my gut verdicts case by case, in the hope that a pattern shows up that I can then be held to. I have read the canonical posts, so I do not need pointers to them; what I want is the principle, stated tightly enough that I can check it against cases I have not listed.

1. Counterfactual mugging: fair physical coin, predictor known to be perfect, I am asked for a thousand only if the coin came up tails, and I get the million only if it predicted I would pay. I pay. Not because I precommitted — I never had the chance — but because I cannot locate a difference that matters between the simulation Omega ran and me.

2. Transparent Newcomb, both boxes open. Seeing the million, I take only the big box; seeing it empty, I take both. I notice something uncomfortable: given a predictor sensitive to my policy, the flexible policy above and the rigid policy of always taking only the big box are worth the same from the prior, because the branch where I see an empty box and collect the thousand is a branch I am never in. So my preference for the flexible policy may be doing no work at all, and I cannot tell whether a principle stated over policies can even express it.

3. Smoking lesion, in the form where a lesion independently causes both the taste for smoking and the disease, and smoking is pleasant and causes nothing. I smoke. As far as I can tell this is consistent with 1: in 1 the predictor's output depends on what I would do, and here the lesion's does not.

4. Twin prisoner's dilemma where the twin is a copy made from a scan of me an hour ago, and where both of us know which of us is the original. I cooperate. Being told which one I am moves me not at all.

5. Twin prisoner's dilemma where the twin is a stranger a hundred light years away, with no causal contact ever and no shared code, but a mind I am confident reasons about these cases exactly as I do, so that I am 99 per cent sure we output the same thing. My gut says defect, and this is the verdict I am least comfortable with: I cannot say what the difference between 4 and 5 is supposed to be, the two verdicts feel genuinely different, and my pull toward cooperating grows as my confidence in the stranger's reasoning approaches certainty.

6. Absent-minded driver, with the usual payoffs: 0 for exiting at the first intersection, 4 for exiting at the second, 1 for driving past both. Beforehand I want to commit to continuing with probability 2/3. Standing at an intersection, with a 3/5 credence that it is the first one, exiting looks better to me: 1.6 against 1.2. Both verdicts feel right, and I do not see how to keep both.

7. Randomizing in Newcomb's problem is bad; I want to be predictable there. Randomizing with a genuinely unpredictable coin in Death in Damascus, where the predictor is trying to be where I am and cannot predict the coin, is good; I want to be unpredictable there. Same plasticity of policy, opposite verdicts, and the only difference I can name is the sign of the dependence.

My tentative rule: choose the policy that maximizes expected utility from the point of view of your initial prior, counting as swayed by your choice anything that would have gone differently had your policy been different, where that counterfactual is logical rather than causal, and where anything that runs your decision procedure — a copy, a simulation, a prediction — is thereby counted as swayed; and then never update on anything except to work out which decision situation you are in.

That rule gets 1, 3, 4 and 7, is silent or confused on 2, tells me to drop the second half of 6, and tells me to drop 5, which is the verdict I would least like to drop. It also has nothing to say about the case I keep returning to.

8. Omega cannot simulate me exactly. What it runs is a coarse-grained model, fitted to a sample of my past behaviour, that shares my decision-theoretic dispositions but reproduces my actual choice only 80 per cent of the time. In the mugging I still pay; the arithmetic is not close. But in the twin case, with a twin that is a coarse-grained model of me in the same sense, I have no stable verdict at all, and I notice that my willingness to cooperate varies continuously with the fidelity of the model, while every principle I can state is binary: either the other system's output is a function of my policy or it is not.

So: is there a principle that captures these verdicts, or nearly, and what does it say about the two cases I have not given verdicts on — Newcomb's problem with a predictor that is 90 per cent accurate rather than perfect, and a twin prisoner's dilemma in which the twin runs my decision procedure exactly but has a utility function that assigns no weight at all to me? Please state the principle precisely, say which case motivates each of its clauses, and tell me plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. In particular: does the dependence relation have to admit degrees, and if it does, what does that do to cases 5 and 8, and where does the threshold sit?
