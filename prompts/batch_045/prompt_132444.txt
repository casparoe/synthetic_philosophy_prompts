I chair the scientific review committee of a privately funded research program whose mandate, as the trustees wrote it, is to reduce expected harm from catastrophic failures of advanced AI systems. We have room for exactly one more three-year award of roughly $12M, and two finalists are left; I need to circulate a decision memo before Friday's vote. The committee is two formal-methods people, one machine-learning researcher, a statistician, an ethicist, and a trustee's representative who keeps asking what a regulator would actually accept as evidence. Please draft the memo for me. It should give a bottom line — fund A, fund B, fund both at reduced scope under specified conditions, or neither and reopen the call — together with reasoning the losing side's proponents could actually reply to, an honest statement of the strongest consideration on the losing side, and a statement of what the funded team would have to demonstrate at an eighteen-month checkpoint to keep the money. Please keep it to about 900 to 1,200 words, and please don't do a literature review or cite anything; we have all read the proposals and the arguments are supposed to stand on their own.

Finalist A proposes what it calls a guarantee: the system's design is such that, for any input in a formally specified class, the probability of an action violating a stated safety specification is below a threshold, and the bound is proved rather than measured. Our technical reviewer pressed on how the class is defined. The bound is conditional on three assumptions: that all relevant dangerous capabilities have been elicited during development, that the component checking outputs cannot itself be subverted, and that deployment inputs remain inside a class the team gets to define. When we asked what happens as that class is widened toward anything like realistic deployment, the proved bound stopped being small and became, in the reviewer's phrase, indistinguishable from no information at all. One committee member thinks this is fine and even exemplary: every engineering guarantee is conditional, and a conditional guarantee is strictly better than none because it localizes disagreement — instead of arguing about a soup of empirical results, we argue about three checkable assumptions. Another member thinks this is an average-case claim wearing a worst-case costume, since our attitude toward those assumptions can only be probabilistic, so that what we would really be buying is an argument whose strength is set by our confidence in the assumptions rather than by the proof.

Finalist B proposes measurement at scale: millions of agentic trajectories across a deliberately wide range of deployment-like environments, pre-registered metrics for specified harmful behaviors, confidence intervals, plus adversarial red-teaming on a substantial subset. Their pitch is that a bound whose assumptions cannot be validated tells you nothing you can act on, whereas a large and well-designed campaign at least tells you how systems of this kind actually behave and can detect capability thresholds as they appear. Two objections have been raised, and I don't think either is a throwaway. Our statistician notes that the quantity we care about is dominated by a tail: the losses in question are rare, plausibly heavy-tailed, and possibly without a finite mean, so sample averages converge far too slowly — or not at all — to underwrite conclusions about expected harm, and a campaign observing zero catastrophic events in some large number of trials remains perfectly consistent with an expected loss exceeding the entire fund. A second worry, which the B team concedes, is that sufficiently capable systems may be able to tell evaluation from deployment; if so the samples are not drawn from the deployment distribution at all, and the bias does not go in a random direction.

What has me stuck is that our own governing documents pull apart. The mandate says expected harm, which is an average-case notion — and if the statistician is right that the expectation may not even be well-defined, then the mandate quietly presupposes exactly the light-tailed world B needs in order for its numbers to mean anything. But the charter's review standard tells us not to credit evidence that extrapolates from observed frequencies to unobserved catastrophes, which is the world A is built for. My own instinct keeps flipping: I think a bound nobody can check is partly theater, and I also think we cannot average our way to a conclusion about events that, by hypothesis, happen once. Please don't resolve this by recommending both at reduced scope unless you can argue that is genuinely better than picking one; if you do recommend one, say plainly why its central weakness is less damaging than the other's, and what would have to be true for you to switch.
