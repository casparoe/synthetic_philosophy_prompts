Hi. I'm on the classification working group at our national health service — we decide what counts as a disease for the purposes of who gets covered treatment, so really we're deciding what medicine is allowed to be about. We're a mixed bunch: roughly half of us think a condition has to involve some kind of malfunction or failure of a mechanism to count, and half think it's mostly about whether we as a society disvalue the condition and think doctors are the right people to deal with it. We've argued for two years and somehow produced a track record anyway:

- asymptomatic stage 1 hypertension in a 52 year old (no symptoms, no organ damage yet, just raised numbers and raised lifetime risk): disease, 9-0
- adult lactose intolerance (most adults world wide lose the enzyme, symptoms only if you drink milk, and you can just not drink milk): not a disease, 8-1
- presbycusis, the hearing loss basically everyone has by 80: not a disease, 7-2, on the grounds that that's aging
- noise induced hearing loss in a machinist who spent twenty years on a factory floor (the ear was fine, the environment wrecked it): disease, 9-0
- male pattern baldness with documented distress and real social costs: not a disease, 6-3
- ME/CFS: no biomarker, no agreed mechanism, real suffering, patients treated terribly for decades: disease, 6-3

Now the submission we recieved. A big epidemiology group wants us to list a haplotype — call it Hap-P — as a disease. What we've been told: about one in four adults carry it, at every age; carriers are asymptomatic and feel entirely fine; the variant does exactly what it looks like it was selected to do, holding blood sugar up during infection, storing fat hard in famines, clotting blood faster; from around fifty, carriers get atherosclerosis roughly a decade earlier than non carriers, but they die less of sepsis and less of autoimmune disease, and on pre 1960 mortality patterns carriers did about as well as anybody else. The excess cardiac risk essentially vanishes for carriers who eat a low salt low saturated fat diet and exercise, and there's also a cheap daily pill that removes most of it. The submission asks us to list it, and in a footnote suggests listing it only in countries with a modern diet, since in the traditional-diet cohorts there is no excess risk at all.

So: what should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves? I don't need the literature, I need the shape of our own mess. Give me exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no preamble and no summing up at the end: (1) the two or three rules that fit all six of our old verdicts, (2) what each of those rules says about Hap-P, (3) the verdict you'd recommend and which old case is the closest analog, (4) what principle we'd be committing ourselves to with each choice, including whether disease status is allowed to depend on where somebody lives, (5) and the one further case you'd put in front of us that would actually tell the rules apart.
