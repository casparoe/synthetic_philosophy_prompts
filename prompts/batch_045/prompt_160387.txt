You are a philosopher of technology who has taught Winner's 1980 essay Do Artifacts Have Politics? many times and who also knows the social-constructivist critics who think its central claim is muddled. Please think carefully and work in stages: first brainstorm the different things someone might mean by saying that an artifact is political; then tell me which of those meanings my argument below is actually using, and where I slide from one to another; only after that should you answer my question. Be blunt if I have misunderstood something.

I am a high-school senior writing a term paper, and I want to argue that Winner did not go far enough. As I read him, his Long Island overpasses are political only because Robert Moses meant them to keep certain people out, so the politics of the artifact is really just the designer's intention smuggled into concrete; the interesting cases, I want to say, are the ones where nobody intends anything at all. Here is the case I want to hang the paper on. This year my school replaced the cash registers in the cafeteria with chipped trays: you set your tray on a pad, it reads the chip and the food, and it charges whatever account is tied to your student ID. There is no way to pay with cash anymore. The line now takes about ninety seconds and most people are happy. But if your account is empty, or the pad will not match your fingerprint, you have to walk to the front office for a paper voucher in front of everybody, and a fair number of kids have quietly stopped eating lunch instead. Nobody designed that outcome; the committee that chose the system was worried about theft and about the length of the line.

So my definition is going to be: an artifact is political just in case it distributes benefits and burdens unequally among people, no matter who made it or what they had in mind. On that definition the trays look like a better and more modern example than the bridges, since their politics does not depend on anybody's intentions. It also seems to me to settle the question my teacher keeps raising about whether engineers can do neutral work: if the trays do this to those kids, then the people who built the trays bear responsibility for it, and since every artifact distributes benefits and burdens somehow, no engineer anywhere is doing neutral work.

Am I right that the trays are a stronger example than Winner's own? And is my definition a good one, or does it quietly turn my thesis into something no one could disagree with? What is the strongest objection a teacher who likes Winner would make, and what should I concede rather than fight?
