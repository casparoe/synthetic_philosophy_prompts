My case. Take a lineage of burrowing frogs. For millenia their skin glands secreted a waxy gunk that waterproofed them, and waterproofing is uncontroversially what the glands were selected for. Then, roughly three hundred generations ago, their range shifts, they end up living permanently submerged in spring-fed pools, and waterproofing becomes completely useless. Around the same time a fungus shows up in the pools that the wax happens to kill. Frogs with a mutation that knocks out the glands get infected and die at age four — but they've already bred by then, and the pools hold a fixed number of frogs anyway, so the antifungal effect has never once fed back into differential reproduction. Nothing about why the wax is still present in these frogs is explained by what it does now.

I say: obviously the function of those glands, in these frogs, is antifungal defence, and a frog whose glands don't secrete is malfunctioning — a field biologist looking at one of the dying frogs would say so without hesitating, and if what you want from a theory of function is an account of what a system is doing and how it breaks, that's the answer. You, if you're honest about your own view, have to say the function is still waterproofing, or maybe that there's no function there anymore at all, and that the glandless frog isn't diseased, just unlucky.
