I'm writing a seminar paper on whether perception is cognitively penetrated, and I've become convinced that the way the debate is normally run — arguing about where perception "really" ends and cognition begins — is a dead end, so I've tried to replace it with something I call the Triple Dissociation Test. On my proposal, a top-down effect on perception counts as genuine cognitive penetration only if three conditions hold: first, the effect still shows up in a speeded task where the subject has no time to form any judgment about the stimulus; second, the effect persists even after the subject has been told in advance exactly what the effect is, instructed to correct for it, and offered a cash bonus for reporting only what is strictly there on the screen; and third, the effect disappears when the very same stimulus is presented in a slightly altered format, which I take to show that what has been altered is genuinely perceptual rather than a broad response bias. If an effect meets all three, then the standard deflationary replies — that it is really a judgment effect, or a reporting bias, or an effect on memory — are ruled out in one go; and if an effect meets none of them, then the modularists were right all along and nothing is penetrated. I also take the clearest positive case to be perceptual learning of the sort you get with a radiologist trained to spot a tumor on a scan, or a sommelier who tastes structure that a novice does not: training plainly changes what these people see, and I can't see what would be left of the anti-penetration position once that much is granted.

When I presented this in the seminar, someone said my second criterion is not merely inconclusive but backwards, and that the test as a whole both settles too much and settles too little — but I couldn't get them to say why in a way I understood. There is also a further worry motivating the paper, which may be where I have really gone wrong. I take it that the reason anyone cares about penetration is epistemic: if what you see is already shaped by what you believe, then perception cannot serve as the independent tribunal that adjudicates between competing theories, and appealing to your perceptions as evidence for the very beliefs that shaped them is straightforwardly circular. On that reading, a single confirmed case of penetration would be very bad news for the thought that observation is trustworthy at all, and that is what makes the empirical question worth settling.

So: where exactly is my test confused, and is criterion (ii) in particular salvageable, or is it the wrong kind of condition altogether? Is perceptual learning really the paradigm case of penetration, or is it a separate phenomenon that both sides can accommodate? And most importantly, if my framing of the epistemic stakes is mistaken — if penetration is compatible with perception's playing much of the evidential role epistemologists want it to play — I'd like to understand why, what distinction I am collapsing, and what the live question actually is if it isn't the one I have set up.
