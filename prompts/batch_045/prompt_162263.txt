I'm a journalist putting together a piece on the paradox of deontology — you know the setup: you may not shove one guy to save five, even though five deaths is worse than one, and even though the one dies anyway if you just stand there. I've been reading around and its striking how little has moved in decades: the same handful of trolley-adjacent cases, the same three moves (bite the bullet and own the constraint, go consequentialist, or dissolve the thing by splitting "what an agent may do" from "what would be best"), nobody budging.

My working theory is that it isn't one disagreement. I think the sides are answering different questions under a single label — one about what an agent has most reason to do, one about what the rest of us should want done or criminalize, one closer to conceptual analysis of "permissible" — and that a partly empirical question is doing hidden work: people's constraint intuitions look less like a clean doing/allowing rule and more like a numbers thing, since they erode when you scale the case up (one to save a billion) and get strange in many-agent versions, where each of five thousand bystanders could shove, no single shove is necessary or sufficient, and collectively they save the five.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a survey: verbal or substantive, different questions or bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for, and where exactly the empirical bit sits. Then tell me what would actually count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a reframing of the question — or make the case that no progress should be expected and why. Commit to one diagnosis and defend it, and flag how confident you are and where a hostile philosopher would hit it hardest.
