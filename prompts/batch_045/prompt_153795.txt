**"All truths are knowable" is a type error**

1. The setup. Anti-realists get attracted to something like: to be true is to be verifiable in principle. Formalize as (KP) p → ◇Kp. Fitch's result is that in a normal modal logic with a knowledge operator, KP *proves* the obviously false claim that every truth is in fact known. Fitch stated it the other way round as Theorem 5: if there's even one truth nobody knows, then there's a truth that couldn't possibly be known. So "actually unknown" collapses into "necessarily unknowable," and the comfortable middle position between truth = what God knows and truth = what we happen to know evaporates.

2. Why it works. Two assumptions do the work, and I think people undersell the second: (a) factivity, Kp → p; (b) distribution over conjunction, K(p ∧ q) → Kp ∧ Kq. Assume p is true and unknown: p ∧ ¬Kp. By KP, ◇K(p ∧ ¬Kp). But K(p ∧ ¬Kp) is impossible: distribution gives Kp, and combining with the second conjunct gives K¬Kp, which by factivity means ¬Kp. Contradiction inside the diamond, so ¬◇K(p ∧ ¬Kp), so by KP ¬(p ∧ ¬Kp). No unknown truths. Factivity alone doesn't get you there — you need both, and that's the bit I'm least sure about.

3. My diagnosis: it's self-application, not verification. The poison is that we get to substitute into KP a proposition that already *talks about K*, and then reason about it using that very same K. That's diagonalization. So index it: K_T means "procedure / theory / test-suite T verifies." The principle becomes: for every true p there's some sound extension T' ⊇ T with K_T'(p). That's trivial and harmless — if T can't prove p, take T + p as an axiom. Gödel says no single sound recursively axiomatized theory gets everything; he says nothing against each truth being provable in *some* sound extension. And notice where Fitch breaks here: from K_T'(p ∧ ¬K_T(p)) you get K_T'(p) and K_T'(¬K_T(p)), but ¬K_T(p) is about T, not T', so there is no contradiction. The index moved. Which makes me think the paradox is basically Gödel in modal clothing: unindexed self-application, fixed by a hierarchy that doesn't collapse.

4. Same trick for knowledge. Read "possibly known" as "some possible agent could know it" — maybe an agent who doesn't actually exist, maybe a much smarter one. Run the proof on q = (p ∧ nobody actually knows p). The agent who knows q in the alternative world is a *different* agent from the ones quantified over in the "nobody knows p" part, so step 2 never gets its contradiction. Verification, like provability, is relativized to a verifier, and a hierarchy of verifiers doesn't collapse. This seems to me like a clean escape hatch for the moderate anti-realist.

5. So the moral I want to end on: Fitch doesn't refute the claim that truth is tied to what could be verified. It refutes the idea of a *single, unrelativized* notion of verification that can be applied to itself. Once you relativize, the principle is fine and the paradox defintely dissolves.
