I've ended up defending a version of Kant's universal-law test that most people I know think died out a while ago, and I want to know what would actually kill it.

The version I hold: the test is not "what would happen if everyone did that". It's a test on a maxim under universalization, with two failure modes — a maxim you can't even *conceive* as a universal law without the law cancelling itself (the borrowing case: a promise no one believes in isn't a promise), and a maxim you can conceive but can't *will* as a universal law, because a will that willed it couldn't still will what any finite rational agent necessarily wills. The second branch is supposed to be non-consequentialist: you aren't totalling up the misery of universal non-beneficence, you're asking whether the will is at war with itself. And on that reading the obvious objections just miss — the "what if everyone did that" parodies, all the partial-compliance stuff, the cases where 99% compliance is lovely and 100% collapses the practice. Wrong question, wrong currency.

So: what would count against it? Not the greatest-hits objections I can already recite. I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want the ones a defender of the test would *concede* as counting against it, not only the ones its critics repeat. Which intuitions or cases does the view stand or fall with? Does it have predictions its rivals don't — for instance, that no amount of bad consequences can by itself sink a maxim, or that the duty/not-duty line should land somewhere other than where a welfarist would put it?

The thing I actually mabye lose sleep over, which I don't think is in the standard lists. Call them wobblers: maxims whose content refers to how other people are behaving, so that universalizing them produces not a contradiction but a law with no fixed point. "I'll take the quieter of the two roads home, whichever turns out to be quieter." "I'll free-ride on a cooperative scheme as long as nearly everyone else is contributing." "I'll believe whatever the local consensus denies." Universalize the second one and it seems to flicker: at full compliance the condition fails, so nobody free-rides, so the condition is met again. There's no stable state the law describes, but there isn't an outright contradiction either, and neither of Kant's two branches obviously has a slot for it. Is that a genuinely new failure mode, does it collapse into one of the known ones, or does it just show that such things aren't maxims at all (no universal form, no real end)? Which of those replies is more plausible and what does each one cost elsewhere? And be honest with me if this is only the old problem about how maxims get individuated wearing a hat.

Related: I want to know whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability. Whenever the test seems to misfire, the move available is "that wasn't really the maxim" or "that's a consequence, not a contradiction". Say whether the version I hold is insulated in that way, and whether insulation is fine for a claim of this kind — it isn't an empirical prediction, after all — or whether it means I'm holding something with no content.

What would move me most, personally: a case where the test gets the intuitively right answer but only by smuggling in a forecast of consequences. If every workable application of the will-branch secretly reasons that way, then what I'm defending is consequentialism with extra machinery, and I'd want to know. Can the conception/will distinction hold that line, or does it leak?
