I listen to a history podcast on my commute where the host does a segment rating explanations that listeners send in for historical events, and she either stamps them as real explanations or dismisses them as hindsight, her word for it being retrofit jobs, and her line is that once you know how something turned out your brain hands you a tidy story and you mistake the tidiness for understanding. She's not an academic, she just reads a lot and has strong opinions, and me and two people from work have a group chat where we keep a spreadsheet of her calls, because the three of us write incident reports at the small logistics company we all work at and we've started stealing her tests for our own post-mortems. Her verdicts so far, as well as I've managed to write them down: YES to the one about cholera in a London neighbourhood in the 1850s being spread by a contaminated water supply, and she specifically liked that the doctor arguing for it was arguing for it while it was still going on and went and did something about it; YES to the Dust Bowl being caused by deep ploughing having stripped the grass that held the topsoil together, combined with a drought, with people warning about ploughed-up land for years beforehand and earlier droughts on unploughed land not producing dust storms; YES to the space shuttle breaking up because rubber seals stiffened in freezing weather, with the engineers having raised exactly that objection the night before; YES to the version of the Soviet collapse where the state's hard-currency income fell apart when oil prices dropped, but NO to the version where it was mostly Gorbachev's character, which she dismissed as a character judgement rather than a mechanism. On the no side: NO to Rome falling because of decadence and lead pipes, her complaint being that it's a mood rather than a mechanism, that nobody could have acted on it, and that the same sentence was true centuries earlier; NO to the first world war being inevitable because of militarism, alliances and imperial rivalry, same complaint plus the point that the whole list was equally true a few years earlier when nothing happened; and NO to the depth of the Great Depression being explained by the 1929 crash, which she said just pushes the question back one step and is a sentence that was equally available for slumps that turned out mild.

The cases I actually want are these. First, November 1989: the Berlin Wall came down, and the explanation on the table is that an East German official at a press conference announced, apparently working from a note he'd only half read, that travel rules were being relaxed immediately, so people went down to the checkpoints, and the guards, who had no order to shoot, opened them. There's a mechanism there and it's a difference maker in the sense that if he reads his note properly the Wall probably stays up that year, but it also hangs entirely on one man's slip, and nobody predicted it. Second, a non-event: nothing much happened on 1 January 2000, and my explanation is that a lot of people spent the previous two years fixing old code and the fixing is why nothing happened, except that the rival story, that the whole thing was hype and nothing was ever going to happen anyway, fits exactly the same evidence, and I genuinely cannot see how her tests would tell those two apart. Third, a small one: my cousin's restaurant died in 2019 and the family explanation is that he refused to sign up to the food delivery apps, his staff had been on at him about it for a year so it was said in advance and there's a mechanism about where the foot traffic went, but it's also a story about one guy being stubborn.

So what does she say about each of the three? I've got three guesses at the rule she's actually running and I want to know which one explains her whole track record best and where they come apart: one, that a real explanation needs a mechanism rather than a mood or a redescription of the outcome in morally loaded language; two, that you have to have been able to say it at the time, although I worry that version is rigged, because for every person who called something in advance there were fifty calling something else and we only remember the one who got it right, so maybe the honest version is about what was visible in the information available at the time rather than about who said it; three, the near-miss test, whether the thing being cited is also sitting there in the closest cases where the outcome didn't happen. And there's a fourth I can't tell if she's committed to, which is just that she doesn't like explanations that hang on one person or one accident. Tell me which of these her past calls actually rule out, which of the three new cases best separates the ones that are left, what further case would settle it properly, and whether her machinery has anything to say at all about the Y2K sort where the thing to be explained is that nothing happened. I mostly want to know which of these tests, if any, I should actually be building into our post-mortem template at work.
