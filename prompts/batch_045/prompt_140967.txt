Here's the question.

A house runs the following game. It picks a positive integer K, puts S = 2^K dollars in one envelope and 2S in the other, shuffles them, and hands you one at random. Let A be the amount in the envelope you end up with.

(1) Before you open anything, someone reasons: "Call the amount in my envelope x. The other envelope contains either x/2 or 2x, each with probability 1/2, so switching is worth (1/2)(x/2) + (1/2)(2x) = 1.25x > x. So switch. And of course the same argument then applies again, so switch back." In three or four sentences, explain exactly what goes wrong. Be explicit about what "x" denotes in each branch of the calculation.

(2) Now suppose P(K = k) = c·2^(-αk) for k = 1, 2, 3, ..., where α > 0 is fixed and c is whatever it needs to be. You open your envelope and see A = 2^k dollars.
  (i) Compute the posterior probability that you are holding the smaller envelope, as a function of α and k. Be careful with k = 1.
  (ii) For which values of α is switching strictly better in expected dollars for every amount you could possibly see?
  (iii) Compute E[S] as a function of α. How does the answer to (ii) line up with it?

(3) Now the house caps the game: K ∈ {1, ..., N}, with P(K = k) ∝ 2^(-αk), and α < 1. Does the verdict from (2)(ii) survive? If not, which observed amounts are exceptions, and why? Last bit: in the uncapped version with α < 1, your answer to (2) says switching is better conditional on every single amount you might observe; but before opening, staying and switching obviously look symmetric. Is there a contradiction here? Explain.

Here's one student's answer, verbatim.

(1) The problem is that "x" is doing two different jobs. In the branch where the other envelope has 2x, my envelope is the smaller one, so x = S. In the branch where the other envelope has x/2, my envelope is the larger one, so x = 2S. Writing one letter for both and then factoring it out pretends that the amount in my envelope is independent of which envelope I happened to get, which is exactly the thing that isn't true. If I parametrize by the smaller amount S instead, then holding gives (1/2)(S) + (1/2)(2S) = 1.5S and switching gives (1/2)(2S) + (1/2)(S) = 1.5S. So there's no gain, and the argument for switching back fails for the same reason. The 1.25 comes from averaging over a variable that shifts meaning mid-calculation.

(2) Let p_k = c·2^(-αk). If I see A = 2^k, there are two ways it could have happened: K = k and I got the smaller envelope (other one is 2^(k+1)), or K = k-1 and I got the larger one (other one is 2^(k-1)). Their prior weights are p_k and p_(k-1), and the envelope was assigned to me independently by a fair coin, so both likelihoods are 1/2 and they cancel. So
P(I have the smaller | A = 2^k) = p_k / (p_k + p_(k-1)) = 1/(1 + 2^α),
because p_(k-1)/p_k = 2^α. Similarly P(I have the larger) = 2^α/(1 + 2^α). When k = 1 the second possibility has probability zero, so I know for sure I'm holding the smaller envelope and I switch.
Expected value of the other envelope is therefore [2^(k+1) + 2^α·2^(k-1)]/(1 + 2^α) = 2^k(2 + 2^(α-1))/(1 + 2^α), and subtracting A = 2^k gives
E[other] - A = 2^k(1 - 2^(α-1))/(1 + 2^α),
which is positive exactly when 2^(α-1) < 1, i.e. exactly when α < 1. So for α < 1 I should switch no matter what I see; for α = 1 I'm indifferent; and for α > 1 I should keep my envelope (except at k = 1, where I always switch).
For (iii): E[S] = Σ_k 2^k·c·2^(-αk) = c·Σ_k 2^((1-α)k), which converges iff α > 1 and diverges for α ≤ 1. So the "switch no matter what" region is exactly the region where the expected amount in the game is infinite. That makes sense, and it is also the reason not to take the α < 1 case too seriously: for α ≤ 1 the weights p_k don't sum to one — the series Σ 2^(-αk) only converges when α > 1 — so those aren't really probability distributions at all. α ≤ 1 gives an improper prior, and the "always switch" result is just an artifact of that impropriety. Once you restrict to genuine distributions, i.e. α > 1, switching never pays and the paradox from part (1) never gets off the ground.

(3) With the cap, K only runs from 1 to N, so the amounts I can see are 2^1 through 2^(N+1). For everything from 2^1 up to 2^N the computation in part (2) is unchanged, so I switch. But if I see 2^(N+1), then S = 2^N and I'm definitely holding the larger envelope, so switching loses 2^N for sure. That one observation is where the ex ante books balance: adding up the small gains across the other amounts exactly cancels the certain loss at the top, which is why the capped game has zero expected gain from switching overall.
The uncapped α < 1 case is different, and this is the interesting part. There, switching really is better conditional on every amount, which means the symmetry argument from part (1) is not universally valid: there are priors on which you should switch unconditionally, even before you open the envelope, and the "1.5S = 1.5S" reasoning only goes through when E[S] is finite. So what part (1) calls a fallacy is really a valid argument with a hidden finiteness assumption, and the closed-envelope case is only paradoxical if you assume that assumption without noticing it.
