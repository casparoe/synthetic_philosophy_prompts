Question 4

The Special Composition Question (SCQ) asks: for any non-overlapping things, the xs, what are the necessary and sufficient conditions under which there is something, y, that the xs compose?

(a) Set out the three canonical answers — unrestricted composition (universalism), nihilism, and van Inwagen's answer in terms of life — and explain which of the standard pressures each handles best: the problem of the many, the argument from vagueness, and the burden of paraphrase. [8]

(b) Suppose a philosopher proposes the Homeostatic Answer: necessarily, the xs compose something if and only if the xs jointly constitute a single homeostatic causal system that is not itself a proper part of any larger homeostatic causal system. Assess this proposal against the problem it is designed to solve and against the argument from vagueness. [12]

Candidate's answer

The SCQ asks when some non-overlapping things make up a single further thing. It is a special question because it can be asked independently of the General Composition Question — the question of what composition itself is, whether identity or a primitive relation — and the two may in principle receive independent answers.

The canonical answers differ in how restrictive they are. Unrestricted composition, associated with Lewis, holds that any non-overlapping xs whatsoever compose something, so that there are such objects as the fusion of my left ear and the Eiffel Tower. Nihilism holds that composition never occurs: every object is mereologically simple. Van Inwagen's answer is intermediate: the xs compose something if and only if either there is just one of them or the activity of the xs constitutes a life. The composite objects are therefore exactly the living organisms, together with inanimate simples should there be any.

The problem of the many tells hardest against unrestricted composition. In a region where we should ordinarily see a single cloud there are many overlapping collections of droplets, each no worse qualified than the others; the universalist must accept that all of them exist, whereas the nihilist can deny that any of them does. The argument from vagueness is standardly run in the opposite direction, in favour of unrestricted composition: a restricted answer must state a condition that admits of borderline cases, and then there will be cases in which it is indeterminate whether the xs compose anything, which cannot be; unrestricted composition states a precise condition, so any residual indeterminacy must lie in our language rather than in the world. But this shows less than it appears to. An object that no one could ever single out cannot be an object at all: one could never demonstratively refer to the fusion of my left ear and the Eiffel Tower, nor know anything about it, and existence and referability go together. Unrestricted composition is therefore untenable, and the argument from vagueness, properly understood, supports nihilism rather than universalism.

Nihilism's familiar cost is that it must paraphrase our ordinary talk of chairs and clouds, and this cost cannot be met. The nihilist must say that there are things arranged chair-wise, and in saying so she quantifies plurally over simples. But plural quantification carries exactly the same ontological commitment as singular quantification over fusions: as is familiar from Boolos-style plural logic, plural quantification has the expressive power of monadic second-order quantification, and with it commitment to the corresponding fusions. The nihilist is thus committed to the chair after all, and nihilism collapses into unrestricted composition.

The Homeostatic Answer is designed to avoid both difficulties at once. In the cloud region, many overlapping collections of droplets are homeostatic systems, but only the maximal one — the one that is not a proper part of any larger homeostatic system — is such that its members compose anything; the others are excluded by the second clause. There is thus exactly one cloud, and the problem of the many is solved without denying that composition ever occurs. The answer also escapes the argument from vagueness, because homeostasis is a precise notion drawn from the sciences: a system either maintains its own organisation or it does not, so the criterion imposes no vague condition on composition. It is in this respect better placed than van Inwagen's answer, whose central notion of a life is itself vague, and which is therefore undermined by the very argument van Inwagen deploys against the other moderate answers.
