Two friends of mine, one trained in physics and one in philosophy, got into an argument about the doomsday argument at a dinner I was at, and I genuinely could not tell who was winning.

(1) Maya opened with the Bayesian core. Take two hypotheses. H-near: the total number of humans who ever live is around 10^11 and our species ends within a few centuries. H-far: we spread beyond this planet and the total is around 10^20. My birth rank is roughly 10^11. Under H-near, finding myself that early is unremarkable; under H-far, it has a probability of about one in a billion. So the likelihood ratio is roughly a billion to one for H-near, and no prior that is smooth across orders of magnitude absorbs a factor that large.

(2) Theo: the computation assumes the self-sampling assumption. Under the self-indication assumption, the fact that there is a you at all is itself about a billion times more likely under H-far, since H-far contains vastly more observer slots for you to occupy, and that factor cancels Maya's exactly. The doomsday argument doesn't refute self-indication; it presupposes its denial.

(3) Maya: self-indication is the worse assumption. It lets you confirm a hypothesis merely by stipulating that it multiplies observers — you could become near-certain that a coin landed heads just by saying heads would have created more people — and it degenerates entirely once the number of observers is infinite.

(4) Theo: the symmetry is granted, but self-sampling has a problem self-indication doesn't: the reference class is underdetermined, and this is not merely verbal, because the verdict swings on whether the units are persons or observer-moments. Suppose the long future isn't a galaxy full of separate people but a million immensely long-lived minds. Then it adds essentially nothing to the person count while dominating the observer-moment count. Count persons and my rank is typical under either hypothesis, so there is no update at all; count moments and Maya gets her billion to one. The bite of the argument is therefore set by an unmotivated choice of unit plus a guess about the shape of the future.

(5) Maya: not unmotivated, because sampling over moments has a checkable consequence. If you are a random moment, you should expect to find yourself in an era of unusually dense experience — near the all-time maximum rate of it. We do in fact live at the largest human population that has ever existed, which is what that assumption predicts and what any hypothesis with a vast future makes very unlikely.

(6) Theo: even granting that, the argument yields a likelihood ratio, and you still have to multiply by a prior. Ours isn't flat: it is informed by ordinary evidence about whether interstellar travel and artificial minds are feasible, and about why the sky appears silent. And those are precisely the questions that move (4) made decisive, since they determine whether the future is thin in persons or not.

(7) Maya: your move just drops the indexical information on the floor. Priors formed from a view from nowhere still have to be conditioned on the fact that we find ourselves among the earliest hundred billion humans, and no amount of mechanistic detail removes the need to do that conditioning.

Then say who is more right overall, and what, if anything, both of them are missing. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
