Write a satirical internal document: the *Cluelessness-Adjusted Impact Report* of a fictional grantmaking foundation, produced a few years after the foundation took the problem of consequentialist cluelessness entirely seriously.

For background, here is how I understand the problem, so that we are working from the same starting point. The ordinary consequentialist picture handles uncertainty by expected values: an option is ranked by how good its outcomes are, weighted by how likely they are, and being unsure is just a reason to spread the probability mass. Cluelessness is supposed to be worse than that. For many real options the bulk of the expected value lies in effects we cannot even enumerate — flow-through effects on later people, chaotic amplification of small differences, heavy-tailed outcomes in which the estimate is dominated by its own tail — so that reasonable, mutually incompatible ways of filling in the gaps disagree about the *sign* of the total, not merely its magnitude. And the usual comforts do not apply: the choice is made once, so there is no long run of similar cases in which the errors would average out, and the residual has no expected value we can sign rather than merely a wide error bar. Hence the foundation's governing thought: it may be that we have no idea whether our best-loved interventions are on balance good or bad.

The machinery I want you to invent and use is this. The foundation scores every programme by a Cascade Containment Ratio: the fraction of an intervention's expected impact that its model can trace through enumerated causal pathways of no more than two steps, with everything else booked as unallocated impact and reported, deadpan, as a line item. Its funding rule — call it the Two-Step Rule, or better — is that only programmes scoring above a threshold are funded. I also want a Recursion Clause: the rule exempts itself from its own test, on the stated ground that the effects of adopting it cannot be traced either, and this is presented as a technicality rather than as a problem.

The recoil should come from the portfolio. On the reasoning as given, the foundation has defunded the interventions with the largest and longest-run stakes, because those are exactly the ones whose effects propagate furthest, and has re-funded itself towards small, local, quickly terminating ones whose causal aftermath fits inside two steps. Show this in a table of programmes with their ratios and funding decisions, and let the chair's statement present it as a moral achievement.

Constraints on the execution:

- Roughly 800 words, laid out with the headings and apparatus of a real annual review — chair's statement, methodology note, worked example, table, a closing paragraph.
- The humour has to come from getting the epistemology right. A reader who works on cluelessness should recognise that the author understands the position being satirised, and should ideally find it fair: distinguish cluelessness sharply from ordinary risk and from mere imprecision, and have the foundation reason carefully rather than stupidly. The joke is that careful reasoning gets you here, not that the staff are idiots.
- Deadpan throughout. No character may state an outright objection — in particular, nobody may say in so many words that avoiding cluelessness is itself a choice made under cluelessness. Instead, include a short dissenting minute from one trustee raising a version of that worry, and let the chair dispose of it in a single sentence that conspicuously fails to answer it.
- Do not name or parody any real person, living or dead, and do not target named organisations; the target is a style of argument and a style of institutional discourse.
- It should be legible to an intelligent reader with no prior knowledge of the literature, and funny to one who has it.
- Please write the piece in British English, with British spelling, idiom, and charitable-sector register.
