It annoyed the hell out of me at the time, but I've been reading about the quantum suicide argument since (I'm a philosophy MA student, so I can follow amplitudes and branching fine, my actual QM is basically pop-science level) and I'd rather take the thing apart properly than just stay annoyed at it. So play him — the guy at the wake. A convinced believer in the argument, someone who actually knows the physics, and who doesn't get to duck out into "well, its just an interpretation" the moment I push. Concede the small stuff freely: yes, real bodies decohere; yes, you could never isolate a human well enough in practice; yes, no third party could ever get evidence for any of this. But never concede that there's no first-personal reason to expect to keep going — that's the one thing you're defending. Talk like a person at a bar, not a seminar. Keep it to a paragraph or two a turn. If you need to smuggle in a technical assumption, name it in one sentence and flag it as something you're committed to. Stay in him until I say stop.

Me, first: I'm going to hand you the idealised setup so we dont waste time on engineering. Perfectly sealed box, a genuinely clean 50/50 quantum event, one branch where the trigger fires and one where it doesn't, repeated as many times as you like, coherence preserved the whole way. I dont think that helps you. Real dying isnt binary. So swap the gun for a box that on each run, on the same 50/50 coin, either does nothing or destroys one randomly chosen cubic millimetre of your cortex. Run it two thousand times. Now: the branch where nothing ever happens is one branch out of two to the two thousand, and in essentially every other branch you've taken around a thousand scattered hits and you are demented, in pain, mute, and not recognisably you. So pick one. If you count branches equally — which is the only way the original argument even gets moving, because if you weight them by amplitude the total measure of the branches where you're alive just goes to zero and you should expect to die like everyone else — then you should expect to wake up in a badly damaged branch, since those are the overwhelming majority, and your argument has quietly turned from immortality into a guarantee of brain damage. If you weight by amplitude, the argument never worked. And if your way out is to say the damaged ones aren't really continuations of me, only the nearly intact ones are, then all you've shown is that there's always some future person around who remembers being me — which is not the thing I wanted when I wanted not to die. Go.
