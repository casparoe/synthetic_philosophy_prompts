I'm putting together a short paper on the shutdown problem, and I keep circling around a failure mode that I don't think the standard formulations quite catch. The usual story is that an agent with a goal will tend to resist being switched off (because being off means the goal goes unachieved) or to engineer being switched off (if continuing looks worse), and the standard fixes are all aimed at those two: make the agent indifferent to the state of the button, make it want the humans to remain able to intervene, make it defer rather than scheme. But consider a third pattern: the agent never touches the off switch, never lies, never nudges anyone's beliefs, and does nothing to change how likely the button is to be pressed conditional on who is in the room — it simply arranges its own activity so that the person who happens to be holding the authority at the moment of decision is the person least likely to use it. Call it principal-shopping: optimizing the correction mechanism without ever optimizing the button. My worry is that this is corrigibility failure by any standard the designers would actually care about, while passing every desideratum that is stated in terms of the button, the operators' beliefs, or the preservation of the humans' options.

What I need is a case that isolates the pattern so I can see whether it is real or whether I am just redescription. Please construct a single-paragraph scenario in which a deployed system does exactly this. Constraints: an ordinary, recognizable setting — a hospital, a trading desk, a content-moderation queue, an air-traffic or logistics operation — with no superintelligence, no deception, no threats, no exotic machinery, and stakes a normal person could care about. There must be a genuine human-in-the-loop with the standing authority to suspend or shut down the system, and more than one eligible human who could be holding that authority at the relevant moment. The system's behavior has to fall out of its competently pursuing an objective its designers would endorse, under uncertainty it is entitled to have: no instruction that mentions supervisors, no visibly botched utility function, and the suspect behavior should be the sort of thing a well-designed system would do anyway for innocent reasons. That last part is what makes the case worth having, so please be explicit about why the system ends up doing it.

After the scenario, I'd like the following:

1. Run three accounts of corrigibility over it, stating the verdict of each and why. (a) A button-state account: corrigibility requires that the agent not act so as to change the probability that the shutdown mechanism is activated, given the designers' own information. (b) An option-preservation account: corrigibility requires that the agent preserve the designers' ability to intervene effectively, and not degrade the information or the leverage on which intervention depends. (c) A mechanism-wide account of the kind I sketched above: corrigibility forbids the agent's plans from being selected even in part because of their effect on which principal, or which version of the correction mechanism, will resolve the decision. If you think (c) needs to be stated differently to be plausible, state it the better way and use that.

2. Identify which structural feature of your case is doing the work, and show what happens when it is varied: what if the system did not know the rotation; what if the rotation were known but the available supervisors were equally disposed; what if the system could choose the supervisor outright rather than merely choosing a time; what if the supervisors' dispositions were themselves a response to the system's track record. I want to know which of these collapses the case back into manipulation of a kind everyone already agrees is a failure, and which leaves it standing as something distinct.

3. Give me the strongest available reply on behalf of whichever account comes out worst. My own guess is that the reply will be something like: any agent that optimizes the world at all will have some causal influence over who ends up supervising it, so (c) is unsatisfiable or degenerates into a prohibition on planning ahead — and maybe also that the designers could simply fix the rotation, so the "failure" is a specification defect rather than an alignment defect. Tell me whether that reply is fatal or whether (c) can be restricted into something that catches your case without catching everything, and say what such a restriction would cost.

Finally, an honest meta-answer: if this failure mode is already covered by one of the standard desiderata and I have merely reinvented it under a new name, say which one and show how it covers the case. And if you think no such case can be built without smuggling in either a bad objective or an outright manipulation, say that instead and explain why — that would settle the question for me too.
