I've recently started as the climate policy analyst for a state energy office, and next month I'm presenting to the technical working group that advises our legislature on which decarbonization instrument to adopt. My thesis is that for a subnational jurisdiction that imports much of its electricity and cannot control leakage, the standard prices-versus-quantities argument is close to irrelevant, and that the choice should turn instead on what I call the fiscal half-life of the instrument: a carbon tax's dividend shrinks as its own base erodes, so its constituency has to vote to raise the price exactly when raising it is hardest, while a declining allowance cap with per-capita scarcity-rent dividends pays its constituency more as it tightens and tightens without any further vote. The room will be two Pigouvian environmental economists who regard the tax as the efficient instrument, a political scientist who works on policy feedback and will want an actual causal mechanism rather than a story, an ecological economist sympathetic to degrowth who thinks both instruments presuppose growth in throughput and that only absolute caps encode sufficiency, and an environmental-justice advocate worried about local pollution hotspots under trading. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the objections in the order they will actually come, phrased the way each of these people would phrase them, ranked by how much damage they'd do, with what I concede up front, what I hold, a two-minute reply, and where each reply is weakest — plus the objection I am hoping nobody asks.
