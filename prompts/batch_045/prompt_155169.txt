I am writing a paper on what trading volume tells us about belief, and I need a case built to specification. The background is the familiar no-trade result: if two parties assign probabilities to the relevant states from a common prior, both maximise expected monetary value, and it is common knowledge that they do so and that any trade is voluntary, then no trade can occur unless there is a genuine gain from trade — risk sharing, a liquidity need, or a difference in the use or hedging value of the asset to the two sides. I want a case in which trade happens anyway, with every item on that exempt list switched off, and in which the trade is generated by the decision rules I specify rather than stipulated by you.

Build me a scenario with these properties. One paragraph, an ordinary commercial setting, sums a small business could actually face. No perfect predictors, no demonic devices, no one irrational, confused, coerced or misinformed about the rules. Two parties hold equal shares of a single financial claim on an uncertain payout — for instance a royalty stream, a lease, or a note whose value depends on which of two incompatible expert methods turns out to be right. They have identical evidence: no private information on either side. Both are risk-neutral over sums of this size, neither needs cash, and the claim has no use or hedging value to either of them beyond its payout. Make those facts explicit in the story so that the standard exemptions are visibly closed.

One party prices the claim by a single probability, a credibility-weighted mixture of the two methods. The other prices it by maxmin expected utility over the set consisting of both methods: its reservation price is the lowest expected value in that set. Give real numbers, so that the two reservation prices differ and there is a price at which each party gains by its own lights.

Then do the following. First, derive each reservation price from its rule and exhibit the price interval: the trade must fall out of the rules. Second, run the no-trade inference explicitly — what does the buyer learn from the seller accepting, and the seller from the buyer offering? Identify which premise of the no-trade argument fails in this case, and whether it fails because someone is irrational or for some other reason. Third, say what an outside observer sees, and whether there is any disagreement about the facts anywhere in the case; state precisely what the case is therefore evidence for. Fourth, run a swap test: replace the maxmin party with an expected-value party who simply holds the pessimistic method as a single prior, holding its reservation price fixed. Report what changes and what does not, whether the trade survives, whether any observable difference remains, and what that implies for whether the case supports the claim I want to make. Fifth, name the single feature of the case that is doing the work, and say what happens to the argument if it is removed.

Answer two objections. One: that maxmin is not a coherent rule, and that a dynamically coherent version of the second party would not sell once the fact that the ambiguity will be resolved before the payout is taken into account — so work out what happens to that party's reservation price under such a rule, and what happens to the trade if it moves. Two: that ambiguity aversion is only risk aversion over model uncertainty described differently, in which case the case collapses back into the risk-sharing exemption and shows nothing new.

Close with one paragraph on what a market made of many such pairs would look like from the outside — its volume and price behaviour — and what would have to be true of real traders for the case to matter beyond the page.
