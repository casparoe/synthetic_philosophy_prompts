I've numbered the sentences:

[1] The argument from illusion starts from cases like the straight stick that looks bent in a glass of water: you are aware of something bent, and nothing bent is there in front of you, so what you are aware of cannot be a mind-independent object — it is a sense-datum.
[2] The argument then points out that a stick-look can be subjectively indistinguishable from genuinely seeing a bent stick, and concludes that even in the good case what you are immediately aware of is a sense-datum too, never the stick itself.
[3] Disjunctivists block the argument by denying its common-kind assumption: they hold that genuinely seeing a bent stick and merely hallucinating one are fundamentally different kinds of state.
[4] That means the indistinguishability premise is simply false — if the two states were really indistinguishable there would be nothing for the disjunction to be about.
[5] It also means they are committed to saying that someone hallucinating a bent stick is not aware of anything at all.
[6] And it means they owe us a story about how you can always tell, just by reflecting on your own experience, whether you are perceiving or hallucinating.
[7] The payoff, if the view works, is that perceptual experience puts us in direct contact with mind-independent objects, so the sceptical worry that experience might float free of an external world has no force.
[8] Really the whole view amounts to the claim that in the good case your experience is causally hooked up to the object, which is why common-factor theorists end up saying we never really see tables.

Then, at the end of the chapter, he floats a fix of his own, which he calls "graded disjunctivism": seeing and hallucinating are not two kinds but the two ends of a continuum, and what varies continuously is how much the environment contributes to the state — poor light, half-attended objects, a hallucination of a pink elephant when there happens to be a pink elephant behind you, and, at the mixed end, a case where you really see a mug on the desk while a neuroscientist's stimulation adds a second, hallucinatory mug beside it. On this picture there is no sharp disjunction, so he says he keeps everything the disjunctivist wanted and drops the awkward commitment to two states with nothing mental in common.
