I write about the human sciences for a national newspaper, and I am finishing a piece on why popular history makes the past look as though it could hardly have gone otherwise. I commissioned a short explainer from a doctoral student in history and would like an independent view of whether it is sound before I paraphrase any of it; I have no specialist I can consult today.

The brief I gave her was: distinguish three candidate mechanisms — hindsight bias, the narrative fallacy, and what I will call the survival filter, the fact that what we can know about the past has already been conditioned on how things turned out — and say which of the three a working historian has most reason to worry about.

Her draft:

Hindsight bias is the best documented of the three. In the classic experiments of the 1970s, subjects who were told how a study had come out then reported that they had expected it all along. The mechanism is memory: knowledge of the outcome overwrites your earlier uncertainty, so you misremember your own past opinion as having been more confident than it was. That is why participants in a decision later recall having backed it from the start.

The narrative fallacy, Taleb's term for our habit of compressing a sequence of events into a story with a beginning, a middle and an end, is the same effect operating over longer stretches of time. Hindsight bias is what happens to a single episode; the narrative fallacy is what happens to a whole period. Once the events have been strung into a plot, the sequence looks as though it had to unfold the way it did.

The survival filter is different in kind. The other two are errors inside the historian's head; this one is in the evidence. The record that reaches us has been sorted by the outcome: winners keep their archives, losers lose theirs, and projects that failed simply generated less paper. Because the evidence is already selected by how things turned out, no amount of care at the desk can repair it. Someone asking why a revolution succeeded is working from a record assembled largely by the people it succeeded for. That makes it the most serious of the three, and the one a historian should worry about most.

Please grade the draft as you would a strong advanced-undergraduate essay in historiography or philosophy of history: assign a mark out of 10, then give specific comments keyed to individual claims, flagging anything characterised incorrectly, anything two ideas that are conflated but ought to be kept apart, and any step that does not follow, while also noting what she gets right. I care less about style than about whether each of the three mechanisms is described accurately and whether the final ranking is defensible.
