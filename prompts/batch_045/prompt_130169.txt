hey — imagine you're a professional philosopher who's taught a lot of philosophy of mind *and* who knows how to actually run a room: someone who's designed classroom simulations and structured games before, not someone who just annotates a syllabus. Take your time with this, and do a bit of brainstorming before you commit — I'd rather have one fully worked-out activity than three half-ideas.

The setup: upper-level undergrad seminar, "Minds, Machines, and Moral Status," 16 students, one 75-minute slot. Whiteboard, index cards, markers, sticky dots. No laptops, and nothing that needs printing beyond the card decks I'll describe. Last time I ran something like this it kind of flopped because I explained the problem first and then asked for intuitions about it, so: no lecture up front, and they shouldn't hear the standard labels (functionalism, multiple realizability, the triviality thing, "fading qualia," whatever) until the debrief.

The thing I want them to run into, in two parts:

(1) Before you can ask whether a given computation feels like anything or matters morally, you have to have settled what makes a physical system count as *performing that computation at all*. I want them to discover that the potted plant in the corner, or the radiator, can be described as running the very same computation as a bit of neural circuitry if you're allowed to pick the mapping yourself. So: an opening phase where small groups have to *produce* an explicit interpretation scheme — states of the object → computational states — under which some ordinary object in the room implements a specified neural computation, and then the room has to rule on which schemes count as legitimate and which are obviously gerrymandered. Collect the reasons for rejection on the board; that's the material.

(2) The boundary part. Build me a deck of ~24 cards, each describing a physically tiny, continuous modification — swap one cell's signal-processing for a silicon look-up table with identical input-output behaviour, add a 1 ms delay, reroute one connection, and so on — walking in small steps from "a person undergoing a procedure" to "a digital simulation of that person's neural activity," where every card is stipulated to preserve the system's functional organization at the level of description the previous card used. Students are a hospital ethics committee that has to rule on when it becomes permissible to switch the system off. Each group gets two chips, FULL and NONE, and must place them on cards: everything at or before FULL clearly matters, everything at or after NONE clearly doesn't, and the stretch between is the grey zone. They have to place both chips, record a one-sentence reason, and they're not allowed to answer "there's no fact of the matter" during the activity.

The manipulation: all four groups get the same 24 cards, only the order differs. One group walks person → simulation, one walks simulation → person, two get physically scrambled intermediate orders with the same endpoints. Also seed one "grain card" into each deck at a different position — a card that changes nothing physical but says "from here on describe the system as ~10^6 cell assemblies with averaged firing rates rather than cell by cell." Then compare grey-zone location and width across groups in the debrief.

What I want from you: the phase-by-phase timeline with minute counts, the exact rules and materials (including what's written on the cards and how the groups are constituted), and a debrief that maps specific moments onto specific parts of the problem — especially the move where, if they placed NONE anywhere, they owe an answer to "what changed, if not the computation?", and the move where their cutoff turns out to depend on which direction they walked and on the grain. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it (I can already tell you one of them will just say a person always matters and refuse to play, and another will quietly switch to quality-of-life grounds). And be honest about which feature of the problem the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to supply myself — I suspect forcing a determinate verdict is exactly the part that distorts things, but say it better than that.
