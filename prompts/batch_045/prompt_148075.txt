I'm on a small team that maintains a performance dashboard for a regional health service, and I keep feeling like the thing we've built only makes sense if some fairly specific (and maybe shaky) philosophical claims are true, and I can't work out which.

Here's the setup. Every clinic gets a score computed by a formula we publish a year in advance and then freeze for three years. The score drives funding and appears in public league tables. We also have a tiny integrity unit that investigates gaming. Real examples from the last two years: one clinic changed the point at which the waiting clock starts, from arrival at the building to completion of registration; one started sending complex patients to a neighbouring trust; one parked patients in ambulances outside the entrance so the clock hadn't started yet. All three got in trouble.

But the part that bugs me: we only ever open an investigation because a number moved oddly. Nobody ever checks whether the number tracks anything real. And when a clinic genuinely got better — hired staff, reorganised triage — and its score went up as a side effect, we rewarded it in exactly the same way and by exactly the same mechanism we'd have rewarded the clock-changing clinic if we hadn't caught it. The score is the only thing anyone outside ever sees. Meanwhile we keep a second set of metrics internally that we deliberately never publish, as a check on the public ones, and we're told the public ones have to be frozen precisely because publishing them makes them gameable.

The comparisons that make it worse for me: my brother-in-law is a driving instructor and openly teaches to the test, and nobody treats that as corruption. My kid's school is threatened over its test results and there teaching to the test is treated as a kind of fraud. In basketball nobody thinks the score is standing in for some truer game happening behind it. And a research funder I applied to last year allocated a chunk of its grants by lottery among proposals above a minimal bar, on the explicit grounds that they'd rather be random than gameable.

So my question is what all of this presupposes. Is there supposed to be some metric-independent fact about how well a clinic is doing, and does punishing gaming commit us to there being one, or could we keep the whole practice while thinking the score constitutes quality rather than measuring it? On our own practice, what actually separates gaming from improvement — intent, the process they used, divergence from the secret metrics, whether the improvement would survive a different measure — or is it several different things we've lumped together? Is the secrecy of the shadow metrics in tension with the transparency of the published formula, or do they presuppose the same thing? Which bits of the practice are strictly forced by any view and which just fit comfortably with several? And what would concretely have to change if we went with, say, the score is all there is, versus the target is real but no measure of it should ever be frozen, versus randomness is more honest than any measure — in particular, does punishing gaming survive any of those?

I don't know the philosophy words for any of this so please don't assume I do, and I'm not looking for citations — just the shape of the view we're already committed to, and where it's defintely weaker than we act like it is.
