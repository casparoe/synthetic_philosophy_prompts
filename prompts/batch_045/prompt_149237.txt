Our philosophy reading group — mostly retired lab scientists plus one very patient philosopher — has got onto AI, and the analogy one of the younger members keeps using is this: an intelligence explosion is just autocatalysis, or a chain reaction. The thing makes more of itself, each generation produces more than one generation, once it's critical the whole thing is over in an afternoon, and therefore there's no point planning to steer it as it goes: all the value-loading has to be finished before anyone switches it on. That conclusion is what he's using it for, and it's the "fast takeoff vs slow takeoff" argument in our group, of which I've now heard far too much.

I spent thirty years on reaction kinetics, so I half love this picture and half don't. Can you audit it?

What maps onto what, first, and I want the correspondence actually written out, because I think it shifts depending on the choices: is the neutron a design cycle, a training run, or a unit of insight? What plays the role of critical mass, of k_eff, of generation time, and what would the moderator and the fuel be?

Then the bit that worries me. Criticality and speed aren't the same thing, and the analogy seems to smuggle one in under cover of the other. The natural fission reactors at Oklo in Gabon went critical around 1.7 billion years ago and then ran off and on for a few hundred thousand years at perhaps 100 kW — a critical system, and nothing like an afternoon. So even granting "it goes critical", the pace has to come from somewhere else, presumably the generation time. What sets that, and is the loop fundamentally serial in a way neutrons aren't — you can't run the experiment until you've had the idea — or is it parallelisable, in sim, the way a chain reaction is?

Second worry, which I think of as the rate constant problem. In my reactions the catalyst speeds things up but doesn't get better at being a catalyst; the rate constant was a constant. Here the quantity that's growing is the thing that sets the rate. That looks to me like it should be hyperbolic rather than exponential — super-exponential, with a finite-time blow-up, so that the total time from roughly-human to wildly-beyond is some modest multiple of the first doubling, not an unbounded climb. Am I over-reading my own maths, or does the chain-reaction picture actually understate the case?

And then saturation. Autocatalytic curves in a flask are sigmoids: lag, log phase, then flattening as reagent runs out and product inhibits. The slow-takeoff people seem to be quietly betting on that stationary phase — data exhaustion, diminishing returns to scale. But is there a reagent here at all? A molecule can't go and find more; this thing could in principle go and get more compute, invent a new pathway, acquire a new input. Is the sigmoid a real feature of the target or an artifact of importing a closed vessel?

The practical question I want tested is the one my group member drew from it: takeoff too fast to steer, so incremental deployment and mid-course correction aren't a strategy. How much of that is actually carried by the analogy, and how much survives if I throw the picture out? If it's the wrong picture, is there a better one — feedback and a timescale, but no fixed reagent? Don't cite anything at me, I just want the argument, and please tell me straight if my chemistry is leading me up the garden path rather than being polite about it.
