I had a long argument with a friend last night about whether there is really such a thing as a *commitment race*, and I want to settle part of it by translating the idea into a vocabulary where I can test it instead of arguing about it.

By a commitment race I mean a situation in which two or more parties compete to be the first to make a move that is irreversible, visible to the others, and valuable to them precisely because it destroys options the mover would otherwise have had. The point of the move is not to acquire a capability but to remove one's own future freedom to do otherwise, so that the other party, still free, has to accommodate. That is meant to distinguish it from three neighbouring things: an arms race, where what is accumulated is capability; Chicken, where a single symmetric encounter leaves both parties their options until the last instant; and a coordination race, where being first is valuable because it sets a shared standard everyone then converges on. The concept is supposed to rest on four structural claims:

1. The meta-game and the ground game have different shapes and come apart in time. Investing in the *capacity* to commit quickly looks like a prisoner's dilemma — each side wants the capacity whatever the other does — while actually committing looks like Chicken. So there is a two-phase structure: a reversible, comparatively cheap, partly deniable investment phase, feeding into an irreversible and public execution phase.
2. A commitment is worth nothing unless the other side recognises it. The race is therefore also a race to be *seen* committing first, and credibility is a function of what the other party believes about your remaining options, which is why costliness, publicity and irreversibility do the real work.
3. The outcome can be worse for everyone: if both sides commit, both may be worse off than under mutual restraint, even though entering the race was individually rational for each.
4. Because the prize goes to whoever commits first, there is pressure to shorten deliberation, and shortening deliberation raises the chance of committing by mistake or of committing to something one would not have chosen with full information. The race degrades the quality of the very decision it concerns.

My friend's position is that the concept is doing no independent work: that it is Chicken with an investment preamble bolted on, or an arms race described from an unflattering angle; that the irreversibility is illusory, since any commitment can in principle be undone by a sufficiently large later action, so a commitment is really a lease with an unstated expiry; and that any translation into my field will look impressive only because my field already uses the word commit for something else.

I am a staff-level distributed systems engineer. I work daily with Raft, two-phase and three-phase commit, leases, fencing tokens, optimistic concurrency control and quorum systems; interleavings, contention and failure modes are what I am paid to think about. I have never taken a course in game theory. Please do not cite anything, name papers, or give me a reading list — I want the argument, not the literature, and any economics term you use should be defined in a clause as you go.

What I want is a faithful translation of the four claims above into my vocabulary, one claim at a time, and then an honest account of what the translation fails to carry. A few constraints. Do not explain my own vocabulary back to me. Do flag explicitly whenever you are using commit in my sense rather than in the strategic sense, because I suspect the shared word is doing hidden work and I want to know how much of the mapping survives once that is stripped out. Run two examples side by side throughout: two divisions of a company racing to be the first to land an irreversible migration on a shared data platform, and two processes contending for a lease or an epoch in a replicated state machine where neither process wants anything at all. That second case is the one that matters to me: if the structural identity holds when the racers have no preferences, the mechanism is real and my friend is wrong about the concept; if it only holds when the racers can want things, then at least part of what I was defending is a fact about agency rather than about races.

Please structure your reply in four named sections, in this order:

- **Translation** — roughly 700 words: each of the four claims restated in distributed-systems terms, as precisely as you can manage without importing assumptions my framework does not have.
- **Where the translation strains** — roughly 300 words: the places where you had to bend something to make it fit, including the commit ambiguity and the lease-versus-permanent question.
