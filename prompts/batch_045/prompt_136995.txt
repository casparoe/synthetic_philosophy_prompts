I'm in the upper sixth — biology, economics and maths — and I help run a small after-school discussion group where we argue about whatever we've been reading that week, so I end up meeting the same kinds of explanation in very different clothes. Over the past month I've noticed one shape over and over, and not having a word for it is starting to get in the way, because I keep wanting to point at it and end up waving my hands. In biology we were told the cell 'reads' the instructions in its DNA and 'decides' which proteins to build. In a documentary, a ant colony 'knows' where the best food is because the scouts 'report' it. In an economics video, prices 'tell' everyone what is scarce, so the market 'learns'. None of these is the flat version of the mistake I already know how to object to — nobody says 'life wants to survive' or 'the colony intends to be efficient', restating the thing to be explained in slightly different words and stopping. Each of these stories has genuine moving parts: it breaks one messy process into smaller steps you could go and check, and it isn't circular in any simple sense, because the conclusion isn't the premise. But each verb — read, decide, know, report, tell, learn — seems to be on loan from the very thing we were trying to account for, and if the loan were called in, what would be left is a list of chemical events that no one would say 'knows' anything. Compare the honeycomb: you get the hexagons out of surface tension and area-minimisation, and nothing in that story has to read or want or know anything. That one stands on its own feet. The others don't, and I can't say why without shrugging.

What makes it slippery, though, is that in the ant and market cases there is no single part that does the knowing. There isn't a little agent tucked inside the colony or inside the price system holding the information; the competence is spread across lots of dumb parts and then attributed to the collection, and the collection is nothing over and above the parts interacting. Sometimes that strikes me as perfectly legitimate — I half suspect the market case is fine, and that the 'knowing' is just a summary of something that really does get built out of the interactions — and sometimes it strikes me as a trick, and I can't tell the difference in a principled way. There's also a mirror-image thing I'd like placed: occasionally the small-parts story is perfectly good, and someone then announces that the big thing was never really there at all. A friend told me after we did neurons that wanting anything is 'nothing but' a firing pattern. I felt cheated but couldn't say why, since he hadn't obviously got the science wrong — he'd just zoomed in until the thing I started out interested in had quietly left the frame.

So: does the first pattern have a name — one established name, or several competing ones — the bottom-up explanation whose explanatory force comes from a word that only makes sense from the top down? If it does, I don't just want the label, I want the carving: what exactly has to be true for the pattern to be present, and how it differs from the neighbouring things it keeps getting confused with — from merely restating the phenomenon, from hiding a smaller version of the agent inside the system, from straight circularity, and from that zoom-in-until-it-vanishes move I described at the end. If several established notions each fit part of it, tell me which fits best and where each one stops fitting my examples. If nothing fits, I'd rather you coin a term with a definition and some boundary cases than gesture vaguely, because I want to be able to bring a new example to our next session and have the others agree on how it classifies. Give me one actual test I can apply to a candidate explanation to tell whether the borrowed word is doing real work or quietly smuggling.
