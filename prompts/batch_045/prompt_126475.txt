I'm applying for a twelve-month dissertation fellowship and need a two-page proposal. The background is personal: my grandmother died last year after a long illness, and several relatives offered it as comfort that she must have earned it in an earlier life. I found that grotesque — and then, a few months later, when someone close to me was badly wronged, I caught my own first reflex being that he would get what was coming to him, with no account at all of why that would be good, or good for whom. So the question I keep circling is whether karma is a claim about moral desert in the first place, or whether the familiar reaping-and-sowing picture is really only a causal and educative thesis — that wrongdoing deforms character and eventually issues in bad consequences — with no desert claim anywhere in it.

My half-formed idea is that the dispute stalls because desert is being treated as a single thing. I want to split it: desert as a debt, which is owed to someone and which someone has reason to settle, versus desert as bare fittingness, on which the wrongdoer is simply the sort of being whose life appropriately goes badly, with nothing owed to anyone and no one under any obligation to arrange it. Karma is the sharp test case, because it looks like desert with no administrator, no claimant, and — in the rebirth versions — no psychological continuity between the one who acts and the one who suffers; and because on most tellings its bookkeeping is humanly unfathomable, which makes it hard to see how the claim could ever guide action rather than only rationalise what has already happened. Please turn this into a proposal that states one precise question, explains why both the standard causal-educative reading and the standard retributivist alternatives in Western ethics leave it open, gives a method — what I would actually do month by month across the year — and says why that method rather than straight conceptual analysis or close textual work, sets out the two or three possible conclusions and which considerations would decide between them, describes what a finished chapter would have to contain to count as having answered the question, and is honest about what would count as the project failing, including whether it is simply too big for one year. Two constraints: the committee is mostly general ethicists with no background in Indian philosophy, so the question has to be legible to them without leaning on Sanskrit terminology; and I will supply every citation myself, so don't cite anything or gesture at a literature I should be reading. Add a short closing note saying how confident you are in the framing you have given me, and naming the single claim in it you would be least willing to defend in front of that committee.
