I manage a three-person outreach team at a city benefits-access office, and most weeks we send short letters, texts, and phone scripts to residents who appear eligible for Medicaid, SNAP, or a utility-arrearage program but haven't enrolled. The decisions I need help with come up constantly — we produce roughly forty message variants a quarter, each reviewed in a single twenty-minute slot by two staff members with no philosophy or behavioral-science training, with me signing off and anything contested going to our director. Because our funder audits a sample of these messages and asks for the reasoning, whatever procedure we use has to end in a short written rationale, and it has to degrade gracefully: we usually have no A/B test, no idea why an earlier message worked, and very little information about the individual recipient's actual circumstances, literacy, or stress level. A disagreement last month made this concrete for me. We sent a letter saying that neighbors in the recipient's ZIP code had already renewed and that they should confirm by Friday to keep their coverage — the deadline was invented and there is no coverage to lose, since the letter goes to people who were never enrolled. My colleague thinks this is obviously fine because it moves people into benefits they're entitled to and that we know are good for them; I think something is wrong with it that isn't captured by "does it work" or "is anyone lied to," and I'd rather have a procedure than keep having this argument.

Reading around on my own, the best core idea I've found is that persuasion engages the target's reasons while manipulation somehow bypasses or subverts them — but I can't hand that to my staff, because the hard question in each case is precisely whether a given technique is tracking the recipient's reasons or going around them. The operational version I've been toying with is a kind of flip test: if the program were mildly bad for this particular resident, or if their own reasons genuinely pointed against enrolling, would the same technique still push them to enroll anyway? A deadline and a social-proof claim would still work in that flipped case, so on my test they aren't tracking reasons; a sentence explaining that the program covers a specific drug the recipient is taking would stop working, so it is. I'm not confident this is the right test rather than a proxy that mostly tracks deception or the sheer effectiveness of the technique, and I'd like you to keep it if it survives and replace it with something better if it doesn't. Please write my team a decision procedure built on whatever the philosophy of this distinction actually supports — ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no and sends the message back for redrafting, and where it explicitly hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts (for instance, whether enrolling people in benefits they're entitled to can justify a means we've already classified as manipulative). It has to be runnable in twenty minutes by a non-specialist, so any term it uses needs an operational test attached rather than a definition — give me a check for "bypasses the recipient's reasons" that doesn't presuppose the answer. It also has to say what to do when a step can't be answered at all, as will often happen when we know almost nothing about the recipient. Please tell me which distinctions have to survive the simplification and which you dropped as unusable at our level, and be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Run it on four live cases and show the work: (1) the invented-deadline social-proof letter above; (2) a reply card on which "yes, enroll me" is pre-checked, so that doing nothing enrolls you; (3) a phone script that opens with what the household loses by not enrolling and states a funding deadline that is genuinely true — I expect the procedure to tell me something here that I will not like, and I'd rather see that than have it quietly tailored to spare my current practice; and (4) a case where the recipient is an eighty-year-old with low literacy and probable mild cognitive impairment, where the procedure should either give an answer or stop and explain why it can't. Please don't give me a literature review, citations, or reading suggestions; I have no library access and only want the operationalized procedure, worked through, plus an honest final note on what the procedure cannot settle and where it will misfire.
