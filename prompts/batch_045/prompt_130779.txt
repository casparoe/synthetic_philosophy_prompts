I run a weekly student reading group on decision theory — mostly CS majors with a few philosophy people who wandered in — and we get 75 minutes in a room with a tiny whiteboard and a projector that has never once worked. Last week we did Newcomb's problem and it went okayish: maybe a third of them left convinced that one-boxing is just wishful thinking in a tuxedo. This week I want to do acausal trade, meaning the idea that two agents who cannot affect each other, observe each other, or communicate might still cooperate because their decisions are correlated through the similarity of their decision procedures rather than through any causal link or shared cause. My co-organizer says this is literally just one-boxing with extra steps and we should spend the session on Bayesian epistemology instead, and I half see his point but can't artiuclate what's wrong with it well enough to teach from.

So don't explain acausal trade to me — plan how I should explain it to them. What I really want is the dependency structure: the order the pieces have to go in and what depends on what. My instinct is that I need them to first accept a case where your action is strong evidence about an outcome without causing it, and only then get them to a case where the correlation isn't explained by a common cause or by somebody simulating them either — but I don't know how to make that second move feel like a real step rather than a sleight of hand, and I worry that starting from Newcomb just replays last week's fight.

I also need one example to carry the whole session. Here's the one I sketched: two agents, one in each of two universes that will never interact; each has 100 units of its own resource; each can burn k units of its own resource to manufacture 4k units of the good the other one values; unspent units are worth 1 util to their owner and the other's good is worth 1 util per unit to the other. Each is fairly but not fully confident the other exists. And the bit I added to make it hard: the two agents run the same architecture and style of reasoning, but one's utility function has been edited to value the other colour of widget, and their confidence that a counterpart exists differs (say 0.8 versus 0.5). They cannot send messages, so they cannot haggle over how much each burns — each just picks a number in private. Tell me whether that carries the session, what it is missing, what it has that it shouldn't, and why not the usual candidates: the exact-copy prisoner's dilemma, the old puzzle where a prize goes to you only if exactly one of twenty people asks for it, or two galaxy-spanning civilisations that value paperclips and staples. Should I start symmetric and then break the symmetry, or start asymmetric and let the copy case show up as a special case?

Then the misconception map, with the point in the sequence where each should be headed off — and I mean headed off, not just corrected afterwards. The ones I expect: that this is a promise, a reputation effect, or iterated cooperation in disguise; that my choice somehow causally pushes the other agent's choice; that correlation without a common cause is incoherent so the whole setup is broken (this one will come from the strongest CS student); that it's still just evidential decision theory so nothing new is on the table; that it only works if the other agent is literally running my code, and the opposite error that any sufficiently reasonable agent will do the same thing; that cooperating with a causal-decision-theory counterpart is still worth something; and that this is Pascal's mugging with better branding, a licence to trade with any alien you can imagine. Tell me which of these I should actually invite rather than prevent, and flag any you think I'll get that I haven't listed.

Finally: what should I openly refuse to explain at this level and say out loud that I'm refusing — I'm thinking the problem of how to weight which computations count as existing, logical uncertainty, updatelessness, whether copies in other branches of quantum mechanics are the same kind of thing as distant civilisations — and which of those I should name-and-drop versus never mention at all; plus one question I can ask at minute seventy where I could actually tell a right answer from a wrong one, so I know whether the idea landed or whether they just got talked into something.
