I hold a specific, three-part version of the position that gets discussed under the label of the gender-equality paradox, and I want it stress-tested rather than summarized back to me.

The pattern: in countries that score high on composite gender-equality indices of the kind published annually by the World Economic Forum, women make up a *smaller* share of STEM entrants and of some high-status occupations than they do in a number of lower-scoring, less affluent countries. Take that stylized contrast as given for the sake of argument; I don't want the answer spent on re-litigating whether some particular dataset shows the correlation.

The mechanism I favour, which I'll call constraint-relaxation amplification: as material constraints loosen and the feasible option set widens, occupational and field-of-study choices come to track individual-level dispositions more closely and to track survival or respectability payoffs less closely. Where the underlying disposition distributions differ even slightly between the sexes — things-versus-people orientation, tolerance for competitive grading, willingness to trade income for schedule flexibility, or whatever the relevant dimensions turn out to be — closer tracking mechanically produces *larger* between-group differences in aggregate outcomes, and it does so most visibly in the tails of those distributions, where small mean or variance differences have the largest consequences for who clears a threshold. On this story, expanding opportunity does not erase group differences in choices; it reveals them, and it reveals them more sharply the more choice there is.

The upshot I draw: gaps of this kind are not by themselves evidence of lingering injustice or of failed policy. A society can satisfy everything I care about procedurally — no legal barriers, no exclusion, genuine option expansion, no differential returns to the same credentials — and still display large occupational sex differences; and, symmetrically, a society with small gaps is not thereby more just, since narrow gaps can be produced by necessity rather than freedom.

Here is what I want. Map the exposure of this package, and say for each item which of the three parts it attacks, because I suspect some defeaters kill the upshot while leaving the mechanism standing, and others do the reverse.

Rank the defeaters from fatal through serious to merely awkward, and separate the ones I should take seriously from the ones that only look strong. In particular I want a verdict on three that I can't size up myself:

The selection-or-denominator worry. In low-equality countries relatively few women participate in higher education or the formal labour market at all, so the ones who do are a positively selected subset, and their field distribution is not comparable to the field distribution of a population where nearly everyone participates. This looks to me like a boring statistical quibble and I suspect it is actually one of the two most dangerous objections.

The time-series test. My mechanism is about a causal process, but the headline evidence is cross-sectional. If countries that became substantially more gender-equal over recent decades did not show widening gaps *within* themselves, my story loses its best evidence while remaining perfectly consistent with the cross-section. Tell me how much of a problem that is, and what the corresponding within-country prediction of the rival explanations would be.

The tail-location prediction. My mechanism says the amplification happens at the tails. So I am committed to the gaps being concentrated in fields and roles that are selective or extreme along the disposition dimension — elite quantitative programmes, high-variance careers, the top of the earnings distribution — and to being much weaker in the mass of the distribution and in fields with no selectivity at all. If the paradox turned out to be just as strong for ordinary, unselective credentials, I think I would have to give up the mechanism while keeping the pattern. Say whether you agree that this is where the load-bearing commitment sits.

Then build me the measurements that would settle it, since I want to know what I would have to go and run. Two I have in mind, and I want you to say which pattern in each would count against me and which would not:

Conditional choice sensitivity: the elasticity of a school-leaver's field choice with respect to a change in that field's relative earnings or employment prospects, estimated separately by sex and separately by country. My view predicts something specific about how this elasticity should differ between high- and low-equality settings, and about whether it should differ between the sexes. What is the prediction, and what result would be fatal rather than merely surprising?

Preference-versus-realization divergence: compare stated aspirations and self-reported enjoyment, measured in a way that is comparable across countries, with realized field and occupation. If high-equality countries show *converging* aspirations alongside *diverging* realizations, that seems to me to move the causal weight from preferences to something in the selection environment, and I take that to be fatal for my upshot even though the mechanism could survive in a weaker form. If instead aspirations diverge in step with realizations, how much does that actually help me? I am not certain it helps as much as I'd like, and I would rather be told so.

Also do the following three things, which I think are where answers to questions like this usually fail.

Say what a defender of my position would themselves concede as counting against it, not only what critics say counts against it. I want the concessions, and I want them separated from the moves a defender would be within their rights to dismiss.

Tell me honestly whether the position has been protected into unfalsifiability by its own qualifications, and if it has, whether that is a genuine defect here. This is the part I am most unsure about. It is open to me to say that "gender equality" means the kind of equality that frees preferences from constraint, that any residual preference is by stipulation authentic, and that any remaining gap is therefore not an injustice — and I can say all of that while remaining consistent with every possible observation. Compare this to how similar insulation is treated elsewhere: sometimes it is a fatal confession, sometimes it is the correct posture for a claim that is doing conceptual rather than empirical work, and I want to know which it is in this case.

Finally, rank the whole set once more by what *I* should find persuasive, given that the two considerations I actually weight most heavily are within-country change over time and any evidence about whether the gaps live in the tails. If you think I am weighting the wrong things, say so and say what I should be weighting instead.
