I'm on a small "behavioural insights" team at a regional health insurer — four of us, and realistically we get to design and ship about three interventions a year, mostly defaults and reordering of options on enrolment forms. Lately we've been asked for a different kind of thing and I've been going back and forth about it, and I'd rather spend the little thinking time we have on the questions that actually decide whether what we're doing is defensible than on the ones that just feel philosophical.

Here's the distinction that got me stuck. Most of what we do is first-order: we change a default on a form, the person either keeps it or opts out, and the usual defence seems to apply — it's easy to get around, and we think it serves the chooser's own ends, so it isn't paternalism in the worrying sense. But a few of the things we've been asked for work differently. Instead of targeting a single decision, they target the procedure by which someone will decide a whole class of things over time: a portal that always surfaces the cheapest clinically-equivalent option first and gradually gets people into the habit of looking for it, or a reminder system that trains people to re-check an annual choice rather than let it roll over. The point of these isn't to defer to an existing preference, it's to shape how the preference gets formed in the first place, and they're meant to keep working even after the person has noticed what we're up to. (I've heard something like this called a "boost", but as I understand it a boost is supposed to build a skill the person then uses knowingly, whereas these are meant to work partly through habit and partly without the person tracking the mechanism — so maybe it's not the same thing, or maybe it is and I'm confused.) A colleague of mine argues that the opt-out defence only covers the first kind, because it presupposes a settled preference that the nudge defers to, and a second-order intervention is upstream of that. I half beleive her and half think it's a distinction without a difference: a first-order default also shapes preferences over time, if people just let it roll.

Anyway. Assume I've got maybe a year of part-time attention from four non-philosophers, and I want us to get as clear as we can on whether and when this second-order stuff is justified, before we ship more of it. What are the open questions you'd tell us to prioritise, and in what order? I really don't want a survey of the field or a reading list — I can't look anything up at the moment, so anything I'd need a citation for is useless to me. What I want is the reasoning behind the ordering: for each question, why it's upstream of the others, whether four of us could actually make progress on it in a year or whether it's the kind of thing that only produces confident assertion, and what we'd have to see — an argument, a hypothetical case, a bit of our own data — to move it up or down the list. Please also say which questions you'd drop as fake problems or distractions; I suspect we're burning time on at least one of them. And be explicit about how confident you are in the ranking as a whole, where you think it's most likely to be wrong, and which single consideration, if you found it convincing, would most change the order.
