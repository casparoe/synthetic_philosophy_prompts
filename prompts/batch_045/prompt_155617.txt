I've been reading up on the old Hart–Devlin fight on my own, the one about whether the state can criminalise something just because it's immoral even when nobody is hurt, and what gets me is that it hasn't moved since the 1960s. Everyone knows everyone's arguments and nobody budges.

My theory is that a lot of what sounds like principle is really a fight about facts that neither side has. Devlin seems to think a shared public morality is load bearing, that if you stop enforcing it the whole social order starts to come apart, and Hart basically answers that we're sturdier than that, and that the cases people actually worried about — the Wolfenden stuff, homosexuality and prostitution — can be handled under the idea that the only thing that justifies force over someone is stopping harm to someone else. If that's right, the debate is stalled because it's secretly an empirical question about how fragile a moral consensus is, and nobody has the data.

But I've also started to think there's a ratchet: enforcement doesn't just express a consensus, it manufactures one. If X is illegal, people grow up treating X as shameful partly *because* it's illegal, so the consensus Devlin wants to point at as evidence is partly a product of the enforcement he wants to justify. Which means the empirical question can't be answered cleanly at all — whatever you'd measure has already been contaminated.

So what kind of disagreement is this really: a verbal one, a hidden empirical one, a bedrock clash about what counts as a reason to coerce somebody, or two people answering seperate questions under one heading? And given whichever it is, what would even count as progress? I want a diagnosis with reasons, not a survey of the two positions.
