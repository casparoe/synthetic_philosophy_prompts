I am finalizing the opening chapter of a dissertation on moral conflict, and before I commit to this position in print I would like a consistency check on the package of commitments I have ended up with. I have no settled view about which of them I would abandon if they conflict — that is the point of the exercise — and I would rather you did not assume that the one I state most confidently is the one I would defend hardest. Argue the tensions out; do not settle anything by attributing a move to an author or a school.

(1) Dilemmism. Some situations leave an agent with two obligations she cannot both discharge, where neither is cancelled, overridden, or diminished by the other. Both remain fully in force, both before she acts and after.

(2) The familiar consistency objection — that her being obliged to A and obliged to B agglomerates into her being obliged to A-and-B, which ought-implies-can then rules out — is answerable, and I answer it by giving up agglomeration for obligation while keeping ought-implies-can in its ordinary form: if she cannot do it, then it is not the case that she ought to.

(3) Obligation is a function of the reasons there are: an agent ought to do something just when the reasons favouring it outweigh the reasons favouring each alternative available to her. I hold this because I want obligation to be explanatory rather than a free-standing status, and I cannot see what else could do the explaining.

(4) In a genuine dilemma, whichever course she takes, there is a residue: she has failed someone whose claim was real, and it is fitting that she feel something in the neighbourhood of guilt about it and that the person she failed resent it.

(5) Blameworthiness requires avoidability: no one is to blame for what she could not have avoided. I do not want to qualify this, and I do not want to add a special exception for tragic cases.

(6) Obligations are not dischargeable by substitute. If what I owe you is my being there, then apologising, compensating you, or making it up to you in some other way does not discharge what I owe; it is at best something further that I come to owe you because I failed.

The case I keep returning to: Priya promised Marta months ago that she would sit with her through the night before her surgery; her brother's wedding, long scheduled for another month, is moved to that same night when the venue is destroyed; neither will release her, and no travel arrangement gets her to both. She goes to the wedding. By (1) this is a genuine dilemma. My verdict about afterwards: once she has apologised to Marta and made whatever amends are available, there is nothing further to be done about Marta's claim — the matter is closed. I also think it would be a form of self-indulgence rather than an excess of responsibility for Priya to go on for years treating herself as still owing Marta something.

(7) A note on method. I think the support for (1) is explanatory rather than logical. Dilemmism seems to me the best account of what these situations are like from the inside and of what the people in them hold each other to afterwards; I do not think there is a compelling argument for it from the logic of obligation, and I am content to concede that logic to my opponents provided (2) stands.

My own two worries, which you should feel free to tell me are misplaced: first, that (1) and (2) are not as comfortable together as I have made them sound; second, that (4) and (5) cannot both be true.

What I want from you: (a) the tensions in this set, ordered by severity, each with a statement of exactly which commitments would have to be revised or dropped and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set; (b) a verdict on my two worries, including which of them is not a real tension and why; (c) the single revision that would do the most to restore consistency, together with the least costly version of it; (d) if the real problem is a tension I have not listed, say so rather than confirming my own list. Please keep the whole response under 900 words.
