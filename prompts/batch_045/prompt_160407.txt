Our food co-op (about 400 members) is picking how to elect its board, and we've narrowed it to instant-runoff, approval, and score voting. One guy on the committee — Dave, an ex-physics type — keeps shutting down the strategy-proofness discussion with an analogy: voting rules are like map projections. Mercator keeps shape and wrecks area, equal-area does the reverse, and no flat map keeps everything. Gibbard–Satterthwaite, he says, is just the same point in social choice: every rule distorts somewhere, so there's no "undistorted" option to prefer, and we should drop strategy-proofness as a criterion entirely and decide on simplicity, ballot length, how annoying it is to count by hand, and so on.

Can you audit that analogy for us? Concretely:

- Give the correspondence table — what plays the globe, what plays the projection, what plays "area" and "shape," what plays "distortion," and who the map user is. I want to see whether there's anything on the voting side corresponding to the fact that a projection of a *small* region can be made arbitrarily close to exact.
- Then the breakdowns, split into the ones that actually matter for the decision Dave is trying to make and the ones that are just noise. Things I'm suspicious about, in no particular order: GS is an existence claim (some voter, some profile, gains by lying) and carries no magnitude, whereas map distortion is graded and measurable; map distortion is shared and visible to everyone, while manipulability mostly transfers value from honest voters to well-informed ones and is invisible after the fact; there's no external "correct outcome" for an election to be faithful to, the way there's a globe for a map; and the escapes from GS look categorical rather than gradational (dictatorship, two alternatives, restricted preference domains where something like the median rule is strategy-proof, rules that randomize, cardinal ballots that aren't rankings).
- One of us read that there's a companion result covering rules that pick the winner by lottery, which would close the randomization escape, and that it also covers schemes where voters report intensities rather than rankings — so approval and score wouldn't dodge it the way Dave thinks. Check whether that's right, and tell us whether it changes anything.
- Finally: does Dave's conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it? If it doesn't survive, is there a better analogy for what GS actually tells you — and what would have to be measurable (share of profiles where anyone gains, expected size of the gain, how much a liar needs to know about other people's ballots) for a trade-off picture like his to be legitimate at all?
