I teach a 26-student undergraduate PPE seminar on markets and planning, and I want to replace last year's session — a prepared debate that spent twenty minutes on anecdotes about the Soviet Union and then collapsed into everyone restating their prior. I have one 90-minute slot, a room with movable tables, index cards, and no projector, and the students will already have read the core material, so I don't want a recap or any framing lecture up front: I want them to run into the mechanism themselves and only be given the names afterwards. Please design a runnable activity in which (1) the knowledge needed for a decent allocation is genuinely dispersed, and at least partly not the kind of thing that can be written down in a report — each participant holds local information about their own situation that changes between rounds and can surface only in how they respond to numbers they are quoted; (2) a small group (3–4 students) plays a Central Planning Board that announces a price vector before each round and may revise it between rounds, but learns about shortages and surpluses only from the arithmetic of orders and returns, in the spirit of the market-socialist "trial and error" proposal associated with Lange; (3) firms have a hidden quality choice on at least one good — they can make a durable version or a shoddy version at different real cost, and the Board's feedback reports quantities, never quality; (4) partway through, a card is drawn that turns a previously worthless by-product into an essential input for a product nobody had been producing, so the announced price vector has no row for the new use yet; and (5) at least one good is a durable machine built in an early round and used in a later one, so an early misallocation can't simply be undone. Give me the rules, the materials, the role assignments, a payoff scheme that needs no real money (chocolate or a leaderboard is fine) and that the Board can score by hand in under two minutes per round, a minute-by-minute timeline, and an explicit statement of what the Board is and is not allowed to say or ask for.

Then give me the debrief: map specific moments in the activity onto Mises's claim that without money prices for the factors of production the planners have no common denominator for comparing heterogeneous inputs and techniques, onto Hayek's claim that the relevant knowledge is dispersed, local, temporal and often not statable, and onto the market-socialist reply that a Board could in principle simulate a market by announcing prices and adjusting in response to excess demand. For each of the three, say whether the activity actually generated evidence for that claim or merely staged it. I also want the participants to end up feeling both sides: that the "just let the planners iterate on prices" reply has real force, and that the surprise card and the hidden quality dial are where it starts to strain. Finally, be honest about what the game cannot reproduce and what I will have to supply in the last fifteen minutes — entrepreneurship and speculation, real time preference and investment horizons, the sheer scale and heterogeneity of an actual capital structure, and the fact that the managers in my classroom have to be instructed to act as if they were profit-maximizers. Include what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (the Board will simply ask everyone what they need; someone will hoard; the quality dial will be ignored because nobody is rewarded for using it; the arithmetic will take three times longer than I think) with rule-level fixes for each, and tell me which of my five mechanics to cut first if we are twenty minutes behind.
