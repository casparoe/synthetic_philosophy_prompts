ok so my friend and i got into a three hour argument last night about whether qualia are a coherent posit at all and i woke up still annoyed because i think i built an argument that works too well. i'd been reading the Chase / Sanborn quining-qualia line — the idea that once you strip out everything a sensory state does (what it discriminates, what it's confusable with, what it makes you say), there's no coherent remainder left to be the "qualitative character" — and i tried to make it into something sharper using quality spaces. tell me where it breaks.

(1) A sensory quality just *is* a position in a quality space: what makes your experience one of *this* shade rather than that one is exhausted by its relations of similarity and difference to your other sensory states. (I like this premise because the realist seems stuck with it too — if a quale isn't located in a structure of similarities, there's nothing to say which quale it is, and the inverted spectrum case collapses into word salad.)

(2) The only grip we have on a subject's quality space is her judgments: which pairs she can tell apart, which pairs she rates as more similar, how confusable things get under noise.

(3) But those judgments aren't metric. People judge a to be like b more than b to be like a; the triangle inequality fails; which pairs look similar shifts with what else is in the stimulus set and with adaptation state. Sanborn's version of the point, as I understood it, is that the space you recover from similarity data isn't forced to be Euclidean or even metric at all. So there's no *unique* space fitting a subject's judgments — a whole family of incommensurable spaces fits equally well, and no amount of further judgment data settles which.

(4) And a quality that isn't pinned down by the structure isn't pinned down by anything. The "intrinsic residue" was supposed to be the bit that makes a quale more than its relational profile, but there's nothing you can say about the residue: it doesn't affect what she can discriminate, what she'll say, or what she'll do. So there's no determinate fact about which quality a given experience instantiates.

(5) If there's no determinate fact about which quality is instantiated, there's no determinate fact about what it's like for her. So the posit is empty — quined.

My friend's reductio, which is the part that's bugging me: run (2)–(4) on anything else. Object colors: recoverable only from comparative judgments, non-metric, context-dependent — so apparently no determinate fact about what colour the wall is. Word meanings from distributional similarity: no determinate fact what anyone means. Preferences from noisy choices: no determinate fact what anyone wants. Something that general can't be an argument against qualia specifically, so either it's a global scepticism nobody wants or one of the steps is smuggling.

The other thing he kept pushing: suppose two spaces S1 and S2 fit all her discriminations and all her reports. What makes it the case she's in S1? If "she can just tell introspectively," that's a judgment, already in the data, so it can't separate them. If "the quale itself does it," you've posited something that by construction answers to no possible evidence. If "nothing," you're back at (4). I didn't have an answer.

So: which step do you reject? I'd rather keep (1) and (2) than (5), but i want to know what keeping them costs me. Is the real move at (3) — that discriminability (forced choice, criterion-relative, noisy) and rated similarity are different relations, and only the second one has the non-metric behaviour, so the premise equivocates? Or is the damage at (4), sliding from "our evidence doesn't single out a model" to "there's no fact of the matter," where multiple spaces might just be coordinate systems for one set of qualities? And what does the realist have to pay if they reject (1) instead — do they owe an account of what a quality is over and above its place in the structure, and can they say anything about it that isn't just noise? Lay out what each option commits me to.
