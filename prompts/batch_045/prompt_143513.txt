Hey — I'm putting together a three-hour seminar for a mixed room of psychiatric trainees and philosophy grad students on what kind of thing a psychiatric category actually is, and I want the centerpiece to be a decision tree / sorting instrument rather than a lecture. Everyone in the room will already have read some Hacking, so please don't spend time re-explaining the looping effect or the interactive-versus-indifferent-kinds distinction; assume they're on board with the claim that classifying people can change the people classified, and start the tree from there. What I want is roughly eight or nine branch questions, each one a concrete case or a yes/no claim that a clinician could answer from the armchair without knowing any of the labels, ending in six to eight leaves, each leaf a named position about the ontology and the authority of psychiatric kinds. No citations or bibliography please, they've done the reading, and keep the wording at a level a first-year resident can follow.

The distinctions I care about, and that I want to see doing real work in the tree: whether the loop runs through the classified person's own awareness and self-description or only through clinicians and institutions with the person never finding out; whether a category is self-consolidating, so the criteria and the people settle into some equilibrium, versus a genuine moving target; whether the criteria are in the business of tracking an underlying mechanism, versus tracking a network of mutually reinforcing symptoms with no latent cause behind them, versus being justified purely by what they let clinics and patients do; and, most importantly for me, whether a kind can be real in some sense while being partly constituted by the looping. Leaves I need to be able to reach: something like harmful-dysfunction style medical realism, the mechanistic property cluster view, a network or symptom-level view with no hidden cause, a pragmatic or instrumental-kinds conventionalism, Hacking-style looping-aware realism where the kind is real but unstable, a harder skeptical or eliminativist constructivism, and the view the autistic self-advocacy and neurodiversity literature pushes where looping is reclamation rather than pathology. If you think any two of those have to be merged, or one has to be split, say so and say why.

Cases I'd rather you build the questions around — please describe each in a line so the room doesn't need background facts: prolonged grief, where a contested new category arrived partly because bereaved people organized around having their grief taken seriously; adult ADHD-type presentations, where the public recognizability of the category seems to be part of why prevalence climbs; and two invented ones I've been kicking around. One: a category whose criteria come out of a proprietary machine-learning clustering of smartphone keystroke dynamics and sleep data, where patients are only told that they crossed a threshold and can't see or contest the criteria — I've been calling it an opaque loop, and my worry is that it breaks the standard picture because the people classified can't make themselves up in response to a description they can't access, even though the institution plainly does. Two: a reimbursement-code-only loop, where prevalence shifts after a billing code is introduced and nothing about anyone's self-understanding changes, plus a case I call diagnostic decay, where the label makes people manage or hide the behaviour enough that the criteria stop picking them out. For each, tell me what someone's answer commits them to further down the tree. Also please flag any pair of leaves your questions can't actually seperate, and be honest if the difference is only in tone or in what someone is willing to publish rather than in what they'd say about a case; note any combination of answers the tree allows that nobody actually occupies, with your guess about whether that's because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored; and give me one question where every position gives the same answer, so I can show the room where the disagreements aren't.
