I sit on the small grants committee of a research centre for ethics and public policy, and we have roughly £25,000 of discretionary money to give away before the end of the financial year. Two members of the committee have been arguing about whether it should go to a longtermist cause — endowing work on catastrophic risks from advanced AI and from engineered pandemics — or to a near-term one of the kind that has randomised-trial evidence behind it. The argument stopped being about the £25,000 some time ago and became an argument about the underlying doctrine, and I have been asked to write up, for the rest of the committee, who had the better of it. I cannot tell: each of them kept conceding something and then recovering. The exchange, as close to how it went as I can manage:

**1. Ade (for the longtermist grant).** Humanity could persist for an extremely long time and spread beyond this planet, in which case the number of lives at stake is astronomical. So even a quite small probability that what we do now affects the trajectory of all that swamps, in expected value, anything we could do for people alive today. Note that this does not require confidence: it is a claim about where most of the expected value lies, and the answer is the far future. And it is not only upside. If humanity dies out this century, everything that would have come after is forfeited, and no one will ever be compensated for it. So: the long-run effects of our actions dominate, and a committee with money to give should give it where the stakes are.

**2. Vera (for the near-term grant).** I accept expected-value reasoning in ordinary cases, and I accept that the far future contains most of the value. I reject the inference to what we should do. Two reasons. First, over centuries-long causal chains our effects wash out; the sign of any given intervention is as likely to be negative as positive, and symmetry gives you no reason to act rather than to refrain. Second, the numbers are unusable: they are dominated by probability mass in a tail that nothing in our experience calibrates, across models that disagree by many orders of magnitude. An expected-value calculation in that situation does not discover anything; it returns whatever you fed it. Where the evidence is like that, the reasonable default is the option whose value we can actually measure.

**3. Ade.** The symmetry point is correct but incomplete. If I genuinely have no idea whether an intervention helps or harms, the right response is not to abandon the stakes; it is to look for interventions whose sign is robust across the range of scenarios I am uncertain about, even when their magnitude is unknown. Not dying out, and not letting the future's shape be irreversibly fixed by whoever happens to move first, are good on essentially every view that regards the future as worth having. That is a robustness argument, not a precision argument. Separately: cluelessness is a gradient, not a wall. If long causal chains disqualified action, it would disqualify large-scale social programmes generally, including the near-term causes you prefer, whose effects also spread through systems over generations.

**4. Vera.** I will grant the gradient — my objection has to be more discriminating than that, or it proves too much. The contrast that actually matters is not long chains versus short chains; it is decisions we can get evidence about versus decisions we cannot. Near-term work is testable, replicable and corrigible: we run trials, we find our mistakes, we hand our successors a working evidence base. Work on far-future risk has essentially no outcome feedback on any timescale available to us; a whole field can be wrong for fifty years and never find out, and because the hypothesis is about rare, unrepeated events, we cannot even learn from near-misses. Deep uncertainty is tolerable when you can buy your way out of it. Deep uncertainty with no feedback loop is not. Under those conditions the rational prescription is to buy information and preserve flexibility, not to place a large bet on a mechanism you will never get to test.

**5. Ade.** But your prescription assumes there will be a later, better-placed decider to receive the information and spend the flexibility. That may not be so. We may be at a hinge: a period in which the risk of irreversible outcomes is unusually high — extinction, or a single value system locked in by whoever controls a powerful technology first — and our capacity to produce those outcomes is growing unusually fast. Deferring is itself a decision, and if the hinge closes badly there is no later generation to do better. Notice also that your own alternative is not really an alternative to my view: buy information, preserve flexibility is an allocation of resources by their effects on the long run. It is longtermist in the only sense that matters; we are just disagreeing about which long-run strategy is best.

**6. Vera.** That is the strongest thing you have said, and I think it quietly abandons your opening. If the case now rests on the hinge, then it is not the sheer size of the future doing the work: a future of untold billions matters just as much in a quiet century when nothing can be locked in, and yet in a quiet century your argument gives us no reason to act now. What moves you is a fragile and contested empirical claim about our particular moment. And there is a further twist. Take the scale argument seriously and it begins to argue against acting at all. If the future is as large as you say, then future people will be far richer, far better informed and far more numerous than we are, with vastly more resources at their command. Confronted with enormous stakes and terrible information, the competent thing to do with money is to let it compound, build the evidence base, and hand both to them — not to spend it now on mechanisms we cannot test. Patience is what the scale argument recommends by itself; present action is only recommended once you add irreversibility. So the defensible version of your position is much narrower than the one you opened with, and when you narrow it, what survives may look like general institutional resilience and basic scientific capacity rather than the headline catastrophic-risk causes.

**7. Ade.** Two replies, and then a convergence. Patience is not free: options decay, institutions decay, windows close, and wait and see is an unchosen bet on the status quo, which is itself one more irreversible-looking path. And optionality is not the neutral fallback you make it sound like. Preserving options is worth something only relative to how you would eventually rank them, and ranking them requires exactly the contested judgments about value and about the future that you say we are not in a position to make; so buy optionality is a substantive policy, and in some circumstances a risky one. But here is the convergence: the one outcome that permanently destroys every option is the one that every version of your argument agrees is bad. Extinction is the enemy of patience, of learning and of optionality alike. So even on your own terms, the risk of extinction stays at the top of the list.

I have read the standard defences and critiques of this view, so please do not summarise them for me. What I need is a verdict on these seven moves as they were actually made: for each one, how strong is it (from clearly mistaken to clearly right), and why. Then tell me who is more right overall, and — this is the part I am most unsure about — what both of them are missing: whether there is a distinction, a consideration, or a piece of the structure that neither Ade nor Vera ever puts on the table and that would change how the argument should come out. Finally, given that we are a small committee giving away a small sum, with a marginal contribution that is in any case tiny and with no way of ever checking the outcome, say whether the narrower, hinge-based version of Ade's case is strong enough to justify this particular grant.
