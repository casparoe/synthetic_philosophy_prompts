I'm a senior at a large public high school and I run our school's Philosophy and Public Affairs Club. Last year we did a unit on punishment; this year we're doing one on religion and the state, and I'm building the materials myself because nothing I've found online is pitched at people who are new to the arguments. I'd like you to take the role of a political theorist who specializes in church-and-state questions and who is unusually good at turning a messy debate into a handout that actually works in a room. Please work through this slowly and in stages: before you write anything, brainstorm; then prune; then build. I would much rather have a slow, well-built instrument than a fast list of isms.

What I want is a diagnostic decision tree: a flowchart that two club members can walk through together in about fifteen minutes, starting from a gut reaction to a concrete arrangement and ending at a named position on whether and how a state may be entangled with religion.

Here are the constraints, which matter to me more than polish:

1. Every branch node must be a concrete case, not a label and not a slogan. No node may ask whether someone supports separation of church and state, or whether the state should be neutral, or whether religion is a private matter. Each node should put a specific arrangement in front of the reader and ask for a verdict: approve it on principle, tolerate it with qualms, or reject it. The point is to sort people by what they say about cases rather than by which label they like. A member who has never heard the words establishmentarian or laïque should be able to answer every question honestly.

2. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a roster of position names. Work out first which distinctions could possibly separate people here, then keep only the ones that actually carve the space, then locate the real views in the resulting tree. Where a combination of answers is empty, say so in the notes and diagnose it briefly: is nobody there because the combination is incoherent, because it is merely unattractive, or because nobody has bothered to occupy it?

3. My angle, which should drive which distinctions you keep: I care most about why someone objects. Coercion of individual conscience, the state having any authority over doctrine at all, the message that establishment sends about the civic standing of outsiders, and the corruption or fungibility problems that come with money are, in my view, four genuinely different objections, and I want the tree to be able to tell apart people who object for different reasons instead of lumping every opponent of entanglement into one leaf. Each of those four should get at least one node that could in principle separate it from the others.

4. Size: six or seven branch questions, two or three branches each, ten to fourteen leaves.

5. Leaves: give each one the name of the position, or the word unnamed if nobody defends it, plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. These must all appear as leaves somewhere: an establishmentarian or Erastian position; a non-preferentialist position on which the state may fund every comprehensive doctrine on identical terms; a strict no-aid separationist; a laïcité-style position on which visible religious symbols in public institutions are the central issue and money is secondary; a principled-pluralist position on which the state should support associations, religious or not, and keep out of their internal life; and at least two leaves that are unnamed or unoccupied.

6. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot actually separate, rather than pretending the questions sort them. I would rather know that two views collapse on my cases than be handed a tree that only looks discriminating.

7. Use the four real countries only as loose illustrations: England's established church; Germany's state-administered church contribution with a formal declaration of exit; France's republican secularism and the ban on conspicuous religious symbols in public schools; the United States' no-establishment rule together with publicly funded vouchers redeemable at religious schools. I do not want legal citations, case names, statutes, or dates, and I do not care about current legal accuracy. If you are unsure of a detail about any real country, generalize it or replace it with one of the hypotheticals below.

8. Most of the branch nodes should use arrangements nobody has memorized an opinion about, so that members have to reason. Here are some I made up; use at least four of them as nodes, and invent two or three of your own in the same spirit.

- The Mirror Rule: any benefit offered to a religious body must be offered on strictly identical terms to every group offering a comprehensive doctrine of life, explicitly including atheistic and secular humanist ones.
- The Heritage Clause: the state funds the upkeep and the rituals of religious bodies, but only those whose buildings and liturgies are more than 150 years old, and it funds them as heritage, never as religion.
- The Exit-Fee Asymmetry: everyone is enrolled automatically in the funding scheme and must file a formal declaration, pay a small fee, and appear in person at a government office to leave it.
- The Caritative Carve-out: the state contracts with religious bodies to run food banks, hospitals, and preschools that it would otherwise have to run itself, at cost, under identical auditing rules, and pays for nothing else.
- The Two-Key System: funding requires both a threshold of popular subscription and certification by a state board, where the board may investigate whether a group preaches hatred but is legally forbidden from inquiring into its doctrine.
- The Silent Establishment: the state has an official religion, but the religion is never mentioned in law, schools, or public ceremony, and is funded only by the income of an old property endowment.
- The Conscience Ledger: taxpayers who object to any religious funding may redirect their entire share into a fund for secular ethics instruction in public schools.

9. Before the handout itself, include a short section called "Notes on how I built this." It should show your actual work: the candidate distinctions you considered, which ones you cut and why, and which combinations of answers turned out to be empty. Then check your own tree once and tell me if two people with genuinely different commitments would land on the same leaf.

10. Format the handout for American high schoolers: readable in one printed page, plain prose, and any term of art should be explained in a parenthesis the first time it appears. No citations, no case names, no quotation, and no bibliography — just the instrument and the notes.
