I help coordinate a small volunteer food pantry, and one of our volunteers — call her Marisol — has missed four of her last six early-morning shifts, which means other people have to scramble and some of our regulars go without. I've been genuinely angry with her, in the way where you think someone *should* have done better and didn't. My roommate, who did some philosophy in college, says blame never makes sense, and gave me a three-step argument for it: everything you do flows from how you already are — your temperament, your habits, what you care about; you can't be truly blameworthy for what flows from how you are unless you're also blameworthy for being that way; but to make yourself be a certain way you would already have to be some way, since you'd need motives and dispositions to do the choosing with — and those would have to have been chosen too, and so on without end. So nobody is ever really blameworthy, and both my anger at her and the rather bitter guilt I've been carrying around my own comparable failures are, on this view, just noise.

I don't want to accept that, but I also can't locate the hole in it. Two replies have been offered to me and I can't tell whether either is anything more than a restatement of the problem. My roommate's own fix, which she calls the Snowball Clause, is that authorship can be accumulated: no single act has to be self-caused, because each small choice nudges the character that produces the next choice, so that after years of nudging you are, in a thinned-out but real sense, the author of who you now are — responsibility as the sum of many dependent steps rather than as a first cause. My co-coordinator's fix, which I'll call the Real-You Clause, is that origins are irrelevant: what matters is whether the failure *expressed* Marisol — whether she noticed her own indifference and went along with it rather than resisting it — and if it did, blame is warranted even if every feature of her was handed to her unchosen. There's also something I half-remember from the book my roommate lent me, to the effect that even if the infinite regress could somehow be stopped it wouldn't give you what you wanted — but I can't reconstruct the reason, and I suspect it matters.

I have to talk to Marisol on Thursday, and I don't know whether I'm entitled to be angry with her, or whether I should treat her unreliability the way I'd treat a broken delivery van — something to route around rather than something to blame. So: which, if either, of the two replies actually answers the three-step argument rather than quietly assuming what it denies? Where is that argument genuinely vulnerable — is it the step that says you'd need to have chosen your own character, or is it something about whether being self-caused would even help if you could manage it? And on the basis of whatever you conclude, what should I actually do and say on Thursday, and what should I do with the guilt I've been carrying about myself?
