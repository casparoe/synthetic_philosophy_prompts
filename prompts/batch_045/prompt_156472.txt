I've got a department seminar slot next Thursday — 40 minutes plus questions, and our chair never cuts anyone off — and I'm presenting a chapter from my thesis, and I need Q&A prep more than I need more feedback on the paper itself. The chapter came out of a fight with my roommate (he's in some DSA reading group, we were up past 2am) about whether Marx's critique of capitalism lives or dies with the exploitation stuff. My claim, in short: exploitation is the weak leg and alienation is the load-bearing one, but alienation is not really about private property, or about the wage relation as such. Two moves: (1) Marx's case for exploitation as a normative verdict needs the labour theory of value to do the work, and once you drop it you're left with something like a property-relations or bargaining-power definition that is basically a distributive complaint — real, but in principle fixable by redistribution and regulation, i.e. reformable. (2) The alienation critique is different in kind, because it survives full worker ownership. Take what I've been calling the co-op case: a Mondragon-ish firm where workers own the means of production, appropriate the whole net product, and elect their managers. Nobody is appropriating anyone's surplus labour there, so on the Roemer-ish way of setting things up the exploitation is gone; but the labour process is still fragmented, still timed, still subordinated to market discipline that the individual worker has no control over, and the product still confronts her as something that isn't hers in the relevant sense. So alienation tracks something exploitation doesn't. I also want to cut the four dimensions down: I argue the species-being one depends on an essentialist anthropology we should just drop, and that what's left all cashes out as claims about *control* over the process and over the product — which means, and I say this out loud in the paper, that the surviving Marxist complaint is a lot closer to a republican non-domination complaint about work than to anything about surplus value. One more wrinkle I'm committed to: I distinguish structural alienation from felt alienation, and I say the survey-psychology measures of felt powerlessness are a proxy that can come apart from the structural property in both directions — contented workers in genuinely subordinate positions, miserable workers who are actually autonomous. I need that distinction because otherwise my best case, the co-op case, just gets answered with worker-satisfaction data.

Who's in the room: two analytic-Marxism-flavoured political philosophers who think the whole point is exploitation plus distributive justice and who'll be allergic to perfectionism and to any essentialist talk about human nature; a Frankfurt-school/Hegelian person who thinks control is a thin liberal juridical notion and that I've psychologised and individualised something that's really about a deformed relation to one's own activity; two empirically-minded comparativists who'll want an estimand and will ask how you'd ever measure a structural property that's been defined so it can come apart from the reported one, in which case isn't it unfalsifiable; a feminist political theorist who works on care and social reproduction and will press me on whether care work — relational, other-directed, often not subordinated in the control sense at all, and yet exhausting and unfree — is alienated in the same way, which I think is genuinely hard for me; and a Rawlsian chair who mostly wants to know whether any of this is a justice-tracking notion at all or a perfectionist one the liberal state should stay neutral about.
