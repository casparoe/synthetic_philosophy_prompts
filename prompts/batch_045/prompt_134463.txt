Consider the following stipulated case. A physical theory admits two empirically equivalent completions, which I will call the plenary completion and the sparse completion. Their agreement over the accessible algebra is total: not merely the same expectation values for every quantity that could in principle be measured from inside our causal patch, but the same algebraic relations among those quantities, and the same verdicts on whether a quantity measured in one experimental context is the same quantity as one measured in another. They disagree only about the inaccessible sector: the plenary completion populates it with a continuum of independent degrees of freedom, the sparse completion with nothing beyond a single global constraint that fixes the boundary data of the accessible algebra.

There is, however, one asymmetry, and I had hoped it settled the ontology question. It can be proved from the two formalisms, as pure mathematics, that the plenary completion admits consistent enlargement: were the accessible class widened to include observables from a somewhat larger region, the plenary completion would extend to a theory over the larger algebra. The sparse completion is rigid: every such enlargement yields a trivial or inconsistent theory. Call this the fragility asymmetry. It is a priori rather than empirical — nothing any observer in our patch can now measure, or will ever be able to measure, distinguishes the two, but only one survives a counterfactual widening of the evidence class.

A colleague has convinced me that the fragility asymmetry does not settle the question, with an argument that I now think proves far too much but whose damage I cannot locate.

(1) The plenary and sparse completions agree on every observable in the accessible class.
(2) Their disagreement is confined entirely to the inaccessible sector.
(3) All evidence is delivered by observation, and every observation that any observer in our causal patch could make falls within the accessible class; that is what makes the sector inaccessible.
(4) So no possible evidence discriminates the plenary from the sparse completion.
(5) The fragility asymmetry is a mathematical fact about the formalisms, not an observation; a priori structural facts can constrain which formalism is more elegant, more tractable, or more fecund, but they are not evidence about what exists.
(6) So the fragility asymmetry does not discriminate them either, and there remains no discriminating evidence, actual or possible.
(7) Warrant principle: a claim about what exists is warranted only if there could be evidence discriminating it from its rivals.
(8) So neither completion is warranted, and agnosticism is the only warranted stance toward the ontology of the inaccessible sector.
(9) Content principle: ontological claims are answerable to evidence not only for their warrant but for their content. If no possible evidence could discriminate between two ontological claims, there is nothing for the dispute between them to be about — no fact of the matter as to which is correct.
(10) Therefore the two completions are not rival ontologies at all. They are one theory in two notations, and the question of what really occupies the inaccessible sector is empty rather than hard.
(11) But nothing in (1) through (10) turns on the specifics of the case. The reasoning applies to any claim about any sector from which no observation can reach the claimant: the distribution of matter beyond our cosmological event horizon, the interior of a black hole for those of us who stay outside it, whatever the very early universe contained during intervals that left no surviving record.
(12) So there is no fact of the matter about any of those either. And since what counts as observationally reachable changes over time, there was no fact of the matter about the atomic constitution of matter at any period when no available instrumentation could resolve atoms — which seems to make the truth about atoms depend on the state of our microscopes.

I want to keep (1) through (3): the first two are the stipulations of the case, and the third is what the word inaccessible means. I also want to keep the claim that there is a fact of the matter about what lies beyond our event horizon and about black hole interiors — call this the realism constraint. So something from (4) onward has to go, and I would rather give up (10) than the realism constraint; but I want to know what that costs me.

Tell me which step fails and why it looked sound. Then price the alternatives for me. In particular: what do I commit to if I reject (5) and allow a priori structural facts — extendability, rigidity, consistency under enlargement — to count as evidence about ontology, and does that make the plenary completion warranted rather than merely preferable? What do I commit to if I reject (9) and accept that there can be a determinate fact of the matter that no possible evidence could ever settle? And is the damage in (3), where the argument may be trading on an unmarked shift between what is inaccessible to observers in our situation, what is inaccessible to any observer anywhere, and what is inaccessible relative to the instruments available at a given time? If more than one reading of (3) is available, tell me which one the argument needs and what happens to (12) under the others.
