I convene a fortnightly reading group at a small ML safety non-profit, where spend most of our time on decisions made under opacity: whether to deploy a system whose failure rate we cannot estimate, whether to trust an eval suite we built ourselves, how much to weight a forecaster whose track record is short. We recently spent a session on the Ellsberg paradox and got nowhere, for the usual reason that everyone used the same words to mean different things. One colleague insisted over lunch that ambiguity aversion is just risk aversion wearing a hat; another said it is the only defensible response when you get exactly one draw from an environment you will never get to model; a third said the whole phenomenon is an artifact of putting two urns side by side and asking someone to compare a gamble they understand against one they don't, and that it would vanish if you elicited the preference in isolation. I want to settle this by sorting the group on what they say about cases rather than on what they call themselves, so I need a classification instrument, not an essay.

Please build me a decision tree (or equivalently a ten-question diagnostic with a scoring key) that takes someone from no commitments beyond expected utility to a position on ambiguity aversion. Each branch node should be one diagnostic question — a concrete case or a claim to accept or reject — phrased so that an engineer or economist who has never read the literature can answer it without knowing which leaf it leads to, and no node may simply ask whether the respondent thinks ambiguity aversion is rational. I want roughly eight to ten questions and eight to twelve leaves. The questions I most want the tree to turn on, roughly in the order I'd expect them to bite: the standard setup with a known-composition urn and an unknown-composition urn; the same setup with the comparison gamble removed, so that the ambiguous option stands alone; a variant in which the ambiguous composition was fixed by a process with no stake in your choice versus one chosen after your bet was recorded; a variant in which you don't know which of those two processes was used, so the ambiguity is about the source of the ambiguity itself; a repeated-play version in which you observe draws and may update before betting again; a pair of cases with identical structure but framed once over gains and once over losses; a case where the unknown quantity is the correlation between two bets rather than the marginal probability of either; a case asking whether you would pay to learn the composition at each of several points (before betting, after betting but before the draw, after the draw but before the reveal); and a case where three people whose plausible-prior ranges overlap but differ must choose once together. Where any of these are my own invention rather than standard, please say so, so I don't cite them to the group as canonical.

For the leaves, give me at least: the plain expected-utility view on which the ambiguity is irrelevant; the worst-case-over-a-set-of-priors view; the view that mixes worst and best case by a fixed weight; the view that puts a second layer of probabilities over the possible priors and bends them through a concave transform; the cost-weighted or confidence-weighted generalization of the worst-case view; the deflationary view that the two-urn comparison manufactures the effect and that a properly isolated elicitation would show neutrality; the view that the effect is really distrust of, or strategic reasoning about, whoever set the odds and that it dissolves when the source is genuinely stake-free; the view that treats it as a heuristic with an ecological or statistical-robustness rationale rather than a preference; and the view that what is really being expressed is a preference about how and when uncertainty gets resolved, not about probability at all. Name a leaf only where the label is genuinely standard; otherwise mark it unnamed and pin it down in a clause. Then do the two things I actually care about: (i) flag every pair of leaves your tree cannot separate in principle or in practice — I suspect the worst-case and certain non-additive-weighting views collapse on the original two-urn case, that the distrust view and genuine aversion are indistinguishable there, and that correlation-aversion and plain ambiguity aversion may be inseparable without the correlation case — and tell me which extra node, if any, would separate them and whether such a node is even answerable by intuition; (ii) list the combinations of answers your tree permits but that nobody occupies, with one line each on whether the empty cell is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored. Finally, mark any answer pattern that is internally unstable (for instance someone who wants the composition revealed for free yet reverses themselves when the same choice is decomposed into stages) and say whether the tree should treat that as a position or as evidence my instrument is broken. Please use no citations, no bibliographies and no reading suggestions — I want the structure, and I'll supply the reading myself.
