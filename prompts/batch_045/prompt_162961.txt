I'm retired now — thirty-odd years as an ecotoxicologist, first bench dose–response work and then far too many years sitting on risk-assessment panels for a national regulator — and I've started auditing a graduate seminar in philosophy as the oldest person in the room by about forty years. We spent a session on the precautionary principle last week and it went the way I suspect it always goes: the people who like it said things that sounded to me like plain good practise, the people who don't like it produced cases where the principle tells you to do X and not-X at the same time, and nobody seemed to notice that they might be using the same three words for four different things. Nobody in that room could write down a loss function, and I kept thinking that half of what they were arguing about would evaporate if somebody did. So I want to try the translation properly, and then look at what's left over.

Here's the vocabulary I actually have, and I'm fluent in it to the point of boredom: NOAELs and benchmark doses, uncertainty (safety) factors and why they're a fudge, threshold versus linear-no-threshold dose–response models and model averaging over a small model class, Neyman–Pearson machinery (alpha, power, one- and two-sided tests, and the fact that a non-significant result from an underpowered study tells you almost nothing), the difference between风险, Knightian "we don't even know the model" uncertainty, loss functions, expected utility, minimax and minimax-regret, discount rates, irreversibility, and the thing the resource economists call quasi-option value. What I want you to do is restate the precautionary principle, the case for it and the standard objections to it, in that vocabulary as faithfully as you can — and please split it into at least these four pieces, because I strongly suspect they behave very differently under translation and that the seminar's confusion came from running them together: (1) precaution as an asymmetric loss function, i.e. false negatives cost more than false positives; (2) precaution as a shift in the burden of proof, the proponent has to demonstrate safety before the thing is allowed out of the lab; (3) precaution as a refusal to average over model uncertainty — don't take expected utility across your model class, look at the worst model you can't exclude; (4) precaution as the claim that some outcomes are unacceptable full stop, whatever the probability, which looks to me like a side constraint rather than a term in a loss function, and which I can only write down by putting an infinity somewhere, something I was taught never to do. If your translation of one of these is really just a prior or just a default in disguise, say so and say what got lost in the disguise.

And I don't want this done on abstractions, I want it run on the case that broke the seminar. A new seed-treatment compound: across about forty field sites it lifts wheat yield by roughly nine percent; the mammal toxicology is clean and well done, chronic NOAEL around 40 mg/kg/day; but there is one small semi-field study — twenty-four colonies, not replicated, p about 0.06 — hinting that a bumblebee species loses homing ability at two parts per billion, which is environmentally realistic. Two models for that endpoint, a threshhold around five parts per billion and a linear-no-threshold one, and nothing in the data that would let you choose between them. That bee is the main pollinator of a wild legume that is what holds the soil on a watershed; bee goes, legume goes, soil goes, and there is no putting it back on any timescale a policy can see. But the yield gap doesn't vanish if you say no: it gets made up by bringing a few hundred thousand hectares of old rough grassland into intensive wheat, and old grassland has its own soil carbon and its own invertebrate community that also cannot be put back. So both branches are irreversible and there is no privileged null — and the principle seemed to tell us to block the compound and to approve it depending on which harm you wrote on the card first. My guess is that in my vocabulary this is just "you must pick a default and no default is neutral" plus "with two irreversibilities minimax-regret has nothing to bite on," but I want to see whether that is the whole of it. So: what translates cleanly; what translates only with distortion, and exactly where the distortion sits; what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all; and then the verdict I actually care about — for each piece of residue, is it something my vocabulary is too poor to say, and what would I have to add to say it, or is it something the philosophers only think they are saying? In particular, tell me plainly if the whole thing collapses into asymmetric losses plus ambiguity aversion plus irreversible options, which I already accept and which nobody in the room appeared to dispute, and if so tell me what we actually spent ninety minutes arguing about. If the honest residue turns out to be moral or political — who owes what to whom, who gets to decide under uncertainty, whether we can speak for people who aren't born yet — say that too, because those words appear nowhere in my toolbox and I'd like to know whether that's a hole in the toolbox or a hole in the argument. No reading lists or citations, please; I can't chase them up and wouldn't know where to begin.
