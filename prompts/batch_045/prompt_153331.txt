I am drafting the methodological section of a paper on epistemic self-accountability, and I want the specification settled before I read another proposal. The work I need luminosity to do is narrower than the work it is usually asked to do, and possibly different in kind. I want it to underwrite a norm of reason-citing: roughly, that a subject may offer p as her reason for acting or asserting only if she is in a position to know that she knows p. That norm needs only a restricted luminosity thesis — luminosity for the propositions a subject is prepared to cite as her reasons, at the time of citing — not luminosity for knowledge generally, and not luminosity for every mental condition. The familiar anti-luminosity argument (Williamson's, in Knowledge and Its Limits (2000): run a condition that changes by imperceptible steps — feeling cold at dawn, a light dimming — across a sorites series of cases, add a margin-for-error requirement linking knowledge to how one would fare in adjacent cases, and derive that the condition cannot be luminous anywhere in the series) is standardly read as threatening any condition that admits of such a series. Whether "the propositions I would cite as reasons" is such a condition is exactly what I cannot see my way through. So: give me the criteria first.

Write the desiderata for a theory of luminosity adequate to that purpose. For each, state the case or consideration behind it, and mark it as non-negotiable for a theory meant to do this job, conditional on some further commitment I could instead abandon, or a camp prejudice — something demanded in this literature that is really a preference for one style of theory. Include, at minimum, coverage of: the sorites cases themselves; the difference between luminosity of a mental condition and luminosity of knowledge; the distinction between being in a position to know and actually knowing, or having attended to the matter; consistency with ordinary non-scepticism about perceptual knowledge; consistency with whatever the correct account of knowledge turns out to be, if the theory is to remain neutral across internalist and externalist options; and the requirement that the class of luminous conditions be picked out by a property rather than enumerated.

Four wrinkles I want handled rather than acknowledged and set aside.

1. Depth. Say that a condition C has access depth n for a subject if, whenever C obtains, the subject is in a position to know that C obtains, and to know that she knows that, iterated to n levels. KK is the claim that knowledge has depth 1; iterated KK, unbounded depth. My norm appears to need only depth 1, but uniformly across a class. Should the spec demand unbounded depth, or is that demand itself a prejudice inherited from the modal logic rather than from the phenomenon? Say what breaks if depth is finite and small.

2. Timing. Call a condition diachronically luminous if, whenever it obtains at t, there is a later time at which the subject is in a position to know that it obtained at t. The margin-for-error argument is synchronic. Consider a climber at altitude whose own judgment about whether she is hypoxic is unreliable while she is hypoxic, but whose companion's log, read an hour later, reliably reveals it. Is diachronic luminosity enough for a norm that governs what she does at the time? Should the spec contain a synchronicity requirement at all, and what would the argument for including one have to be?

3. Escaping the argument. The argument needs both a graduality premise and a margin premise. A theory can keep a restricted luminosity thesis by denying graduality — positing sharp but unknowable boundaries in the underlying condition, in the spirit of epistemicism about vagueness — or by constraining the margin principle, or by accepting the conclusion for knowledge while relocating the work to a nearby notion. Say which of these exits the desiderata should permit, which they should close, and on what grounds; I suspect that my list can only forbid the sharp-boundary escape by smuggling in a prejudice of its own, and I want that made explicit if so.

4. Non-ad-hocness of the restriction. My norm restricts the luminosity claim to a class picked out partly by the subject's own dispositions to offer propositions as reasons. Tell me what a non-ad-hoc restriction would have to look like: what property must the class have so that it is not merely the set of cases in which the margin-for-error argument happens to fail? Must the class consist of conditions with sharp boundaries, or is there another route out?

Then the part I most want: is any subset of the desiderata jointly unsatisfiable? Show the tension rather than assert it — if some combination of the margin requirement, iteration, the ban on ad-hoc restriction, and the ban on scepticism cannot all be met, that is the result I am after. Name the single desideratum that does the most work in excluding candidate theories, and say which one you would give up first, and why that one.

Finish with a short scorecard, three or four lines each: full KK; the view on which no non-trivial condition is luminous; a restricted view on which some thick mental-state conditions are luminous while knowledge is not; and a view on which luminosity fails but a successor notion — a safety-based or higher-order-evidence-based surrogate — does whatever work my norm needs. If none of them meets your own list, say so plainly.

Two last things. Keep the criteria and the scorecard differently hedged: state the desiderata with the confidence each actually warrants, flagging where you are extrapolating. And end with a short note on where this list is most likely to be wrong: name the one argument, the one feature of the cases, or the one development in the formal treatment of margins for error that would make you reorder the desiderata or drop one, and say which desideratum you take to be the weakest link in your own reasoning.
