me and two friends have been going round on this for weeks and we are not converging. One of them, who does econ and is building a screentime app, says akrasia is just hyperbolic discounting: the later good is like a distant object seen through a lens that shrinks things with distance, so of course the near thing wins once it gets close, and there is no mystery about the will at all. His conclusion is that the only real fix is a commitment device — delete the later option, or make the near one expensive right now — and that any talk of willpower, self-command or self-respect is pre-scientific. A second friend backs him with the planner-and-doer picture from behavioural econ: the person is like a firm with a planner who writes policy and a doer who grabs whatever is on the desk, so the grabbing is not irrational, the firm's internal contract is just badly written. Same conclusion, same policy, and the added claim that weakness of will is not really a philosophical problem about agency, it's a mechanism design problem.

Can you audit both analogies properly — the correspondences spelled out explicitly (a table is fine): what plays the role of distance, of the lens, of the viewer's standpoint, of the planner, the doer, the contract, the enforcement? And then where each one breaks, sorted by whether the disanalogy actually matters for the use they are making of it or is just decorative. Concretely, here is where I think the trouble is and I want you to press harder than we managed. First: where is the standpoint? In spatial perspective there is one viewer in one place and everything recedes from it, but in the temporal case every moment claims to be the present, so the perspective analogy seems to quietly import a privileged standpoint — a time-zero planner — and I want to know whether the conclusion that the present self is the one being irrational depends on that import, or whether it survives if you refuse to pick a standpoint at all. Second: does the hyperbolic shape even do the work people think? Is the extra weight attached to immediacy specifically or to nearness in general, and does the model need a separate term for that? And more important: discounting gives you a change in preference over time, whereas the case I care about is the person who acts while still judging, at that very moment, that what they are doing is worse. Which of those two does each analogy describe, what does it have to say about the second one, and what does it have to assume about what better judgment is in order to say anything? Third: synchronic cases. Pressing snooze eight times, not looking away at the crucial second, the hand that keeps going back — nothing is being delayed there, there is no distance left to shrink. Does either analogy reach those, or do they just fall outside the model, and if they fall outside, how much of the phenomenon are we leaving on the table?

Then the practical bit: does the conclusion either of them is being used to support actually survive — that commitment devices are the answer and willpower talk is obsolete? Which of the disanalogies bite on that specific conclusion and which dont? And if both pictures fail somewhere, what would a better analogy be? The one I keep coming back to is holding your breath: you decide to hold it, there is no delay being discounted, and then you stop, and the stopping is not obviously a change of mind about whether holding was good — it is something that happens to you which you were also, in some sense, doing. Is that actually capturing something the perspective picture cant, or does it just relabel weakness of will as exhaustion and lose the very thing that made it a problem?
