I am giving a paper at a joint Legal Theory / Philosophy seminar in three weeks: 40 minutes of presentation, 25 minutes of questions, roughly twenty-five people in the room. I need a Q&A preparation sheet — objections, replies, and triage — not a polished defense of the view.

The view I am defending, so that the objections have something concrete to bite on. Call it Expressive Desert, Managerial Magnitude. It holds that retributive desert does real normative work, but only on the *mode* of a criminal sanction and never on its *magnitude*. Desert determines whether the state's response must take the form of moral address to a responsible agent — condemnation, censure, a demand for answerability — rather than the merely managerial form we use for hazards and carriers. Magnitude, however much hard treatment is actually imposed, is fixed entirely by forward-looking considerations: risk reduction, deterrence, the disruption of ongoing criminal activity, subject to the quarantine-model principle of least restrictive effective means and to a dignitarian ceiling against using anyone as a mere instrument. Two consequences I lean on. First, the old hard cases get separated rather than balanced: the grave wrongdoer who now poses no risk is still owed censure but no further hard treatment, and the minor wrongdoer who is dangerous may be restrained for as long as the risk lasts but must not be restrained as payback. Second, I propose a procedural device, the severity ledger: for every component of a sanction the court must record whether the component is expressive or managerial, what risk estimate drives its magnitude, and whether the expressive aim could be discharged by a less harsh means; and a double-spend prohibition, forbidding any component from being justified once as desert and again as risk management. I also accept a voice-matching requirement: punitive address has to be delivered in a register the offender could in principle answer, which is what makes the case of offenders who cannot be addressed, of corporate defendants, and of harms caused by autonomous software systems genuinely hard for me rather than merely awkward.

The room. There is a senior criminal law theorist, a retributivist of the proportionality school who has spent her career arguing that desert fixes upper and lower bounds on sentence severity and that preventive detention is the distinctive abuse of the modern penal state. There is a philosopher of action in the compatibilist tradition associated with Dennett: he thinks moral responsibility is an empirically grounded status of reasons-responsive agents, that skeptical denials of it undercut the forward-looking rationales as much as the backward-looking ones, and that desert-talk does irreplaceable social work we should not pretend to do without. There is a free will skeptic in the tradition of Caruso's public health quarantine model: he thinks desert attributions are metaphysically indefensible, that incapacitation, rehabilitation, and disruption do all the legitimate work, and that any residual expressive element is either idle ceremony or disguised vengeance. There are two empirically minded criminologists who care about deterrent elasticities, actuarial risk instruments, and the racial skew of risk scores, and who will treat anything unmeasurable as a decorative addition. There is a visiting appellate judge who wants to know what any of this would require a sentencing court to write down. The rest are graduate students, mostly sympathetic, one of whom has read everything on both sides.

What I want from you:

1. Two or three things I should concede explicitly in my first two slides, before anyone objects, chosen so that the concessions cost me little and disarm the objections that would otherwise be fatal.

2. Eight to ten objections, ordered by how likely they are to be raised multiplied by how much damage they would do if I handled them badly. For each: which member of the audience raises it; how that person would actually phrase it, in their idiom and with their own commitments visible rather than translated into neutral philosophical English; a reply I could deliver in about two minutes; and an honest note on where that reply is soft or where it silently depends on a claim I have not argued.

3. The one reply that has to land. If it fails, the session is lost regardless of how the rest goes. Say which it is and why.

4. The question I am hoping nobody asks — ideally one that presses on the voice-matching requirement or on what desert can be if it has no magnitude at all — together with a candid note on why I do not currently have an answer and what the least dishonest thing to say would be if it comes.

Assume I have no access to anything beyond my own notes during the session, so anchor every reply in the machinery of the view and in ordinary examples rather than in literature. Write the whole preparation sheet in American English.
