I sat in on a workplace mediation training last month, and two of the other participants — I'll call them Ines and Tobias, neither of them a philosopher, both of them people who run disciplinary conversations for a living — got into an argument during the coffee break that I haven't been able to settle since. The trainer had used an example: a department head who for years blew past his own grant-reporting deadlines sends a junior colleague a formal, stern written reprimand for missing one. Most of the room thought the reprimand was somehow out of order; the argument was about exactly what is wrong with it, or whether anything is. I care about this more than academically, because I'm helping draft a short internal guide on when managers should recuse themselves from disciplinary conversations, so I need to know what actually follows rather than just what sounds sophisticated.

Ines opened by saying the man has no right to blame. Blame, she said, isn't just the assertion that something was wrong — anyone can say that about anyone — but the act of holding someone to a norm, and you can't coherently invoke a norm against another person while quietly exempting yourself from it. The tax cheat who prosecutes a tax evader isn't saying anything false; he is simply in no position to say it. Tobias replied that this is just tu quoque: whether the missed deadline was a real fault doesn't depend on the character of whoever points it out, and any rule that blocks blame on the basis of the blamer's record is a gift to the powerful, who generally have the most skeletons. He added that hypocritical blame still does useful work — it marks the norm for everyone else and gives the wrongdoer a reason to answer — and that the ex-smoker who nags you about smoking is the paradigm of someone with standing, where the only thing that changed is that he now knows the temptation from the inside, which if anything makes him more credible. Ines conceded that the proposition "you did wrong" can be true and said that the act of blaming can still be defective: blame is a demand, and demands can be out of order even when what they demand is genuinely owed, the way a creditor demanding a debt that has already been discharged is out of order. She also said the silencing worry is overstated, since on her view the hypocrite can still warn, protest, ask, or press for reform — what he cannot do is sit in judgment — and she distinguished the ex-smoker, who stopped and owned it, from the department head, who hasn't: standing isn't lost by ever having failed, it's lost by the continuing self-exemption, the implicit claim that this binds you and not me.

Tobias said that makes standing hostage to the blamer's psychology and to how thoroughly he has owned up, giving a continuum where a line was promised, and he offered a case he thought was decisive: someone who broke a promise in the past but was fully excused for it — she broke it to get an injured child to hospital — now blames a friend for breaking a promise. If the test is whether you did the same thing, she lacks standing; if it's whether you were blameworthy for it, she has it; if it's whether you accept the norm, she has it too. So which is it? He also argued that even granting some kind of standing condition, the right response to hypocritical blame isn't "you may not blame me" but to accept the blame and file a counter-complaint — yes, and you did worse — which does all the work standing-talk does without pretending the original reprimand was invalid. Ines replied that this doesn't capture the injury: being blamed by someone who exempts himself isn't just being blamed, it's being condescended to, made to occupy a lower rank in a hierarchy the blamer has rigged, and that is an independent wrong rather than a diagnosis of invalidity. Tobias then added that in the trainer's example the junior colleague didn't know about the boss's own record, so nothing could have felt condescending to him, and asked how there could be a wrong with no victim's-side effect; Ines said the defect is in the blamer's stance and not in the target's uptake, that you can be unfair to someone without their noticing. Finally she conceded part of Tobias's continuum point and shifted ground: perhaps hypocrisy doesn't nullify standing but discounts it, with the size of the discount weighed against how serious the original fault was and how much the target needed to hear it.

Neither of them moved after that and I genuinely can't tell who has the better of it. Could you work through the exchange move by move — for each move say whether it is clearly right, partly right, or mistaken, and briefly why — and then say what, if anything, both of them are leaving out of the picture? Please also be explicit about how confident you are in each of your verdicts, and single out the one move you're least sure how to assess.
