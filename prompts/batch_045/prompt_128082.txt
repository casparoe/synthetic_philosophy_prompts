Compare, and then adjudicate between, two families of account of slurring terms. Let S stand for any slurring term and C for its neutral counterpart — the expression a speaker would reach for in order to pick out the same group without slurring it.

Set out each family in its strongest form before you begin the comparison.

- Prohibitionism: S has exactly the same semantic content as C — the same extension, the same contribution to truth conditions, the same behaviour inside embeddings — and what distinguishes S from C is not meaning at all but a social fact: S is a prohibited word. The ban is what makes the term offensive, not a consequence of its already being offensive, and the offense consists in breaking a convention rather than in saying something false or extra.
- Expressivism: S contributes nothing distinctive to truth-conditional content; instead it conventionally encodes a derogatory attitude on the speaker's part — contempt, dismissal, a claim to standing over the group — and the offense derives from that encoded attitude. What S and C differ in is use, not in what is said.

Then work through the following four probes, and end with a verdict. Aim for roughly 1,000 to 1,200 words, in British English.

1. The embedding test. Take the behavior of S under negation, in the antecedent of a conditional, inside a yes-or-no question, and, above all, in indirect reports of what someone else said. Does each family predict the observed pattern, or does one of them have to stipulate it? Say what you take the observed pattern to be, and where your confidence in it comes from.

2. The sealed ban. A large hospital trust strikes a clinical label for a group of patients from all of its forms and forbids staff to use it, for reasons of pure administrative tidiness; the replacement term is no kinder and no more accurate, and nobody in the surrounding community holds the group in contempt. A generation later the old word is unmentionable inside the institution, and people who hear it recoil. Does either family predict that recoil? If prohibitionism does, is that a point in its favor or a reduction of its own position; if expressivism does not, can it explain what the patients are reacting to?

3. The orphaned word. In a small speech community, S was once a slur for a neighboring group. The community disappears; the word survives in the language of its neighbors as the ordinary, unmarked term for that same group, and neither the speakers nor the group's own members know its history. A historical linguist then publishes the etymology. Ask three things: does the word become offensive when the history becomes known; does the publication make the earlier, innocent uses offensive retroactively; and does it matter which language the word is uttered in? Now vary the case: the word is borrowed into a third language where it lands on a completely different group. Which family has the resources to say something non-arbitrary here, and which is reduced to shrugging?

4. The ventriloquist. An actor delivering a historical line, a judge reading out a defendant's words, a documentary narrator, a linguist writing the term on a blackboard during a lecture, a witness recounting an assault, and the defendant himself quoting what he said. Some of these are licensed and some are not, and the licensing seems to shift with role, medium, and audience. Explain the pattern on each family, and then ask the awkward question: is the explanation an application of the family's core machinery, or is it an exception bolted on afterwards? If the latter, how damaging is that?

In closing: state which family you think is in better shape overall; say whether the two are genuinely rivals or whether each ends up needing the other's machinery, and if so which direction the dependence runs and whether it is damaging; and give the single strongest objection to the family you reject.

Two constraints. First, argue from the cases and from the commitments of the views, not from the literature: do not name or cite authors, papers, or titles, do not supply a bibliography or suggestions for further reading, and assume no access to any source — the question should be answerable from the armchair. Second, use placeholders such as S and C rather than writing out real slurs. Where a family's core commitments underdetermine what it should say about a probe, say so explicitly, and assess whether the extra stipulation needed to settle it is principled or merely ad hoc.
