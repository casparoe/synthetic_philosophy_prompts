I edit the internal newsletter for the Workplace Operations group at a large research employer with campuses in Houston and Berkeley, and for the next issue I want a short piece of office satire built around a location request my team has supposedly been asked to adjudicate. Here is the case: one of our senior research philosophers, after a medical procedure, now has his brain maintained in a laboratory vat in Houston while his body remains in Berkeley, kept in touch with the vat by a two-way radio link; he has taken to calling the brain Yorick and the body Hamlet. He has filed a request to be reclassified as a fully remote worker on the grounds that the part of him that thinks, decides, and attends meetings is in Texas, while simultaneously claiming the on-site parking permit, the Berkeley cafeteria allowance, and the in-office attendance bonus on the grounds that his body still walks through the turnstile every morning.

What I want is the memo itself: about 600 words, written in deadpan corporate voice by a Workplace Operations analyst to the heads of Payroll and Tax, with Legal copied, and formatted as one continuous piece of email prose — no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold or italic emphasis, no tables, just paragraphs. Please make the analyst genuinely competent rather than baffled, and have them work through the actual administrative complications rather than gesturing at them. Two in particular. First, the radio link has a measurable latency; the analyst should rule on whether that delay counts as compensable working time or as commuting (and, if commuting, commute to which campus, since the body's badge swipes and the brain's meeting attendance now disagree). Second, they need to address the classification during a signal outage, when the body goes on moving around the Berkeley campus without guidance and, on one occasion, walked into a drainage ditch while the brain in Houston saw and heard nothing of it: the employee's position is that nothing done in that interval was done by him, and there is a workers' compensation question attached to whether the injury arose out of and in the course of employment. You can add a ruling on the one-device-per-employee policy or on which state withholds what, if it serves the comedy.

The piece should never name the philosophical problem or use the phrase personal identity; the humor has to come entirely from watching ordinary administrative categories grind away at a case where the employee's brain and body are in different states. A reader who knows Dennett's story should be able to tell that you do: the naming of the brain and the body, the total dependence on the transmission link, the way the question of where the man is stops being a gag and becomes a practical one about who can be paid, insured, and held responsible. But it should still be funny to a colleague who has never heard of it. The target is not the philosopher — it is the bureaucratic faith that once the administrative facts are settled correctly, the deeper question quietly settles itself, and I would like that faith to be the thing the reader laughs at without anyone ever saying so. Please write in American English.
