I'm in a graduate reading group on the ethics of ideals, and I've been trying to rebuild the classic anti-sainthood argument — the one saying that a life wholly given over to moral commitment would be a worse life, and not one that morality demands — in a form I can actually defend under pressure. To block the obvious reply that the saint can simply schedule friendship and music into her week, I added two stipulative devices of my own: call a good *attitude-dependent* when you get it only if, while you're in it, you are not treating it as a permitted way to spend time you could have spent better; and call it *the revocability discount* that a good you hold only on a lease, and would surrender the moment something more important came up, is worth less than the very same good held unconditionally. A friend in the group says my devices make the argument far too strong: they seem to refute every kind of single-minded devotion and then to turn around and refute themselves. I can't tell whether that is a genuine reductio of the argument or just a consequence I should quietly swallow. Here is the chain:

(1) Many of the best nonmoral goods — being absorbed in a friendship, in a game, in music, in a conversation — are attitude-dependent in my sense: you get them only if, at the time, you are not allocating them as a permissible use of hours owed elsewhere.
(2) The moral saint, as I'll define her, is someone disposed to do whatever is morally best and who treats every hour as owed to whatever moral demand is strongest. So even her leisure is leased: she is with her friends because nothing more urgent has arisen, and she would leave the instant it did. By the revocability discount, her friendships are worth less than the same friendships held unconditionally.
(3) So the saint's life necessarily lacks a class of genuine goods, and lacks them not through bad luck or a hostile world but as the price of the very commitment that makes her a saint.
(4) A life that necessarily lacks genuine goods as the price of its defining commitment is not a life we should want for ourselves or for the people we love, and morality does not require it. So maximal moral goodness is not a desirable ideal.
(5) But nothing in (1)–(4) turns on the value in question being the moral one. Whoever devotes her life to physics, or to the cello, also holds her friendships on a lease, also pays the revocability discount, and also necessarily lacks goods she might have had. So (1)–(4) appear to show that no dominant commitment is desirable and that we should all aim to be moderate, well-rounded people.
(6) And moderation is itself a dominant commitment: the person who renounces absorption in each activity for the sake of balance pays the discount on all of them, and so never fully gets any attitude-dependent good at all. So the generalization of (1)–(4) says that no life is desirable in the sense of (4).
(7) That is absurd. We admire Curie, we admire the saints, and we admire them precisely for the absorption we decline to imitate; admiration is supposed to track a life well lived, not one that is defective.

I want to keep (4) — that is the claim I set out to defend — and I want to reject (7), but my friend insists (5) and (6) follow and that the flaw is in (4) itself. Please take a definite position rather than surveying the literature for me: name the step you reject, state precisely what is wrong with it (an equivocation, a scope or quantifier shift, a slide from one kind of claim to another), and then price the alternatives — for each other step I might reject instead, tell me what giving it up would cost and whether (4) survives. If you reject (1) or (2), you owe me a positive account of what the saint's friendships and hobbies are actually like from the inside, since (1) is what blocks the scheduling reply.
