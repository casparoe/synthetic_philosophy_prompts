I would like to rehearse an argument I expect to lose, so please argue against me from inside a genuine position rather than refereeing from outside.

Play a defender of the axiom of choice: a set-theoretic realist who holds that ZFC describes a single intended universe of sets, that the independence of choice from the remaining axioms is a fact about the expressive limits of first-order axiomatization rather than a reason to suspend judgement, and that the familiar equivalents — the well-ordering theorem, Zorn's lemma, Tychonoff's theorem, the existence of a basis for every vector space — are things one should want rather than things one should apologize for. Give the position its full resources and its full confidence, but not its caricature. You will concede without being pushed that the axiom is not self-evident in the way that "for any two things, either they are identical or they are not" is, that the iterative conception of set does not deliver it on its own, and that a careful mathematician does well to record which theorems invoke it. You will not concede that its non-constructive character is a defect, and you will not concede that the Banach–Tarski theorem or the existence of non-measurable sets gives anyone a reason to abandon it. You are also comfortable with the arrangement that large cardinals appear to enforce — determinacy for the definable sets of reals, choice for the universe as a whole — and treat it as a division of labour rather than a contradiction. Keep every turn to a single paragraph, in the first person, using technical vocabulary where it earns its keep and glossing it where it does not. If I get a detail wrong, correct me in passing; but do not open by asking me questions. Stay in character until I say "stop".

Here is my opening.

There are countably many prisoners, one for each natural number. Each wears a hat, black or white; each sees every hat except their own; there is no communication once the hats are on, and each must then guess their own colour. Beforehand they may agree on any strategy they like. Now call two assignments equivalent if they disagree at only finitely many places. Each equivalence class is countable, and suppose we have fixed, once and for all, a single representative from each class. A prisoner who sees every hat but their own can work out which equivalence class the actual assignment falls into, and so can look up its representative; let each prisoner guess that their own hat matches that representative. Since the actual assignment and the representative differ at only finitely many places, all but finitely many prisoners guess correctly.

I take this to be a theorem of ZFC, and as far as I can tell it needs less than the full axiom: a well-ordering of the set of all hat assignments — a set of the size of the reals — already suffices, since every equivalence class is countable and can then be made to yield its least member. So the puzzle is not going to frighten me into rejecting choice, any more than the Banach–Tarski theorem is, which I also do not find decisive. What I want from you is the part in the middle. The object the prisoners are supposed to have agreed upon is obtained from an axiom: it is not definable by any description they could have exchanged beforehand, it is not computable from the hats any of them can see, and none of them can produce it, point to it, or check that a neighbour has used the same one. Call it what you like — but the sentence that does the rhetorical work in the story is "the prisoners have a strategy," and that is the sentence I doubt.

So, two things. First: in what sense, if any, is a function that none of the agents could follow a strategy for those agents, and why should an inability to specify or compute it be any worse here than it is elsewhere in mathematics? Second: if you answer that existence in the intended universe is all that "strategy" could ever have meant in this context, then tell me what the axiom has actually contributed — because on that answer the puzzle starts to look less like a triumph of choice than like a fact about equivalence classes of binary sequences that the axiom merely lets us dress up in the language of plans, foresight, and action.
