I'm finishing a novel — literary fiction, set about fifteen years ahead — and I need one in-universe document to drop into it, to be read straight through on the page with no framing, no explanation, and no character present who knows enough to be surprised by it.

The document is a letter. It arrives at the kitchen table of a retired music teacher, Marianne Pell, who has twice put the government's values questionnaire straight into the recycling. It comes from a fictional statutory body — call it the Office of Value Provenance — and formally it is a Notice of Annual Values Refit: the courtesy communication sent to households once the Office has finished re-aggregating the public's values and re-fitting the disposition of the general-purpose AI systems licensed to act on the public's behalf. Model it on a pension statement or a utility letter: letterhead, section headings, a sidebar of frequently asked questions, one or two footnotes, the mandated plain-English voice of a communications office that is quietly proud of its readability scores.

The tone must be cheerful, reasonable, and entirely without menace. No dystopian thunder, no official who doubts himself, no villain. The coldness a reader should feel has to come from the reasonableness.

What I need from you is that the comedy be technical rather than topical. The satire has to be built out of the actual machinery of aggregating values at scale, so that a reader who works on alignment laughs at particular choices and a reader who doesn't simply feels the room get colder. Everything below should be present in the letter's content and visible to an attentive reader, but never named or objected to by the letter itself:

— Who is in the aggregate. Children entered through parental proxies; non-resident citizens through a standing cohort; persons not yet alive carried at a statutory placeholder weight, set by regulation and subject to review; the recently deceased retained for one cycle and then dropped. Give me at least one number in passing that quietly gives the game away.

— The instrument. Values are only legible if entered as trade-offs across a fixed schedule of disposition items. Anything a stakeholder declines to trade off is recorded as an abstention, and abstentions are imputed from demographically similar respondents. Marianne has never filled in a form in her life, so everything she is credited with was imputed, and the letter must thank her for her participation as though she had.

— Comparability. Intensity of feeling gets normalized onto a common scale so that one person's conviction can be weighed against another's mild preference, and the letter should treat this normalization as a clerical step.

— The combination rule, and the claim of neutrality that sits on top of it. The Office takes no position on what is good; it takes a position only on how positions are combined. Let the letter state that sincerely and then let its own combination rule — a weighted mean with some rider that up-weights intensity, or whatever device you choose — do the work of a substantive view about the good life without ever acknowledging it.

— Drift. The aggregate is re-fitted on a fixed cycle, so the systems currently act on the public's values as they stood at the last refit, not as they stand today.

— Appeal. There is one, and it consists of restating your values into the same instrument, which is simply more data for the same procedure.

— Her share. She has been assigned to a cluster — name it in the Office's own jargon — and is served only in proportion to that cluster's weight in the composite. A number like one part in several million, offered as reassurance.

Please invent the Office's vocabulary throughout: the names of the clusters, the items on the schedule, the name of the imputation method, its motto. Two constraints. First, it must read as a document from inside a world where everyone already understands what it is talking about — nothing may be explained for the benefit of my reader. Second, no real companies, real researchers, real policy documents, or real white papers: invent the entire provenance.

Give me roughly 800 words, formatted, and close with a line — a sign-off, a postscript, a line of small print — that would make a reader who works in this field have to put the book down for a moment. I'd rather you find that line than I supply it.
