So I'm a philosophy prof who somehow ended up as the parlimentarian for our faculty senate, which means I write the voting rules for about fifteen standing committees — promotion, curriculum, naming and memorials, budget — and I keep running into the same thing. A body has to decide one yes/no question that turns on a couple of other yes/no questions, and whether they vote the sub-questions and infer the answer, or vote straight up-or-down on the final question, changes who wins, and then everyone assumes someone cheated. Last year our promotion committee voted separately on research, teaching, and service, all three came out yes, and the up-or-down tenure vote would have been no.

What I want is a procedure I can hand a committee chair who has never heard of any of this, has ten minutes, and meets monthly: how do you decide what goes on the ballot as separate questions versus one package, when do you aggregate the sub-questions and when the conclusion, what do you do when the two disagree, and in what order do you take the votes (order obviously matters, and I'd rather specify it than let it be an accident). One page, no software, and it has to survive an appeal to the provost and a student newspaper story. Where it can't be turned into a rule — choosing the agenda, deciding whether the body is answerable for its reasons or only for its decisions, what to do with members who are only competent on one sub-question — say so, tell the chair to make a judgment call, and tell them to record it.

Run it on three: promotion with three criteria; a curriculum committee deciding on a college-wide data-science requirement that turns on whether majors need it and whether we can staff it; and the naming committee deciding whether to take a donor's name off a building, where the sub-questions are a historical claim, whether the conduct was condemnable by the standards of his own time, and whether removal is proportionate. As far as I remember from grad school, no rule respects majority on every proposition and always returns a consistent set unless some fixed subgroup effectively gets a veto — so tell me plainly what your procedure does about that, and give me at least one case where it gives an answer I'm not going to like.
