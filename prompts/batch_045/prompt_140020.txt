I sat in on a graduate seminar on the narrative-gravity account of the self, the view that a self is not an inner or material thing but an abstract posit, standing to the stories a creature tells about itself roughly as a center of gravity stands to the mass distribution of a body. Two participants, Elena and Marcus, got into an argument that neither of them resolved, and since I was the only other person in the room I have been asked to say who won. I would rather not guess. Their exchange, condensed but I think not distorted:

(1) Elena: The analogy cannot bear the weight put on it. A center of gravity is fixed by a quantity, mass, so that once the distribution is given there is one determinate answer. For a life there is no analogue of mass: no principled quantity settles how much any episode contributes to where a person's self lies. Whether some stretch of her past is central to who she is is precisely what is in dispute when a person reinterprets her own life. So the account yields an object with no determinate location, which is to say no object at all.

(2) Marcus: Relativity to a chosen system is not indeterminacy. The center of mass of a body, of the body together with the chair it is sitting in, and of the solar system are three different points, and each is perfectly determinate once the system is specified. Selves are like that: specify the corpus of narratives and a weighting, and there is exactly one narrative center. The view was only ever a claim about what kind of thing a self is, an abstract and relational posit that is real without being material, not a promise that each human organism comes with exactly one self independently of any specification.

(3) Elena: Grant the relativity point. The trouble is that physical systems compose and narrative corpora do not. The body's center of mass and the body-plus-chair's are related by determinate compositional rules, and the smaller systems are parts of the larger; there is no analogous rule for composing the self-as-narrated-in-her-thirties with the self-as-narrated-now, or the self she presents to her family with the one she presents at work. And there is a further disanalogy, which she called the veto problem: mass cannot refuse to count toward a center of gravity, but a subject can repudiate a story about herself, and whether that story counts toward who she is seems to be a normative, first-personal matter rather than a quantity. Two narrative corpora can therefore come apart with no fact of the matter about how to add them up.

(4) Marcus: The veto problem is not an objection but the payoff. Because a self's identity depends on an activity of self-interpretation that can go better or worse, a self is the kind of thing that can be alienated from itself, misrecognized, and held to account, and no merely material object could be any of those. That subjects have authority here is exactly why the posit earns its keep. The analogy was meant to secure realism about a non-material object, not to transfer the mathematics. And anyone who wants a determinate, veto-proof self instead owes an account of what fixes it: the natural candidate, sub-personal psychological and neural fact, cannot settle whether someone acted for the reason she now gives, since such questions are settled at the level of the person.

Neither persuaded the other, and I genuinely cannot tell where the burden of proof lies. Please adjudicate the exchange.

Format: exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no closing summary. Items 1 through 4 should assess moves (1) through (4) respectively, saying for each whether it is right, partly right, or wrong and giving the reason in a sentence. Item 5 should state what both parties are missing and, in light of that, who has the better of the argument.
