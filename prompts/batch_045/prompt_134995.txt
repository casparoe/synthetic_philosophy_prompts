I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy, and I've been asked to interview a visiting speaker for our department's graduate-run interview series: she has published several papers defending the procreation asymmetry — that the absence of pain is good even when there is nobody for whom it is good, while the absence of pleasure is not bad unless somebody is thereby deprived of it — together with the antinatalist conclusion she draws from it. I want eight to ten questions that go beyond inviting her to restate the argument, each with a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to make. Please include at least one question on each of the following. First, on whether the asymmetry is a claim about the comparative betterness of outcomes or about our reasons and duties: if the former, it seems to need a good that belongs to no one yet still makes one outcome better than another; if the latter, it is hard to see how it yields the claim that coming into existence is a harm to the person who comes into existence. Second, on whether she can retain a deprivationist account of harm, and on what the view therefore implies about prenatal versus posthumous nonexistence — in particular, why a painless death is supposed to be bad for the person who dies while never having existed is not bad for anyone. Third, on the distance between the asymmetry and the antinatalist conclusion: what empirical or psychological premises about the amount of suffering in even a very good human life, and about hedonic adaptation, are carrying the weight, and whether the conclusion shrinks to mere permissibility if they are false. Fourth, on the selection case: if she is going to have a child either way, does the asymmetry leave her any reason — for the child's sake or otherwise — to bring into existence the child expected to fare better rather than a worse-off one, and if it does, why that reasoning does not also favor bringing the better-off child into existence in the first place. Close with a single question about what kind of evidence could count against the view, and avoid anything she could answer with an anecdote about how she came to the topic.
