I am a third-year undergraduate in a seminar on Rousseau's political thought, and next week I am responsible for leading one fifty-minute discussion section on the *Letter to d'Alembert on the Theatre*. I would rather stage the argument than present it, and I would like an activity designed to the constraints below.

The setting: eighteen students, one seminar room with a table we can push against the wall, no projector, no money, and nothing that could be mistaken for a real emergency. I do have permission to bring in one genuine, optional, non-financial action that costs about half an hour — signing up for a shift with a local tenants' advice service, say, or writing a letter to a city councillor — and to collect anonymous opt-in pledges at the end.

There are three theses I want the section to arrive at from the inside rather than from me. First, Rousseau's inversion of catharsis: the theatre discharges pity where it is safe, so that we leave with the passion spent and with nothing left for the street — the emotion is complete in itself and asks nothing further of us, which is precisely why it is not a rehearsal for action but a substitute for it. Second, the scandal of the actor: the performer's excellence consists in not feeling what he represents, in producing the signs of a passion he does not have, and we pay him for a counterfeiting that would be contemptible outside the playhouse. Third, the alternative Rousseau proposes is not better plays but a different structure of attention: open-air civic festivals with no stage and therefore no spectators, in which a people is at once performer and audience and gives itself its own spectacle.

What I need from you is a runnable design: the rules, the materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, any roles that must be assigned in advance, a debrief, and an honest account of what the exercise cannot do.

Some design requirements. There must be no lecture up front; the words sterile pity, catharsis, and paradox of the actor should not be uttered until the debrief. Because I have only eighteen people, I want both conditions run in sequence with the whole group rather than a between-subjects split: a spectacle condition, in which a handful of volunteers perform a short scene of misfortune and the rest are confined strictly to the spectator's part — watching, rating how moved they were, applauding — and then a participatory condition with no spectator position available at all, in which everyone does the same simple thing at the same time, a song or a shared piece of bread or a circle. Both conditions must end in the same real opportunity to act, and the only thing measured is how many follow through. I want the group to feel the gap between the intensity of what they felt and the smallness of what they did, and then to argue about whether the gap means what Rousseau says it means.

I would also like a short block on the second thesis: the same four lines read twice under two different instructions, once to feel the grief and once to make the room feel it, with a vote afterwards on which was more moving and a report from the reader about which was easier to sustain. Please supply the lines, or tell me what such a passage has to contain.

One role card I want invented rather than drawn from the text: in the participatory condition, a participant instructed to refuse to join, to stand aside and simply watch. I have become convinced that the festival is the more dangerous half of the argument and not the safer one — a gathering with no spectators has nowhere to put the person who dissents, and the unanimity it manufactures is exactly what the irony and distance of the theatre protect against. I want that objection generated inside the exercise instead of named by me, and I want the debrief to say honestly what should be done with it when it appears.

For the debrief: map specific moments onto the three theses; distinguish the theses the exercise actually earns from the ones it merely illustrates; and tell me what I will have to supply myself in the last eight minutes because the form of the activity cannot show it. I assume this includes the point that Rousseau's claim is about mores formed over generations in a small republic rather than about a single afternoon, and the point that the division of a people into actors and spectators is for him the political harm itself, prior to any question about the content of the plays.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure: the acting being bad enough that nobody feels anything, someone guessing the hypothesis and performing accordingly, the pledge count being too small and too noisy to be worth reading out, the first condition contaminating the second or the reverse, and the objection that I have manipulated the room — which, if it comes, I would like to be able to turn into a point about the legislator who stages the festivals, so please say how.
