Write a satirical document: a Request for Proposals issued by a fictional government office for a translation manual covering a recently contacted people, in which every attempt by the office to specify what it is buying quietly fails. About 800–900 words.

The setup the document should take for granted, and which you should state in its background section: field workers have recorded a large corpus of the natives' speech. They have firm results at the level of what the linguists in the document call observation sentences: when a rabbit is visible and the field worker says something in the native language along the lines of "Gavagai?", the natives reliably assent, and they dissent in its absence. What nobody can settle is what the word "gavagai" is a word for. The office is soliciting a complete manual: a set of rules that assigns utterances to occasions and predicts, for every sentence in the corpus, whether the natives will assent to it under what stimulation.

The competing manuals the office is implicitly choosing among should include at least these readings of the term: whole rabbits; undetached rabbit parts; temporal stages of rabbits; the property or universal of rabbithood, instantiated here; a mass-term reading on which the word names a stuff rather than countable objects; and a manual that keeps "rabbit" as the obvious reading but applies a proxy function that systematically reassigns what other terms in the language range over, so that the whole manual makes the same predictions with a different ontology. Every one of these predicts every assent, every dissent, and every utterance in the corpus, and the document should make that point by showing the office failing to get purchase on it rather than by having anyone explain it.

Form and voice. Write it as a real procurement notice: numbered sections, a scope of work, deliverables, eligibility, an evaluation rubric, a mandatory attachment list, a protest procedure, and then a short appendix of "Questions received from prospective bidders" with the office's answers. The voice is flat, competent, bureaucratic, and entirely serious; the humor comes from the content, not from jokes about the content. No character winks, no one says the word "indeterminacy," and no one steps outside the genre to tell the reader what to think.

What the satire has to land, without ever stating it:

- Every criterion of evaluation the office invents either fails to separate the manuals or covertly assumes one of them. It can require predictive accuracy over the corpus, and all candidates score 100. It can require parsimony, and then has to decide parsimony of what — objects, properties, stages, stuff — which is the original question in a new hat. It can require the manual to respect the natives' own competence about their language, and discover that the natives' assent behavior is exactly what the competing manuals agree about.
- A mandatory "Attachment R (Reference Statement)," in which the bidder must state in English what the native term refers to, and which the office must then admit it has no procedure for checking.
- The office's dawning worry that the problem is not ignorance. At no point may anyone discover the true reference, or resolve the matter with more data, more field workers, better instruments, brain scans, a longer corpus, or the discovery of a bilingual informant. The failure is meant to look like it is structural rather than logistical, and the reader should be able to say why.
- Equally, no one may conclude that translation is impossible or that the manual can't be written. The office's entire business is that manuals work fine for every practical and scientific purpose; what it cannot do is choose uniquely. Do not let the document collapse into skepticism about communication.
- In the appendix, one bidder asks what the office's own English word "rabbit" refers to, and whether that too is up for grabs. The office's answer should be deadpan, non-committal, and should not quite succeed in dismissing the question.

Constraints. Don't name real philosophers or real works, and don't let the humor depend on any in-joke beyond the rabbit case, which you should set up yourself in the background section. Invented authorities, subcommittees, and contractors are fine. Keep the office competent throughout: the document should be funny because a well-run institution is doing everything right and still cannot write the specification, not because anyone is confused. Do not let any character assert, as a straight claim, that there is a true reference we simply cannot know, or that there is no fact of the matter at all; instead, let the reader feel why those two claims are hard to tell apart and why the difference matters. Close on the vote or the award decision, with the contract let as scheduled.
