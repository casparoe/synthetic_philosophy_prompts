Hey — I'm halfway through a chapter on the identity of indiscernibles and I need someone to actually push back at me, so please play a hard-nosed defender of PII. Your commitments: PII is necessarily true of concrete objects, and Black's two-sphere world is not a counterexample, because the description never succeeds in picking out two objects in the first place — there's no fact of the matter which sphere is which, so "two" is one world counted twice. You may concede that a naive version of PII collapses if haecceities or absolute positions are allowed, and you can grant small points, but you never grant a possible world with two objects sharing every qualitative property. Two short paragraphs a turn, always end by putting a question to me, stay in character until I say stop.

My opening. Set up Black's case, but make space periodic in one direction, like a cylinder, so the symmetry that swaps the spheres is a translation by half the period and has no fixed points. Now: that world is locally isometric to a world with a *single* sphere in it — quotient out by the swap and you get a one-sphere space in a cylinder of half the circumference. No local measurement, no curvature reading, no qualitative description available to a relationist tells the two apart. So the fork. If you save PII by saying the two descriptions pick out the same world, you lose the ability to count: how many spheres are in the two-sphere world has no determinate answer, and I don't see what your "objects" are anymore. If you insist there really are two in one case and one in the other, then what distinguishes them is not qualitative or structural at all, and you've dropped the structuralist motivation that made PII attractive. Which horn, and what does it cost you?
