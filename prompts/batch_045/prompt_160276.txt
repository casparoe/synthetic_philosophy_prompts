I spent rather more than forty years as an isotope geochemist — radiocarbon and uranium-series work, mostly on the calibration side — and since retiring I have been sitting in on a philosophy of science reading group at the university where I used to teach. The group has asked each of us to set down, in a single page, where we have landed on some question, so that the others can attack it. Mine concerns the demarcation of history from pseudohistory, which I came to by way of an argument with a friend who has become fond of Fomenko's New Chronology. Before I hand it in I would like an audit, because several of the things I want to say seem to me to pull in different directions and I cannot locate the fault. Where I have landed is this:

(a) I do not think the classical demarcation problem has a solution, and I am inclined to think it is largely a pseudo-problem. Falsificationism fails because of the auxiliary-hypothesis point, and I see no prospect of a bright line between science and non-science in general, or between history and non-history in particular.

(b) I nonetheless think "pseudohistory" picks out something real. Fomenko's chronology, ancient-astronaut accounts of megalithic construction, and the rest are defective rather than merely unfashionable, and the defects are ones that can be pointed out to anyone willing to look.

(c) I refuse to dismiss them with the slogan about extraordinary claims requiring extraordinary evidence. As I read it, that slogan is either a trivial restatement of Bayesian updating or else it smuggles in the very prior that is in dispute, and I have watched it used to close conversations that should have stayed open.

(d) What I would put in its place is a rule imported from my own laboratory practice, which I call the anchoring condition. A chronological claim earns admission only if it rests on at least two physically independent processes — radiocarbon, tree rings, varve counts, ice-core layering, archaeomagnetism, eclipse records — and a proposed revision must reproduce the existing anchoring network as a whole, not merely the nodes its author finds awkward. On this test Fomenko fails, and I am comfortable saying so.

(e) I have appended a further clause that seems to me to follow from the same instinct: textual transmission is itself a copying process, subject to attrition, interpolation and deliberate alteration, so a chronology anchored only in manuscripts is not anchored at all.

(f) I think what actually marks pseudohistory is not falsity but degeneracy in something like Lakatos's sense — a hard core protected by ad hoc auxiliaries, with nothing predicted in advance that anyone then went and found — and I am drawn to the corollary that degeneracy is visible only after the fact, never while a programme is still running.

(g) I am a thoroughgoing fallibilist about scientific consensus. In my own field the assumption that the atmospheric radiocarbon reservoir had been constant was treated as settled for a good while and was simply wrong, as tree-ring calibration eventually showed. Yet I also hold that deference to the relevant expert consensus is the right default for a non-specialist — which is what I am the moment I open a history book.

(h) On a live case: I regard the hypothesis that an extraterrestrial impact triggered the Younger Dryas cooling as a respectable and probably false hypothesis, not pseudohistory, notwithstanding that most specialists in the relevant fields reject it and that it began as a fringe proposal with an enthusiastic popular following.

What I would like is an audit, not a tutorial. Go through the pairs that genuinely conflict and order them by how much it would cost me to repair them; for each, say what would have to give and what the cheapest repair is. Say also which pairs only look as though they conflict and why the appearance dissolves. Tell me which single revision, if I were allowed only one, would clean up the most. Two things in particular I would like checked: whether (d) as I have stated it really delivers the verdict I want in (h), or whether that verdict is coming from somewhere else in the set; and whether (e) is quietly doing more damage to the ordinary chronology than I have noticed, given that so much of it is textually anchored.

Please close with a single final line, with nothing after it, in the form \answer{(x)}, naming by letter the one commitment you think I should revise if I may revise only one.
