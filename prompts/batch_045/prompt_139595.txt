Hi — could you help me set one exam question? Bit of background first so you know where I'm coming from.

I'm retired, forty-odd years as a field biologist (estuarine ecology, mostly wading about counting invertebrates), and for the past few years I've been sitting in on a second-year philosophy course — Philosophy and Political Economy, or some such — a mix of evening-class adults and a handful of nineteen year olds. The lecturer has very kindly let each of us auditors contribute one question to the take-home exam, and I've been given Hume on money and trade.

I like the material because it reminds me of my old job. Hume's picture is basically a self-righting feedback loop: gold flows in, more money chases the same goods, prices rise, the country's exports get dear, gold flows back out again. Same shape as things I used to model, except his loop has a time lag in it — the money arrives and does its work before prices have caught up — and Hume says explicitly that industry is quickened during that interval.

What I can't work out is how to ask about that in a way that isn't just "describe the price-specie flow mechanism." Every draft I do can be answered by someone who memorised two slogans: money isn't wealth, and the balance of trade self-corrects. I want the good students to have to think.

So: one essay question, part (a) short and part (b) long, for a 90-minute take-home, about 800 words total, aimed at students who have read the essays on money, the balance of trade and the jealousy of trade from the 1752 Political Discourses. The crux I want them to hit is something like this: if an influx of money only reshuffles relative prices before washing back out, then Hume's case against the mercantilists looks like an argument about the long-run equilibrium that quietly concedes the short run to them — so is the stimulating effect during the interval merely a transient on the way to the same steady state, or can it leave a permanent mark? And since habit and custom are the centrepiece of Hume's own psychology, it's a bit wierd that his economics treats the interval as if nothing sticks — I'd like students to notice that and say whether it's a tension, and if not, why not.

Please give me: the question itself (with the marks split between the parts), a model answer of 600–700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20, built around five or so must-hit points with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on how students typically get each one wrong. I want the scheme to say clearly what separates an A from a B, and to state what a bare recitation of the mechanism earns — I suspect a low 2.2 and no more.

Two more things. A couple of the keen ones will drag in modern stuff — fiat money, central banks, 2008 — and I never know whether to reward that or tell them it's off-topic; give me a line on how to handle it in the scheme. And please don't build the question around my control-theory vocabulary (lag, transient, steady state): the students haven't got it, though a footnote pointing me at where those terms would go in the model answer would be very welcome.
