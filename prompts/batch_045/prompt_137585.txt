In a reading group I run on long-term futures, the Fermi paradox keeps getting settled with the same analogy: a spacefaring species, even at a modest fraction of light speed, would fill the galaxy in a small fraction of the galaxy's age, the way humans peopled the Pacific islands or the Americas — so the fact that nothing is here means nothing is out there. One member goes further and uses it to argue that we are almost certainly alone, that the great filter therefore lies in our past, and that the long-term future is entirely ours to lose; he takes this to settle a priority, putting existential-risk reduction above everything else.

I want the analogy audited rather than the hypotheses relitigated. Please lay out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the part of the islands, the craft, the crossing, the settlers' descendants, the known habitability of the destination, and the drive to keep going — and then the places the mapping fails. Two imports I am suspicious of: that the destinations are known in advance to be worth having, and that each colony inherits the motive to found further colonies. Say which disanalogies actually matter for the work the analogy is doing here and which do not.

Then the part I really care about: does the conclusion survive? My own guess is that it partly does — even if the most the analogy supports is that we have no evidence of anyone, rather than that no one exists, the priority he draws might be unchanged, because a galaxy that is merely silent and one that is empty may both leave the future in our hands. Is that right, or does his argument need the strong reading and collapse without it? And if the colonization picture is the wrong one, what would be a better analogy — I have in mind the Norse reaching North America and not following through, but I am not confident it has the right shape.
