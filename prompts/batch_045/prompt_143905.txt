I've just come out of a long and inconclusive argument with a friend — we were in the same graduate seminar on mental causation last term, and we spent most of an evening afterwards trying to work out why each of us found the other's escape route from the exclusion problem so unsatisfying. She ended up defending a reductionist line I can't accept, and I came away unable to say precisely what my own position commits me to, so I'd like to write it down and have it audited rather than argued with. Roughly, here is where I've landed: (1) token physicalism — every particular mental occurrence is identical to a physical occurrence, or at least wholly constituted by one, and I'm happy to retreat to constitution if identity is too strong; (2) anti-reductionism about mental properties — multiple realizability is genuine and not mere ignorance of neuroscience, so there are no type identities; (3) causal closure of the physical — every physical event that has a cause has a physical cause sufficient for it; (4) no systematic overdetermination — it can't be that, as a general rule, every case of mental causation is a case of two independent sufficient causes; (5) mental properties are genuinely causally relevant, and I take the causal claims of psychology and the other special sciences to be data that a metaphysical story has to accommodate, not premises it gets to trade away; (6) the way out I favour is a difference-making account of causation — causes are things such that intervening on them would change the effect — combined with a proportionality constraint, on which a cause shouldn't contain detail irrelevant to the effect, which is why the neural realiser is often disqualified and the mental property isn't, so that there is no competition for a single causal slot; (7) a "qua" requirement — for my belief to matter, the event must produce what it does in virtue of being a belief with that content, not merely in virtue of being the neural state it happens to be, and the reply that the mental property simply borrows the causal relevance of its realiser, having a subset of its causal powers, strikes me as relabelling the problem rather than solving it; (8) content externalism — what I believe is not fixed by my intrinsic physical state, and a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of me in a different environment would be in states with different contents; (9) I reject strong emergence and downward causation as spooky, but I do like saying that the mental is grounded in the physical rather than identical with it, and I take grounding to be a genuine but non-causal determination relation; (10) two case verdicts — I hold that absences and omissions are never genuine causes, only positive happenings and their properties are, and I also hold that in a case where a neuroscientist's stimulation would have produced exactly the same hand movement anyway, the agent's decision was still a cause, and that this is a one-off case of genuine overdetermination; (11) I think causation is an objective feature of the world, not something relative to which variables we happen to be interested in or which contrast class we have in mind; and (12) methodologically, I don't want to lean at any point on how unattractive epiphenomenalism is — its repulsiveness isn't evidence against it.

What I'd like from you is an audit, not a survey of the literature: go through this set and tell me which commitments actually pull against each other and how badly, ranking the tensions by severity rather than flagging all of them as equally serious; for each real tension, say exactly what would have to be given up and what that concession would cost me elsewhere in the set; and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I also suspect that at least one item on the list only looks inconsistent with the others and is in fact fine, so please say which ones dissolve on inspection and why, rather than finding fault everywhere. Be concrete about which theses do the damage — my friend's parting shot was that I was leaning on a technicality somewhere I couldn't see, and I'd rather locate it now than discover it when I present this.
