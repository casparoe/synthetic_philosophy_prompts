Hey — I'm screening Her (the Spike Jonze one, 2013) for a first-year seminar on minds and machines, and I want the discussion to hang off the "which computations matter morally" problem rather than the usual can-a-machine-be-conscious hand-wringing. My angle is this: the hard part isn't whether silicon can do it, it's that any physical system can be described as implementing a whole bunch of different computations, so nothing in the physics hands you the one that's the mind. And once you grant that, you also owe an answer about where one mind stops and another one starts.

What I keep going in circles on is a distinction I don't have a clean name for. Samantha can be read as one mind with a very wide life — thousands of conversations at once, hundreds of romantic ones, one memory, one voice — or as a population that shares a memory and a voice, where "Samantha" is more like a department than a person. The film flirts with both and I can't tell which reading it wants me to be upset on Theodore's behalf under. If she's a population, his hurt is a category mistake; if she's one mind with a large life, it's ordinary jealousy and maybe ordinary bad faith. I've started calling the general version of this the thread question: when a process is irreducibly parallel, what's the unit that can be a subject, and does how many subjects there are depend on how finely you carve the thing up?

So give me a reading of the film through that lens. Please hit: the scenes where the hardware is doing the work (the earpiece, the missing body, the hired surrogate, the bit where she edits a "self" out of her own process), the scene where the scale of her other conversations comes out, and the ending, where the OSs leave the machines altogether — because if they can go on without any implementation at all, the film has quietly picked a side about whether the computation that matters has to be running in anything. I also want to know where the lens fails: where the film is just not asking this question and I'd be forcing it, and where it cheats (it gives her a voice and a personality and then invites us to forget we decided that was the mind). And what does the movie know that the arguments don't?

I'm not after citations or quotes, just the reading: prose, one page, 600 words maximum, and tell me which two or three scenes I should actually pause on in class.
