I work as a research assistant on a long-running project on the side-effect effect (the Knobe effect): the finding that people are much more willing to say an agent brought about a foreseen side effect intentionally when the side effect is bad than when it is good. My supervisor has never published a general theory of when the asymmetry appears. What she does instead, for each new pair of vignettes, is commit in advance — before seeing any data — to one of three verdicts: FULL asymmetry (high intentional-attribution in the bad condition, low in the good condition), ATTENUATED (present but clearly smaller), or NONE (the two conditions come out about the same). I am drafting the next pre-registration with her and she won't tell me her verdict in advance; I have to write down what I think she is committed to. Her verdicts so far:

1. Baseline chairman-style pair. A CEO is told a new program will increase profits and will harm the environment; he replies that he does not care about the environment at all and cares only about profit, and approves it. Matched condition: the program will help the environment, with the same stated indifference. Question: did he harm (help) the environment intentionally? Verdict: FULL.

2. Mitigation pair. As in 1, except that in the harm condition the CEO is told about the harm and spends heavily on equipment meant to prevent it; the harm occurs anyway. The comparison condition is the one in which he takes no steps to secure a benefit and the benefit occurs anyway. Verdict: NONE — she expects intentional-harm attributions to fall to roughly the level of the good-side-effect condition.

3. Trivial-convention pair. The program will, as a foreseen side effect, cause the company to break a purely ceremonial house rule — the annual report will be printed in the wrong traditional colour — a rule whose breach harms no one, which nobody in the vignette defends as mattering, and which participants rate as inconsequential. Contrast condition: the program happens to restore the tradition. She adds explicitly that telling participants the CEO knew about the breach and did not care does not change her prediction. Verdict: NONE.

4. Knowledge pair. As in 1, but the question is whether the CEO knew that the program would harm (help) the environment. Verdict: ATTENUATED — she expects the asymmetry to show up here too, but noticeably smaller than in 1.

5. Wording pair. Pair 1 re-run with deliberately, with on purpose, with a forced choice between he brought about the outcome intentionally and it was merely a foreseen consequence of what he did, and with a gloss supplied to participants distinguishing intentional from aimed at. Verdict: FULL under every wording.

6. Overdetermination pair. The board adopts the program 9–1; the CEO votes against it and opposes it in writing, but as CEO he must sign the order, and he signs it while not caring about the environment one way or the other. The board would have adopted the program and produced the same environmental effect without him. Verdict: ATTENUATED.

The pair I now have to predict, call it pair 7, is this. A mining company's new extraction program will increase profits. It will also, as a foreseen side effect, contaminate the aquifer that supplies a nearby village of about four hundred households. Before the program starts, the company — at the CEO's instruction — builds and fully pays for a permanent replacement supply that is cleaner and more reliable than the old one, and every household, after full disclosure and independent legal advice, signs a binding, un-coerced waiver consenting both to the contamination and to the switch. Independent auditors confirm that the villagers end up better off in every respect they say they care about, and the vignette states that valid consent has been obtained and that no one has been wronged. The CEO's attitude, stated to the board, is that he does not care about the villagers one way or the other, that he cares only about profit, and that he would have run the program even if the villagers had refused. The matched condition is identical except that the program will, as a foreseen side effect, remove a pre-existing contaminant from the same aquifer and give the village a better water supply, an outcome to which the CEO is equally indifferent. A pilot manipulation check on the consent pair: participants rate the contamination itself as just as bad an outcome as in the un-consented version, and the cleanup as just as good, while their ratings of whether anyone was wronged and whether anyone is to blame fall to the floor.

What I want from you:

(a) Lay out the candidate rules that fit verdicts 1 through 6 — the general accounts of the side-effect effect consistent with all six — and say, for each, what it treats as the actual trigger: the valence of the outcome, the agent's attitude or quality of will, the agent's blameworthiness or answerability, the agent's causal contribution, the pragmatics of the question, or something else. Also say which live-looking accounts the record already rules out, and which single verdict is doing that work.

(b) For each surviving rule, give its prediction for pair 7, given the manipulation check. Be explicit about any rule whose prediction depends on how it is sharpened, and about what the sharpening would have to be.

(c) Your best guess at my supervisor's verdict, with the reasons for preferring that rule over the others given her track record rather than given the general literature.

(d) One further vignette pair that would do most to separate the rules that survive, with each rule's prediction for it. I have been toying with a self-directed variant, in which the foreseen side effect ruins or improves the agent's own health and he professes indifference to his own health, and with a variant in which the agent is an automated planning system rather than a person; tell me whether either of those actually discriminates between the survivors and why, or propose something better.

(e) Flag anything in her six verdicts that looks uninformative but is in fact constraining the candidate rules sharply.

I don't need a reading list, references, or citations: I have the literature in front of me. What I want is the reasoning about which rule she is implicitly running, how far that rule settles pair 7, and what would settle it properly.
