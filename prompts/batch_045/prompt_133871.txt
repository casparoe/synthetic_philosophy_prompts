I'm putting together a survey-paper scorecard on chance–credence principles, and I've been going in circles on one narrow question for weeks, so I'd like a second opinion with numbers attached rather than another round of prose. Set the stage as follows: write Ch_t(A) for the objective chance at time t of proposition A, and read the core norm as saying that a rational agent's credence in A, conditional on the claim that Ch_t(A) = x together with any admissible evidence E she also has, should simply be x. Everyone agrees this is worthless until admissible gets spelled out, and there are two familiar headaches: first, the obvious readings of admissibility either make the norm vacuous (any evidence that moves your credence about A can be repackaged as evidence that moves your credence about the chances) or circular (admissible evidence is just evidence the chance already screens off); second, on the reductionist picture of chance, evidence about what the chances will be later is itself evidence about what they are now, so it undercuts the very quantity you were told to defer to. I'd like you to work with this menu of eight candidate formulations, all described self-containedly so we don't need to argue about who said what: (1) the historical version, on which admissible evidence is exactly everything about the history of the world up to now, including the chances up to now, and nothing about later chances; (2) the outcome-insensitivity version, on which E is admissible just in case E would still have been available to you however the chancy event turned out, i.e. E's holding doesn't depend on A; (3) the chance-screening version, on which E is admissible just in case the chance of A, conditional on E, is still x, so admissible evidence is precisely what the chance itself already screens off; (4) the theory-conditional version, on which you first require the agent to be certain of the complete true theory of chance for her world and then let all historical evidence in unrestrictedly; (5) the index-to-the-event version, on which you defer not to the present chance but to the chance that obtains at the time the event happens, so that evidence about later-but-still-earlier-than-the-event chances is admissible and you defer to the most recent chance you learn; (6) the global expectation version, which drops admissibility entirely and requires instead that your unconditional credence in A equal your own weighted average of the possible values of the chance of A, weighted by how confident you are in each; (7) the diachronic or holistic-updating version, which treats the norm as a constraint on how you revise when you learn a chance value, so that you move your credence to the announced figure while simultaneously revising your views about the chances themselves if the announcement is itself undermining evidence, using something other than plain conditionalization; (8) the defeasible-default version, on which there is no admissibility clause at all because deferral to chance is a defeasible default whose authority comes from long-run calibration and accuracy considerations rather than from coherence. Test them against four cases: an ordinary coin about which you have only mundane historical evidence; the standard undermining case, where you learn what the chances will be after the toss and this rationally shifts your view of what they are now; a case I've been calling the backward-channel coin, where the bias of the coin is reset by a mechanism whose setting causally depends on how a later toss turns out, so that evidence about the later chance is genuinely informative about the outcome but not by way of telling you anything about the true chances; and a chance-gap case, where the chances are well defined now and at the time of the event but go undefined for an interval in between, so that there is no continuous chance trajectory to defer along.

What I want back: sort these eight into three tiers — workable as stated, promising but only at some cost, and not rescuable — and give each entry two scores out of ten, one for how much of the original guidance work it preserves (how much it still tells an agent who is standing in front of an actual coin) and one for how gracefully it handles the four cases, plus a single sentence saying why it lands where it does. Then, since I keep insisting to my co-author that most of the difficulty here is really about admissibility: give me a number between 0 and 1 for the share of the problem you think actually lives in the admissibility clause rather than somewhere else, and say in a sentence where the rest of it lives if you think my number is too high. Finally, name one further test case, ideally one that isn't in the four above, that would separate the top two tiers, and say which entries it would promote or demote. Assume a reader who knows Bayesian conditioning and nothing else about this literature, don't cite or attribute anything, and keep the whole thing under about 700 words. My own prior, which you should feel free to demolish: I came in expecting the theory-conditional version to win and I now suspect the global expectation version is quietly doing all the real work.
