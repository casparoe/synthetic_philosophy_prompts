I teach a first-year seminar on the meaning of life, and next week we are screening Everything Everywhere All at Once alongside a unit on absurdism and the contemporary analytic literature on meaning in life. I want to use the film as a text rather than as decoration, so I need a reading that takes both the film and the philosophy seriously and that I could hand to students without misleading them. The material I actually care about connecting to the film is threefold: the Camus of The Myth of Sisyphus, where the honest response to a silent universe is revolt rather than either suicide or a leap of faith, and where the closing image asks us to imagine the pointless labourer content; Nagel's essay on the absurd, where the problem is generated by the collision between the seriousness with which we live and our capacity to step outside our own lives and see them as arbitrary; and the contemporary analytic accounts, above all Wolf's hybrid view, according to which meaning arises when what a person genuinely cares about meets something valuable independently of their caring, and Metz's fundamentality theory, which as I understand it ties meaning to the orientation of a life toward fundamental conditions of human existence. The film invites all three because it stages the threat to meaning in a very particular form: Jobu Tupaki has experienced every possible life at once and has concluded that nothing matters, her everything-bagel being the image of every distinction and every value collapsing into one undifferentiated lump, while Waymond answers her not with an argument but with a practice of attention and kindness, symbolised by the googly eyes stuck on everything.

The complication I keep getting stuck on, and where I would most like your help, concerns the multiverse machinery itself. A colleague of mine in the department argues that there is a condition on meaning that philosophers have not named, which he calls the costliness condition: a life can be meaningful only if the goods it is organised around are not cheaply revisable, so that to have a life it must be possible to lose it, and to make a commitment it must be possible to fail at it. If that is right, then the film's own structure seems to work against its own happy ending, since verse-jumping makes every choice undoable and every failure somewhere else redeemed, and Evelyn's final return to her family would be a purchase made with counterfeit money. But the opposite reading seems available too, and the film seems to want it: precisely because Evelyn has lived the glamorous alternative lives and acquired every skill, her choice of the ordinary one is maximally informed rather than merely defaulted into, which is the kind of choice that on Wolf's account should count for more, not less. There is also a third worry, in Metz's direction: if meaning requires orientation toward fundamental conditions of human existence, then a being who has lived every life and can do everything has transcended the human condition rather than situated herself within it, so the multiverse might be meaning-defeating on his view even while it is meaning-enhancing on Wolf's. I would like you to work through these three lines and say which one the film's structure actually supports, and whether the costliness condition is a real requirement on meaning or a confusion about what makes a choice significant.

Two further things I would like from the reading. First, I want to know which of the philosophical positions the ending is really committed to, and I suspect the answer may be that it is not one of them: whether Evelyn's reconciliation with Joy is a Camusian revolt that declines to appeal to objective value at all, a Wolfian claim that love and family really are valuable and that her caring has finally met something worth caring about, or something closer to Nagelian irony, the comic and self-deprecating mode that Waymond's googly eyes and the absurd fight choreography seem to embody. If the film smuggles in objective value after having let nihilism pose as the only serious cosmology, I would like that diagnosed rather than celebrated. Second, I want an honest account of where the film resists the reading: the bagel is presented as much as exhaustion and depression as it is as a thesis, which may mean the film psychologises a philosophical position in order to dissolve it by therapy and affection, and the emotional resolution in the laundromat may be doing work that no argument could do. Is that a failure of nerve, or is it the one thing a film can add to this debate that a journal article cannot, and how would you put that to students who will otherwise leave thinking that being nice is a refutation of nihilism?
