I'm writing the final for my third-year seminar on Victorian political thought (small seminar, about fifteen students, mostly politics and PPE people with two philosophers mixed in), and I need one big essay question on Mill's late socialism — by which I mean the material from his last twenty-odd years: the chapter on the probable futurity of the labouring classes in the Principles, the essays on socialism that came out after he died, and the retrospective passage in the Autobiography where he describes what he and Harriet Taylor expected of the future of society. I have a colleague who teaches the same period as history of economic thought and insists Mill's socialism is basically sentimental drift he caught from Harriet, superceding nothing in the earlier system — and I want a question where that answer, or the equally available one ("Mill was a utilitarian, utilitarianism is impartial, so of course he drifted socialist"), scores badly, not because it's false but because it doesn't touch anything.

The crux I keep circling is that Mill's defence of socialism seems to rest on two claims that pull apart, and I want students to feel the pull. First: on his own telling, most of the standard objections to socialism (that people won't work without the spur of personal gain, that the idle will live off the industrious, that the lazy will starve the careful) are objections about human nature as it happens to be constituted now, not about socialism as such — human nature is improvable, and part of what recommending socialism involves is betting on that improvement. Second: he also insists that socialism has to be tried freely, as an experiment, side by side with existing arrangements, and not imposed. Here's the worry I want them to face — if it has to be voluntary and experimental, has Mill given any reason at all to expect it to win, or does his position quietly collapse into "remove the legal obstacles to worker association and see what happens," which isn't a claim about socialism at all? And if it doesn't have to be voluntary, if the transition needs coordinated coercion, then it runs straight into the machinery of On Liberty: the tyranny of the prevailing opinion, the fear of a uniform and commercially mediocre society of the sort he describes in the chapter on individuality. So either the socialism is toothless by design or it's illiberal. I also want them to work with a distinction I invented for the course, ownership vs. authority: is Mill's socialism a thesis about who owns capital, or about the relations of command and dependence in production? His enthusiasm for peasant proprietors and worker co-operatives, and his retention of both competition and property in the products of labour, suggests the second — and if it's the second, then a society of competing co-operatives with markets intact might count as the fulfilment of his hopes even though almost nothing has been socialised. Please put a version of that into the question: I'd like part (b) to hand them a case I've been calling New Rochdale (after the Rochdale Pioneers, sort of) in which every firm is a worker co-operative, markets and competition are untouched, wage labour survives as a short-term contract people enter and leave freely, and income differences between firms are large — and ask whether Mill should count that as socialism achieved, socialism betrayed, or a category mistake, and why, given his own criteria rather than ours.
