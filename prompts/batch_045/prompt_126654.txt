Over the past few weeks a colleague and I have been working through the commissurotomy literature, and we have backed ourselves into an argument whose conclusion neither of us accepts. I would be grateful for a diagnosis. Here is the argument, cleaned up.

(1) Unity. Necessarily, if two experiences belong to a single subject at a single time, then they are co-conscious: there is something it is like for that subject to undergo the two together.

(2) Datum. In the standard cases, a word or picture presented briefly to the left visual field, and so to the right hemisphere rather than to the language-dominant left one, cannot be named or verbally described; but the left hand will retrieve or point to the matching object, and the right hemisphere demonstrably guides behaviour by the stimulus. Asked why the left hand is reaching, the speaking hemisphere confabulates a story with nothing to do with what was shown.

(3) Report. If the right hemisphere's state were co-conscious with the left hemisphere's ongoing experience, then the subject — an intact speaker, with the experience present to them — would be able to report it when asked.

(4) So the right hemisphere's state is not co-conscious with the left hemisphere's experience; so by (1) they belong to different subjects. In the split case there are two streams of consciousness, hence two subjects. Call this the two-streams view.

So far I can live with it. What I cannot accept is the sequel, which runs the same reasoning on ordinary, unoperated people.

(5) The only difference the surgery makes is to inter-hemispheric signalling. The callosum and the other commissures are fibres along which neural signals travel. Sectioning them removes a channel; it adds nothing.

(6) No fusion by cable. Signalling between two systems cannot by itself make their states co-conscious. Join two people by a fibre bundle of arbitrarily high bandwidth, so that everything in A's visual field is available to B and vice versa, and you have two extraordinarily well-informed people, not one subject with one experience.

(7) So the pre-operative unity of the hemispheres was never constituted by signalling, since signalling does not constitute unity. Nor was it constituted by anything else the surgery destroys: the hemispheres themselves, their intrinsic processing, and the subcortical routes all survive.

(8) So the two streams were there before the operation. What surgery changes is not how many subjects there are but how much they can tell each other — including, tellingly, how much they can tell each other by way of the patient's own observable behaviour, the left hemisphere reading the left hand's movements and inferring what the right hemisphere must have seen.

(9) Therefore every neurologically ordinary human being is not one subject but two, and a commissurotomy changes nothing about that number.

Two things make (9) worse than it looks.

First, the argument does not stop at hemispheres. Any two subsystems of a single brain that interact only by passing signals to one another — the ventral and dorsal visual streams, say — stand in exactly the relation that (6) treats as insufficient for unity. If (5) through (8) are sound, then ordinary experience is not a stream but a crowd, and my conviction that I am one subject is a story manufactured by whichever subsystem happens to control speech. An argument that yields a crowd of subjects in every healthy human being has indicted itself, or so I want to say.

Second, take the other end of the series. If (9) holds, then an adult who loses a hemisphere to hemispherectomy for intractable epilepsy does not lose half of a subject's experiences; a subject goes out of existence, and there is no principled way to say whether the person who walks out of the hospital is the one who died or the one who survived.

What I want is a verdict, not a survey. Which step should I reject, why did that step look compulsory when I wrote it down, and what does rejecting it cost me elsewhere? Two constraints: I would like to keep (1), and I would like to keep (2) as an accurate description of the data — I would sooner give up philosophy than neuroscience here. And two replies have already been offered to me that I cannot make work, so please do not simply reissue them. The first is that sectioning a brain creates two subjects rather than revealing two that were already there: I cannot see why the severing of a fibre tract should have the metaphysical power to create a subject, and the reply seems to leave the number of subjects in a room hostage to the position of a neurosurgeon's instrument. The second is that reportability and co-consciousness can diverge, so that (3) fails: I accept that in the abstract, but I do not see how to say what makes a neural route a report route, and the reply seems to leave consciousness dependent on a piece of contingent plumbing.

One further datum, which you should use only if you think it is one: people born without a corpus callosum are, as I understand it, impaired mainly in transferring complex material across hemispheres, and are not, so far as I know, described as two people.
