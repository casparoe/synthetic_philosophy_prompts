I'm a journalist — I mostly cover science and ideas — and I'm midway through a long feature on the many-worlds interpretation, specifically on the bit everyone skips: if every outcome of a measurement really happens, what on earth does it mean to say one of them is more likely? I'm not interested in whether branching is true; the piece takes it as given and asks how probability gets back in. I've been talking to physicists on and off for months and I think I can follow the standard moves — the decision-theoretic derivations in the Deutsch/Wallace vein, the envariance-style symmetry arguments, the self-locating-uncertainty story where for a moment you don't know which branch you're about to be — but I didn't want the section to be me paraphrasing other people's papers, so I tried to write the analytic core in my own words, as a small argument of my own. It's pasted below. My editor thinks the whole thing is a tangent and wants it cut, and I definately can't tell whether she's right or whether I've accidentally written something a physicist would find embarrassing.

Here's the draft section:

Step 1 — bookkeeping, not physics. Forget probability and ask a narrower question: what constraints are there on any scheme for spreading a person's concern across branches? Call a branch's share of concern its weight. I want three constraints. (a) Splitting-invariance: if a branch of amplitude alpha later splits into sub-branches with amplitudes beta and gamma, the weights of the children add to the weight of the parent, and it doesn't matter how finely you draw the lines — a coarse branch's weight equals the sum of the fine branches inside it. (b) Indifference: branches with equal amplitude get equal weight. (c) Continuity: tiny changes in amplitude make tiny changes in weight. Claim: (a)–(c) force weight to be proportional to the squared amplitude, because amplitudes of orthogonal branches combine Pythagoreally, so the only continuous measure that adds across orthogonal decompositions is the squared norm. On this picture the Born rule isn't an extra law bolted onto branching; it's what you get from demanding that your bookkeeping over branches be consistent, the way double-entry bookkeeping constrains a ledger. Corollary: naive branch counting is out, since you can always manufacture extra branches by splitting, and a count isn't invariant under that.

Step 2 — from bookkeeping to caring. A physicist friend said (a) just re-states the Pythagorean structure of the Hilbert-space norm, so I've derived the Born rule from the Born rule; and worse, a measure over branches still doesn't say I should care about a branch in proportion to its measure. So I add two principles meant to be about agency rather than physics: (i) conservation of concern — the concern I now have for my future selves equals the summed concern those future selves will have for theirs, with no concern created or destroyed at the moment of splitting; (ii) no free tickets — I can't raise my expected concern-weighted payoff by rigging up extra branching that leaves the amplitudes alone, i.e. no buying a hundred lottery tickets by arranging to be split a hundred ways.

Step 3 — the bit I actually like. Decoherence never makes branches exactly orthogonal or exactly discrete, so there is no well-defined number of branches at all: branching is continuous in time, and there's no instant at which there are n copies of you. Branch counting needs that number; a measure doesn't. To me that isn't a technical annoyance, it's the reason the Born rule has to be about something other than a head count.
