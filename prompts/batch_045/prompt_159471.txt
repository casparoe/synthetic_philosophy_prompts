I teach a film-and-philosophy seminar and I am building a session on behavioral criteria for mind, using Ridley Scott's Blade Runner as the case study. What I want is a reading of the film, not a list of parallels, and I want it built around a test of my own devising rather than the one everyone reaches for.

Call it the Attrition Test. It replaces the single interrogation with months of unscripted, low-stakes coexistence and asks not whether the candidate is momentarily indistinguishable from a person but whether its behavior over time has the right shape. It counts as evidence of mind: selective and failing attention — boredom, distraction, refusals to answer; habits and private references that no one who had not shared the candidate's days could predict; characteristic errors, that is, misrememberings that track the candidate's own past rather than the facts; the capacity to be surprised by, or to disown, its earlier self; and a standing incuriosity about its own workings, so that being asked to explain itself is met with something other than articulate self-report. Notice the consequence I am betting on: a system engineered to maximize indistinguishability at every instant should be expected to fail, because it would stay too attentive, too consistent, and too articulate about itself. So the test remains behavioral in the minimal sense that it looks only at what the candidate does, yet its criteria are irreducibly diachronic, and it appears to penalize exactly the competence that passing the classical imitation game rewards.

I want two things done with it. First, the philosophy: is this a genuine behavioral criterion on a longer timescale, or has it smuggled in a historical or etiological condition — that the responses be produced by the right kind of biography — of the sort a behavioral criterion was supposed to do without? That is the objection I expect, and I want it answered rather than conceded.

Second, the reading. Run the Voight-Kampff sequences, the Rachael scenes, and Roy Batty's last acts through the Attrition Test and tell me who passes and who fails, and why: Rachael, whose memories belong to someone else and whose life is short; Roy, whose entire biography is a few years and who closes it with a speech of extraordinary self-awareness; Deckard, who is bored, drinks, and volunteers almost nothing about himself. Does the film's own argument turn on the claim that what the replicants lack is not a behavior but a history — a childhood, a mother, photographs — and is that the film's position or only Deckard's? Is the fact that the test is administered under threat of death, and that one examinee answers it with a gun, a point about the ecology of any behavioral test that the philosophical debate has failed to make? And where does the film resist the reading? The scene audiences most often take as the strongest evidence of mind is precisely the one my test should treat with suspicion, and I would rather you said so than smoothed it over.

Constraints: do not reduce the film to an illustration of a position — I can get that anywhere; say what the film does to the question that argument alone does not. Do not settle whether Deckard is a replicant — tell me what turns on it for the reading instead. No verbatim quotation from the film and no secondary sources. As you go, separate what is settled among people who know this material — what the imitation game and its standard objections actually are — from what is contested: how far those objections really bite, what the film is doing, and every verdict that depends on my invented test rather than on anything anyone has argued. Where a claim is yours or mine rather than the literature's, mark it as such. Aim for about nine hundred words.
