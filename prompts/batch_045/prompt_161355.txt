I'm a retired physical chemist — thirty-odd years of NMR, and almost no philosophy until last year — and the reading group I go to on Tuesday evenings has got onto Dennett: the physical, design and intentional stances, plus that business about real patterns being whatever lets you compress the data rather than something you just read off the world. I'm enjoying it in a suspicious kind of way, meaning I think I follow it but I can't tell whether I'm learning anything or just recognising moves I already made in the lab. My grandson, who does machine learning, keeps saying his networks "want" things and I tell him off, and then I caught myself doing exactly the same thing about a slime mould, which is what prompted this.

The case I want to try the framework on is the mould experiment that was all over the news a while back: a Physarum plasmodium grown in a dish mapped onto the region around Tokyo, with food sources sitting roughly where the cities sit, and the tube network it built up over a day or so got compared to the actual Tokyo rail system — comparable total length, reasonable fault tolerance, that sort of thing. It got written up as the mould solving a network design problem, even out-designing the engineers, and as far as I remember there was some grumbling afterwards that the comparision was flattering to the mould. I don't care about the priority dispute or about the ecology; take the episode roughly as I've described it and use it as a test case.

So run it through the three stances properly, and be disciplined about the mapping. For each stance say what it would actually let you predict about this setup and where it breaks: the physical stance is complete in principle but buys you nothing anyone could have used; the design stance looks strange here because there is no designer in the usual sense, only selection and a developmental history, so tell me whether that counts as design for these purposes; the intentional stance is the one the news coverage reached for, and I want to know whether it was doing real explanatory work or only decorating a pattern the other two could have delivered. Then the bit I actually care about, real patterns: on the compressibility criterion, is "rail-network-like" a real pattern of the mould's behaviour, or is it really a pattern of the experimenter's setup together with the comparison we chose to draw? Does the completeness of the physical stance threaten the reality of the higher-level pattern, or does worrying about that just show I have misunderstood what real was supposed to mean? And here is the thought that ocurred to me and that I want tested rather than indulged: I'd like to propose a fourth stance, call it the optimality stance, where you predict what a system will do by asking what would be extremal, cheapest or most efficient — least time in optics, energy minimisation, optimal foraging — with no mechanism, no designer's spec and no attributed beliefs. My hunch is that this, and not the intentional stance, is what people were genuinely using on the mould, and that it isn't just the design stance because it never has to say what the thing was built for. Tell me whether Dennett's three already have a slot for it or whether it is genuinely extra, and whether the mould case is evidence for a stance his list doesn't contain. Finally, be honest about where the framework is silent: there isn't an agent with options here, so I don't see how it says anything about what the mould was faced with, and I'd rather you said so than stretched the theory to speak.

Lead with a single sentence that is your bottom line, then the rest. Under 700 words, prose paragraphs, no bullet points.
