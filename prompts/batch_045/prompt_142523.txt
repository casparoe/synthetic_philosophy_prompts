I spent a career in experimental physics and have come to decision theory only in retirement, so I may be missing what the field takes for granted; but before I read any more proposed solutions I want to know what I should be demanding of one. The case that stopped me is the Pasadena game. A fair coin is tossed until it first lands heads; if that first heads falls on toss n, the payoff is (−1)^{n−1}·2^n/n dollars — plus 2, minus 2, plus 8/3, minus 4, and so on. Each state has probability 2^{−n}, so summing term by term in the natural order gives the alternating harmonic series, which converges to ln 2, about 0.69. Yet the positive terms alone sum to +∞ and the negative terms alone to −∞, so the ordinary integral is not merely unknown but undefined: the same series can be rearranged to converge to any real number you please, or to diverge outright.

In physics we had a name for the manoeuvre of extracting a number from such an expression. One introduces a cut-off, isolates a finite part, discards a divergence, and then argues about whether the finite part depends on the cut-off. When it does not, the number is physical; when it does, the honest conclusion is that we were asking a question that has no answer. I would like the analogous discipline stated explicitly here.

So: write me the specification. What must a rule for evaluating or ranking gambles with undefined expectations deliver in order to count as adequate — the phenomena it must handle, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must respect, the formal properties it must not violate — with the case or consideration behind each requirement? Take the purpose fixed: the rule should be good enough to guide a one-off choice, to guide the same agent's choices sequentially as uncertainty resolves, and to be usable in policy reasoning where a very small probability gets multiplied by an astronomically large value. Mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular temperament, and say which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidates you know of.

Here are the cases I would insist on, including two I have not seen handled. (i) The Pasadena game with one dollar added to every payoff: it weakly dominates, and I take it as a non-negotiable datum that it is preferable. (ii) The same gamble re-presented: lay the states out as intervals on the unit interval in the usual order, then re-lay exactly the same intervals, with exactly the same payoffs attached, in a permuted order — a measure-preserving reshuffling, so the probability distribution of outcomes is unchanged. Summing along the new arrangement of the state space yields any value one likes, say +100, by the rearrangement theorem. Is invariance under such a re-presentation a requirement, or is it a prejudice? It is the requirement I care about most, since it is the analogue of demanding that a physical quantity not depend on the coordinates. (iii) The Pasadena game held together with its mirror image, the gamble that pays the exact negative in every state: the pair is worth precisely zero whatever happens, so any rule with even a shred of additivity is constrained here. (iv) A sequential version: the agent pays about 0.69 to play, the first toss falls tails, and the same procedure applied to the conditional problem now returns about −0.61, since the conditional expectation is 2(ln 2 − 1). Does the rule mandate that the convention used to sum be held fixed across nodes, and what happens to an agent who is free to change conventions as he goes? Relatedly, can a bookie who merely re-describes an agent's own holding, without changing its distribution, extract money from him by cycling descriptions? (v) A compound of the Pasadena game with an independent St Petersburg gamble, where one pathology is conditional convergence and the other is a genuinely infinite integral. (vi) A tiny probability of an enormous good, where I do not want the rule to work simply by declaring utilities bounded, because the boundedness escape seems to me to purchase determinacy by refusing to say what I most want to say about such cases.

Three further things I would like flagged rather than settled. First, whether the list is itself the result: I suspect that state-relabelling invariance, determinacy of a finite value, weak dominance, and additivity across independent gambles cannot all be had together, and I would like the contradiction located precisely. Second, whether the rule should output a number at all, or only a partial ordering, given that a number invites a price and a price invites a pump. Third, briefly — a paragraph each, no more — how the familiar families fare against the list as you have built it: the response that utility must be bounded so that the case never arises; the response that extends expectation by a privileged summation or principal value; the response that evaluates only differences against a status quo, hoping the difference gamble is integrable; the response that takes dominance as primitive and abandons numerical valuation; and the response that moves to nonstandard or infinitesimal values. I do not need names, dates, or references for any of this.

Finally, tell me where my cut-off analogy helps and where it misleads. In physics the test was empirical: a regularization that predicted different numbers for the same measurement was wrong, full stop. Here there is no measurement to appeal to, so I want to know what plays the role of that constraint — and whether the absence of it means that the choice among these rules is a choice of temperament rather than a discovery.
