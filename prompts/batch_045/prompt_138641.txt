I'm a PhD student in philosophy, and for the past two years I have been the teaching fellow for a first-year seminar cross-listed between philosophy and cognitive science, twenty students, 75 minutes, meeting once a week. Late in the term there is a session on mechanical behaviour and agency: by then the students have met fixed action patterns and vacuum activities in the ethology material, and they have discussed the digger wasp that parks its paralysed prey at the threshold, goes in to inspect the burrow, comes back out — and, if someone nudges the prey a few inches while it is inside, inspects the burrow all over again instead of just getting on with it. What I have never managed is to make them feel the discomfort rather than sort it into a box. They leave able to repeat that rigidity comes in degrees and that humans are more flexible, and nothing has actually happened to them in the room.

The distinction I want them to run into is a three-way one that I have been trying to work out and cannot yet get onto a page as a reading. Call a routine brittle when re-triggering its terminal feedback after it has already completed makes the whole sequence restart from the top — the wasp's case. Call it elastic when, in the same circumstance, the agent re-identifies what it is trying to do and performs only what is still needed. The third category is the one I care about and the one I think breaks the comfortable story: a routine that looks elastic because there is a second routine one level up for the case where the first one has stalled, which itself has a routine above it for diagnosing which fallback to use, and so on upward, where every layer is as mechanical as the bottom one and no layer involves the agent ever reconsidering what it is up to. My worry, which is the thing I want the session to press, is that our confidence in not being waspish rests almost entirely on the observation that our behaviour is elastic, and that observation is exactly what an indefinitely deep stack of brittle fallbacks would also produce. On that picture the free-will question is not whether we could have done otherwise but whether there is any such thing as reconsidering, as opposed to merely running the next routine up, and whether we would know the difference from the inside.

So I need the session designed as an activity rather than a handout case: twenty people doing a structured task in real time, small stakes (I have a participation-credit budget and can bring candy), with the facilitator naming nothing at the start. My instinct is that the brittleness has to be undergone rather than observed — the participants themselves should be the ones who restart, should afterwards be able to report what that was like from the inside (that they knew the step was already done, that they did it anyway, that from where they were standing I chose to and I was cued into it did not feel different), and that the room should be split so that those who got re-triggered and those who did not can compare notes at the debrief instead of taking my word for it. Give me rules and materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, what I do at each stage, how the reveal is staged, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the room onto the three-way distinction — including what to do with the students who will say that flexibility just means having a bigger library of routines, and with the two computer-science majors who will say something about finite state machines and think the matter is closed. I also need three harder things. First, failure modes: the two times I have tried versions of this, one student spotted the setup, refused to play along, and the hour dissolved into an argument about whether I had deceived the class, and I want both a way of handling the student who meta-games the exercise and a way of handling the accusation itself, which I think is fair — I am engineering twenty people into behaving mechanically in order to make a point about them, and I want to keep that while not spoiling it and not leaving anyone feeling used. Second, the debrief has to reach the free-will question without letting it float: if the third category is the crux, what they need to see is that having a routine for every possible failure is precisely the kind of flexibility a machine can have, so that more of it never gets you closer to agency — give me the two or three discussion moves that make that land, and the point at which a good student can legitimately push back. Third, be honest about what the exercise cannot deliver: which part of the problem I will have to supply myself in the closing five minutes because no amount of staging will produce it in the room, and what that costs the argument I am making.
