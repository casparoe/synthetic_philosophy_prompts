I'm writing the take-home final for my fourth-year seminar on consequentialism and infinite value — 12 students, 48 hours, they can use notes and their own problem sets, nominal target 1500 words though I never actually police it. The last third of the course was on what happens to aggregative ethics when the population is infinite, and I want one question worth 25 marks that they can't get through by reciting the menu of fixes (totals go to infinity minus infinity, so: switch to hyperreals, or discount, or use some extended decision rule, the end). They all know that menu. What I want to find out is whether they can see what each fix costs and where the real crux is.

So here's roughly the machinery I want the question built around, and you can rework it however makes for a better question. Stipulate a world that is a bi-infinite chain of people, one person per integer, no earliest and no latest, with cardinal-comparable welfare levels given as integers, so that person n has welfare n in the baseline. Then consider the "shift" world where person n has welfare n+1: everyone is strictly better off than their counterpart, but the multiset of welfare levels is unchanged, since shifting the integers is a permutation of them. That puts anonymity (only the distribution of welfare levels matters, not who gets which) straight against Pareto, and I want them to have to say which one bends and what bending it costs — ideally with some attention to the fact that Pareto itself needs a cross-world matching of persons, and anonymity is usually justified by saying a permutation is a mere relabelling, and those two thoughts pull apart here. One of last year's students objected that the construction only works because welfare is unbounded below; another claimed you can run the same argument with bounded welfare levels using a clever enough permutation. I'd like the question to force them to settle that rather than stipulate it away, since I genuinely don't know who's right. Then bring in a rule one of my old students proposed and that I've been calling the "tethered difference rule": fix a nested sequence of finite sets of people whose union is everyone (a "tether"), look at the difference in total welfare between two outcomes inside each finite set, and rank outcome A above B if that difference is positive from some point on, below if it's negative from some point on, and declare them incomparable if it keeps changing sign. The questions I want pressed: whether this actually escapes paralysis for ordinary local actions like saving a child; whether it violates anonymity; and whether its verdicts depend on the tether — e.g. take a world with welfare 1 on the even-numbered people and 0 on the odds versus the world that swaps them, which are permutations of each other, and show that one tether says the first is better, another says the second is, and a symmetric one returns incomparable. If the rule's verdicts are an artifact of an arbitrary choice of tether, I want them to work out what's left once you demand tether-independence, and my suspicion is that what's left is basically robust dominance, i.e. the cases where nobody needed the rule in the first place, with honest incomparability everywhere else. I want at least one part that makes them construct something (a pair of worlds, two tethers that disagree) rather than discuss, and one part that asks them to sketch why you can't have all of: responsiveness to finite local improvements, independence from how you carve up the infinite population, and something like acyclicity or Pareto — say which leg they'd drop and defend it.

Practical constraints: nothing in the question should require looking anything up. No named theorems, no papers, no citations, no "discuss X's view" — every ingredient has to be in the stipulation itself, since the whole point is whether they can reason from it. Please give me (i) the question in clearly separated parts, sized so a strong student does it in about 90 minutes of the 48 hours, (ii) a model answer of roughly 800-900 words, and (iii) a marking scheme with five must-hit points at 5 marks each, each with a one-line note on the mistake students typically make there. Also tell me explicitly which part is the A/B separator: the A answer should notice something structural — that the apparent determinacy supplied by the tether is fake, or that giving up anonymity isn't a free move because anonymity was doing justificatory work — while the B answer correctly spots the conflict between the principles and then just picks a side and stops there.
