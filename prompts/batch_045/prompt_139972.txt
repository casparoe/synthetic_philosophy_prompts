Putting together a paper on rights and social choice for a small workshop in the spring — I'm a philosopher rather than an economist, so go easy on the notation — and before my colleagues hand it to me I'd like someone to audit my own position, because I have a sinking feeling I'm helping myself to something somewhere. Here's where I've landed. (1) Weak Pareto: if everyone strictly prefers a to b, then a is socially better than b. I hold it for flatly welfarist reasons — social betterness just is something like the satisfaction of people's actual preferences, and I have no independent currency of social value to fall back on. (2) Something like Sen's minimal liberalism, heard as a real side constraint and not a tie-breaker: for each person there's a pair of alternatives differing only in that person's own private conduct over which they are decisive, and the fact that everyone else would rather they went the other way is simply not to the point. (3) The social relation has to be at least acyclic, because I use it downstream in policy evaluation and I don't want to be the guy who shrugs and says cycling is fine when what's on the table is hospital budgets. (4) My escape from the liberal paradox: call a person's preference over a pair meddlesome if they wouldn't hold it if they didn't know how other people's private conduct was going to turn out, and meddlesome preferences can't trigger anyone's right. (5) The methodological comfort blanket: rights are restrictions on the feasible set — game forms, not entries in a social ordering — so the paradox is an artefact of the ordering formalism and doesn't really touch institutions. (6) A case verdict, below.

The case I build the paper around: three flatmates and an offensive novel. Alternatives: z, nobody reads it; x, only Ana reads it; y, only Bo reads it. Ana ranks z > x > y: she'd rather nobody read it, but if one of them has to, she'd rather it be her than Bo, whom she thinks it would corrupt. Bo ranks x > y > z: his favourite outcome is Ana reading it, he'd quite like to read it himself, and nobody reading is worst. The third flatmate, Cy, has no stake in the reading at all — his own private business is whether to keep his guitar in the living room — but he's a thoroughgoing busybody and also ranks x > y > z. My verdict: the right outcome is y, Bo reads and Ana doesn't, the two rights get exercised, and the unanimous preference for x is not a reason to override anything. I also claim that adding a fourth alternative w, both of them read it, changes nothing — Ana and Bo each rank w between x and y — though the best-response stuff in that version honestly makes my head spin. And when people tell me (4) is just the conditional-preference objection wearing a hat, I tell them it's different, though I'm not sure I can say how.

So: go through the list, tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly, ranked by severity rather than all flagged as equal, say for each what would have to give, and tell me which single revision cleans up the most. Two standing anxieties to sort into real versus noise while you're at it: (i) that (1) and (2) are flatly incompatible, that a welfarist can't believe in trumps; (ii) that filtering preferences leaves individuals' relations incomplete, which I half think breaks something downstream but can't locate. Don't be polite about it — I'd rather hear it from you in private than from the commentator in the spring.
