I've just come out of a long argument with my friend Miriam — she does quantitative work on mortality data, I'm a general reader with a taste for the conceptual side — about whether the world is becoming less violent, and neither of us moved an inch. Hers was the per-capita position: raw counts mostly just track how many people there are, so rates are the only honest way to pose the question, and on every rate she trusts the trend is downward. Mine was that rates are the wrong thing to care about if what you actually want to know is how much violence there is: hold the rate fixed and let population multiply and the toll rises, and it seems evasive to describe that as a decline. Then we found two further things we couldn't settle. The first was that a population-weighted global rate can fall while the rate in every region stays flat or rises, simply because the fast-growing regions are the quiet ones — the weights shift, the average drops, and nothing anywhere got safer — which made us wonder whether "the world" is even the right unit to be measuring. The second was what goes into the numerator. She counts deaths caused by violence. I wanted to count harms that never occur as an event at all: a life that ends thirty years early, a harvest withheld by a landlord, a threat that never has to be carried out. Once those are in, it isn't obvious there is a quantity left that has a trend. My suspicion at the end of the evening was that we were never disagreeing about a number; we were asking different questions and each treating our own as the only one there is.

I'm closing the final session of a reading group on this debate next week and I'd like to end on a poem rather than a summary — something that stages the disagreement instead of adjudicating it. Here is the design I have in mind, which I'd like you to execute properly: five stanzas, and in each stanza exactly the same number of violent deaths — four, countable, the same in every stanza — while the frame each stanza is set in grows by orders of magnitude. First a band of a few dozen, then a village, then a city. The absolute number never changes; what changes is how many people it is spread across, so the rate collapses stanza by stanza while the toll does not, and a reader should feel both Miriam's point and mine at once. The fourth stanza should jump to the whole human population and carry the aggregation problem: the global figure falls because the denominator has moved to where the dying isn't, not because anyone has been made safer. The fifth should keep that same vast population and break the device: the four countable deaths should stop being countable, because what is killing people has stopped happening at a moment and become a condition instead, and the poem should end on whether there is a fact of the matter to be had rather than on a verdict.

Give me exactly five stanzas, numbered I through V, with no introduction, no epigraph, and no closing note explaining the trick — the poem has to carry it on its own. Someone who knows the decline-of-violence literature should be able to say, reading it, which denominator is doing the work in each stanza and why the last one cannot be measured; someone who knows nothing about it should still find a good poem rather than a puzzle with a key. Keep the diction concrete and plain — bodies, grain, ledgers, distance, weather — and spare me abstractions like "the arc of history"; and please don't rhyme "peace" with anything.
