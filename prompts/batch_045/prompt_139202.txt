I spent last evening arguing with a friend about whether persistent disagreement is ever evidence of irrationality. Her position was that it always is: two people who both reason well, who respect each other's intelligence, and who have talked at length cannot end up still disagreeing about a matter of fact, and if they do, then either one of them is reasoning badly or the two of them never really shared a common starting point. I thought that was too strong, but I could not locate where it went wrong. This morning she sent me a passage from a bestselling popular book on rational thinking which she says settles the matter. I reproduce it as she sent it:

"Aumann's agreement theorem answers a question that philosophers had argued about for a long time: can two perfectly rational people disagree? The answer is no. Suppose two agents who reason perfectly, who have pooled all of their evidence, and who began with the same prior probabilities. Each announces how confident she is; each then hears the other's number and revises on it. A short argument shows that this process cannot continue indefinitely — it terminates with both agents stating exactly the same number. Persistent disagreement is therefore always a symptom: either someone is reasoning badly, or the two parties never shared a prior to begin with. There is a lesson here for the rest of us as well. Because neither party is entitled to privilege her own judgment, the rational response to finding yourself in disagreement is to move toward the other person's view, and in the simplest case just to split the difference."

She adds a claim of her own that the passage does not make: that the number the agents land on is the probability that the evidence taken as a whole supports — what either of them would say if she could see everything both of them have seen.

I want to know how much of this I should accept. Please go through it claim by claim: what someone who works on this material would endorse, what they would wince at, and what is simply mistaken, including whether my friend's conclusions survive once the passage is corrected. Three things in particular trouble me.

First, the passage says the agents "have pooled all of their evidence." I have a suspicion that this makes the result trivial, and that the interesting version is precisely about agents who have not pooled anything and who each hold private information the other lacks — but I cannot tell whether I am reading the condition correctly.

Second, I tried overnight to build a small counterexample to my friend's extra claim. Take eight equiprobable states of the world, numbered 1 through 8, and an event E that obtains in states 1, 4, 5, and 8. One agent can only distinguish the pairs {1,2}, {3,4}, {5,6}, {7,8}; the other can only distinguish {1,3}, {2,4}, {5,7}, {6,8}. As far as I can tell both agents assign probability 1/2 to E in every state, so they agree immediately and permanently, and the announcements convey nothing; yet at state 1 their information together pins the state down exactly, so the probability supported by the total evidence is 1, not 1/2. Is my example coherent, and if so does it refute what she said? She replies that it is irrelevant because in my example the two never disagree in the first place, and that the theorem only has bite once they do. If that reply is correct, can the example be repaired — by adjusting the priors or the partitions so that their first announcements differ, while the gap between what they converge on and what the pooled evidence supports remains?

Third, she maintains that everything still goes through if the agents can only exchange numbers rounded to the nearest tenth, with "exactly the same" weakened to "within a tenth of each other." Is that right, and if the bound is wrong, what would the correct one be?
