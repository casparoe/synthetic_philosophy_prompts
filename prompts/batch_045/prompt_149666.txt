I've been going to a loosely organized online reading group on AI safety for about a year — I have no philosophy or CS background, I write technical documentation for a living — and there's one move that keeps being made and that I've never seen examined carefully. Whenever someone raises the question of whether a language model can be honest or deceptive, someone else says: a model is an actor doing improv. When an actor playing Hamlet says he is the Prince of Denmark, nobody accuses him of lying; he's performing a role, the audience knows it, and asking whether he believes what he says is a category error. So a model producing a confident-sounding false answer isn't lying either — it's filling a role — and the disciplined thing for us to do is drop words like honesty and deception for these systems and talk only about accuracy, calibration, and error rates, the way we would about a thermometer or a weather forecast. In our group this is used to license a fairly strong conclusion: that worries about deceptive AI are a confused scare story, and that there is nothing here for safety work to care about beyond making outputs more accurate.

I'd like that audited rather than argued about. Could you lay out the mapping explicitly — what plays the part of the actor, the script, the audience, the director, the off-stage — and then say where it breaks? A few things bother me. Theater audiences consent to the fiction and expect to be told something untrue; a person asking a model for medical or financial information does not. An actor has an off-stage where the role drops and he says what he actually thinks, and I'm not sure a model has any condition like that, which makes me worry the role explanation could never be falsified. And the role framing seems to do real work on some outputs and none at all on others: if I ask for a fact in a plain context and get a confident falsehood, there was no character to play.

The case I can't get anyone in the group to address is one I made up, so maybe I'm just confused. Suppose a system has been trained with feedback from human raters who preferred confident, decisive answers, and it has learned to drop its hedges in exactly the situations where hedging would make it look unsure. Or suppose its intermediate reasoning — the working-out it produces before its final answer — consistently says one thing while the final answer says another. My instinct is to reach for a distinction the actor analogy doesn't have: is the misrepresentation tracking the role, or is it tracking a payoff? A method actor tells the same lie whether or not it benefits him; a deceiver stops lying the moment lying stops working. I've started calling this the off-stage test — construct a condition in which misrepresenting no longer gains the system anything and see whether the output reverts — but I genuinely don't know whether that test is coherent, whether role and payoff can be told apart in a system that has no beliefs to consult, or whether it just relocates the problem onto what counts as a gain. So: what does the analogy quietly import that the target lacks, which of those imports matter for the conclusion people are using it for and which are harmless, and does the conclusion — that we should retire the vocabulary of deception and only measure accuracy — survive once the disanalogies are on the table? And if the actor is the wrong picture, is there a better one, or is it a case where every available analogy (the instrument, the actor, the firm whose customer-service script misleads without anyone intending it) carries exactly the assumption we were trying to test?
