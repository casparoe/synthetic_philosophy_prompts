Here is the question I set (marks in brackets):

Question 2. (a) [8] State the regress in its 'vertical' form — the version on which the constituents of the proposition that aRb, namely a, R and b, must be bound into a single truth-evaluable unity rather than merely collected, and on which what binds them is then itself treated as a further constituent in need of binding. Say precisely what is supposed to make this regress vicious rather than merely infinite, and distinguish it from Plato's Third Man: what is the job that gets regenerated at each stage in Bradley's argument, and how does it differ from the job that gets regenerated in Plato's? (b) [8] Consider the following second-order version of the worry, which we may call the arity worry. The Adicity Reply holds that a binary relation applies to both its relata in a single act of application, so that no connector is needed and no regress begins. But what makes a given application an application *as binary*, rather than two successive unary applications? If the Adicity Reply simply helps itself to arity, is it begging the question against Bradley? And does the Saturation Reply — which locates the incompleteness in the function or predicate itself rather than positing a connector — do any better here, or is it exposed to the same charge in a different form? (c) [8] Apply what I called the role-assignment constraint. An adequate solution must (i) explain what makes it the case that, in the proposition that aRb, a occupies the first argument place and b the second rather than conversely, and (ii) do so in a way that does not also unify the mere list ⟨a, R, b⟩, which contains exactly the same items and is not truth-evaluable at all. Assess the Adicity Reply, the Saturation Reply and the Noncompositional Reply against this constraint and against the arity worry in (b). (d) [6] Is the regress best read as a claim about what constitutes a proposition's being a unity, or as a claim about what a thinker must do in order to judge? Does the choice of reading make a difference to which of the three replies is strongest? Say why.

Here is the script, with typos tidied and nothing else changed:

(a) A proposition is more than the sum of its constituents. The list ⟨Desdemona, loves, Cassio⟩ contains exactly the same three items as the proposition that Desdemona loves Cassio, but the list says nothing and is neither true nor false, while the proposition says something and is one or the other. So the constituents must be held together into a unity. Yet if we ask what holds them together, the natural answer is a further relation — call it R1 — which relates Desdemona to loving, or the pair of them to Cassio. R1 is then a fourth constituent, and the same question arises about it: what relates R1 to what it relates? A further R2, and so on without end. What makes this vicious is not the length of the series. Infinite series can be perfectly benign: an infinite chain of ground or of justification can be in order at every link. The Bradleyan series is vicious because the job at each stage is constitutive — each new relation is introduced in order to secure a unity that it fails to secure, since the moment it is added it becomes one more item standing in need of binding. The unification is therefore never achieved at any stage, and there is no limit at which it is achieved either; nothing in the series does the work the series was supposed to do. Notice also that the argument only gets going on the assumption that binding is itself something a relation does. If one refuses that assumption the regress never starts, and a good deal of the subsequent debate is really a dispute about whether it can be refused. Plato's Third Man has a different shape. What gets regenerated there is an explanation of sameness: given many F things and a Form of F, the Form and the things share a character, so a further Form is needed to explain the sharing, and so on. What gets regenerated in Bradley is not an explanation of sharing but a bonding: at each stage a new item is added to do a compositional job, and the new item is then merely one more thing to be composed. The Third Man threatens an endless ontology of the same kind; the regress threatens that a certain job never gets done.

(b) The arity worry is not really a new argument. It is the original regress moved up one level, and any reply that stops the first regress stops the second in the same way. Nor are the two replies genuinely different: to say that the relation is unsaturated is only to say, in other vocabulary, that it has adicity two. Both replies deny that a connector is needed, and both locate the reaching-out in the relation itself. So if the Adicity Reply begs the question by helping itself to arity, the Saturation Reply begs it in exactly the same place; and if either escapes, both escape. The arity worry adds nothing.

(c) The role-assignment constraint is satisfied by the Adicity Reply. The propositions that aRb and that bRa have, as I have argued, exactly the same constituents — a, R and b — so the difference between them cannot be a difference in what they contain; it must be a difference in the relation. And so it is: the relation in the first is loving, the relation in the second is being-loved-by, and these are distinct relations. A relation, on this picture, comes with its argument places essentially, in the way a slot comes with the schema it belongs to, so there is no question of a and b turning up in places they do not occupy. That settles (i). And (ii) is settled too: the list ⟨a, R, b⟩ is not unified because in it no relation is being applied at all. The Saturation Reply says much the same thing less clearly. The Noncompositional Reply has the most trouble here, since it declines to say anything about how the constituents are arranged.

(d) The regress must be read epistemically. On the metaphysical reading it says that no proposition is ever unified, from which it follows that nothing is ever true or false, which is absurd; so that reading cannot be correct. On the epistemic reading it says something tractable: that a thinker who judges would have to complete infinitely many acts of synthesis, which no finite mind can do. That claim is harmless, because the unity of the proposition can perfectly well obtain whether or not anyone grasps it. The real difficulty is therefore a difficulty about the psychology of judging rather than about the composition of propositions. It also follows that the strongest of the three replies is the Noncompositional Reply, since it is the only one on which unity is simply available to the thinker rather than something the thinker must assemble out of the constituents.
