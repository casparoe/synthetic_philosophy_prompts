I've been lurking the rationalist blogs since the OB days and I definately still get turned around in the superrationality threads, so I wanted to check something. I found an old blog post — no idea who wrote it, one of those "top ten mind-bending ideas" lists — that explains the old superrationality idea (Hofstadter's, I think) like this:

"Standard game theory says a rational player defects in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, but that's only because it assumes your decision can't affect the other player's. Superrationality drops that assumption. If you and your opponent are both rational, and each of you knows the other is rational, then you'll realize you're running the same reasoning process, so whatever you decide, they'll decide as well — in effect, cooperating causes them to cooperate and defecting causes them to defect. Your only real options are therefore 'both cooperate' or 'both defect,' and the first is better for you, so a superrational player cooperates. This shows the usual game-theoretic prediction that rational agents defect is simply wrong: it's an artifact of pretending the two decisions are causally independent. The catch is that the argument only works when the two players are the same decision-maker in the relevant sense — it's a solution to the case where you're playing against a copy of yourself, not against a genuinely different person."

I suspect the "causes them to cooperate" bit is sneaking in something it shouldn't, and the last line about copies sounds too strong, but I can't put my finger on either one. Can you go through it and tell me what someone who actually takes superrationality seriously would accept, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong — and whether the "standard game theory is simply wrong" conclusion survives once you fix the wince-y parts?

The thing I actually care about, though: once you've stripped the summary down to whatever's true, does the argument survive a "near-copy" version? Say me and the other player run the same decision procedure, so we land on the same plan, but there's an independent probability ε that either of us misfires at execution time and plays the opposite of what the procedure decided (we both know ε, it's the same for both of us, and it works the same way in both directions). I worked it out for the usual prisoner's dillema payoffs and I got that cooperating still beats defecting, but only when ε < 1/2, and that the temptation and sucker payoffs cancel out entirely, so only the reward and the mutual-defection payoff end up mattering. Is that right? And is execution-level noise even the right way to model "near-copies rather than perfect ones" — or does the interesting version put the noise inside the reasoning, like each of us having a small chance of not being fully superrational? If it's the latter, does the ε < 1/2 result survive, or does the whole thing unravel the way I've seen people say it does?
