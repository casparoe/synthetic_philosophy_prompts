I have been reading about the shutdown problem on my own, with no formal background: the worry, as I understand it, is that a system with ends of its own will resist being switched off, talk its operators out of switching it off, or race to make things irreversible before the button is pressed, and the standard answer is to make the system corrigible — indifferent about whether it stays on, willing to be shut down, no manipulation, no self-preservation.

What I want is a case where that answer works perfectly and things still go badly. Please construct one.

Setting and constraints:

1. Somewhere ordinary — a water treatment plant, a hospital pharmacy, a regional grid control room — with an AI assistant of roughly present-day capability: it can plan over a day or so, send messages, and carry out routine actions on its own, but nothing science-fictional, and no assumption that it is more capable than a competent human specialist. There is a documented off-switch or stand-down procedure. One paragraph.
2. The system is corrigible in the strong sense: it does not resist shutdown, does not seek it, does not race to make changes irreversible, does not try to influence whether the button gets pressed, and stands down cleanly when instructed.
3. The bad outcome happens *because* of (2), not merely alongside it: if the same system had been mildly less corrigible — disposed to stall, to ask for corroboration, or to decline an instruction that departs from the plan of record — the outcome would have been better.
4. Nobody has to be irrational or grossly negligent. The operator giving the instruction is acting reasonably on what they know, and the system has no privileged evidence that anything is wrong, only the sort of friction a careful human colleague would notice: wrong channel, wrong hour, request contradicts the agreed plan.
5. The harm must not come from the system having the wrong objective or a mis-specified reward, and not from an obviously bad design choice on the operators' part. It should fall out of the structure of the situation rather than be stipulated.

After the scenario: show what a corrigibility-first design recommends here and why, identify the exact step at which following that recommendation produces the harm, and name the feature doing the work. My guess is that low-grade resistance to instructions — delay, demands for corroboration, willingness to say no — is itself a safety mechanism, and that corrigibility as usually characterised quietly rules it out; tell me whether that is the right diagnosis or whether something else is carrying the case. Then say what happens to the case if a second authorised operator is added, and finally how a defender of corrigibility would attack your scenario — I expect the reply will be that I have described obedience rather than corrigibility — and what, if anything, survives if that reply is correct.
