I sit on the crisis-standards-of-care committee of a regional hospital system, and we have to recommend one of two ventilator-rationing protocols before the respiratory season begins in six weeks. Both require us to withdraw life-sustaining treatment from patients who would have a real chance of recovery if left on it, so honoring the constraint against killing is not on the table; the choice is only between lesser evils. Protocol A withdraws from two long-stay patients, freeing ventilator-days for eight short-stay patients who would otherwise die; Protocol B withdraws from one such patient, freeing capacity for four. Half the committee says we should minimize deaths, which favors A; half says we should minimize the number of patients we ourselves kill, which favors B, and adds that if our real reason for preferring A is simply that fewer people die, we owe an account of why the body count does not settle things in the ordinary case where killing is avoidable — where we all insist that it does not. Draft the decision memo I will circulate on Friday: recommend one protocol, with an effective date and a trigger for revisiting it, and identify which of the two reasons is doing the work and why that reason does not swallow the constraint against killing altogether. Before you reject the other protocol, give the strongest version of its case, including what we spend in clinician integrity and in patients' willingness to trust us, and say what a bedside clinician who is asked to withdraw from a recovering patient should be told.
