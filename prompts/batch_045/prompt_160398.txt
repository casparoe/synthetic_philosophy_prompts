**Two channels and a cache: what pain asymbolia does and does not show about the badness of pain**

Everyone agrees on the rough phenomenon, so I'll state it quickly. After certain kinds of brain damage, patients will say of a pinprick, a burn, or an inflated pressure cuff that they feel it: they can say where it is, say roughly how strong it is, and sort it into the right category — sharp, burning, throbbing — but that it does not bother them. They may laugh. They do not pull away in the way the rest of us do, or stop what they are doing, or file it away as something to avoid next time. Call this pain asymbolia. I know the clinical detail here is second-hand and probably simplified, and I want that checked; but the argument doesn't turn on the exact presentation, only on the dissociation being real.

The case gets used for two opposite purposes. If you want to argue that the badness of pain is something extra, added to a sensory state that could in principle occur without it, this is your case: the sensation appears to survive while the unpleasantness is gone, so the two must be separable components rather than two descriptions of one and the same state. If you want to argue that badness is intrinsic to the sensation — that a pain just is a state that hurts and there is no having the one without the other — then you have to explain the case away: the patient is not feeling pain minus unpleasantness, they are feeling something thinner plus a preserved capacity to classify a stimulus as the kind that normally hurts. Call these the separatist and the unitarian readings.

My thesis has two halves. The separatists are roughly right about the conclusion. But almost everyone on both sides is far too trusting of the patient's report, and once you see why, the argument changes shape.

Here is the engineering analogy that got me there. In a service you typically have a component that produces a signal and a separate component that consumes it. When the consumer dies, the pipeline does not go quiet immediately: there are buffers, retries, cached dashboards, and health checks that someone configured once and never revisited. For a while, every piece of evidence available to the operator says the signal is being produced and consumed, because the evidence the operator has — the dashboards, the log lines — is generated on the producing side and on the transport, not at the consumer. I want to suggest that the asymbolic patient's report has exactly this structure. The machinery that generates the sentence *yes, that's a pain, it's sharp, it's about a six* is classificatory machinery, and the classificatory machinery is intact. Whether an affective signal is still being generated and consumed elsewhere in the system is not something that report is well placed to tell us, because the report sits downstream of the classifier and not downstream of the consumer. So at least three states are compatible with the report: intact sensation with affect removed; partly degraded sensation with classification intact; and a sensation reduced to whatever the classifier alone produces. The standard reading assumes the first. The report cannot distinguish the first from the third.

This is not skepticism about the case. I think the dissociation is real. It is a claim about where the burden of proof sits: the verbal report alone cannot carry the separatist argument, because the report is itself one more output of a subsystem whose integrity is not in question. You need evidence that does not pass through the patient's own classificatory and linguistic machinery — whether they still protect an injured part, whether the usual physiological accompaniments show up when the stimulus is applied but not when it is merely described, whether they learn to avoid it over repeated trials without being able to say why. I'm not confident any of these is a clean test, and part of what I want to know is which of them are known to be useless.

Suppose the dissociation is real. What then is the badness? My second idea is that it is neither an intrinsic property of a sensory state nor a second phenomenal ingredient mixed into the first, but the output of a comparator: specifically, the size of the gap between the system's current interoceptive state and its predicted trajectory for itself, multiplied by how much weight the system gives that gap when selecting what to do next. Three things follow, and they are the reasons I like the account. Gradedness comes for free, with no need for a separate quantity of unpleasantness. Asymbolia becomes a case of the weight being set to near zero rather than a quale being deleted — which is precisely why the case is compatible with both readings and why the report is unreliable, since a comparator can be running while disconnected from everything downstream. And nothing in the account ties badness to tissue damage or to any particular kind of signal; it is a structural role, so anything that plays it counts.

The third idea is an application, and it is the part I suspect is weakest. Arguments about whether artificial systems could suffer tend to reach for functional criteria: a state counts as suffering if it plays roughly the role pain plays in us — registering damage, motivating avoidance, and so on. Run that criterion against the asymbolic patient and it returns *not suffering*, which strikes me as the wrong verdict if the patient's sensory state is intact. And the criterion has a second face that matters more for the artificial case: a system can have entirely good internal damage telemetry and a working comparator and no connection whatsoever from either to what it does next — which is, not coincidentally, the architecture we actually build — and the functional criterion says that system does not suffer, for the same reason and with the same confidence that it says the asymbolic patient does not. My claim is that the two debates are one debate with the polarity reversed, and that a criterion which speaks confidently in one direction and shrugs in the other is a criterion nobody yet understands. There is a further wrinkle from the telemetry point: you cannot read the absence of the signal off the logs, so the uncertainty here has a moral shape and not just an epistemic one.

One loose thread, offered as a question rather than an assertion. As far as I can tell there is no mirror image of asymbolia — no condition in which aversion and avoidance are intact while sensory discrimination is gone, such that a person is distressed by and flinches from something they cannot locate, judge the intensity of, or categorize. If that asymmetry is real, the separatist picture of two independent components looks too symmetrical: discrimination seems to be the robust channel and aversion the fragile one, and a pure two-independent-channels story does not predict that. If the mirror-image condition does exist, this paragraph goes in the bin.
