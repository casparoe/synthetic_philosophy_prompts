I am preparing a twenty-minute talk for the Organ and Tissue Donation Advisory Committee of the teaching hospital where my son died two years ago, and I would like your help with the forty-minute question period that follows it, rather than with the talk itself.

Some background so you know who is speaking and why: I have no academic or clinical training of any kind. I run a small freight-brokerage business. My second son was nineteen when he had a cardiac arrest and, two days later, was declared dead by the whole-brain criterion; our family was approached about donation within about twenty-four hours of that declaration. Since then I have volunteered with a donor-family support group and read a good deal on my own, mostly general-audience material plus whatever abstracts I could get without a library account. I am not equipped to cite the literature and I do not intend to try. What I am equipped to do is say clearly what I think is wrong with the way we handle this, and I would rather be caught out on a point I can concede than bluff my way through one.

The audience, as the chair described it to me: a transplant surgeon who runs the abdominal program and who I expect is there mostly to protect the donor pool; a neuro-intensivist who regards the whole-brain criterion as settled science and the surrounding debate as a nuisance; a palliative care physician whose concerns are the withdrawal process and the family experience; a nurse manager from the neuro-intensive care unit who has been present at more death determinations than everyone else in the room combined and who, I am told, is the person the committee actually listens to; the hospital chaplain, who is theologically conservative and believes death is not ours to redefine; a lawyer from risk management, whose operative question will be whether the hospital can be sued or prosecuted; and one philosopher who does clinical ethics consultations, who is likely to be the only person there who knows the technical vocabulary and who I suspect is sympathetic to the claim that our criteria are conventions but hostile to the idea of letting patients choose among them.

The thesis I plan to defend has two parts. First: brain death and circulatory death are not two tests for a single underlying event that we are trying to locate more precisely; they are two different conventions serving different purposes, and the honest response to that is to stop insisting on one official moment and instead let a competent adult register in advance which criterion is to govern the determination of their own death, with whole-brain as the default for anyone who has not registered. I have gone back and forth on whether people should also be permitted to register for a stricter standard, requiring that both criteria be satisfied before they are treated as dead, and on what should happen when a registered choice conflicts with what the family at the bedside wants. Second: the requirement that organ donors be dead before procurement does no real protective work, because what actually protects patients is valid prior authorization plus a determination that they have permanently lost the capacity for consciousness. I want to argue that the claim that such patients are already dead is a comfortable fiction, and that it now does more harm than good, since it forces us to stretch the criteria whenever the fiction becomes inconvenient, for instance by quietly substituting permanent for irreversible cessation of circulation in donation after circulatory determination.

My argument in outline: the two criteria already come apart in practice and we paper over the gap; a registration system is just the extension to death determination of the advance-directive logic we already accept elsewhere; and once the authorization and the loss-of-consciousness finding are both genuinely in place, insisting on the additional statement that the patient is dead adds nothing except emotional cover, and that cover is purchased at the price of incoherence.

What I would like from you is a preparation sheet. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get from this particular room, not the objections I would get from a philosophy seminar, ordered by how much damage they would do if I handled them badly. Phrase each one the way that person would phrase it, in their own concerns and vocabulary, and tell me who is likely to raise it. For each: a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, an indication of where that reply is weakest, and a note on whether the right move is to concede the point, partly concede it, or hold firm. Tell me what I should concede at the start of the talk so that it does not look like a retreat later. Flag any place where I would be relying on a term or a distinction that this audience will expect me to be able to define on my feet, and tell me plainly if any part of my argument is something I should cut rather than defend. Finally, give me the one question I am hoping nobody asks, and a first sentence for my answer to it, since I expect to freeze.

One constraint: I cannot cite anything, and I have no reliable way of checking references, so please keep every reply in terms of the reasoning itself and the ordinary clinical facts that anyone in that room would grant me.
