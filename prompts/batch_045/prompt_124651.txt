I sit on the allocation committee of a foundation that gives away roughly forty million dollars a year. Our grants come in units of about half a million, committed over several years, and our due-diligence capacity means we can run at most four programmes at a time; a few of the things we could fund also have thresholds, in the sense that nothing at all is achieved below a certain minimum scale and a great deal is achieved once that scale is reached. So whatever rule we adopt has to tell us what to do with lumpy sums, not with dollars. We score candidate causes by importance, tractability and neglectedness, multiplied together. A colleague on the committee, who is an economist and who will not let this go, argues that once the framework is made precise it eats itself. I have reconstructed his argument as charitably as I can and I cannot find where it cheats.

(1) A funder should allocate so as to maximize the expected good its spending does, and should count only the difference its own money makes: the expected difference between the world in which the grant is made and the world in which it is not.

(2) For a budget fixed in advance, that comes to comparing, across the causes open to us, the expected counterfactual value of the next unit of money in each cause, evaluated at the level at which that cause is currently funded. Call this the point-marginal return.

(3) The three-factor framework is standardly defended as a proxy for exactly that comparison. Importance fixes the size of the prize, that is, how much value there is in the problem's being fully solved. Tractability fixes the slope, that is, how much of the remaining problem an extra dollar buys. Neglectedness fixes the position on the curve, that is, how little of the problem has already been bought up with other people's money. On this reading the framework says nothing beyond what those three features do to the comparison in (2). Call it the proxy reading.

(4) Consider two live options. Programme A is a large, moderately tractable and fairly well-funded global health intervention that we have supported for years. Programme B concerns a genuinely minor problem, one where the total value at stake over a decade is a rounding error beside A's, but almost nobody funds B and it is very cheap to make progress in it, so that by our own estimates the first million dollars in B does somewhere between three and five times as much good as the next million in A.

(5) By (2), our next unit of money belongs in B. And this is not an artefact of the example: take any two causes; either their point-marginal returns are equal or one of them is larger, and if one is larger, (2) sends the next unit there. So (2) directs money, in every case, to whichever cause has the highest point-marginal return, however trivial that cause is.

(6) But no committee could act on that. A foundation that moved its entire health budget into a minor cause on the ground that the first million went further there would deserve the criticism it received, and the criticism would be about the smallness of the problem, not about the marginal million. Importance is therefore a consideration in its own right, and not merely an input to a comparison of per-dollar values.

(7) So the proxy reading is false: the framework's first factor does work that (2) gives it no room to do.

(8) And then the framework has no interpretation left. If importance carries weight independently of per-dollar value, the framework is maximizing two things at once, more good per dollar and a larger share of a bigger problem, and multiplying them produces a number that means nothing while adding them leaves the relative weights arbitrary. Either way the framework collapses: it is either a cost-effectiveness calculation that ignores scale and therefore issues recommendations no one could act on, or it is an uninterpreted score. In neither case do we have a decision rule.

He adds a second limb, which worries me more than the first. Neglectedness, he says, is self-effacing in a way that tractability is not. If a cause's being unfunded is a reason for us to fund it, then it is a reason for every funder, so the causes that score highest on neglectedness are precisely the ones that stop being neglected as soon as anyone acts on the score; and a consideration that cancels itself when acted upon cannot belong in a decision rule. Taken at face value this seems to prove too much, since it would also show that being the only person in a position to help is never a reason, which I do not believe.

I do not want to give up (1), and if forced I would sooner keep the proxy reading in (3) than keep (6); but (6) is the step I am most confident of, which is why I am stuck.

Please do three things. First, name the step or the transition you would reject and state exactly what it assumes. I suspect there is a slide somewhere between comparing a unit of money and comparing a programme, but I cannot state it cleanly, and I want the assumption made explicit rather than gestured at. Second, price the rejection: which of the claims I would prefer to keep has to go, and what do we lose by giving it up. Third, give me the allocation rule you would actually hand a committee that makes half-million-dollar grants, can run four programmes at a time, and faces causes that differ in scale, slope, current funding and lumpiness; say plainly whether importance survives in that rule as anything other than a factor in per-dollar value, and whether neglectedness survives at all in light of the second limb. Where your rule is silent, or where it merely restates the disagreement, say so instead of papering over it.

Finish with a single last line of exactly this form:

Verdict: step N — six words or fewer naming the flaw.
