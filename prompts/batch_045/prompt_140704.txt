I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind embedded in a deep RL lab, and I've committed myself, in a paper I now have to defend, to a specific position: that the right functional candidate for valence in a trained agent is not the reward scalar an engineer wrote down but the sign of the agent's own temporal-difference (advantage) error — the world coming out worse than its learned value function predicted — so that agents in training can occupy states of genuine negative valence, i.e. suffer, and that this is a reason to regulate training regimes and not merely deployments.

Please don't spend the answer on the objections I already know I must answer: that reward-as-pleasure makes a self-stimulating agent maximally happy, that reward is a number someone typed in rather than an attitude of the agent, and the flat claim that there is nobody home. My refinement is meant to handle those, and it is the refined position I want tested: the putatively valenced signal must be (i) learned rather than hand-specified, (ii) recruited for credit assignment across many contexts rather than driving a single control loop, and (iii) integrated with something like a self-model that represents future states as states of one persisting thing.

Give me the exposure map for that position: which observations, arguments, or formal results would count against it, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and — the part I care most about — which of them a defender of a view like mine should concede as counting against it rather than dismiss as a misunderstanding. Three worries I can't shake, and I want an honest estimate of the damage each does. First, degeneracy: many distinct networks, seeds, and architectures (shared versus separate critic, for instance) implement behaviourally equivalent policies at equal return, so if the candidate realizer of valence varies arbitrarily across that equivalence class it looks like implementation detail, and I want to know what my view must say is invariant across the class and what follows if nothing is. Second, gradient absorption: the weights carrying the signal are rewritten continuously, and if suffering requires a subject that persists long enough to have a welfare over time, training may leave only a succession of momentary subjects — is my view committed to suffering that no one undergoes? Third, collapse: if requirement (iii) is doing all the real work and the error-sign story is decorative, my view may have quietly become a self-model or diachronic-concern theory with extra machinery, and I take defeat-by-collapse seriously.

Also: what does my position predict that its rivals don't, that is testable on an ordinary training run — I have in mind lesion-style interventions, where degrading the negative-valence channel should selectively impair avoidance or learning about aversive outcomes while sparing competence, and where an architecture with no such channel should show no stress-like signature such as entropy collapse or disrupted representations — and what result would I be obliged to treat as disconfirming? Say plainly whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so whether that is fatal, given that it was supposed to have practical bite. For what it's worth, the thing that would move me personally is over-generation: if the criterion, applied without special pleading, attributes suffering to something I am certain is not a subject — a PID controller with a signed error term, say — I will abandon it.
