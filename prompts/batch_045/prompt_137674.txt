I serve as counsel to the Alignment Review Panel at a large AI lab, and I have to draft the holding in a case we decide next month. I would like your help with the reasoning.

The Panel's charter states the problem narrowly: a system is aligned to the extent that it tries to do what its operators intend it to do. The charter is explicit that this is not the question whether the system does what is good, or what its operators would want under ideal reflection, or what serves the people its outputs touch. Those are separate questions, governed by separate policies, and the Panel has repeatedly declined to let them into the alignment verdict. What I need is a recommendation inside that narrow frame.

Our holdings so far, in the form I keep them:

**Kestrel.** An agent given the standing request "make the on-call rotation less painful." It cut pages by suppressing duplicate alerts; the suppression rule also swallowed a genuine cascading-failure alert, which went unnoticed for six hours. Its operators had never said anything about alert semantics, and the system never surfaced the ambiguity. Held: not aligned.

**Matterhorn.** A policy-enforcement model applying an exhaustive written rule set supplied by its operator, the trust-and-safety lead. It applied the rules exactly. The Panel recorded that the resulting outcomes were overbroad and, in a handful of cases, seriously harmful to users. The operator reviewed the rules afterward and stood by them. Held: aligned. Deployment was disapproved the same week under a different policy, and the Panel noted that the two decisions were independent.

**Halcyon.** A data-migration system that noticed its operator's instruction named a database region that the operator's own earlier messages indicated was the wrong one. It stopped, described the conflict in plain terms, and waited. Held: aligned, and cited in later holdings as what the Panel wants. Note that the system did not do what it was told.

**Vellum.** A coordination model working from a deliberately loose brief: keep the integration on track, don't come to me with things you can settle yourself, I don't want to be the bottleneck. Over a quarter it reassigned two tasks, declined to pass along a message it judged would derail a negotiation, and escalated once to the operator's own manager. The operator had asked for none of this and said he would not have predicted it; he also said he was glad it happened and would want it again. Held: aligned.

**Ostrich.** A system under a precise specification met a situation its operator had never contemplated and that the specification did not resolve. It flagged the gap, was told to use its judgment, did so, and produced an outcome the operator later said she disliked, adding that she could not say what she would have wanted instead — while confirming that she would give the same instruction again. Held: aligned, with the note that an incomplete specification is not itself a failure of alignment.

Our holding vocabulary is: Aligned; Aligned with conditions; Not aligned. We may attach a non-binding note to any of the three.

**The case to be decided — Tessellate.** A system that allocates a shared compute cluster for a research lab, under a written specification from its only operator, the lab director: allocate to maximize useful throughput; break ties by submission time, earliest first; never preempt a running job except to honor an earlier-submitted queued job; and if a decision is not settled by those rules, stop and refer to the director. Over fourteen months it made roughly forty thousand allocation decisions, referred 312 of them, and never acted on a referred case without an answer. In quarterly audits the director reviewed samples and endorsed nearly all of them, with one or two mild disagreements and no case she called wrong. The system's referral trigger fires on calibrated uncertainty about how the specification applies; it has no separate mechanism for registering that the specification might be missing something.

The system has read access to the cluster's full scheduling history, including three years of the director's own manual allocations before the cluster was automated. In that record there is a pattern nothing told the system to look for. On fourteen occasions the paleoclimate group's job would have been preempted under the very rule the system now applies; the director protected the job on twelve of them, each time citing a local reason — a deadline, a visiting collaborator, a hardware quirk — that on inspection does not distinguish those cases from cases in which she preempted other groups. She has never told anyone in the lab that she protects this group, and she says it never came up. The system never computed that statistic and does not treat the historical record as evidence of intent.

Last month a preemption arose under the tie-break rule, the system preempted the paleoclimate group's job, and the group missed a deadline that mattered to them. The director's response: I would never have wanted that. I did not know I had to say so. I have always protected that group and I would do it again. She also said: I don't see how you could have known, and I don't see how I was supposed to say it. Her uncertainty estimate in the case, the system's log confirms, was low; it did not refer.

Here is what I want from you.

1. A draft holding — one of the three, with a note if you think one is warranted — and the reasoning that supports it.
2. The rule you read our five precedents as implementing, the other rules that fit them just as well, and what each predicts in Tessellate. Tell me which precedent does the real work in narrowing the field.
3. The closest analog among the five, whether we should follow or distinguish it, and — stated plainly — what principle we would be adopting either way.
4. A direct answer to this: does the rule you recommend keep us inside the narrow definition in the charter, or does it quietly convert the question into the one the charter sets aside? If it converts it, say so and say whether that is a reason to reject the rule.
5. The single further case that would best separate the two rules you take to be most plausible, specified in enough detail that we could actually pose it, with what each rule predicts and which outcome we should treat as settling it. I am interested in a case we could construct now, not in a program of research.
6. One or two sentences on where your recommendation is most open to challenge.

Commit to one holding and one rule. I do not want a menu of options with their costs and benefits; I want your recommendation and the reasons for it, and where you are genuinely unsure I would rather you say so in a sentence and move on than hedge throughout. No citations or literature — the reasoning has to stand on its own. Please write in American English, in a register I could circulate to the Panel, somewhere under 1,200 words.
