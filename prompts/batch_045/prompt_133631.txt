Two people in my reading group got kind of stuck on Hempel's raven paradox and I said Id ask someone who knows more about this than me.

Dan: It's a reductio of the whole idea that confirmation is a logical relation. My white sneaker obviously tells me nothing about ravens, so something in the setup is broken.

Priya: Nothing's broken. Seeing a non-black non-raven confirms all ravens are black by an absurdly small amount — it knocks one potential counterexample out of an enormous pool. The intuition that it tells you nothing just conflates confirms with confirms enough to care about.

Dan: Then any observation confirms anything. I can stare at the ceiling, describe what I see as a non-black non-raven, and collect my nudge. The content of the observation does no work at all.

Priya: Not anything — a white raven would have killed the hypothesis outright. Only some observations can do that, and the asymmetry is real.

Dan: Fine, but here's what actually gets me. The nudge is supposed to be tiny because non-black objects vastly outnumber ravens. That's a fact about the furniture of our universe, not logic. In a sealed warehouse where non-black objects are rare — say a hundred of them, among millions of ravens — inspecting one of those hundred is the strongest move on the board and inspecting another black raven is nearly worthless. So the standard answer doesn't explain why ornithologists should look at ravens; it smuggles our demographics in and calls it a solution. Which means either look at the ravens is only accidentally right, or the degrees story isn't the real answer.

Priya: That's the view working, not failing — of course the background assumptions set the size of the update. The real moral is different: raven and black are projectible predicates and non-black and non-raven aren't, so the rule that instances confirm applies to one formulation and not the other, and the shoe never gets in the door.

Dan: But that just drops the equivalence condition, which was the premise of the paradox. And now you owe an account of which predicates are projectible, which is its own nightmare.

Can you go through this move by move and tell me who's right about what, and what both of them are missing? I genuinely cant tell.
