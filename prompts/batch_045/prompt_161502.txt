I am the staff analyst and minute-writer for the allocation committee of a statutory compensation scheme set up for several thousand people who were implanted, over roughly fifteen years, with a medical device that has since been found to fail at a much higher rate than was represented to them. The fund is capped by statute at a sum that everyone accepts is far short of total assessed harm, so every allocation decision is a decision about who goes short. We meet quarterly, we have six members (a retired judge, two clinicians, a health economist, a lawyer, and a claimant representative), none of them with more than a first course in philosophy, and we have about half a day per meeting. Our determinations are binding on the scheme administrator, must be published with written reasons, and must survive ministerial sign-off and the possibility of judicial review, which means we are constantly under pressure to distinguish two things we currently run together: having a normative reason for an allocation, and having a justification that will be accepted as legitimate. The clause in our establishing instrument that we keep coming back to instructs us to allocate the fund "as fully informed and impartially situated deliberators would agree to allocate it." The instrument nowhere says whether that is meant as a way of discovering what we already have reason to do, or as a way of constructing what we should do where no such fact is available, and it nowhere says which deliberators, informed about what, impartial with respect to what.

An old philosophy tutor of mine, whom I consulted informally, made the difficulty feel much sharper than our clerk's notes do, and I want the procedure to take her worries seriously rather than paper over them. First, she asked what I take to be the shmagency-style worry: suppose we could establish with certainty that fully informed, fully coherent, fully impartial versions of ourselves would converge on allocation X — why should that fact move us at all, unless it is independently true that X is what we have reason to do? If the idealized-deliberation fact is supposed to be what makes X right, we can always restate it in a way that drops the normative load ("there are shmallocation facts about what idealized deliberators would choose, and separately there is the question of what we have reason to do"), and then nothing has been settled. Second, she pointed out that "idealized deliberator" is not one specification but a family: you can add information, add coherence, add impartiality, add empathy, add a veil about the deliberator's own risk level or their own conception of a good life, and these pull in different directions, and the output seems to be a function of which contingencies you strip away and which you retain — which makes the whole exercise look less like a discovery of something and more like a choice we launder through a hypothetical. Her own suggestion, which I found attractive but cannot make precise, was to treat idealized deliberation the way we would treat an expert or a measuring instrument rather than a source: find out where its verdicts track the judgments we are already confident about, calibrate it there, and trust it only in the region where it has been calibrated, while treating disagreement between equally reasonable specifications the way we would treat disagreement between epistemic peers. That last move is the one I most want operationalized, because in practice we cannot compute what an idealized deliberator would choose; we can only argue about it for an afternoon.

So what I need is a procedure my committee can actually run, in half a day, on the material in a case file, in the order I should put the questions. For each question, tell me what would count as a sufficient answer given the evidence we can realistically assemble, where the procedure terminates with a no, and where it must hand the decision back to a person because the issue is one of values rather than fact — and, crucially, tell us how to mark that handoff in our published reasons so that we are not passing off a political judgment as a normative finding. Say explicitly what the committee is to do when a required input cannot be estimated, as when we have no defensible estimate of how risk-averse the claimant pool is or no way to model what a fully informed deliberator would make of a claimant's own contributing behaviour. Then run the procedure on three live proposals, in the order it would consider them: first, a flat payment of equal size to every eligible claimant as against a severity-scaled schedule that tops out far higher but leaves most claimants with a token sum, given that we cannot afford both; second, whether to make any payment at all to the group whose devices have not failed but who face elevated lifetime risk and real monitoring costs; and third, whether to reduce awards where the claimant's own smoking or an untreated comorbidity materially contributed to the harm. On the first of these I should say that the committee is strongly drawn to the flat payment, for reasons of dignity and solidarity, and I would rather you told me plainly if the procedure undercuts that preference than that you adjusted the procedure to preserve it. Finally, tell me what this procedure cannot do: which questions it will systematically get wrong, where it will give us false confidence, and which of the worries I have described survive even a well-run version of it. Please write your reply as continuous prose, in the manner of a long letter to me: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables, even where a list or a table would seem the natural way to set out the steps.
