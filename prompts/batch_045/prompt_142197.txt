I'm on the programme committee of a small academic society — one conference a year plus the proceedings. No written policy, no staff, seven of us, and we argue things out over email. There's a case in front of us now and I keep going in circles, so: what should we do if we want to be consistent with ourselves, and what rule have we actually been running this whole time without noticing?

Our track record, as near as I can reconstruct it:

- Two years ago we pulled a keynote about a month before the conference, after people found blog posts he'd written arguing that some ethnic groups are just less intelligent. Nothing to do with his talk, which was on memory consolidation. We told him he was off the program. We never published anything, never gave a reason in writing, never said for how long.
- Same year we kept a paper on the program that a lot of members said was dangerous — it argued for a partly genetic explanation of a group difference in school outcomes. We added a respondent and ran a critical commentary beside it in the proceedings. Our line was that it was an argument made with data, inside the normal norms, and that it isn't our job to police which conclusions people argue for.
- Last year we withdrew a paper and barred the author from submitting for three years after we found he'd fabricated data. That one we did call a sanction, we did put a term on it, and we did put a note in the proceedings saying what happened.
- We suspended a member for a year over a long run of humiliating emails to a junior member. Completely outside conference business, from her personal account, about a personal dispute. We said it didn't matter that it was off-platform.
- We did nothing when someone complained about a member who'd done prison time for fraud about twelve years ago and whose talk was on something unrelated. We said he'd answered for it already and it had nothing to do with his role with us.
- We did nothing about a member who publicly called one of our own decisions cowardly and named the people who'd complained. We said public criticism of the committee is protected even when it's rude.
- We did nothing about a member who was filmed at a protest chanting something fairly violent, unconnected to our field. We said it was outside our remit — which, as one of our members pointed out at the time, sits oddly with the first and fourth items.

Now: the psychologist from the first item has submitted a paper for this year's proceedings. It's a straight memory paper, co-authored with his PhD student who did the work and needs the publication. Reviewers say it's fine. Separately, his department has put him forward for our service award, which is mostly honorific but comes with a named lecture. He's fairly senior and well known.

And we've since worked out that after we dropped him, at least two other societies quietly stopped inviting him and a job talk was withdrawn. As far as we can tell nobody in any of those places looked into anything — they just saw he wasn't on our program and drew their own conclusions. He doesn't know we know.

So: do we take the paper, and do we give him the award? Which of our own decisions is the closest match, and what are we committing ourselves to either way? Because it feels like whatever we decide, we're also deciding something about what we did two years ago, which at the time felt like us just choosing who's on the program and now looks, to everyone outside, like a verdict. Does the fact that other people copied us change anything we owe him, or is that just other people's business? And is there some decision we could take now that would force us to stop dodging this next time?

I care less about which way we go than about which rule we'd be running and what else it commits us to. Please don't just tell me to write a policy or take legal advice — we know, and we have to answer by Friday.
