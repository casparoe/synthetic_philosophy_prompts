so my friend Sam and I had another one of our marathon arguments about moral relativism last night — four hours, two bottles of wine, zero movement — and I've come away less annoyed than usual and more puzzled. I don't want a verdict on relativism here. I've heard the standard moves on both sides and they're not what I'm after. I want to know what kind of disagreement we're actually having, because it doesn't behave like a normal one.

Setup, so this isn't hand-wavy. Sam is a truth-relativist of the fairly standard kind: a moral claim is true or false only relative to some framework, and there's no framework-independent fact about which framework to adopt. I'm some kind of shy objectivist: at least some moral claims are just wrong, whoever says them.

The annoying part is how much we agree on. We agree there's a lot of cross-cultural variation in moral judgment and that some of it survives full information — you can hand both parties every relevant non-moral fact and they still don't converge. We agree the crude version is dead: nobody thinks "X is wrong" just means "my crowd disapproves of X." We agree that basically nobody gets argued out of either position by a philosopher. We even agree about which arguments are the best ones on each side. His: you'd hold the opposite view if you'd been raised there, and there's no convergence over time the way there is in science. Mine: the first point applies just as hard to your belief in induction or your memory, and relativism entails that every reformer in history was morally wrong by definition.

Two things make me think this isn't a normal factual dispute. First, Sam conceded the tolerance point instantly — yeah yeah, relativism doesn't give you tolerance, if a society's framework is permissive about crushing dissent then that's right *for them* — and then just kept the view. So he isn't holding it for its practical upshots, and neither am I. Second, we each have a debunking move available and they're structurally identical: he says my confidence is explained by where I was raised rather than by reasons, I say his relativism is explained by something equally unflattering about his intellectual milieu. It's not that one side has an argument the other lacks.

And here's the bit I keep chewing on. We invented a case that made everything worse. Two communities, A and B, disagree about some practice. A's framework contains a bridge principle: when outsiders who seem reasonable and well-informed disagree with us, their verdict counts as much as ours and we should revise. B's framework contains the opposite — outsiders' verdicts count for nothing. On Sam's picture both communities look equally right, since morality is relative to framework and no framework is privileged. But they aren't symmetric: by A's own lights A should end up treating B's verdict as binding, while by B's lights nothing changes at all. Sam's answer was that A's framework is just internally unstable and would collapse into something else. My reply was that if that's right, then what his view says about a case depends on facts about the *content* of the framework, and now he owes me an account of which frameworks are admissible ones as opposed to self-undermining ones — which is starting to sound like the objectivism he says he's rejected. He didn't have a reply and we went to bed. A variant that also bugged me: community C's framework explicitly claims universal scope, i.e. it says our rules bind everybody. If relativism says C is right about that, relativism seems to have manufactured an objectivist truth; if it says C is only right that they bind everybody *relative to C*, that feels like a maneuver rather than an answer, though I can't say why.

My working hypothesis, offered as one: we're asking different questions under one name. Sam is asking a first-person deliberative question — what should I do, given that I find myself with these commitments and there's no neutral court of appeal — and I'm asking a semantic-cum-metaphysical one about whether there's a fact of the matter my sentence is tracking. Each of us hears the other's answer as a bad answer to our own question, which would explain why every exchange feels like irrelevance rather than error. But I'm not confident, because the A/B case seems to generate a genuine yes-or-no question that both of us feel pressure about, and that shouldn't happen if we were just talking past each other.

So: is the different-questions diagnosis right, or is there a real substantive disagreement buried in there — about what moral terms mean, about what fixes their content or reference, about whether moral judgments are beliefs or something more desire-like, about which cases count as data, about what a framework even has to be? Engage what each side is actually committed to rather than surveying positions. And then, given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result, a change of question, a case that discriminates between our two readings — or tell me honestly that no progress should be expected here, and what it is about the question that makes that the right answer.
