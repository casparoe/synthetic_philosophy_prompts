I've been trying to work out a view where sentience, and moral status along with it, is a matter of degree rather than a switch, and I've basically been going case by case on gut and now want to know whether my guts add up to anything coherent. Here's where I land:

- A thermostat: nothing. Zero, not even a whisper.
- An adult human, awake: obviously a great deal.
- That same human in dreamless sleep, or under a general anaesthetic: I want to say basically the same status, even though nothing much seems to be going on at the time.
- A person who's brain-dead with their body kept going on a ventilator: almost nothing, plus some leftover residue from how other people feel about them.
- A housefly: small but definitely not zero. I feel bad swatting one for no reason and I don't think that's pure sentimentality.
- A chicken: a lot. Way above the fly, somwhere in the neighbourhood of a dog.
- A whole bee colony, which solves problems no individual bee can and persists for months: no extra subject as far as I'm concerned. I don't think the colony is anyone.
- A patient who can locate a pinprick exactly and describe it perfectly but says it doesn't bother them at all: their status hasn't dropped, but I want to say the badness of the pinprick has gone down enormously, so it looks like sentience and welfare can come apart.
- A working whole-brain emulation of a human running on a server: the same as the original.
- The same emulation run at 1000x speed: I don't want to say it's 1000x more important, but I also don't want to say the extra centuries of experience it packs into a working day count for nothing. This is the one I keep flip-flopping on.
- A current large language model: I genuinely don't know. I lean towards nothing or nearly nothing, but I can't say why without saying something that would also kill the fly, whose brain is a fraction of a gram.

The principle I've been toying with, which I made up and which is probably wrong, is what I call temporal thickness: what matters isn't how much information gets integrated at an instant but how long a stretch of its own activity a system unifies into one state that it then acts on — whether it has a "now" thick enough for anything to matter to it across time. Plus a second clause I had to bolt on, that the thick state has to be available to some single locus rather than smeared across semi-independent parts. Which is awkward, because on that criterion a bee colony looks like it should score pretty well: it persists, and what it does today depends on states laid down days ago. My own principle seems to give me a verdict I reject.

So: is there one graded principle that gets all of these, or close to all of them? State it precisely, tell me which case motivates each clause, and be honest about which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and which you'd overturn. I suspect my fly verdict and my language model verdict can't both survive, and I suspect the sleeping human forces some split between current sentience and moral status that I don't know how to phrase. Then tell me what your principle says about cases I haven't listed — an octopus, a foetus at about 24 weeks, and a lump of cultured human cortex wired up to a reward signal would be the three I most want. Please write the answer in British English.
