So here's a view I've slid into over the last year and I can't tell anymore whether its deep or just slippery: truthlikeness isn't a property of theories at all, it's a property of theory-question pairs. The verisimilitude of T = how well T does at answering questions drawn from some specified distribution over some specified domain. No such thing as closer-to-the-truth simpliciter, only closer-to-the-truth-about-this, for a given bunch of thises. I like it because it sidesteps the impossibility results from the 1970s (those only bite if you define closeness structurally, over a theory's true and false consequences), and because it lets me say Newtonian mechanics was more truthlike than Aristotelian mechanics without committing myself to any story about what the world is really like down at the bottom.

What I can't shake: this might just be reliabilism in a fake beard. If truthlikeness collapses into expected accuracy on the questions we happen to care about, it collapses into usefulness, and the whole point of the notion — that science aims at truth and not merely at what works — has been quietly thrown out the window.

Tell me what would count against it. Specifically: is there a case where our comparative judgments (T2 really is closer to the truth than T1) come apart from every plausible question-distribution in a way I couldn't patch by reweighting? Does having to stipulate a distribution make the view circular, or empty, or both? Is there a version of the collapse objection a defender can't just absorb by biting the bullet and saying yes, verisimilitude just is domain-relative reliability and the aim-at-truth slogan was always confused? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely embarrassing, and separate the ones a committed defender should concede are real hits from the ones they'd rightly shrug off. Also say whether I've insulated the thing into unfalsifiability by making the question-distribution a free parameter, and if so whether that's a bug for a view like this. What would move me most is a historical episode where the view gets the direction wrong — where it says the later theory was no closer to the truth, or less close, and that verdict is intolerable.
