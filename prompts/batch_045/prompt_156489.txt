I was reading a chapter in one of those pop books about collective decision-making (airport purchase) and it explains the discursive dilemma roughly like this:

A three-judge court has to decide a breach-of-contract case. There are three propositions on the table — P (the defendant did the action), Q (there was a contractual obligation not to do it), and C (the defendant is liable) — and every judge accepts that C holds if and only if P and Q both hold. Judge 1 accepts all three. Judge 2 accepts P but rejects Q, and so rejects C. Judge 3 accepts Q but rejects P, and so rejects C. So a majority accepts P and a majority accepts Q, but a majority rejects C: the court's judgment set is inconsistent even though each judge individually is perfectly consistent. The book says this is just the old doctrinal paradox under a fancier name, that the lesson is that majority voting itself is defective, and that List and Pettit then generalised it: the problem isn't specific to majority rule, because no aggregation method satisfying a few obvious conditions — everyone counts equally, each proposition gets decided on its own merits independently of the others, and the group's output has to be logically consistent — can avoid it. So the only remaining option, apparently, is dictatorship. Then it extrapolates: the same holds for any body that pools separate specialist judgments, so a central-bank committee, a hospital ethics committee, or an AI system in which several fine-tuned sub-models vote on the final answer are all provably going to contradict themselves.
