I'm writing a feature on collective intentionality for a magazine that covers science and ideas, and I want to hang it on a single historical case: the unofficial Christmas truces on the Western Front in December 1914. Before I build a thousand words around it, I need to know whether the case actually does theoretical work or whether it is just atmospheric. So I want the analysis I would need in order to commit to it.

Take the facts as given — I have done the reading and I do not want a historiography seminar. In the last week of December 1914, in a patchwork of sectors along the Western Front, small units facing each other stopped firing. Men left their trenches, met in no man's land, exchanged tobacco and buttons and rum, sang, and in some places arranged short local ceasefires to bury the dead lying between the lines. Nothing was authorized from above; in some sectors officers on both sides tried to stop it while it was happening. The truces were local and patchy — dense in some regiments, entirely absent from others — they lapsed within days, and they were never repeated at scale. Afterwards both high commands issued standing orders against fraternization. Grant all of that and move on.

Now the theory. I want three accounts kept apart, because writing about this subject usually runs them together:

One, the reductive shared-intention view in the style of Bratman: mutual responsiveness in the pursuit of a shared end, commitment to the joint activity, commitment to mutual support — all built out of individual we-intentions plus common knowledge, with no irreducible group mind.

Two, the joint-commitment or plural-subject view in the style of Gilbert: the truce creates a normative bond that neither side can unilaterally dissolve, and obligations that neither participant has simply as an individual.

Three, a deflationary view — call it the convergent-equilibrium view — on which there is no shared intention at all, only each side's individual intention not to fire, made rational and stable by common knowledge that the other side has formed the matching intention.

What I want from you is three things.

First, a disciplined mapping. Who or what is the agent — the two facing platoons taken together, the regiments, the pair of armies? What were the options available to each party at each stage of the episode, and what counted as uptake of the other's move: a shout, a raised hand, a lapse in firing that might have been coincidence rather than signal? Then say which features each account explains and which it misdescribes: the unnegotiated onset; the gift exchange and the joint burials, which look like joint action proper rather than parallel inaction; the patchiness across sectors; the abruptness of the ending.

Second, three complications I want handled rather than gestured at. (a) Avowability: the truce was forbidden by the army whose members these men were, so the local we could not be declared to the superordinate collective without dissolving it. Does any of the three accounts require that a shared intention be publicly avowable, and does a covert plural subject cause trouble for that requirement? (b) Membership turnover: units were regularly rotated out of the forward line and replaced. What happens to a shared intention when some but not all of its participants are replaced — does the joint commitment survive partial substitution, and which account gives the cleanest answer? (c) Nested agents: the platoon-level we was in open conflict with the army-level we, which had its own shared intention to take and hold ground and not to fraternize. Does joint-action theory have any resource for saying which we has normative priority, or is that a question it cannot so much as formulate?

Third, the return direction — what the episode shows about the theory. Does it favor the reductive view, the plural-subject view, or the deflationary one? In particular: if the whole sequence can be modeled as individual intentions plus common knowledge, so that the episode looks like a genealogy of a we-intention out of non-we parts, is that a debunking argument against treating collective intentionality as primitive, or is it neutral because the debunking story still has to use the we-form to describe what emerged? And be honest about where all three theories go silent. I would much rather print one sentence saying the theory does not cover this than a paragraph that forces it to speak. If your conclusion tells against one of the three views, finish by giving me the strongest thing that view's defender says in reply — I would rather anticipate it than be corrected on the letters page.

As long as it needs to be, but no throat-clearing.
