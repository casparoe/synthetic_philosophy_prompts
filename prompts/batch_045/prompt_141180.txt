ok so I'm in a groupchat with people from a forecasting competition I did last summer, and one guy in there keeps pushing this idea that government should stop voting on policies and instead run prediction markets on them — like you'd have a market on "if the city builds the bike lane, average commute time in five years will be X" and then automatically do whatever the market says. He brought it up again because our city council is fighting over the budget.

Any time someone pushes back he has this one line: "vote on values, bet on beliefs." He used it on me when I said it just sounds like handing decisions to whoever has the most money and the loudest opinions. He used it on my friend who said most of what actually matters — like whether a neighborhood feels safe, or whether the city is a nice place to live — cant be turned into a number you'd bet on, so the market would just ignore it. And he used it on another person who pointed out that once the bets are what decides the policy, people can bet in a way that makes their own bet pay off, so your not really predicting the outcome anymore, your steering it.

I cant tell what that sentence is actually supposed to mean and it feels like it's doing three different jobs at once. Can you lay out the different precise things it could be claiming, like a menu from the most boring reading to the most radical one, and for each one say whether it's basically a tautology, or a real substantive claim, or actually kind of false? And then the part I really want: which of those readings would actually answer which of the three objections? I have a feeling it only really handles one of them and he's getting away with the other two, but I want to know which is which.

Also, in case it matters: my friend's fix was that instead of betting on a number like commute time, you'd bet on what a randomly picked panel of residents would say about how things turned out a few years later. He says that's still just betting on beliefs. I dont think it is but I cant explain why.
