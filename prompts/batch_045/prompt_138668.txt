I'm in the fourth year of a PhD in philosophy, and in three weeks I'm presenting a thesis chapter at my department's work-in-progress seminar: forty minutes of talk, then about thirty-five minutes of questions. The chapter defends something I call *gapped luminosity*, and I'd like you to build me a Q&A prep sheet for it. A condition C is luminous, in the sense I use, when in every case in which C obtains the subject is in a position to know that C obtains. Williamson's anti-luminosity argument runs a subject through a long series of cases — a slowly warming room, a feeling that fades by imperceptible degrees — notes that in some case in the series the subject is in a position to know that C obtains, and then uses a margin-for-error principle to force a case in which the subject is in a position to know that C obtains although it does not. My claim is that the construction needs the series to be fine-grained: for every case in which C obtains there must be a neighbouring case, differing from it arbitrarily little, in which C fails to obtain. That premise does no work for *gapped* conditions, where C's obtaining is realized by a magnitude that must jump by at least some fixed amount whenever it crosses from a case where C obtains to a case where C fails — the reading on a digital thermometer, whether a neuron has fired, whether a latch is closed. For such conditions, I argue, the gap does by stipulation what the margin-for-error premise was meant to do, the series cannot be built, and the condition is luminous; so the received slogan that nothing is luminous should be restricted to analog conditions. The last two sections cash out the upshot: I revive a weakened KK principle — if one knows that p, one is in a position to know that one knows that p — for knowledge whose grounds are gapped in this way, and I argue that this weaker principle, rather than the full KK principle, was all that the debates over the knowledge norm of assertion and over knowledge as evidence ever actually needed.

The room will be about fourteen people. My supervisor Dana works in the knowledge-first tradition and holds that luminosity was never the load-bearing thesis, that nothing important about knowledge depends on it either way, and that my gapped conditions are simply the trivially luminous cases the anti-luminosity argument was never aimed at. Marcus, who has published on safety and margin-for-error principles, worries me most: I expect him to re-run the argument one level up, against the condition that the instrument is reliable or that I know how large the gap is, and to ask what stops the original construction from applying there. Priya, our formal epistemologist, will want a model — an accessibility or indistinguishability relation over cases, plus a definition of *being in a position to know* — and will ask whether my result is anything more than the observation that a condition is luminous exactly when it is a union of equivalence classes of that relation, which would make the talk of gaps and thresholds mere decoration. Tom, who works on measurement and perception, will press the empirical side: real instruments drift, have hysteresis and noise, and the conditions that anti-luminosity arguments are really about — feeling cold, being in pain — don't come with clean thresholds, so my class of gapped conditions may have no interesting members. The rest are graduate students and two or three agnostics who will mostly take their lead from Dana.

I have limited time in the question period and I'd rather spend it on the objections that can actually damage the chapter than on the ones I can bat away, so I need the prep sheet to be selective and ordered.

Please structure your answer as follows:

1. **Audience read** — about 150 words: who in that room is the real threat to the chapter, who is a threat only in appearance, and where the centre of gravity in the discussion will end up.
2. **Objections, in the order I should expect them** — about 900 words total: eight or so, each headed with the person most likely to raise it, the objection phrased the way that person would actually phrase it (in their vocabulary, with their commitments showing), a reply I could deliver in roughly ninety seconds, and a single line on where that reply is softest.
3. **What to concede before the first question** — about 120 words: the two or three things I should give away in the talk itself so that they don't get spent as objections.
4. **The claim I have to hold** — about 80 words: the one proposition such that, if I concede it, the chapter has no thesis left, stated in the shortest honest form I can get away with.
5. **The question I hope nobody asks** — about 150 words: the objection that would do the most damage, why it would hurt more than the others, and the best two-minute reply I could give even knowing it isn't fully satisfying.
