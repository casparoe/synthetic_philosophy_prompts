Me and two colleagues just landed a small three-year grant to run a working group on something we've been loosely calling the epistemology of heuristic arguments, by which we mostly mean the presumption of independence: when you're reasoning about a system you can't fully model and you treat some pile of events as independent because you don't know of any mechanism linking them, what exactly are you entitled to conclude, and when does that move quietly turn into self-deception? The composition of the group is me (formal epistemology / phil math), an analytic number theorist who is sympathetic but will only come to every other meeting, and someone from a risk-analysis shop who mostly cares about tail estimates for things like simultaneous infrastructure failures or multiple safeguards holding at once. Our money is tiny: visitors, a couple of two-day workshops, no postdoc. We want the output to be something a practioner could actually use — a checklist, or an audit procedure, or at minimum a sharpened set of distinctions — not another survey paper, and definately not a bibliography.

Let me sketch the candidate lines of work as we've listed them so far, because I want you to push back on the list and not just rank it. (1) The saturation rule: my hobby-horse. The idea is that ignoring a dependency is licensed only if restoring it would move the answer by less than the model's own demonstrated error on some nearby case where the truth is known — so the presumption gets cashed out as a testable bookkeeping condition rather than a vibe. (2) First-order versus second-order use: does the reliability of an independence heuristic track whether you're using it to get a mean versus a variance or a tail? In the prime case the naive random model gets counts roughly right and apparently gets the fluctuations wrong, and I want to know whether that's the deep fault line or whether the real distinction is between rigid constraints the model violates outright (like a number's being divisible by three) and soft correlations it merely averages over. (3) A case zoo: assemble a few dozen worked cases where the outcome is known, sorted by how the heuristic performed — right answer, right order but wrong constant, right for the wrong reason, qualitatively wrong including flipped sign — and then score candidate licensing rules against that sorting. This feels unglamorous and might be the thing everything else depends on. (4) The tail asymmetry: when the target is a conjunction of many rare events, independence makes it exponentially small, so the presumption seems to do enormous work precisely where it's least checked; is there a principled reason to reverse the default there, and does any of the number-theory evidence speak to it at all? (5) Is the presumption of independence a credal state or a convention about which correlations a modeller has priced in — and is that question deep or merely verbal? (6) What I think of as phantom independence: when you randomize along one axis you silently inject structure along another, and I'd like a general diagnostic for which axis a model has quietly structured. (7) The transfer question: in number theory you eventually get theorems, so a heuristic gets graded; in the risk cases ground truth basically never arrives. How much of the number-theory lesson survives the move, and is this the question that should come first because if the answer is nothing, our whole project collapses?

What I want is a defended agenda, not an overview. Rank these — or tell me which of them collapse into each other, which are muddled as I've stated them, and what I'm leaving out — against explicit criteria: which questions are upstream of which, which are tractable in three years for a group with our mix and no compute, which are already well covered by people we haven't read, and which would change what some outside field actually does. Be honest about the trade-offs between those criteria rather than pretending they all point the same way, tell me what evidence or result over the next two years would move an item up or down the list, and flag anything where I'm obviously just reinventing something. Also tell me if the whole framing of a usable checklist is the wrong deliverable for this group.
