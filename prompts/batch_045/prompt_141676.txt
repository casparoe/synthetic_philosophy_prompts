Two researchers have spent two years in an adversarial collaboration on a single question: does destroying or degrading natural habitat reduce the total amount of suffering experienced by wild animals? They have not converged, but they have stopped talking past each other, and they now need the closing document. Write it for them.

Party A is the *subtractionist*. Her position: because most wild animals born in intact habitat die young, and because the lives of such animals are on balance bad, permanently lowering the carrying capacity of an area lowers the steady flow of sentient lives that begin in deprivation and end early; the concentrated suffering of the destruction event itself is real but brief, and is outweighed on a sufficiently long view. She holds this as a claim about the expected sign of an effect, not as a policy recommendation.

Party B is the *preservationist*. His position: the lives of animals in functioning ecosystems are on balance worth living; habitat destruction imposes a dense pulse of injury, starvation, exposure and predation on animals who already exist; and the landscapes that replace degraded habitat — farmland, plantation, suburbia, secondary scrub, invasive-dominated edge — are not welfare improvements on what they replace, but tend to be dominated by exactly the short-lived, high-fecundity generalists whose lives the subtractionist worries about.

By the end of the collaboration both had accepted a common accounting framework, and the joint statement must use it.

1. The *three clocks*. Score welfare effects separately over the destruction *pulse* (the days to years in which resident animals die or flee), the *transition* (the decades of succession, recolonisation, edge effects and invasive turnover), and the *steady state* (the centuries in which the new land use persists). A claim about "the effect of habitat destruction" is incomplete unless it says which clock it is about.
2. The *displacement ratio*. Of the animal-years that would have been lived in the destroyed area, what fraction are genuinely prevented rather than merely moved elsewhere or deferred — into adjacent habitat, into the replacement land use, or into a later population rebound? Both agree this ratio, not the raw headcount of residents, is the quantity that matters, and that it is not one.
3. The *replacement counterfactual*. The comparison is never "habitat versus nothing" but "habitat versus whatever that land would otherwise become" — and the welfare ranking may differ depending on whether the alternative is a city, a cereal monoculture, a tree plantation, a drained wetland or an abandoned field left to secondary growth.
4. *Taxon-stratified ledgers*. Do the accounting separately for a large-bodied vertebrate with a slow life history, a short-lived, high-fecundity invertebrate, and a soil-dwelling group, then say whether the sign of the total is robust across the three, or whether it depends on which taxon dominates the numbers.

The document should contain, in this order:

**A numbered section of agreed claims** — the propositions both are willing to sign, phrased so that neither is vacuous nor tilted towards one side. Where a claim holds only under one of two competing theories of welfare, or only on one of the three clocks, or only for one of the three taxa, say so in the claim rather than averaging it away. Include at least one claim each side would have refused to sign at the start of the collaboration.

**A disagreements table**, with columns: the issue; Party A's formulation, worded as she would word it; Party B's formulation, worded as he would word it; and a classification of what kind of disagreement it is — empirical, axiological, about the counterfactual, merely verbal, or a mixture. Identify at least one disagreement the two of them now regard as verbal, and say what distinction dissolved it; and at least one they agree is genuine.

**A cruxes section.** For each crux: state it precisely; give a concrete case, comparison, or measurement both accept as bearing on it even though they expect it to be read differently; and record in advance what each party predicts it will show, together with what each would concede if their prediction failed. Among the cruxes you should consider, at least: whether the average welfare of a wild animal-year is positive or negative, and for which taxa; how much weight the mortality pulse should carry relative to the steady state, given that the two fall on different clocks; whether the displacement ratio is nearer zero or nearer one in realistic landscapes; whether degraded and early-successional habitat supports more or fewer animal-years per hectare than the mature habitat it replaces; and whether the sign of the net effect flips depending on the replacement land use.

**A sign-flip table.** For each of the parameters the dispute turns on, record the threshold value at which that party's own conclusion would reverse, and whether either party thinks the true value is near that threshold. This is where the disagreement should be made exact.

Finally, a short closing section headed *what neither of us is claiming*, which should defuse the misreadings both sides have grown tired of.

Constraints on the drafting. No party may rest on uncertainty alone: saying "the effects are too complex to predict" is not a position either of them may hold in this document, and each must state the expected sign under their own parameter estimates. No party may use the value of naturalness, wildness, or non-interference as a free-standing reason; if such a consideration is doing real work in someone's thinking, it must appear as an axiological crux in its own right, stated as a claim about welfare bearers rather than about ecosystems. Neither may cite or gesture at the literature: no references, no reading lists, no named studies, and no appeal to what "the research shows" without specifying what would have to be true for the appeal to hold. Both must remain recognisably committed to their own side: the point of the exercise is an exact residual disagreement, not a compromise position that neither would actually hold.

Write the joint statement in British English, in a formal register, at roughly 1,200 words.
