I'm seventeen, in the second year of sixth form, and I recently spoke at a debating competition on whether state support should come with conditions attached. I prepared it by reading about conditionality, sanctions, workfare-style schemes where you work for your payment, and the recurring panic about benefit fraud, and I came away with the opposite of a settled view: I have very firm reactions to individual cases and no idea what general thing I believe. I've written my verdicts down below in the hope that someone can tell me whether a single principle generates them, which of them I would have to abandon to get a principle, and what the principle says about the cases I can't make up my mind on.

Marta is fifty-four, worked twenty-five years in a textile factory that closed, and is offered warehouse night shifts an hour's bus ride away, which she refuses because she looks after her mother three afternoons a week. She is sanctioned. I think that is wrong. Dwayne is twenty-three, walked out of a call-centre job he hated, refuses that employer's offer to take him back, and wants six months to finish a novel. I think it is fine to require him to take work or lose the payment, and I feel none of the anger I feel about Marta. But I also think unpaid volunteering at a food bank ought to count as meeting his conditions, and I cannot say why that is different from the novel. Aisha works four shifts a week for cash at her cousin's restaurant while claiming jobseeker's allowance and doesn't declare it. That is not just something the state may stop; it deserves an actual penalty, and it feels to me like a different kind of wrong from Marta's or Dwayne's, not merely a worse version of it. Ravi has severe depression, can't get a diagnosis in time, is recorded as fit for work, and loses money for missing a work-focused interview. I think that is worse than Marta's case, and I'm not sure why. Grace spends a lump sum on an enormous television. My considered view is that it is nobody's business, but I feel a flicker of irritation I can't defend. Sunil misses one appointment because the letter went to the wrong address and loses four weeks of money, which strikes me as grotesque, even though I find a four-week reduction proportionate for flatly refusing an offered job. Those two are structurally the same, and I react to them as if they weren't.

The best I can do on my own is this: support is insurance-like, owed in virtue of past contributions together with present inability, so the state is entitled to investigate whether you are able to work but not to pass judgement on how well you use your time; fraud is a separate wrong rather than a failed condition, because it involves taking money under a false description of the facts, which is why it can be punished and not merely withdrawn; and any condition must be one the claimant could have known and met at the point of claiming, and must not be quietly tightened afterwards. A friend of mine says the whole matter reduces to whether someone is a net contributor over a lifetime, and I don't think that reaches Marta at all. Please test my three-part principle against the cases above and repair it where it breaks, stating the finished version precisely, clause by clause, with the case that motivates each clause. Then tell me, honestly, which of my verdicts no defensible version of it can keep. Finally, I'd like its verdicts on cases I haven't listed: Nadia, who refuses an offered job because the employer makes components for weapons; a lone parent who turns down work paying less than the childcare that would make the work possible; someone who stays in cash work because taking the formal job would cost them their housing top-up; and whether the same principle governs a pension or a student maintenance grant, or whether the insurance framing has been doing all the work in my verdicts about workers.
