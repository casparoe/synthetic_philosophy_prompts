my mate has this argument he's been sitting on for months and I cant find the hole in it. It's supposed to show that habitat destruction is, other things equal, a good thing, and he genuinely believes the conclusion. Here it is more or less as he gave it:

(1) The welfare value of an outcome is just the sum of the lifetime welfare of every sentient individual who ever lives in it. Adding a life that is net-negative makes the outcome worse, holding everything else fixed; adding a net-positive one makes it better. Call the net welfare of an ecosystem the sum over all the sentient lives it contains across time.

(2) In a typical terrestrial ecosystem, the overwhelming majority of sentient individuals alive at any moment are the juveniles of r-selected species: insects, fish, small rodents, things that produce hundreds or thousands of offspring of which nearly all starve, get eaten, or die of parasitism within days or weeks. Their expected lifetime welfare is negative.

(3) So the net welfare of most ecosystems is negative, and what makes it negative is the headcount, not the quality of the few long lives in it. He has a name for this part, the Headcount Principle: per-individual lifetime welfare varies over maybe one or two orders of magnitude across the animals in a system, while the numbers vary over four or five, so once you start summing it's the count that drives the sign and the mass of short miserable lives swamps the handful of decent long ones.

(4) Habitat destruction permanently lowers the carrying capacity of an area, hence lowers its equilibrium population, hence raises net welfare: fewer net-negative lives get lived, and not just once but year after year.

(5) The animals killed in the clearing itself are a one-off cost, but the reduction in headcount is ongoing, so the gains swamp the losses at basically any discount rate you like.

(6) Therefore, wherever it doesn't set back humans or livestock, we have strong moral reason to convert wild habitat into something with lower carrying capacity: drainage, ploughing, asphalt, whatever.

(7) And it doesn't stop there. If it's really the headcount that's doing the work, then bulldozing is only one delivery mechanism among many, and anything that lowers the birth rate would do as well or better. And if the sign ever flips for some taxon, if some wild population turns out to be net-positive, then the very same argument seems to tell us to raise its carrying capacity as far as we can, i.e. to breed them on an industrial scale. It also seems to say we should regret every successful reintroduction programme.

The bit I can't swallow is (6) and (7): the claim that a retail park on a wetland counts as an intervention against suffering. I'd rather keep (1) and (2), they feel like the load-bearing parts and the best supported ones. I think something slides between (3) and (5) — (3) sounds like it's counting lives and (5) sounds like it's counting something per year — but I can't get it into focus and he just says I'm having an intuition attack. Which step do you reject, why did it look fine, and what do the other exits cost me?
