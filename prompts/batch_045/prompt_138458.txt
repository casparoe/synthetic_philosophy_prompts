I need a prediction about how a colleague will rule on a case, and I would rather have the reasoning checked before my group meeting than after it.

Dr. Ilse Bracht is a philosopher who collaborates with our lab. She is a Dennettian about the physical, design, and intentional stances, and she audits every abstract we write for what she calls stance hygiene. Her rulings sort intentional-stance language into three boxes: (1) warranted — there is a real pattern here that the lower stances do not already give you, so "wants," "believes," "tries" are doing genuine predictive work and may appear in the results; (2) mere shorthand — harmless in the discussion, but every prediction the gloss makes is also available from the design or physical stance, so it does not belong in the results; (3) pseudo-pattern — the gloss compresses the record you already have and falls apart under intervention, so it should go.

Her verdicts so far, with the one-line reason she gave each time:

1. A domestic thermostat: shorthand. "The design stance gives you the set point; 'wants twenty degrees' adds nothing."
2. A deep-search chess program with a hand-tuned evaluation function: warranted. "Nobody, not even someone holding the source code, predicts its moves from the design stance in the time available; 'it wants to control the centre' predicts moves that nothing else predicts."
3. A pneumonia classifier that hit 94 percent on the archive and was reading the hospital's metal ruler marker: pseudo-pattern. "It compresses the archive and dies the moment you intervene; there was never a pattern about pneumonia."
4. A honeybee colony choosing between nest cavities: warranted. "'The colony prefers the larger cavity' predicts the outcome under every intervention we have devised, and nobody derives it from the micro-dynamics of the bees."
5. A deep RL trading agent trained on profit and deployed after a regime change: pseudo-pattern for "wants to maximise returns," warranted for "wants the proxy." "What it was selected for is not what it now tracks; the proxy is the only real pattern."
6. A self-organising spot migrating up a fuel gradient in an oscillating reaction–diffusion dish (Belousov–Zhabotinsky type) whose recipe was designed to produce exactly that behaviour: shorthand. "The goal is just the attractor; the reaction–diffusion equations already tell you where the spot ends up. The gloss is vivid and buys nothing."

The new case is a dish from a colleague's experiment, and it is a negative control: the recipe was drawn from a random-number generator, nobody designed or selected it for anything, and nothing was expected of it. In it a persistent structure spontaneously forms that (a) migrates up a nutrient gradient from anywhere in the dish; (b) detours around a barrier we slide in after filming has begun, rather than running into it and stalling; (c) goes to a decoy instead — a chemically similar but nutritionally inert bead placed where the nutrient used to be; (d) re-routes around a laser lesion to its leading edge within seconds and resumes. Nobody, including the people who mixed the dish, has predicted any of this in advance. The only ways to get these behaviours are to run the dish or to run the full microphysical simulation, which costs about an hour of compute per ten seconds of medium. "It wants the nutrients and believes they are behind the barrier, or at the decoy" predicts all four in about a second.

So: which box does she put this in, and what is the argument? I want her verdict predicted, not yours. Specifically: state the rule or rules consistent with all six precedents; say which fits her stated reasons best; identify the closest analog among the precedents and say precisely how the new case differs from it in the respect that should matter, and whether that difference flips her verdict; if the precedents underdetermine it, say what principle she would be committing herself to with each answer; and name one further case — specify it concretely, don't just gesture at a type — that would best separate the rules you take to be live, with what each predicts. Finally, tell me how confident you are in each possible verdict, what argument or additional fact about the dish would change your prediction and in which direction, and where your reconstruction of her rule is most likely to be wrong — which precedent is doing all the work, and which of her one-liners you may be reading too much into.
