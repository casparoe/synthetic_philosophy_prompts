You are a professional philosopher who works on the ethics of war, and you have been commissioned to write a dialogue for a magazine of ideas that is read by thoughtful non-specialists. Before you draft, work out privately what the strongest available version of each position is and where each is genuinely weak, and use that to decide who gets which good line. Do not print that private deliberation, and do not let it leak into the piece as a preface, a footnote apparatus, or stage directions that editorialize about who is winning.

Write a dialogue of roughly 1,100 to 1,500 words, tagged by speaker name, with only minimal stage directions. Nothing in it may depend on looking anything up: no citations, no named authorities, no appeals to particular books, treaties, or historical episodes. The arguments must stand on their own, so that a reader with no access to a library or the internet could follow and assess every move.

Setting and cast. Two participants at a week-long residential seminar at a war-studies institute get into an argument after dinner.

- Colonel Halvard Bruun, retired infantry officer, now on the institute's teaching staff. He defends the traditional view: combatants on both sides of a war have the same rights and the same liabilities, a soldier's moral position is fixed by how he fights and not by the cause he fights for, and responsibility for the war itself belongs to the political leaders who chose it.
- Dr. Leila Vosoughi, a philosopher. She defends the revisionist view: what matters morally is the objective justice of what a particular person is doing, a soldier fighting for an unjust cause has no right to kill and is morally liable to defensive force in a way his adversary is not, and there is no principled place to stop applying this below the level of political leaders.

The case they are arguing about is invented, and you should treat it as given. The Republic of Kavala has invaded the neighbouring state of Meridia. Kavala's intervention stops an ongoing campaign of ethnic cleansing against the Shen minority, and that intervention is necessary, proportionate, and a genuine last resort. But Kavala's government also intends to annex Shen province permanently in order to control its cobalt deposits, has said so in domestic speeches, and has structured roughly a third of its planned operations around the annexation rather than around protection. Meridia's army is overwhelmingly conscript; Meridian state media presents the war as a foreign resource grab; most Meridian soldiers therefore fight in the reasonably but falsely held belief that they are repelling a war of conquest.

Both speakers must be equally competent: each gets to state their position at its best, each must answer the other's actual claim rather than a nearby weaker one, and each must make at least one concession that a supporter of their own side would find uncomfortable. Do not let either of them win on rhetorical polish, and do not manufacture a tidy resolution: the disagreement should still be open at the end, but the reader should be able to say precisely where it now lies.

Work through all four of the following pressure points, in this order, and let each one arise naturally in conversation rather than as a checklist.

1. Private Amara Sen, a Meridian conscript who drives the supply trucks that keep an anti-aircraft battery fuelled. Her belief that she is resisting aggression is reasonable given everything available to her. Bruun should press the point that she is epistemically blameless and therefore fully the moral equal of the Kavalan soldiers trying to kill her. Vosoughi should respond by separating three things that the argument keeps running together: whether Sen is blameworthy, whether Sen is morally liable to be killed defensively, and whether it is permissible for others to kill her anyway. She must say which of these she thinks holds of Sen and why, and Bruun must then test that combination rather than just restating that Sen is blameless.

2. The mixed aims of Kavala's war. Introduce and name, stipulatively, two views about this. Call them the severance view: when a war has both a just and an unjust aim, and the just component is genuinely independent of the unjust one, soldiers whose actions serve only the just component retain the rights they would have had in an unmixed just war. And call it the contamination view: the addition of an unjust aim to an otherwise just war alters the moral character of the whole campaign, so no Kavalan soldier can claim the rights of a just combatant. Have Bruun argue that revisionists must pick one and that neither choice is comfortable, and have Vosoughi give her reason for picking the one she picks. Then bring in Sergeant Tomás Iriarte: a Kavalan volunteer who enlisted explicitly to help annex Shen and who still wants that, but whose unit has in fact spent the whole war securing a refugee corridor and who has never fired his weapon. Ask, through the characters, whether his bad aims, or his actual contribution, should settle his status.

3. The exchange test. Have Bruun introduce it as a device of his own: to find out whether a soldier is morally at fault for fighting, ask not what his side's cause is but whether this same person, raised and placed on the other side, would have fought just as willingly. Because most soldiers on both sides would have, he argues, combatants are morally interchangeable in the only sense that matters, and the law of war is right to treat them as equals whatever their cause. Vosoughi must grant that the test returns the result Bruun describes and then explain why she thinks the test is measuring the wrong thing: say what, on her view, liability actually tracks, and why facts about how obedient or biddable a person is do not settle whether he may be killed.

4. The worry that revisionism cannot be used. Bruun's strongest remaining move is practical: if a soldier's right to kill depends on the objective justice of his war, and if people on both sides of every real war disagree reasonably about that, then soldiers can almost never know whether they are permitted to fire, and the rules of war become a moral trap rather than a guide. Vosoughi must answer this without pretending the difficulty is small, and should say what she thinks an ordinary soldier in that situation ought to do. Then give Bruun the hardest case for his own side: a Meridian conscript who personally endorses the ethnic cleansing and fights eagerly, and one who was beaten into uniform and fights as little as he can. Ask whether the symmetry thesis really requires treating these two as morally alike, and let Bruun say how he lives with whatever he admits.

Close with an epilogue of no more than four sentences in which each speaker names the single consideration that would actually move them off their position.
