my flatmate and I had a three hour arguement last night about whether the state should pay when someone breaks their back doing something obviously stupid — he'd just read about a climber who needed a helicopter rescue and he was going on about how the rest of us shouldn't subsidise other people's thrills, and I kept saying no, you can't do that, and by the end I'd said so much about what I actually think that I'm no longer sure it all fits together. he couldn't tell me where the crack was either. so here's the set, please audit it.

(a) the core bit: it's unjust, not merely unfortunate, when one person is worse off than another through no choice of their own; but if you end up worse off because you knowingly took a gamble or took the easy option, the resulting inequality isn't in itself an injustice. that's the whole point of the view as far as I'm concerned, and it's why I'm not just a "give everyone more money" person.

(b) expensive tastes get nothing. we equalise resources, not welfare. if my friend is perfectly happy on a modest income and I can't be content without opera tickets and good coffee, that's not a claim on anyone else. tastes are things you have, and you bear their cost.

(c) but we never let anyone sink. however stupid the choice, however well-informed and voluntary it was, if someone ends up with nowhere to live or no treatment or nothing to eat, we help them. a decent society does not leave people to starve or die of their own bad decisions, and I don't care if that makes the rest of (a) wobble.

(d) I'm also pretty convinced that in real life you basically can't separate choice from circumstance — everything anyone chooses is shaped by genes, upbringing, peers, what was cheap, what was normal where they grew up. so I'm deeply suspicious of any actual policy that tries to be responsibility-sensitive case by case.

(e) and I don't want the state asking people what they did, or whether they deserve help, or what their preferences really are. no means-testing of character, no official judgement about anyone's life. the thought of having to stand up and explain yourself to get help is genuinely repulsive to me.

(f) the thing I've actually been pushing for years, which is meant to solve all of the above at once: mandatory prudence insurance. everyone is auto-enrolled at eighteen, nobody opts out, nobody makes a claim. it's funded by a levy on the risky activity itself — a surcharge on the motorbike licence, the climbing permit, the gambling stake, the cigarettes — scaled to how much that activity costs the scheme. if you crash you get a flat floor: treatment, housing, a basic income floor, no questions asked, nobody ever investigates whether it was your fault. responsibility gets handled once, up front, by pricing the activity, not afterwards by judging people.
