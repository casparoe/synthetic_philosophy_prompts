I teach an introductory philosophy of language course, and I am writing a problem set on Russell's theory of descriptions. My students have worked through the analysis — a sentence of the form *the F is G* is true just in case there is at least one F, at most one F, and that thing is G — together with the stock objections that run through empty and non-unique descriptions. The following week they meet the referential/attributive distinction, but I want them to run into the underlying problem cold, before they have a name for it. So I need a case that puts pressure on Russell's analysis without using any of the machinery they have already learned to deflect pressure with, and without being one of the famous referential-use stories they would recognize on sight.

What I want you to build:

A scenario of no more than about 120 words, set inside an ordinary institution — a hospital ward, a school, a union local, a condominium board, a university department, an airline crew desk — that ends with one speaker uttering a single sentence containing a definite description. The sentence must be a single, unembedded clause: no negation, no modal, no conditional, no verb of thinking, saying, or hoping. That constraint is deliberate. I want the primary/secondary scope distinction to have nothing to get a grip on, so that Russell's analysis yields exactly one verdict for the utterance and the Russellian cannot appeal to a second reading.

The specifications the case has to meet:

1. Russell's analysis, spelled out as its three conjuncts, returns TRUE. There is a satisfier of the description, it is unique on any domain the scenario makes available, and it satisfies the predicate.

2. A referential treatment — one on which the description contributes an object to the proposition, so that the utterance says of that object that it is G — returns FALSE. The object it contributes must be the one the speaker is talking about and the one a hearer in the scenario would take the speaker to be talking about, and the predicate must fail of that object.

3. You have to argue, not stipulate, that the description's semantic content is satisfied by the first object rather than the second. The mismatch must not be produced by anyone's mistake about perception, identity, or names: nobody misidentifies a person, points at the wrong one, or mishears a name. It must fall out instead of an institutional or documentary arrangement — a roster, a charter, a job title, a schedule, a standing rule about who counts as holding a role — that makes one object the satisfier while everyone in the conversation is talking about a different one. Everyone in the story should be fully informed about the relevant facts; the trouble is not ignorance.

4. The Russellian gets her two usual escape hatches, and you have to show that they fail, or concede honestly where they do not: first, adding a hidden domain restriction or contextual completion to the description; second, treating the datum as pragmatic — the speaker was misleading, or implicated something false, while the sentence itself was true. Build the case so that the second move is as hard to run as you can make it, and then give me your verdict on whether it still works.

5. Off the table by fiat: empty descriptions (no French monarchs), bare failure of uniqueness, corpses in libraries, spies on beaches, men across the room with glasses in their hands, murder trials, and two tables in one room.

After the scenario, in no more than about 600 words:

- Write out the Russellian expansion of the utterance and show that it comes out true.
- Give the referential verdict, and in one sentence say why the utterance would strike an ordinary hearer in the scenario as false rather than as true but unhelpful.
- Name the single feature of the case that is doing the work, and say what happens if you remove it. My expectation is that without it the case collapses back into an ordinary misdescription, about which Russellians and referentialists can both shrug; if that is what happens, show it, and if it is not, say what the case becomes instead.
- Say what a Strawson-style treatment — one on which the existence and uniqueness contributed by the description is a presupposition rather than part of the truth-conditional content — would say about the same utterance. I expect it to side with Russell here; if it does not, explain the departure.
- Say how a defender of whichever theory comes out worse would attack the case, and whether that attack succeeds.

Finally, commit. Give me one scenario, not a menu of candidates. Commit to both verdicts, and commit in particular on the empirical claim that ordinary speakers would call the utterance false rather than true-but-misleading. Then flag the single step in your argument you are least confident about, and in one sentence say what would change your mind about it.
