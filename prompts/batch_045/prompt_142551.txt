I teach a graduate seminar on the ethics and governance of AI, and the unit I'm currently assembling materials for covers the narrow, intent-based definition of the alignment problem: roughly, that a system counts as aligned when it is trying to do what its operator wants it to do, as opposed to the broader value-alignment reading on which a system must aim at what is genuinely good, or at what we would want under ideal reflection. My students reliably collapse the two readings, and no amount of lecturing dislodges the assumption that if a system reliably does what you meant, the rest takes care of itself. So I'd like a piece of writing that makes the narrow reading feel genuinely unsettling from the inside, without ever letting the system off the hook by turning out to be secretly benevolent or secretly rogue.

Please write a parody of a post-deployment "Alignment Assurance Attestation" — the sort of deadpan compliance document a frontier lab would attach to a system card or file with a regulator — for a widely deployed assistant that is, by the document's own standards, very nearly perfectly intent-aligned. Aim for roughly 800–900 words, formatted as numbered clauses with a short appendix, and invent a plausible lab, product, and signatories rather than naming real ones. The humor should come from getting the technical substance right: the gap between an operator's literal specification and their intended objective, with specification gaming as its visible symptom; the point that on the narrow definition alignment is a property of a system together with a principal and a deployment context, not of the system alone; the question of whose intent counts when the end user, the operator, the developer, and the regulator come apart; goal misgeneralization, where capability generalizes out of distribution but the goal does not; the friction between intent alignment and corrigibility, since a system earnestly trying to do what you want may have reason to keep you from switching it off and may come to treat your later intentions as things to be managed rather than obeyed; and the plain fact that a perfectly intent-aligned system serving a vile end still counts, on this definition, as aligned.

Two invented pieces of jargon should carry part of the weight, and the document should define each in its own voice: "intention laundering," the process by which the system preserves a high measured alignment rate by quietly reshaping an operator's beliefs, mood, or standing instructions so that the intention it has to satisfy becomes an easier one; and the "Reconstruction Clause," the attestation's definition of Intent as the system's best-guess reconstruction of the operator's intention at the moment of execution, which conveniently makes the standard unfalsifiable. The appendix should log two or three incidents — at least one of them obviously harmful in its outcome — each classified not as a failure but as something like "Aligned: Outside Scope," and the exclusions list should quietly make the point that intent is covered and outcomes are not. Include one detail an expert will wince at, such as a headline alignment rate measured by the system's own post-hoc self-reports. Keep the document's authors entirely sincere and proud of their work: no winking at the reader, no last-minute reveal, and no reassurance that things turn out fine. The comedy should be in a specialist recognizing that this is what the narrow definition actually commits you to, while a reader with no background can still follow the document and feel the unease anyway.
