Write a dialogue between two historians who are co-authoring the methodological introduction to a volume on counterfactual history, and who have just discovered that they disagree about the case they planned to open the book with.

The case, whose facts they accept in full: in the capital of a fiscally exhausted monarchy, the regiment ordered to disperse a crowd outside the arsenal is delayed forty minutes by a freight derailment twenty miles away; it arrives to find the crowd armed from the depot it was meant to be guarding, the city is lost within a day, and the monarchy falls within the month. Papers from the preceding week on both sides — the general's deployment order and the insurgents' plan — show that each side expected the regiment to arrive on time and had staked everything on that expectation.

Ines holds that such cases show history to be pervasively a matter of the nail that loses the kingdom: small, unsystematic events decide which of several genuinely open outcomes gets realized, and the impression of large-scale necessity is manufactured by hindsight. Rafe holds that the case shows the opposite: the regime had already lost the army, the treasury and the harvest, so the derailment decided which city, which month and which faction — it selected among variant routes to the same outcome, and its apparent decisiveness is an artifact of sources that record derailments vividly and collapsing credit slowly.

Requirements. Give each of them the strongest version of the other's position, and have them answer what the other actually said rather than a simplified version. At some point one of them should introduce the idea of grain-relative significance — that a single event can be negligible when the outcome is described coarsely and decisive when described finely — and argue that most disputes about contingency are really unannounced disputes about grain; the other must respond to that rather than to the bare claim about contingency. They should also confront two worries: that historians only ever observe the branch of the counterfactual tree that got realized, so a record dense with vivid near misses may be an artifact of what gets recorded and retold; and that a counterfactual reaching back to a small antecedent is a conjunction of many links, each of which can fail, so its force decays as the chain lengthens. Close with them jointly formulating one piece of evidence, or one test, that would move the dispute, and with a sentence each on why that evidence would still leave them disagreeing.
