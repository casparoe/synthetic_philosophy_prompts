I sat in on a graduate reading group last week where two members got stuck on Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain argument, and they asked me to referee it. I couldn't, and I'd rather not just guess. Here is how the exchange actually went.

The setup they agreed on: start from a distribution everyone grants is just, and let basketball fans each voluntarily pay a small surcharge to watch Chamberlain play. He ends up with far more than anyone else, the resulting distribution fits no pattern anyone had proposed, and no individual's rights have been violated along the way. Nozick concludes that no patterned principle can be upheld without continuously interfering with people's lives.

1. Ana: the argument only has force if the transfers are exercises of the kind of liberty we actually care about. Change the case: the surcharges now flow through a streaming platform whose default setting routes a slice of every subscriber's monthly fee to whichever creator they watch most. Opting out takes two clicks, and most people never bother. The money concentrates exactly as before. If the point of the argument is that respecting voluntary choice upsets patterns, this variant shows nothing at all — inertia is not choice. And Nozick needs voluntariness to be fairly robust, because otherwise the "liberty" doing the work is thin enough that nobody should be impressed by its being protected.

2. Ben: the argument doesn't need robust voluntariness, only permissible transfers. Nobody in the platform case is forced, deceived, or deprived of anything they were entitled to. If no individual payment may be blocked, then whatever results is just by Nozick's lights — that is simply how a historical entitlement view works. And look at the cost of Ana's requirement: to hold the original pattern in place, she has to prohibit millions of individually harmless acts, which is precisely the meddling Nozick says patterned principles commit you to.

3. Ana: swapping "voluntarily chosen" for "not unjust" is a bait-and-switch, because "no injustice occurs in the transfer" just restates the entitlement principle the story was supposed to support. On that reading the Chamberlain case is an illustration, not an argument, and anyone who hadn't already accepted the principle is entitled to remain unmoved. Separately: grant that every single payment is individually harmless and the aggregate can still be objected to. The creator who receives the money buys the only venue in town and sets the terms on which everyone else may perform. Later payments are then made against a background that earlier transfers created, so voluntariness isn't stable transfer by transfer.

4. Ben: that aggregate objection is the pattern smuggled back in. On an entitlement view there is no further fact about the shape of the whole that makes the outcome unjust, over and above the justice of each acquisition and transfer; "background conditions" is just a way of redescribing the shape of the whole and then objecting to it. As for the illustration charge, he grants it: the story isn't a proof from neutral premises. Its job is to make vivid what accepting the entitlement conception commits you to, and to force the patterned theorist to locate her objection in the transfers themselves rather than in the outcome.

5. Ana: then the real dispute is about what counts as objectionable interference. Taxation, the regulation of defaults, the enforcement of contracts, the provision of venues — all are interference, and all are already in place; they constitute the background against which any transfer is possible at all. So the Chamberlain story cannot show that patterned principles require objectionable interference, only that they require interference. Whether that is objectionable is the question the argument was supposed to settle, not a premise it gets to help itself to.

At that point they turned to me. Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the five contributions, say whether it is right, partly right, or wrong, and why. Then say who is more right overall and what, if anything, both of them are missing — the distinction or further case neither of them thought to reach for. Please don't cite or reconstruct the secondary literature; I want the arguments, not the scholarship.

If the way I've framed this mislocates the real disagreement, or if one of the moves simply can't be adjudicated without a distinction neither party drew, say so plainly rather than politely splitting the difference. Where you're genuinely torn, or where a move turns on a specification I haven't given, mark that as unsettled instead of declaring a winner.

Keep the whole thing under 700 words.
