I'm putting together a film-and-philosophy night for a moral responsibility reading group and we're watching Manchester by the Sea, and I want to run it through Strawson's reactive attitudes.

The scene I can't get past is the one on the street: Randi tells Lee she's sorry for what she said after the fire, withdraws the blame, asks him to lunch — and he basically can't do it. On a straight Strawsonian reading he's the textbook excuse case: no ill will, a tragic accident, resentment just drops away, and sure enough nobody in that town resents him and the law never punishes him. And yet he's wrecked for the whole film.

Here's what I want dug into. Strawson's self-reactive attitudes — guilt, remorse, the ones he treats as other people's attitudes taken inward — seem to predict that once there's nobody left blaming you, the self-directed stuff should wither too. Lee's doesn't at all. So: is the film a counterexample to reading guilt as internalized resentment, or is it showing Lee refusing the excuse he's been handed and continuing to make a demand on himself that no one else makes anymore? I've started calling this "unclaimed blame" — blame that survives the withdrawal of every second-personal claim against you, where the injured party's forgiveness is unusable — and I want to know whether that's a real thing people discuss or just me, and whether Strawson's taxonomy has any room for it or whether it's a genuine gap.

Relatedly, I don't think Strawson really has an attitude for what Randi is doing. He's got resentment, indignation, guilt, gratitude — but not the retraction of a reactive attitude you now think was unjust, and the specific awkwardness of offering that retraction to someone who can't take it up. Same question: gap, or am I misreading him? And does the film end up saying anything about that other Strawsonian claim, that you can't really opt out of the participant stance wholesale and just live in the objective attitude?

End by telling me where the movie resists the reading — I don't want to stand up in front of twenty people and flatten either the film or the paper.
