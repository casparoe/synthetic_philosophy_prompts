hey — i'm doing a five-minute jokey opening for a reading group on social choice next week, and i want one gag that's genuinely about the discursive dilemma in judgment aggregation, not about voting in general. everyone in that room has seen the courtroom version (two premises, contract valid + breach, liability = the conjunction), so please set it somewhere else.

Setup i've been toying with: a three-person committee deciding whether the department should buy an absurdly expensive espresso machine. They agree in advance on P = it would genuinely improve quality of life in the department, Q = the department can afford it without cutting anything else, and they agree the machine gets bought iff both P and Q are accepted. The individual views are: member 1 accepts P and Q; member 2 accepts P, rejects Q; member 3 rejects P, accepts Q. Everyone votes sincerely and everyone is individually consistent. So a majority accepts P, a majority accepts Q, and a majority votes no on buying it.

What i want from you:

1. The joke itself — a short scene, dialogue is fine, ideally under 200 words. The laugh has to come out of the real mechanics: that voting premise-by-premise and voting straight on the conclusion come apart, or that the group ends up "accepting" P and Q while rejecting P-and-Q. No generic "academics can never decide anything" gag, and don't name any real theorists or papers inside the joke.

2. A second beat where someone tries to fix the mess and makes it worse. Two candidates, take whichever is funnier (or invent a better one): (a) the chair says fine, from now on we only vote on the outcome, never on reasons — so the buy vote fails, but the minutes now record that the committee agreed it would improve quality of life and that they can afford it, and someone reading the minutes later concludes the committee has lost its mind; (b) the chair proposes a meta-vote on which procedure to use, and the joke is that this either just relocates the same problem one level up or quietly becomes a disguised vote on the outcome. Whichever you pick, the way the fix fails has to be an actual feature of the aggregation problem, not bad luck.

3. After the joke, a short note — a few lines plus a tiny table of the three members' votes on P, on Q, and on the outcome — saying which bit of the mechanics each beat is riding on, so I can check I'm not accidentally telling a joke about the Condorcet cycle, or about strategic voting, or about aggregating preferences instead of judgments.
