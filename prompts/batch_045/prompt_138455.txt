I need a hand getting ready for a talk and I'm not at all sure of the conventions, so forgive the ramble. I'm 71, retired three years ago from a career doing electrophysiology of the somatosensory system (a good chunk of it on nociception and pain pathways), and since I stopped I've been auditing the philosophy seminars at the university and reading my way very slowly into philosophy of mind. They've asked me to give one of the talks in the Cognition and Consciousness seminar series next month, which is flattering and slightly terrifying.

The position I want to defend is that epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness is coherent and, specifically, that it is not refuted by the paradox of phenomenal judgment. My argument in outline: every physical effect has a sufficient physical cause, phenomenal properties are not physical properties, so there is no causal work left over for them to do; then, on the paradox itself, the epiphenomenalist can say that the judgment I am in pain is not caused by the pain but is nevertheless made correct by it. I have been calling this the inert-normativity move: experiences are normatively loaded and causally idle, they fix what it would be right to judge without bringing it about that anyone judges it. Reliability, on my story, is secured downstream by the fact that the neural vehicle of the experience and the neural vehicle of the judgment are the same event described two ways, so the judgment tracks the experience without the experience pushing anything around.

The audience is about thirty people. Most are cognitive neuroscientists and psychologists: several run masking and binocular rivalry experiments, two are keen on no-report paradigms, one does metacognition and perceptual confidence. Then maybe four philosophers: one works on higher-order and global-workspace theories and is friendly but thinks phenomenal realism is optional, one is an outright illusionist, and the other two are graduate students who will say whatever the loudest person in the room says. Almost nobody there is going to be a dualist already.

What I want from you is a Q and A prep sheet. Roughly eight objections, ordered by how likely they are times how much damage they'd do, each one phrased the way that particular person would actually put it rather than in tidy philosophical prose, with a two-minute reply I can give standing up and an honest note on where my reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede in the first five minutes so the room doesn't turn against me before I get to the argument, and what the one reply is that has to land if the talk is going to survive at all.

Two things I specifically want you to go after. First, I have a feeling there's a version of the paradox that survives my move even if you grant all of it: reliability is not the same thing as entitlement, and if whatever produced my judgment was insensitive to whether it was correct, it's not clear why I'm entitled to it rather than merely right by accident. I don't have a line on that and I think that's where I get hurt. Second, the illusionist is going to say that if consciousness does no work at all, the honest version of my view is hers, and I don't know whether to preempt that or let her spring it. And please include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
