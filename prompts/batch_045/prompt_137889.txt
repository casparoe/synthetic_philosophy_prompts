Someone in my department (works on decision-theory adjacent stuff, so presumably knows what he's talking about) posted a short explainer of why you have to be an expected utility maximizer. I'm paraphrasing from memory so its maybe not word for word, but roughly:

"It's two arguments glued together. If your degrees of belief break the probability calculus, a bookie can offer you a package of bets, each of which you're happy to accept at your own odds, that loses you money no matter how the world turns out — a Dutch book. And if your preferences are intransitive, or you violate independence, you can be money-pumped: walked through a series of choices each of which you prefer at the time, ending up with strictly less than you started and having paid for the privilege. So probabilism and expected utility maximization aren't optional norms of rationality, they're just what not being exploitable comes to. 'Utility' here just means money corrected for risk, so the theory says take whatever option has the higher expected dollar payoff — someone who prefers a guaranteed hundred bucks to a coin flip that pays 110 or 90 is making exactly the kind of mistake the theorems rule out. And that's really why the Allais paradox is fatal: ordinary people violate these axioms systematically, and a norm of rationality that nearly everybody fails can't be the right norm."

I mostly believed it when I read it, but it feels too clean, and two bits have been nagging at me: the bit about expected dollars, and "just what not being exploitable comes to". Can you go through it and separate what a defender of these arguments would actually endorse from what's a simplification or a slide, and then say whether his bottom line — that a non-EU-maximizer can be reliably drained of money and is irrational for that reason — survives once the dodgy bits are corrected? If the money-pump half is weaker than the Dutch-book half, I'd like to know exactly where it weakens.
