I work in the safety department of a regional transit authority — light rail plus buses, about twelve hundred vehicles — and roughly forty incident reviews a year cross my desk: collisions, signal failures, maintenance errors, and near-misses that come in through our confidential reporting system. I write them up; a committee of five (operations, maintenance, HR, a driver representative, and me) signs off; the board reads the summary page and decides which recommendations get funded. Two features of our process matter a great deal. The first is that our template has a single line labelled root cause, and the board will not accept a report that leaves that line blank or fills it with a list. The second is that, under our agreement with the union, we may name an individual in that line only if the committee finds the individual was personally responsible; naming someone sets off a separate process that everyone involved dislikes. So in practice every review ends in one of two ways — we name a person, or we say it was the system — and I have come to think that this is an artefact of the template rather than a finding about the world.

I have been reading about causation on my own, with no formal training in philosophy, precisely because the reviews keep turning on it, and I want a procedure, not more theory. Here is where I have got to. The test we all reach for is the but-for test: would the outcome have happened if this had been different? It seems to me to give the wrong answer when two things were each sufficient on their own — each can say the outcome would have happened anyway, so butt-for says neither is a cause — and it also seems to let almost everything in, since the outage would not have happened if the part had never been manufactured or if the authority had never been built. The alternative I have read about, which I believe is called the interventionist account, says something like: X caused Y if there is some possible change to X, brought about in a way that does not itself change anything else except through X, that would change Y or the chance of Y, with the other relevant factors held at the levels they actually had. I can see the appeal. I can also see three places where it might be hollow: the answer depends on what else you hold fixed, on which alternative value of X you imagine (double the maintenance budget? halve it? check the bolt, or check it twice and sign for it?), and on whether the change is one anyone could actually carry out. If those are choices we make, then the procedure is not discovering causes; it is recording our decisions about which comparisons to care about. I would much rather have that stated openly in the procedure than have the board discover it in a report.

What I want from you is a decision procedure my team can run on each review, in the order we should run it, written so that someone with no philosophy can apply it. For each step I need the question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a no and the report says the candidate is not a cause, and where it stops to hand the question to a named person or to the committee because it is a question of values or of authority rather than of fact. I need a fixed form for the cause sentence itself, so that forty reports a year are comparable, and I need the form to force us to say what we are holding fixed and what alternative we are comparing against. I also need the procedure to say what to do when we cannot get the number we want, which is most of the time: we almost never have data on how often a step is skipped or how much a change would reduce risk, and we have about two working days per report and ninety minutes of committee time.

There are specific hard cases the procedure must be able to handle rather than wave at: two independent faults each of which would have produced the outcome on its own; a backup that would have caught the failure except that the backup was itself defective; a failure to act rather than an act; a factor nobody in our organisation can change, such as a driver's undiagnosed medical condition or the geometry of a junction as built; a factor that changes nothing by itself and only matters in combination with another; a cause far back in time, such as a hiring freeze two years ago; and the ordinary case where the best we can say is that something made the outcome more likely rather than certain. I have also been using an informal test of my own when we are deciding whether to name a person: I ask whether swapping that person for another competent employee, same shift, same conditions, same equipment, would have changed anything. Tell me where that test fits in the procedure, whether it is sound, and where it breaks.

Then run the procedure on three cases.

One. A signal failed at a junction and a train was brought to a stop in a tunnel. The investigation found a power supply module that had been degrading for months and was past its replacement interval, and also a wiring fault introduced during repair work three weeks earlier that would on its own have taken the signal out that same night. Either alone would have done it. Our but-for instinct says neither is the cause. What does the procedure say we write in the line, and if the template permits only one entry, how does the procedure decide which of the two goes there?

Two. A bus struck a pedestrian in a marked crosswalk at dusk. The driver was within the speed limit and had no sightline difficulty until the last moment; the pedestrian was looking at her phone and stepped out against the signal. The committee's instinct is to write pedestrian inattention. My instinct is that this is both true and useless, and that naming the one variable we cannot act on while ignoring the ones we can is a choice rather than a finding. Is her inattention a cause by your procedure? And what should the procedure tell us to do about the fact that the manipulable variables on our side — crossing lighting, signal timing, the speed limit on that corridor, vehicle front design — each change the probability only slightly?

Three, the case I expect to dislike. A light-rail car was pulled from service after a brake component was found torqued incorrectly. Six technicians work that shift; all six, including the one on duty, routinely skip the re-torque step, because the job card places it at the end of a twelve-hour night shift and the tooling is kept at the far end of the depot. The board wants a name in the root cause line, and the driver representative has said the report will be used to discipline someone. My own swap test says the individual is not the cause: any of the six, on that night, in those conditions, would have done the same. Run the procedure and tell me honestly what it outputs — including, if this is where it lands, that causation and accountability come apart, and that we are being asked to use a causal report to do a moral or administrative job that it cannot do. Then say clearly which part of that decision belongs to the committee and not to the procedure.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: which questions it will systematically get wrong, which it will refuse, where it will be quietly steered by whoever chooses the variables and the contrasts, and what I should watch for in the first six months if we adopt it.
