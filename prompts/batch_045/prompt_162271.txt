My brother and I got into an argument about this and I need someone to tell me which of us is less wrong, or at least where each of us is going wrong.

A few weeks ago I read a pop-science article on my phone about what it means to say what a body part is for. I don't have the link and it was one of those pieces that never names any philosophers, but the gist was: there are two accounts. On the older etiological one, a trait's function is whatever it was selected for, so a heart with a birth defect that can't pump blood still has the function of pumping blood, it's just malfunctioning. On the newer causal-role one, a trait's function is whatever it's actually doing right now that helps the organism survive and reproduce. The article said the causal-role account is the one working biologists really use, since it lets you say what an organ does without knowing anything about its evolutionary history. Then it spend a paragraph saying the causal-role account runs into trouble because it can't tell the difference between the heart's pumping blood, the heart's making a thumping noise, and the heart's sloshing the fluid around inside the chest, so it has to count all of those as functions. And it wrapped up by saying that if you go causal-role, there's no deep difference between a heart and a bolt in an engine, or between a trait shaped by selection and a lucky accident: function just means doing something useful toward a goal somebody picked, and the only goal biology hands you is surviving and reproducing.

Two things bugged me. First, the article attacked one view for a paragraph and then seemed to conclude in favor of it anyway, so maybe I misread which one is supposed to be dead. Second, my brother read the ending as saying there's no real fact of the matter about whether some stretch of so-called junk DNA does anything, or whether a trait that helps an animal right now but was never selected for counts as having a function at all. I think that's too strong but I can't say why. I also keep feeling like the two accounts might be answering different questions, one about where a trait came from and one about what it's currently doing, but again I don't know enough to make that precise.

The case that actually started the argument, which I made up: a bacterium living in someone's gut aquires a gene that lets it digest an artificial sweetener that's only been in the food supply for a few decades. It clearly helps the bacterium, extra food source, it outcompetes its neighbors, but there's no way that gene was selected for breaking down that chemical, because the chemical basically didn't exist back then. Same flavor of case: cells in one of those lab-grown organ-on-a-chip setups. Nobody selected those for anything, but researchers still talk about what the cells are for.

So: can you go through the article's claims one at a time and tell me what a philosopher of biology would accept, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong? Does the no-fact-of-the-matter conclusion my brother drew actually follow from anything true in the summary? Is the article right that one of these is the account scientists use and the other has been superseded, or is it mixing up two things that should be kept apart (I've since seen people online mention a selected effects version, which sounds like it might be a third thing)? And does my sweetener-eating bacterium really seperate the two accounts the way I think it does, or can both of them handle it?
