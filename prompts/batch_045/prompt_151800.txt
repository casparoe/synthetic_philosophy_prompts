I'm a high school senior working on a term paper for a philosophy elective, and I've gotten stuck on the moral realism versus anti-realism debate. The way it was presented to us: realists hold that moral claims are true or false regardless of what anyone thinks or wants; anti-realists deny that, and the versions we looked at treat moral judgments as something closer to expressions of feeling, which strikes me as obviously wrong about how moral argument actually works. What I want to know is why nobody considers an option that seems to me to fit much better: moral standards being real the way the rules of chess or the value of a dollar are — created by us, but once in place genuinely binding, genuinely the sort of thing you can be wrong about, and genuinely able to improve. If that works, the whole debate seems to rest on a mistake, namely the assumption that morality can have real authority over us only if it exists independently of us; drop that assumption and there is nothing left to argue about, so shouldn't this settle the question? And if my option is really just one of the two sides wearing a different label, which side is it, and what does it lose by being that?
