Me and my study group have knocked together an argument about old evidence in Bayesian epistemology that we're all sure is broken somewhere, but we can't agree where.

(1) On the Bayesian story, E confirms H only if P(E) < 1, because otherwise conditionalising can't budge P(H) at all.
(2) Anything you already know has probability 1 for you.
(3) So nothing you already knew can confirm anything.
(4) Wich is nuts: the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion had been on the books for decades, and it's the stock example of a bit of evidence that confirmed general relativity in 1915.
(5) The fix everyone reaches for: what you really learn isn't E, it's the logical fact that H plus the auxiliaries entails E. That entailment wasn't certain for you, so conditionalising on it does the confirming work.
(6) But then no observation ever does any confirming: swap in any other known truth that H also entails and you'd get the same boost, so all confirmation turns out to be confirmation by logic and maths rather than by the world.
(7) And a logically omniscient agent, which is the Bayesian ideal, already knows every entailment, so for the ideal agent nothing could ever confirm anything, making the ideal a kind of disability.
(8) So either the fix is wrong, or Bayesian confirmation can't be the whole story about confirmation, or ideal rationality is bad for you.

I want to hold on to (4) and I think (7) is absurd, so I'd rather drop something else. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me?

One wrinkle we made up that might be where it really bites: suppose a colleague I trust completely tells me that H entails E, and a year later I work through the derivation myself. The proposition is already certain for me by then, so (5) says I get no confirmation that day at all, but it really feels like I do, and like it's the physics doing the work rather than the logic. Is that probly just a feeling I should junk, or does it show the fix is tracking the wrong thing?
