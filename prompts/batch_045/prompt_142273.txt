say i want a theory of shared intention i can actually *use* — not one that just sounds plausible in a seminar room. the use i have in mind: working out who is on the hook (blame, owed apologies, debts of aid and of consultation) when a group project goes sideways, in settings that are heirarchical, partly automated, and where people drift in and out mid-project. so write me the desiderata. what does an account of what it is for some people to share an intention and act together have to deliver, with the case behind each requirement, and for each one say whether you think it is genuinely non-negotiable or just a prejudice of one camp.

the cases i keep tripping over and want handled:

- the relay crew: five people on rotating shifts who are never all awake at the same time, each knowing only her own leg and that somebody came before and somebody comes after. is this one joint action or five? when does it start, who is in it, and what does 'in it' even mean for someone asleep in another timezone?
- the infiltrator: someone who slots into a crew's plan perfectly, meshes subplans, is fully counted on by the others, but is secretly planning to blow the whistle and hopes the thing fails. does she share the intention that we J? I don't want the answer to just be 'no, because her end is different' — tell me what has to be true for that answer to fall out.
- the checked-out member: someone who agreed to the plan, then quietly stopped caring, but kept showing up and doing his bit anyway. when does he stop being a member? can he exit alone, or does quitting need uptake from the others?
- the stand-in: a piece of software, or a trained animal, that reliably fills a role in the plan because of how it was built or trained, with nothing intentional going on inside it. is it a member of the joint action? if your theory says 'obviously not, it has no intentions,' is that a real requirement or just the intentionalist camp's preference — and what does it cost you when a human's entire contribution was delegating her role to it?
- nested crews: a team that is itself one participant inside a larger operation, where the team has nothing it intends over and above what its members intend. can a group be a member of a joint action without having a shared intention of its own?

sort these into real requirements vs negotiable preferences: whether the theory has to work for agents who can't communicate and know almost nothing about each other's plans (so no full common knowledge); whether it has to be built out of individual intentions only or is allowed an irreducibly collective state; whether membership has to be all-or-nothing or can come in degrees; whether it owes a verdict in coerced cases — the junior person who 'intends that we J' only because refusing costs her her job; and whether it has to explain why shared intention carries that distinctive you-owe-me-a-say-before-you-change-course normativity instead of just being more individual intentions with more content.

then tell me which single requirement knocks out the most otherwise decent theories, and flag any pair of desiderata you suspect can't be jointly satisfied. I'd rather have the list turn out to be jointly unsatisfiable than have you quietly drop one of my cases to make it come out clean.

last thing, one short paragraph each on three candidate shapes — describe them rather than name them if you like: (a) shared intention as interlocking individual plans plus mutual responsiveness plus common knowledge, (b) shared intention as an irreducibly collective we-intend that isn't assembled out of I-intentions, (c) shared intention as a joint commitment that neither party can rescind unilaterally. just enough to see where each breaks. the list is the part i care about.
