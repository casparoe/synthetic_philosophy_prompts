I'm preparing a position paper on teleosemantics for a graduate seminar and would like a consistency audit of the package before I defend it. My commitments:

(1) Content is fixed by etiological function. A state represents what the mechanism producing it was selected, over the history of the lineage, to produce states that covary with; and where several properties are equally well correlated with the state's occurrences, the tie is broken by the proper function of the downstream consumer system that uses the state.

(2) I accept the standard swampman verdict: a spontaneous molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a genuine representer has states with no representational content. I take the moral to be that content is historical and extrinsic, not fixed by the system's current intrinsic structure.

(3) I hold content ascriptions to a causal-explanatory demand: they earn their place only by doing work in causal-mechanical explanations of a system's success, not merely by making the system interpretable to us. In practice I also want the notion to stay usable in cognitive science, including for engineered systems, where what we have to go on is largely organisation and training history rather than natural selection.

(4) Misrepresentation has to be a substantive phenomenon the theory grounds rather than one stipulated in: a state's being wrong must be a real fact about it, and I don't want an account on which error is just statistically abnormal functioning relabelled.

(5) Content must reach novel cases. Consider the founder: the first individual in a lineage whose prey-detection mechanism, redeployed from a mechanism with a long selection history for tracking prey of one kind, now covaries with a prey type no ancestor ever encountered and which it successfully catches from its very first trial, with its descendants later selected for tracking the new type. I want to say that this individual's state represents the new prey type rather than the ancestral one.

(6) The history that does the work is actual selection history; I don't want to lean on counterfactual or modal facts about what selection would have favoured had the variant arisen earlier.

I can't locate the fault line myself: each thesis reads fine on its own, and I only get uneasy when I try to apply the set to cases like the founder or to engineered systems. Please tell me which pairs genuinely conflict and which only appear to, rank the real tensions by severity, and for each say what would have to be given up and what that costs me elsewhere in the package. I'd also like a verdict on whether there is a single revision that clears up the most, and if so which one and what I would have to accept in exchange.
