Our lab meeting derailed completely last week and "the sciences carve nature at its joints" got deployed like it settled something. Setup: we were arguing about whether the diagnostic category half the lab works on is a real thing out there or just a useful filing convention. One of the senior postdocs said it obviously isn't, because psychiatry doesn't carve nature at its joints. Then I asked, maybe obnoxiously, whether a set of categories could carve joints for one science and not for another — same population of people, same time, just different sciences asking different questions — and I got told the slogan covers that too: nature has one set of joints, not one per department.

I've been chewing on this since and I cant tell whether we were disagreeing about the world or about a sentence. So: what are the different precise claims that line could be making? I can sort of see a range, from something nobody in the room would deny (some ways of grouping things let you predict more than others) up to something extremely strong (there is one privileged partition of everything and the mature sciences are the ones converging on it), with a bunch of stuff in the middle about hidden essences, about which predicates you're allowed to use in an inductive generalization, about whether a kind needs necessary and sufficient conditions or can be a loose family of overlapping features, about whether joints have to be joints for everyone or only for the science doing the cutting. But I don't trust my own list and I definately can't say which of them is the one people actually mean when they use it to close off an argument.

Can you lay the readings out as a menu I can bring back to the group, with a verdict on each — platitude, defensible but contested, or just false — and then tell me specifically which of them, if any, would actually do the two jobs it was made to do: (a) showing a messy diagnostic category isn't a real kind, and (b) ruling out my "one set of joints per science" question. My suspicion is that (b) only follows under a reading that's false, or at least one nobody in a psych department is entitled to help themselves to, but I want to know what I'm accusing them of.

To make it less abstract, here's the case that made me ask. Same thousand patients. You can sort them by symptom trajectory into five groups, or by which treatment they respond to into three, and the two sortings cross-cut: knowing someone's trajectory group tells you a lot about course and nothing about which drug will work, and knowing their treatment-response group tells you a lot about drugs and nothing about course. Both sortings support tons of prediction inside their own domain. Does "carving nature at its joints" have anything to say about which of those two is the real one, and if the honest answer is "both" or "neither," which reading of the slogan does that answer come from?
