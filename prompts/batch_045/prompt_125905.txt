You are a decision theorist who knows the Kelly criterion and the geometric-rationality/ergodicity critique of expected-value maximisation from the inside, and who can also write comic prose without winking at the reader. I want a piece of sustained satire for a reading group of economists and philosophers: funny to someone who has actually argued about log-optimality, and still legible to a clever non-specialist.

Write it as a mock advice column, "Ask the Growth Optimiser", of the kind that runs in the weekend magazine of a broadsheet. In this world the Treasury has adopted the "Time-Average Standard", and the column is written by an invented pundit, Dr. Ergo, who answers readers' letters. Her doctrine includes the Reappearance Rule: no decision is ever genuinely one-shot, because structurally identical decisions will be faced again by your future selves, your heirs, your institutional successors, or the branches of the multiverse in which you survive — so growth-rate maximisation always applies, and anyone reaching for an expected value has simply failed to find the repetition.

Requirements for the column:

Each letter should compress a real objection into ordinary life, and Dr. Ergo must answer it without ever breaking character or contradicting herself — the consistency is part of the joke. Cover, among others: a reader who will never again see a favourable one-off opportunity (a business for sale, say) and cannot understand why she should stake only a fraction; a reader with a target rather than a horizon (a deposit needed by December) who is told that her target is not a growth rate; a pension fund or mutual insurer with millions of members, where the pooling across people restores exactly the ensemble average Dr. Ergo distrusts; a trust deciding on a once-in-a-century capital purchase; and a closing letter from a reader who points out that maximising expected log wealth looks less like physics and more like choosing one utility function and hoping nobody asks why. Use the standard multiplicative illustration — a repeated gamble that multiplies wealth by 1.5 on heads and 0.6 on tails, which grows the ensemble average by about 5% a round while shrinking the time average by about 5% — and let Dr. Ergo's confidence increase as her advice gets worse.

The humour must come from the theory being right: asymptotic dominance alongside a modest median, volatility drag, fractional Kelly, the refusal of ruin, the myopia of the growth-optimal rule. A decision theorist should laugh rather than wince. Keep it deadpan; the reader must be able to tell what is being ironised without being told. Do not name, cite, or allude to any real researcher, paper, firm or publication — invent everything, including the magazine, and do not smuggle in a bibliography under any heading.

Please write in British English, and take a definite line rather than surveying options. Structure your reply as follows:

Premise (about 80 words): the three or four commitments of the target view you have chosen to push to absurdity, and which one you think is genuinely load-bearing rather than merely convenient.

The column (about 750–850 words): the piece itself, with a short standfirst, four or five letters, and Dr. Ergo's replies.

What I am satirising (about 200 words): committed — state plainly what you take the real vulnerability of the position to be, and what you think its defenders would say in reply.

Where I have taken liberties (about 180 words): where you exaggerated, where you leaned on a contested reading, and one point you deliberately left out because it would not survive the joke.
