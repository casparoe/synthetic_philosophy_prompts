Me and my friend Sam ended up arguing for about three hours last night about whether the regress of reasons is vicious, and I woke up still not sure who won. She's a coherentist, and the thing she kept coming back to was Neurath's boat. The version she read out was:

We are like sailors who on the open sea must reconstruct their ship but are never able to start afresh from the bottom. Where a beam is taken away a new one must at once be put there, and for this the rest of the ship is used as support. In this way, by using the old beams and driftwood the ship can be shaped entirely anew, but only by gradual reconstruction.

Her use of it: there is no dry dock, no moment where you get outside all your beliefs and rebuild from the ground up, so foundationalism is confused at the starting line — and if we have in fact been staying afloat all this time by swapping beams while standing on other beams, then justification from within isn't a desperate fallback, it's the actual structure of knowledge, so coherentism wins sort of by default.

I pushed back on three things and she more or less waved them away, so I want a proper audit.

One: the sea. A boat only floats because of buoyancy, and the water is not a plank. If what holds the whole system up isn't part of the system, isn't the boat picture a foundationalist picture in a coherentist t-shirt — the sea playing the role of experience / the world / whatever the given was supposed to be, and the ship playing the role of the superstructure? She says the sea isn't a belief so it can't be a foundation, which felt too quick to me but I couldnt say why.

Two: the ship is doing quiet work that she never argued for. Neurath has it that a beam taken away has to be replaced at once, using the rest of the ship as support — but that imports a lot: you can't revise everything simultaneously, some bits are structural (you can't swap a keel while you're afloat), and there's a hard external constraint, staying afloat, that isn't itself one of the planks. Which of those correspond to anything in an actual theory of justification, and which are just carpentry dragged in by the choice of metaphor?

Three, and this is the one she really waved away: as far as I can tell, the boat doesn't say whether the chain of support bottoms out in a loop or just never bottoms out at all. A ring of planks each resting on the next, and an endless raft where every plank holds up the one behind it, both look like "no bedrock." So does the analogy favour coherentism over infinitism at all, or is it neutral between them? She says infinitism is obviously silly — an infinite chain of reasons is like paying off a debt by taking out another loan forever, nothing is ever discharged. But my reading was that the infinitist only needs each reason to be in good standing in virtue of the next one, not for the chain to terminate: like a rope ladder hanging from a balloon that rises as fast as you climb, no top rung needed, you just need no rung to snap. Is that a fair picture of what infinitism is committed to, and does the boat — which is entirely about support that never reaches bedrock — turn out to be closer to it than to her view? It didn't help her case that foundationalists sail the same boat: there's a version, I think due to Lorenzen, where you jump into the water and build a raft while swimming, i.e. you can start from scratch after all, and Quine is the reason anyone's heard of the thing. If everybody can sail it, can it be an argument for anybody?

What I actually want: (a) the correspondence table — sea, ship, beams and planks, sailors, driftwood, dry dock, buoyancy, staying afloat — with what each is supposed to be in the theory of justification. (b) Which disanalogies really bite against the conclusion she's using it for, and which are harmless carpentry. (c) Whether the anti-foundationalist conclusion survives once the sea and the buoyancy constraint are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole time. And (d) whether there's a better source domain: I was wondering if one of those tensegrity sculptures — where nothing rests on a base and every strut is held where it is by continuous tension from the others — is a better picture of coherence than a boat, or whether it just hides the same problem one level down. Dont give me citations or a reading list, just argue it out.
