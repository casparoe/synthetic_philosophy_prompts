Ok so here's one I keep going back and fourth on. Einstein died in 1955, which means he missed basically everything that now drives the free will debate, and I want a prediction, not a survey of positions.

Set up what he actually held, no quotes needed. Physics side: thoroughgoing determinist — the God-doesn't-play-dice thing, laws that fix the whole history, no room for an agent to have done otherwise. Agency side: his stock move was the Schopenhauerian formulation, that a man can do what he wills but cannot will what he wills, and he combined that with a flat refusal to convert determinism into a licence to stop blaming anybody — he was perfectly willing to hold people responsible for atrocities while insisting they weren't free in the metaphysical sense. My reading, which I want you to push on, is that these were never really in tension for him because they lived on different levels: determinism was a requirement on the *form* of a physical theory, and the Schopenhauer line was a claim about what deliberation can do and how it looks from the inside, not a rival physics. Call it the split-level Einstein.

Now drop him into three post-1955 things. (1) Bell's theorem plus the loophole-free experiments of the 2010s: the world isn't locally causal, and the two live escapes are nonlocal hidden variables — Bohm's pilot-wave version existed in his lifetime and he thought it was too cheap — and superdeterminism, i.e. giving up the independence of measurement settings from the system being measured, which these days is a real research program and not just a loophole people mention to be thorough. (2) The Conway–Kochen free will theorem from 2006: if the experimenters' choice of what to measure isn't a function of the past, then given some quantum-mechanical and relativistic axioms the particles' responses aren't a function of the past either — whatever freedom you grant the experimenter gets transferred to the electrons. (3) The neuroscience: readiness potentials turning up before reported intention, and the more recent reinterpretation of that as ongoing neural noise crossing a threshold rather than an unconscious decision that has already been made.

So: what happens to the split-level reading under (1)–(3)? Concretely:

- Would he have taken the superdeterminist exit? It saves determinism, but it also turns the experimental confirmation of, well, his own theories into something like a conspiracy of the initial conditions, and my guess is that's exactly the sort of move he'd have called cheap. But is that just my aesthetic guess, or does it fall out of something he actually says elsewhere about what he wanted from a theory?
- Would he have swallowed the Conway–Kochen transfer? My own bet is that he'd deny the antecedent and stay a determinist — determinism applies to experimenters too, so the conditional is vacuously fine — and then I want to know what he says to the person who points out that denying the antecedent just *is* superdeterminism, so he can't have both the cheapness objection and the vacuity reply.
- Or is it (3) that would actually have moved him? The physics only threatens libertarian free will, which he never had. The neuroscience threatens the deliberation-does-something picture that I think he was quietly leaning on, and it threatens it in a way that no amount of grumbling about dice can fix.
- And which would he have found more repulsive: electrons with some kind of freedom, or a universe arranged so that every experimenter's every choice is pre-correlated with the particles they measure? I lean toward him hating both badly enough to insist that free is doing two different jobs in the two places — in which case tell me what he'd say those two jobs are, and whether that answer is compatibilism under a new name or something that doesn't fit the standard menu.

Second bit, shorter, on the religion end, because I think his fans get this wrong. The two posthumous things he'd have to react to are Gould's non-overlapping-magisteria picture — science and religion as separate jurisdictions, each authoritative in its own — and the various attempts to actually build the thing he gestured at, awe at the order of nature as a practice, congregations for people who don't believe in a personal God. My hunch is that he'd have detested the first far more: the religious feeling he described is a response to the success of physics, downstream of science rather than parked beside it, so a treaty that fences it off from science guts the only part of religion he thought was worth anything; and that he'd have found the actual churches embarrassing, in a way that tells you something about whether cosmic religion was ever more than a metaphor for a mood. Sell me on that or take it apart.

Argue with my hunches rather than agreeing with them, and skip the reading list and citations — I just want the reasoning, plus an honest note on where you think there's genuinely no fact of the matter about what he'd have said.
