Me and two other people in a little giving circle I've been in since like 2015 (it came out of an old LessWrong meetup, we're on zoom now) have been going around in circles about where to put this year's ~60k, and I want to force the argument into numbers because I think we're actually disagreeing about three different things and not noticing.

Setup so the question has a truth value: imagine an oracle that in 2045 hands us the true cost-effectiveness of every marginal $1M we could have given, denominated in some welfare unit we all agree in advance to accept as the currency (call them oracle-QALYs, and yes I know the choice of unit is itself doing work, see below). Conditional on that, the proposition I want a number on is:

P: the true expected oracle-QALYs per marginal $1M given to the currently top-ranked *measurable* interventions (the usual global-health shortlist: nets, cash transfers, deworming, vitamin A, the standard GiveWell-shaped menu) is lower than the true expected oracle-QALYs per marginal $1M given to the best *hard-to-measure* thing you'd actually be willing to write a check to — institutional capacity building, policy/movement work, scientific field-building, meta-level stuff, whatever you'd pick.

I'm at about 0.55 and I think thats mostly vibes. One of the others is at 0.8: he thinks the measurability critique is basically decisive, that picking by measurability is picking by a proxy, proxies get Goodharted, and the sign of the resulting bias is reliably negative because the interventions that are easy to RCT are the ones that are simple, short-run, logistically tractable and politically uncontroversial, whereas the real leverage sits in things that are none of those. He also thinks the same bias eats career choice: earning to give selects for legible high-income paths whose counterfactual impact is even less visible than the donation's. The third person is at 0.2 and says the critique is real but second-order: unmeasurable things aren't unbiased, they're just unfalsifiable, and if you let people deploy the same money into unmeasurable stuff you get a different and worse selection effect — you select for whoever tells the best story.

Give me your own number, but mostly the decomposition. Roughly how I'd want it cut up (add terms I'm missing):

- P(measurability is anticorrelated with true cost-effectiveness strongly enough to flip the ranking *at the top of the list*, not just among randomly chosen interventions)
- P(the headline numbers are inflated by measurement and delivery infrastructure that's subsidized from outside the ledger — states, academia, prior philanthropy, "someone already built the distribution network" — so the marginal donor's true counterfactual cost per unit delivered is much higher than the published one)
- P(we're on the far side of diminishing returns *within* the measurable menu: the marginal RCT or the marginal net is worth a lot less than the average one, and the ranking is built out of averages)
- P(the uncertainty on the unmeasurable options is roughly symmetric, versus a one-way ratchet where every layer of "we can't measure it so be conservative" pushes the estimate toward zero for reasons that have nothing to do with the truth)
- P(movement/meta giving has its own Goodhart that's as bad or worse — optimizing for converting people already inside the reference class, i.e. people like us — and that this eats the case for it)
- P(the whole comparison is being run against a do-nothing counterfactual when the actual counterfactual for the money is the rest of the philanthropic and state sector, which is itself unmeasurable)
- P(cross-cause cost-effectiveness comparisons in one currency are coherent at all, i.e. that the oracle's unit isn't hiding the disagreement)

And then two things: (1) which of those terms does the final number actually swing on — where should I spend my limited thinking; and (2) where does the disagreement between the 0.8 guy and the 0.2 guy really live. Is it one of the terms above, or is it upstream of all of them? Also feel free to tell me the framing is broken and the cut isn't where the action is; I half suspect the real distinction is something like "effects that are local and repeatable" versus "effects that are one-shot and path-dependent", which cuts across measurable/unmeasurable.

Context so you don't answer the wrong question: we're not choosing between nets and nothing, we're choosing between a GiveWell-ish grant and a couple of speculative things, and we're fine with high variance as long as EV is higher. Nobody needs to cite studies at each other, we've all read the same blog posts.
