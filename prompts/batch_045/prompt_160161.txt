I am a senior and I run the ethics and philosophy club at my school. We meet for 70 minutes on Thursday afternoons, about fourteen members, mostly ages 16 to 18. The mix matters: three are debate-team people who will argue whichever way the wind blows, four are scientifically minded and will hold the genetics to a real standard, one member is a wheelchair user and has been outspoken about how disability gets discussed in this school, and two or three come from religious backgrounds and will be uncomfortable the moment embryos are discarded. Nobody has read any professional bioethics and I would rather not hand them any, both because it would kill the discussion and because I cannot check sources myself.

Next term we are doing a unit on reproduction and choice, and I want one session built around the position that when prospective parents can choose among the children they could have, they morally ought to choose the one expected to have the best life, and that declining to do so without a good reason is a kind of failing toward the child who results. This is the family of views usually labelled procreative beneficence and liberal eugenics, but I do not want either phrase spoken in the room until the debrief.

Design me a runnable activity for that session. What the group should walk out having felt, not having been told: first, how short the distance is between avoiding a catastrophic condition and maximizing; second, and this is the part I care most about, that the obligation cannot be about making anyone worse off, because in these cases the choice settles which possible person comes to exist rather than how an already-existing person fares, so whatever moral force the principle has is comparative and impersonal, and that is the load-bearing and contestable commitment; third, that parents ought to choose and the state may compel or must fund or must merely permit are three separate claims that come apart.

Constraints on the design. There must be no lecture up front. The members have to build the ladder themselves and then notice where they have arrived. Use physical materials only, index cards and a whiteboard, no devices. Every scenario should involve a couple who has a fixed number of embryos and will end up with the same number of children either way, so the decision only determines which possible person exists; state this in the scenario plainly, without any jargon, so it actually bites rather than being skipped past. At each stage the group makes a forced choice between two cards, then writes on the board one sentence stating the principle they used. The next stage's cards are built so that their own sentence applies to them, and the sentence cannot be revised going forward, only at the debrief. That ratchet is the engine; I want it specified precisely.

The cards must not cheat. No labels like healthy or affected. Give only information about the predicted life: life expectancy, expected pain, expected schooling and health risks, with rough probabilities, the sort of thing an ordinary monogenic test or an ordinary polygenic score would actually give. No dials, no IQ sliders, no science fiction. Every profile should describe a life clearly worth living, including the worse one, so the ranking cannot ride on the thought that nonexistence would be better. At least one profile should be a child who would be deaf or disabled and would have a good life, and the design has to keep the distinction between preferring not to have a child with a condition and judging such a life to be lesser, in a room where that distinction is personal to someone present. Some stages should also make selection cost something the family could otherwise spend on the child they raise, so it becomes visible whether the obligation is overriding or just one consideration among others. The last stage should turn the principle back on the members themselves: their own parents did not select them, so what does their sentence imply about their own conception. Then a final stage asking the same principle three separate questions: may the state forbid refusing to select, must the state fund it, must the state permit it.

Please also be honest about what counts as cheating here. I do not want a stage that simply asserts that one ought to pick the best-off child, or profiles worded so that the verdict is stipulated rather than generated by the members applying their own stated principle to new cards.

What I want back: a prose plan I can actually run. Exact card text for each stage, the board instruction for each stage, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief script with facilitator lines that maps each moment in the room onto the part of the position it is meant to expose, including where each step could have been resisted and what it would have cost to resist there. Then the failure modes: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, including someone bringing up the history of coercive eugenics programs before I have earned the right to address it, a scientifically minded member derailing into the accuracy of polygenic scores, and the debate-team contingent refusing to commit to a sentence. Say how to handle each. Then name plainly what the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself.

After the prose plan, encode the same design as a single JSON object with this schema, with no fields omitted and no extra fields:
