You are a philosopher of biology who has actually worked on the functions literature — not a generalist summarizing a SEP article — so please be precise, and do a bit of unstructured brainstorming before you commit to an answer. I want a principle stated sharply enough that I could apply it to a fresh case on my own, with an honest accounting of where it breaks.

Some background: I'm teaching a graduate seminar on philosophy of biology this spring (it's half philosophy of medicine, and we spend a couple of weeks on function because it shows up in the disease discussion), and I've been trying to write down my own verdicts on a list of cases before I turn my students loose on the literature. Every time I try to state the principle behind them, it comes out as a mess. Here is what I actually say, going by gut: (1) A heart that can no longer pump blood because of disease still has the function of pumping blood — failing at something and not having it as a job are different things. (2) The heart does not have the function of making a thumping noise, even though it reliably does that and even though a cardiologist can use the noise diagnostically. (3) A tiger's stripes have the function of camouflage. (4) A tumor has no function, even though it has plenty of effects, and even though there is selection of a sort going on among cell lineages — although I waffle here about whether I want to say it has a function at the cell-lineage level but not at the organism level. (5) A molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a tiger heart that pops into existence by cosmic accident in a swamp and gets struck by lightning: I want to say it has no function, that nothing is what it is supposed to do. (6) A synthetic bacterium we engineered last year to break down PET plastic, built from a circuit someone designed on a laptop: I want to say breaking down plastic really is its function, even though it has no selection history whatsoever and none of its ancestors ever did anything like that. (7) A mutation that arose two generations ago in a population of moths, which happens to make them harder for bats to echolocate and is currently spreading: I want to say its function already is camouflage, not "nothing yet, ask me again in ten thousand generations." (8) Feathers: the function of feathers in a modern hawk is flight (and insulation), and whatever they were first selected for has either been demoted or dropped. But then (9) the vestigial wings of a flightless beetle on a windswept island: I lean toward saying their function is not flying, or something in that neighborhood, which I can barely say out loud. And (10) the appendix: it now seems to serve as a reservoir of gut bacteria, and I do not think that's its function — but that sits badly with case 7, where I let a function appear almost immediately, and I can't articulate the difference.

The best I've managed on my own is something like: a trait's function is what it was designed to do, where design covers both natural selection and the intentions of engineers, plus a clause letting a trait aquire a new function once selection has recently begun acting on a new effect. That's obviously gerrymandered, and I don't think it handles the beetle or the tumor, and it definitely doesn't tell me how much selection is enough selection.

What I'd like from you: a principle, or a short list of the serious live candidates (the etiological/selected-effects family, the Cummins-style causal-role and contribution accounts, the forward-looking propensity views, the organizational-closure accounts, and anything else you'd put on the table), each stated precisely enough to apply, with a note on which of my cases motivates each clause. For each candidate tell me (a) which of my verdicts it delivers, (b) which verdicts it forces me to give up, and (c) what it says about cases I haven't listed — say, a de novo gene that arose out of junk DNA and is now under selection, a lab-grown organoid that secretes insulin in response to glucose, the human female orgasm, or an organ whose original function is long gone but that is now being maintained by selection for something new. Be straight with me if no single account gets all ten; if so, tell me which verdict is the one I should overturn and what it costs me elsewhere. And please also say whether you think the whole debate is really two different concepts — the physiologist's "what is this part for in this system right now" and the evolutionary biologist's "what is this trait there because of" — sharing one word, because half my students are convinced that's what's going on and I don't yet have a reply I'm happy with.
