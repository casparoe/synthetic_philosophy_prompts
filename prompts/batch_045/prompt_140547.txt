The chapter I was sent (paraphrasing from my notes, so the wording is mine, not the author's) argues roughly this: "Heritability is a number between 0 and 1 that measures how much of a trait is genetic as opposed to environmental. Height in a well-fed population is about 0.8, which means 80% of your height comes from your DNA and 20% from how you were raised. Once a trait is highly heritable it is essentially fixed: you can't rewrite genes, so no amount of policy, schooling, or nutrition will shift it, and the environment only really gets a grip on the low-heritability traits. That's why the heritability of years of schooling, which comes in somewhere around 0.4, is such bad news for education policy — most of the variation we see is already written into children before they walk into a classroom, so interventions are mopping up. It also tells us something about group differences: if a trait is 40% heritable within each of two groups living in the same country, then a good slice of the average gap between those groups must be genetic too. And there's a clean empirical test built into all this. The more we equalize environments — school funding, nutrition programmes, housing — the more we squeeze out environmental variation, so heritability estimates should fall. A study finding that the heritability of school achievement dropped after a big equalizing reform would be about the clearest possible proof that the reform worked." One extra thing that's confusing me: two pages earlier the author says something that sounds right to me, about heritability being a statistic about a population at a particular time and place rather than a fact about you or about the trait itself — and then seems to forget it completely when drawing all the conclusions above.
