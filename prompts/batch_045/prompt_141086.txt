I've been going to the opera for maybe fifteen years and I'm trying to write a short blog-length piece defending it against the standard eye-roll — that it's ridiculous because people sing instead of talking, faint decoratively, take four minutes to die. I want to say something stronger than "it's just a convention, deal with it," and I'd like comments on whether my argument actually holds. Notes below.

1. The usual defence is stylisation: opera is stylised like verse drama or ballet, and stylisation isn't a representational failure. I think that defence is too weak, because it concedes the accusation and then adds that the accusation doesn't matter. My claim instead is that the singing does representational work that speech can't do.

2. The work, I want to say, is temporal. Events move at one speed; what someone feels or decides while they happen moves at another. An aria halts the plot and stretches a single moment of deliberation across several minutes. So opera can display the shape of a passion over time — a resolve forming, wobbling, hardening — which plot alone can only report after the fact. I've been calling this the temporal microscope thing.

3. Corollary: the death scene isn't a lapse in realism (someone dying of consumption does not produce a well-formed melody). It's the character's life being presented as a finished whole, from outside their own time. That's why the lucidity isn't absurd — it isn't a dying person singing, it's a life being totalised.

4. Ensembles: several characters voice incompatible emotions at once and the music makes them consonant. That represents opposition that isn't disagreement — nobody is trying to win, nothing needs resolving. Prose has to turn this into either an argument or a stalemate; music can leave it standing.

5. Where Williams comes in, and the bit I'm least sure of: I want to lean on his general stance that truth in the arts matters but not as correspondence at the level of plot, and on the essay he wrote about immortality and boredom that takes off from Janáček's Makropulos opera, as evidence he treated the form as a source of philosophical material rather than decoration. Then claim the truth of an opera lives primarily in duration rather than in incident.

What I'd like from you:

- Is (2) really about opera specifically, or does it generalise to anything that stretches time — recitative, a soliloquy, a slow-motion film shot, a dance solo? If it generalises, my argument collapses back into the weaker stylisation defence, and I want to know whether it can be narrowed and whether it's worth narrowing.
- Is (3) vulnerable to some obvious objection I'm missing? It feels too neat to be mine.
- A friend says (5) misrepresents Williams: that he'd hate the duration formulation as too metaphysical, and that he was suspicious of grand claims about what art essientially is. Is that fair?
- Overall: is any of this interesting, or has it all been said better already?

Also, please keep clear what's settled among people who actually know this material and what's genuinely contested — don't smooth a live disagreement into a consensus, and say where your own take is a guess.
