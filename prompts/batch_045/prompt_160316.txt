Two positions about responsibility for harms caused by autonomous systems have been arguing past each other for roughly two decades. Call them the control-first view and the redress-first view; define them here only by their commitments, not by any author.

The control-first view holds that blameworthiness is answerable to facts about control, foresight, and the availability of alternatives, so a system whose behaviour is shaped by its own post-deployment learning can produce harms for which no human agent is blameworthy. On this view that is a genuine responsibility gap, and the correct response is upstream: constrain design and deployment so the gap does not open. Concretely, it favours requirements that a specificable human choice — a design decision, a training-data curation, a deployment parameter, an oversight omission — can be shown to be a but-for cause of any harm, and it treats systems that cannot meet this requirement as unlicensable, much as a structure below a safety factor is unbuildable.

The redress-first view holds that responsibility is an institutional practice to be judged by what it achieves — compensation of victims, deterrence of careless deployment, answerability to those affected, organisational learning — and not by whether it tracks a prior moral fact about desert. On this view gaps are artefacts of an over-demanding picture of blame; where they appear we should redesign the practice: no-fault compensation funds, enterprise or collective liability, mandatory insurance, designated-owner duties, audit and reporting obligations. It further charges that control-first requirements tax capability: because traceability trades off against performance, such rules will keep worse systems in service precisely because better ones cannot be traced, and will produce hollow human-in-the-loop arrangements in which an operator signs off on recommendations she has no real capacity to second-guess.

Anchor the disagreement in one decision. A city transport authority must decide whether to renew the licence of a robotaxi fleet whose driving policy is re-estimated nightly from pooled data across all vehicles, so that no individual engineer specified, or could have foreseen, the particular manoeuvre that injured a pedestrian. Two novel instruments are on the table, invented for this decision and not drawn from any existing regime:

1. An attribution floor: the fleet may operate only if, for an estimated share of its decisions above a set threshold, an identified human role-holder's conduct could be laid out as a but-for cause within a fixed evidentiary budget (say, a bounded amount of expert testimony). Harms falling below the floor are unattributable.
2. A mandated dissent rate: operators retained in the loop must override or query the system's proposal at least a set fraction of the time, on pain of losing the oversight credential, on the theory that continuous agreement is evidence of rubber-stamping.

A third element both camps must address: harms that fall into the unattributable pool would be paid from a no-fault fund, and the control-first camp predicts that such a fund will not stay no-fault — that courts, insurers, and the public will re-personalise blame onto whoever is nearest the event (the remote operator, the fleet engineer, the safety officer), a pressure call it scapegoat drift if you find the prediction worth naming.

Draft the joint statement these two positions could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on this licensing decision. Format it as follows.

Section 1 — Agreed claims. A numbered list of substantive propositions both camps accept: about the facts of the robotaxi case; about the distinction between desert-aptness and liability-assignment; about the functions a responsibility practice might serve and how those functions can conflict; about the many-hands structure of the harm; about the hollowness of nominal human oversight; about the traceability-versus-capability trade-off as an empirical matter. Each claim should be strong enough that a reasonable outsider could learn something from it and specific enough that a third camp could deny it.

Section 2 — Disagreements table. Columns: crux; the control-first formulation of it; the redress-first formulation of it; the test, case, or argument both camps agree bears on it; the control-first prediction about that test; the redress-first prediction. Each side's own formulation must be worded so that the other side would accept it as fair, while neither formulation may be watered down into a truism. Include at minimum these cruxes, and merge or split them as you judge best: whether backward-looking blame is an indispensable function of a responsibility practice or a detachable one; whether an enterprise or collective can be a culpable agent when no individual within it is blameworthy; whether the attribution floor is net harm-reducing or net harm-increasing once its effect on which systems get deployed is counted; whether no-fault schemes suffer scapegoat drift and under-deterrence in practice; whether a mandated dissent rate preserves operator competence or degrades outcomes. Where a crux admits no feasible test, say so and specify what kind of argument would bear on it instead.

Section 3 — Verbal and genuine. State plainly which parts of this quarrel are disputes about the word responsibility rather than about the world, and which residual disagreement is genuinely normative or empirical. Justify the partition.

Section 4 — Defeat conditions. For each camp, what finding, case, or argument its own adherents would count as reason to abandon or substantially revise their position, stated concretely enough to be recognisable if it arrived.

Section 5 — Joint recommendation to the transport authority. A course of action both camps can sign, with the residual disagreement recorded as an explicit, flagged divergence rather than smoothed over.

Constraints: argue from the concepts as defined above and from general reasoning about institutions, incentives, and evidence. Do not cite, name, or allude to scholarly works, authors, reports, or legal regimes, and do not supply references, a bibliography, or a reading list. Do not quote anyone. Where you introduce a term of art, define it in place. Predictions must be specific enough to be checkable in principle, and where you judge that a prediction is not checkable, say why.
