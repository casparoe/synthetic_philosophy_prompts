I'm a third-year PhD student (nominally evo-devo, though my lab is really a functional morphology lab) and I have to write the framing chapter of my thesis where I say where I stand on the adaptationism stuff. We also have a reading group working through the Gould–Lewontin spandrels paper and the literature that grew out of it, and the annoying thing is that when I write down what I actually believe, it looks like a pile of commitments that don't go together — except I genuinely can't tell which ones are in real conflict. My advisor says I've just reassembled a standard package and stops there; I don't think that's right, but I also can't locate the problem myself. So here's the list, roughly in the order I think of it:

(1) I hold an etiological, selected-effect view of function: a trait's function is what it was selected to do, and a trait doesn't acquire a function merely from being useful now.
(2) I think the spandrels critique is right as a methodological demand — an adaptationist hypothesis has to be tested against the alternatives (drift, developmental constraint, phylogenetic inertia, byproduct status), and I mean constraint as a genuine explanation, not as a fancy way of saying we don't know yet.
(3) I also think that, as a matter of fact, for a randomly chosen trait in a randomly chosen organism, the best explanation of why it's there will cite selection.
(4) I think reverse engineering and optimality modelling are the right way to find out what a trait is for: write down the design problem, solve for the optimum, compare it to the animal.
(5) I think spandrels and exaptations are a real and sizable phenomenon, not just a methodological caution — I don't want to end up making the dismissive oh-sure-there-are-a-few move and then never let it change a single hypothesis I actually hold.
(6) But I also think that once a co-opted trait starts being modified by selection for its new role, it is an adaptation for that role, full stop: how a trait originated doesn't settle its current status.
(7) I'm a pluralist about levels of selection and about bookkeeping — multi-level selection partitions or inclusive fitness, whichever is less confusing for the case in front of me.

The case that made me notice something is off is my own system: a clade of burrowing skinks (I call them the sand-swimmers in my notes) with enlarged, fused scale patches on the flank just above the hind limb — flank plaques. They appear on the tree at the same node where the lineage shifts to burrowing through coarse gravel; in the lab they have no measurable effect on burrowing speed, abrasion resistance or water loss (I ran all three, and honestly those null results are the solidest data I have); but in the two most derived species the plaques are reshaped into a shallow gutter that channels sand away from the cloacal region, and ablating them costs those species something like fifteen percent of burrowing efficiency, while the basal species don't care at all if you glue theirs shut. My instinct is to say spandrel at origin, adaptation now, in the derived species only — and I've started using a rule of thumb that a trait counts as a spandrel only if selection has never acted on it for any of its current effects, so that spandrel status is a claim about the whole history rather than about the origin.

So please audit the seven. Which pairs actually pull against each other and how badly, which only look like they conflict, and for each real tension what the cheapest thing to give up would be and what giving it up costs me elsewhere on the list. Rank the tensions by severity instead of flagging everything as equally bad, and tell me if there's a single revision that would clean up more than one at once. If my rule of thumb is doing something I haven't noticed — especially if it quietly collapses the distinction I'm trying to protect in (5), or quietly contradicts (6) — I'd rather find that out now than during my defence. Please don't just tell me which combination is the orthodox one.
