I have to teach a single fifty-minute section of an introductory course on action and choice, and my topic is Buridan's ass: an agent facing two options, with no reason to prefer either one, and a reason to take one of the two. The students are first-years who have met the distinction between a reason and a cause and can work out an expected value, but who have not met the suggestion that a rational agent's preferences might be incomplete, or that two options might be comparable while being neither better, nor worse, nor equally good. Please don't write the explanation for me; plan how I should give it.

What I mainly want from you is the dependency structure: which piece has to be in place before which, and what breaks if I reorder it. My worry is that the practical upshot — that there is nothing to deliberate about, you just pick one — deflates the room if it arrives before they have felt the difficulty, and my other worry is that if I hold it back they will conclude I'm withholding a solution. Tell me where it goes and what has to be earned first.

I also want one case to carry the whole session. It has to meet constraints I've been turning over, and I'd like you to say if any of them is wrong: the options must be indivisible and exclusive, so that taking neither and taking both are unavailable; there must be a genuine reason for the agent to take one of them, so that the puzzle isn't dissolved by saying there is nothing to choose for; it must not turn on what the agent does or doesn't know, so that it can't be mistaken for decision under uncertainty; and it should be morally weightless, so that we are arguing about rational choice rather than about which life is more meaningful. Give me the case and say what each constraint is buying me.

There are two puzzles that travel under this name, and I want the plan to take a position on them: the case of exact ties in value, and the harder case where the options are comparable but neither better, worse, nor equally good, so that there is no fact of the matter which to take and no tie to break either. Which one is the fifty minutes about, or are both in it, and what do students lose if I only ever give them the tie?

Then the misconceptions, in the order they will actually surface, with the point in the sequence where each should get defused rather than merely answered. Here are the ones I expect: that this is weakness of will, or the ass being spineless; that it is ignorance, i.e. the ass doesn't know which bale is better; that there is a reason after all, since the ass must eat one of them; that a coin settles it, including the sharp version I got last year, that the expected value of randomizing equals the value of either bale, so randomization is rationally permitted and the problem is over; and that the whole thing is academic, since a real donkey would twitch. I especially want to know how to handle the follow-up to the coin: once it lands, the student says, now I do have a reason for the left bale. Tell me too whether any of my diagnoses above misdescribes the mistake.

One more thing I couldn't answer cleanly last time, and would like placed rather than just answered. A student said: if selecting needs a reason, and every reason to select is already a reason to select the left or the right, then telling the agent to pick doesn't solve anything, it moves the problem up a level, because you now need a reason to pick-left rather than pick-right. Call it the selector's regress. Is that argument sound, or does it rest on a confusion, and if it is a confusion, what should I already have given them so they can see it themselves?

Give me one closing question whose answers would let me tell, on the spot, who has the idea and who has only learned the vocabulary — and tell me what a right answer and a merely fluent wrong answer would each sound like. Also tell me what I should openly refuse to explain in fifty minutes, and where the historical question of who first used the example and what Buridan himself thought should go (my instinct is nowhere, since I can't check it).

Last: I don't want to present contested claims as settled. In your plan, mark what people who work on this would grant without argument and what is a live dispute — I have in mind whether rationality requires complete preferences, whether ties may be broken at random, and whether there really is a third value relation besides better, worse, and equal. For the contested ones, give me the one line I can say to the room instead, so that I'm honest about it without spending the session on it.
