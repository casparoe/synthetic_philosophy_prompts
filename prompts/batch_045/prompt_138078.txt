I'm in an undergraduate seminar this term on the rhetoric of political concepts — we've spent most of it on essentially contested concepts and on how a single term can do several different jobs in an argument at once — and the final paper is a single 3,500-word position piece on some phrase that gets used far more than it gets defined. I've picked "Western values," partly because a couple of us got into an unproductive argument about it after class last week and I'd like to actually settle something. Since I only get one paper, I can't cover the whole topic, and I want to spend the words where an answer would genuinely change what someone should think or say.

Here's roughly where I've got to on my own, so that you can push back rather than start from scratch. The phrase seems to me to slide between at least three readings: an origin claim (these values arose in a particular civilizational lineage), a custody claim (whatever their origin, they're currently best protected or best exemplified by certain actually existing societies), and a content claim (a definite list: something like constitutional democracy, individual rights against the state, freedom of inquiry and speech, equality before the law, toleration). These can come apart — a society can fail the custody claim while fully accepting the list, and an item can be on the list while having perfectly good non-Western genealogies — and I suspect many arguments about "Western values" are really arguments in which the two sides are working with different readings and never notice. I've also been playing with what I've started calling the substitution test: take a typical but invented sentence using the phrase — say, "we must defend Western values," or "human rights are just Western values in disguise" — and substitute the list, or substitute the origin claim, and see what force is lost. Whatever force is lost is the work the adjective was doing. So far it looks to me as though the adjective usually does one of two jobs: it either quietly implies that the values are parochial and therefore optional for others, or it implies that they're embattled and therefore in need of defense — and which of those it implies depends almost entirely on whether the speaker is friendly or hostile, which seems like a suspicious property for a term to have.

So my question is really about allocation. Given one 3,500-word undergraduate paper, which two or three questions about "Western values" should I actually spend it on, and why those? I want the ordering defended rather than asserted: what makes a question worth the words here — does answering it settle other questions, is it tractable at this length with the tools I actually have, is it something people tend to skip over or something people always say and never cash out — and where do those criteria pull in different directions? I'm especially torn on whether the most philosophically interesting question I can see, which is roughly whether the items on the list hang together as a package or are just a grab bag that comes apart under pressure, deserves the space, or whether it's a rabbit hole whose answer wouldn't change anything anyone should say in public. I'd also like to know what would move a question up or down your ranking — what you'd have to find out to reorder. One practical constraint: I'm writing this offline from a few printed handouts, so please don't suggest readings or give me citations I can't chase; just reason it through. Lead with your answer in the very first sentence — the two or three questions and their order — and then give me the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of what I've asked.
