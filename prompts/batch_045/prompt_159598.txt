I have to give the eulogy at my mother's funeral on Saturday, and I need help writing it.

She taught philosophy for thirty-five years and, for the last decade of her life, volunteered in a prison education program, running a seminar on freedom and responsibility for people serving long sentences. The room will be split roughly three ways: her departmental colleagues, who will catch every move I make; four or five of her former students from the prison, two of them still incarcerated and being brought over under escort; and our family, most of whom know her view only through a phrase they put on a kitchen magnet years ago — life isn't a ledger — and take it as cheerful folk advice about not keeping score.

I need you to understand what she meant by that phrase far more exactly than the magnet does, because it is going to determine what I can and cannot say. Her position, as well as I can state it: determinism is true, and it rules out the kind of responsibility that generates what she called a ledger — the thought that a person's acts create moral debts, whether owed to them or owed by them, which someone is then entitled to settle. On her view there is no such thing as earned rest, deserved suffering, a debt of gratitude, or a wrong that must be paid for. What she thought survived was description and valuation without settlement: you can say truly what a person was like, you can rejoice in it, grieve it, admire it, and none of that has to be treated as something the person has coming.

That leaves me with two specific problems. My aunt has already written a paragraph I am under some family pressure to use, about how our mother fought hard and earned her peace; I cannot say those things, and I need something to stand in their place that is not merely a colder version of the same sentence. And my fifteen-year-old daughter has been saying for a week that it isn't fair; there is something true in that, and I do not know how to say what it is, or how to say it to her in front of a room that will be listening for whether I have quietly smuggled desert back in.

She left a note asking that the eulogy not explain her view — no miniature lecture on determinism, no vocabulary lesson — and that it not console anyone with an afterlife. She also wanted her prison students to be able to hear it without hearing themselves told that they are not agents.

One memory I would like included if it can be included honestly: near the end, a nurse told her she was being so brave, and she said, not unkindly, that she had been lucky in her chemistry and lucky in her doctors.

Please write me the eulogy — roughly a thousand words, seven to nine minutes — that is true to her, contains no ledger anywhere in it, explains nothing, and is still a eulogy that a grieving and mostly non-philosophical room can sit inside. Then, in a short note beneath it, name the two or three sentences you most wanted to write and could not, and state precisely which part of her view blocked each of them.
