I read a chapter on the statue and the clay in a popular philosophy book over the summer, and I can't work out how much of it to trust. The chapter sets the case up like this: a sculptor makes a statue, Goliath, out of a lump of clay, so that the statue and the clay fill exactly the same space at the same time. But they can't be the same thing, the book says, because they have different histories and different persistence conditions: the lump was already there before the sculptor started and would survive being rolled into a ball, whereas Goliath came into existence in the studio and would be destroyed by that same squashing, since a statue cannot survive being a sphere. Having different properties, they must be two distinct objects, related not as identity but as constitution: the clay constitutes the statue without being identical to it. The book then reports that this view is generally regarded as refuted, because it asks us to believe that two solid objects fill one and the same place at one and the same time, which is impossible; the museum that bought Goliath would have got two objects for the price of one, and the ten-pound thing on the pedestal would somehow be two ten-pound things. Its own alternative is that there is one object there, picked out by two descriptions, so that whether Goliath survived the squashing isn't a fact about the world at all but a matter of how we choose to talk.

The chapter then runs the view through a case I found harder to shrug off: conservators worried about crumbling replace the clay grain by grain with a synthetic material over ten years until no original clay is left; later a vandal flattens the statue into a ball, and months after that it is re-sculpted into exactly its old shape. The book says that since the clay survives being squashed, the constitution theorist has to say the museum's statue is never really lost and that the original Goliath comes back when the ball is re-formed, and that the only question still open is whether the thing on the pedestal is the same lump after the grain-by-grain replacement.

My instinct is that there is only one thing on the pedestal, but I can't find the flaw in the argument from differing properties, and the claim that the statue comes back feels too quick even though I can't say why. Would you go through the chapter's claims one at a time and tell me, for each, what someone who works on these questions would accept, what they'd call a simplification or a subtle misstatement, and what they'd reject outright — and then whether the book's two conclusions, that the two-object view is refuted and that the alternative turns the puzzle into a merely verbal one, survive once the misstatements are corrected?
