I want a case analysis, not a survey, and I want it run through the Hart–Fuller exchange rather than through the usual textbook summary of it.

The episode is the Mauerschützenprozesse: the German prosecutions, from the early 1990s into the 2000s, of East German border guards who shot people trying to cross to the West, and of the military and political superiors who issued and sustained the orders under which they shot. I take the following to be uncontested, and I stipulate them so that the analysis does not turn on anything you would have to look up. The defence at trial was that the shootings were authorised by East German law — a published provision of the GDR Border Law permitted the use of firearms as a last resort to prevent a border crossing — so that convicting the guards breached the prohibition on retroactive criminal punishment. The answer of the Federal Court of Justice was in two parts: first, that the justification in the Border Law had to be read in a human-rights-friendly way, with a proportionality limit drawn from the GDR's own constitutional text; second, that where the provision resisted such a reading it could not justify the shootings at all, and here the court revived Radbruch's 1946 formula, on which positive law yields only when its conflict with justice becomes unbearable, while a norm that deliberately betrays the equality at the core of justice never had the character of law in the first place. The Federal Constitutional Court, in the mid-1990s, found no violation of the ban on retroactivity; the European Court of Human Rights reached the same conclusion in 2001. The outcomes were graded and in some ways odd: roughly half of the defendants were acquitted, rank-and-file shooters commonly received short suspended sentences, senior officers and members of the political leadership received substantial prison terms, and the highest officials were convicted as indirect perpetrators behind the immediate shooter. The German legal literature was largely critical of the retroactivity reasoning. Finally, the materials before the courts were not one tidy statute: alongside the published Border Law there were higher-level instructions and standing practices, never promulgated in the GDR's law gazette, that pressed guards to prevent escape at any cost. If you think any of this is mistaken, say which item and re-run the analysis without it rather than silently correcting me.

What I want you to work with is a decomposition I have been using in seminar, because I think the debate conflates questions that the case separates. Call them the validity question, whether the norm authorising the shooting was law; the excuse question, whether acting under a legal authorisation defeats criminal liability even if the authorisation is valid; the adjudicative question, what a court of the successor order ought to do with acts of the predecessor order; and the transmission question, what it does to the analysis that the Federal Republic absorbed the GDR by treaty and presents itself as the continuation of a single German legal order rather than as a conqueror replacing it.

Three tasks, in order. First, a disciplined mapping: for each of Hart's and Fuller's positions, say what the guards, the commanders, and the courts were facing — what their options were, what the content of their obligation was thought to be, and what would count as getting it right. Second, say which features of the episode each position actually explains: the graded sentences, the insistence on individual rather than systemic guilt, the striking fact that the men who pulled the triggers did far better than the men who gave the orders, the courts' need to present the invalidity of the East German norm as discovered rather than created, and the fact that so much of the reasoning was an exercise in interpretation rather than in invalidation. Be explicit about where each theory is silent.

Third, and most important to me, run it in the other direction. Does the episode vindicate Hart — the courts arguably did just what he recommended, conceding that this was law and then punishing under honestly acknowledged retroactive legislation, except that they did not concede it — or Fuller, given that the defects of the border regime are precisely defects in what he called the internal morality of law, in particular promulgation and the congruence between declared rule and official action? Or does it show something neither of them contemplated: that the outcome was produced by interpretive and intertemporal doctrine, so that the positivism/natural-law quarrel was adjudicatively inert, a way of arriving at the verdict without ever answering the jurisprudential question? Take a position on which of these three is right and defend it. And while you are there, tell me whether a regime can satisfy most of the desiderata of the internal morality — generality, clarity, prospectivity, stability — while being substantively abominable, since that was Hart's standing challenge to Fuller, and say what your answer does to the case for treating Fuller's morality as a morality of law rather than of efficacy.

No citations, no reading lists, no bibliographies. I know the secondary literature; I am interested in your argument. Around a thousand to thirteen hundred words, and where the theory will not speak to the case, say so plainly rather than forcing it.
