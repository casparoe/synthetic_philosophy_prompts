my reading group got stuck on this and I've started to think the liar paradox is the wrong name for the problem, because I can run the same argument without the word true anywhere in it.

Take a shared notebook that a lab treats as authoritative and cumulative. Two rules, both of which just look like what treating a source as authoritative and cumulative means:

- Release: if a sentence is in the notebook, anyone may use it as a premise without re-deriving it.
- Capture: if a sentence has been derived from notebook entries by the group's normal inference rules, anyone may write it in the notebook.

Now put exactly one sentence on page 7: "If the sentence on page 7 is in the notebook, then the department has infinite money." Call it S. (I'm told you don't even need self-reference here, that the sentence can be built by a coding trick so it never mentions itself and just ends up saying this — so please don't tell me to ban self-reference.)

1. Suppose S is in the notebook.
2. By Release, S itself is available as a premise: if S is in the notebook, then infinite money.
3. From 1 and 2 by modus ponens: infinite money.
4. Discharge the supposition: if S is in the notebook, then infinite money. But that just is S.
5. So S has been derived outright, and by Capture S is in the notebook.
6. From 4 and 5 by modus ponens: infinite money.

No negation, no false, no truth predicate, so the usual move of declaring the sentence neither true nor false — gap, ungrounded, whatever — doesnt obviously get a grip, though maybe I'm wrong and I'd like to know how. Swap the consequent and you get the moon made of cheese; swap the notebook for is true and it collapses into the conditional version of the liar people talk about. One friend says step 5 is where it breaks because step 4 gets used twice — once to get S into the notebook and once as the conditional we detach from — and that this is contraction, so a logic without contraction blocks the whole thing. Another friend says that doesn't save you, because you can rebuild the argument with a predicate for derivable and it comes back even without contraction. I can't tell who is right and the argument itself looks valid to me.

What I want to keep if I can: modus ponens, conditional proof, classical logic for everything that isn't about the notebook, and Release as a wholesale thing — the claim that everything in the notebook is reliable should be statable and assertable without my having checked the entries one by one. That matters to me because its basically how anyone uses testimony, citations, or a textbook at all. What I'd give up without much regret: the thought that being in the notebook, or being true, is a thin transparent property, nothing more than the sentence itself.

So which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If it's Capture, does restricting it really solve anything, or does it just make the general reliability claim unstatable inside the language that has the predicate? If it's contraction, what actually breaks in ordinary reasoning? If it's modus ponens or conditional proof, what's supposed to replace them? If the ungroundedness move does work here even with no negation in sight, what does that cost — do we lose the ability to say, in the same breath, that the pathological sentence isn't in the notebook? And does anything turn on the predicate being true rather than in the notebook, or is this really only about any rule that lets you move freely in and out of a quoted sentence?
