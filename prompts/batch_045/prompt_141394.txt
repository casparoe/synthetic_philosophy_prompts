I used to write pricing software for an insurance company; I now help run a puzzle club and keep a notebook of betting problems. I have a question about a pattern I keep meeting in that notebook, and I am not sure it has a name. To be clear about what I am after: I do not want a reading list or a list of authors and papers — I have no library access, and in any case I am not asking who got there first. I am asking what the thing is, what it is not, and where exactly it runs out.

The background is a game I first met as a puzzle. A fair coin is tossed until it lands heads for the first time; if the first heads falls on the nth toss, then for odd n you are paid 2^n/n dollars and for even n you pay 2^n/n dollars. The payoffs run +2, −2, +2.67, −4, +6.40, −10.67, +18.29, −32, and so on. Weighting each payoff by the probability of its branch, 2^−n, gives 1, −1/2, 1/3, −1/4, …, which added in that order comes to about 0.69. Added in a different order — two gains for each loss, say — it comes to about 1.04; with contrived enough orders it can be made to come to anything, large negative numbers included. So the game has no value, in the only sense of value the puzzle supplies. I gather this is a known curiosity; it is not what I am asking about.

Here is what I noticed. Take that game — call it A — and leave everything alone except that every losing payoff is doubled: the losses run −4, −8, −21.33, −64, … and the gains are untouched. Call this B. B is worse than A in every single outcome: identical when you win, exactly twice as costly when you lose. No adding up is needed to see that. But B has no value either: its weighted payoffs run 1, −1, 1/3, −1/2, 1/5, −1/3, …, whose gains sum to infinity and whose losses sum to minus infinity.

Now suppose you hold B and are offered A in exchange for a fee. The improvement is zero on every winning branch and 2^n/n on every losing branch. Every term is positive or zero, so nothing here can depend on the order in which the branches are taken, and the probability-weighted total is 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/6 + …, which grows without bound. Two conclusions seem to follow, and they pull against each other. The first looks unusually safe: I never valued A and never valued B, I only asked what changes on each branch, and every change points the same way, so the reordering that destroys each game's value separately appears unable to touch the verdict. The second looks absurd: the arithmetic seems to say that any finite fee is worth paying to escape B — a million dollars, say — even though with probability 2/3 the swap does nothing at all, and the chance that it makes any difference you would notice at that fee is on the order of one in fifty million.

Two neighbouring cases, which I take to be boundary markers. If instead I take A and simply charge a dollar to play it, I get a game C that is worse than A by exactly one dollar on every branch; the same branch-by-branch comparison now returns an ordinary, sane answer — the swap is worth one dollar — even though C has no value on its own, for exactly the reason B has none. And if I take A with every sign reversed, I get a game D where the comparison route gives me nothing: A does better on the winning branches, D does better on the losing ones, the branch-by-branch difference is just twice A and has no value either, and when I tried lining the two games' payoffs up from best to worst the verdict kept flipping depending on where I looked.

So what I want a name for is this: the move of extracting a determinate verdict from two options that individually have no value, by comparing them branch by branch — or by lining their payoffs up in order of size — rather than by valuing each and subtracting; and the situation that results, in which there is a firm ranking, no firm magnitude, and, when the total improvement diverges, a recommendation to pay any finite price whatever.

Is there an established concept for the move, or for the situation? If there are several near neighbours — the general phenomenon behind the original puzzle, the various ways of ranking gambles without valuing them, the old worry about prizes large enough to make any price look worth paying — I want to know which fits best and precisely how the case above differs from each, because I suspect it fits none of them cleanly: here the verdict survives the reordering problem even though the separate values do not. If there is no established concept, please say so plainly and then do the carving yourself: give it a name, define it, and set out the boundary cases — when the route yields a finite price, when an unbounded one, when a ranking with no magnitude, and when nothing at all. Three further things I would like settled. Does the improvement's unbounded total count as a value in the same sense in which A was said to have none, or is it a different kind of object? Does the argument need the two games to be played with the same coin, or does it survive if they are played with separate ones? And which half of my second case is the fragile one — the ranking, or the any-finite-fee — since I take it they do not stand or fall together?
