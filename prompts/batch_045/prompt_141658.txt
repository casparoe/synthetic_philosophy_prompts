Our reading group is doing a short series where each of us writes up roughly where we stand on one thinker and then gets poked at by the others. Mine is Marx on exploitation and alienation, and I would much rather find out now that it doesn't hang together than in front of eight people. Here's the bundle, off the top of my head:

(a) I've given up on the labour theory of value — I don't think relative prices are explained by labour times — but I think exploitation talk survives that just fine.
(b) Exploitation is a structural matter: workers are forced to hand over part of what they produce to whoever owns the means of production. No individual contract has to be coercive, the worker can be recieving the full value of their labour power, both sides can walk away better off, and it's still exploitation.
(c) That is the central moral wrong of capitalism. Not inequality as such, not poverty: the forced transfer.
(d) Alienation is a seperate thing and doesn't reduce to exploitation. Roughly four dimensions — the product, the labour process, species-being, other people — and I want to say you could in principle have one without the other, so they're independent axes.
(e) If I'm honest, alienation is the bit I actually find compelling. The complaint I really care about is that work isn't self-directed and that your own capacities confront you as something belonging to someone else.
(f) The fix is on the ownership side: worker-owned co-ops, or social ownership, competing in a market. That's my actual politics.
(g) I'm strongly pro-automation, to the point where I think a nearly fully automated economy is the goal. Note that on my own definition in (b) such a society would have no exploitation either, since there'd be almost no labour left to extract surplus from. One person in the group said that's a reductio of (b); I said no, it's just a consequence I'm happy to accept, and we didn't settle it.
(h) And I hold the old Marxist suspicion that framing any of this as a matter of justice, rights, or fair distribution is ideological and should be avoided.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture. Which of these actually pull against each other, ordered by how much trouble they're really in — not everything flagged as equally fatal. For each genuine conflict, say what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would cost me. Also tell me which pairs only look inconsistent, so I know which ones to stop worrying about. Please don't just tell me what Marx's own view was; I want to know whether my set is stable and, if there's one revision that would clean up the most, which it is.
