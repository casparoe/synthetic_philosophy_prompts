I am revising a paper on the restraint requirement in public reason liberalism, and a reductio I built in order to pressure-test my own view now appears to swallow the view and a good deal more with it. I would like a diagnosis of where it breaks.

Two stipulations first. Call a consideration *uptake-accessible* for a citizen C iff, in a counterfactual deliberation in which C reasons correctly from C's own existing commitments and evidence, C could take that consideration as a reason bearing on the law. Call a law *doctrinally dependent* for C on some premise p iff the best argument available to C for that law contains p as a load-bearing premise.

The argument:

(1) The coercion principle: state coercion is legitimate only if, for every citizen subject to it, there are considerations that justify that coercion to that citizen.

(2) Justification is recipient-relative: considerations justify a coercion to C only if they are uptake-accessible for C. A reason whose force depends on a premise that C reasonably rejects does not justify the coercion to C.

(3) Religious considerations are paradigmatically uptake-inaccessible under pluralism: their force depends on premises — a revelation, the authority of a text, an interpretive tradition — that co-citizens reasonably reject.

(4) So religious considerations cannot do justificatory work in the public defense of coercive law. Note that the familiar proviso — that a citizen may adduce religious considerations provided she also offers political ones "in due course" — does not defuse this, since (2) constrains the justificatory structure of the law and not the order in which speakers happen to present their material.

(5) Symmetry: nothing in (1)–(3) turns on religiousness as such. Secular comprehensive doctrines — maximizing utilitarianism, autonomy as a political value, perfectionist liberalism, structural critiques of the property baseline — rest on premises that are at least as reasonably contested, and are uptake-inaccessible in precisely the same sense.

(6) So, if the argument is sound, restraint is general: comprehensive reasons of every stripe are excluded, and only a thin, freestanding political rationale may justify coercion.

(7) Derivative coercion: the trigger in (1) is coercion, and every state act qualifies, because declining to intervene — enforcing the property and contract baseline, permitting a private employer to dismiss, tolerating a school's curriculum — is as much an exercise of state power as prohibiting is. The restraint requirement therefore attaches to the whole of the state's activity, omissions included.

(8) Self-application: (1) and (2) are themselves contested moral-epistemological claims. Citizens who reject the reciprocity norm — some religious traditionalists, some perfectionists, some democrats for whom legitimate will suffices — have no uptake-accessible reason to accept them. So public reason cannot justify the coercive enforcement of public reason on its own standard.

(9) The evident escape is that public reason is not a first-order moral doctrine but a second-order module specifying the terms of cooperation, and is accordingly self-exempting. But this requires that the module be uptake-accessible to all reasonable citizens, and "reasonable" here cannot be defined as "accepts the module" without circularity, nor as "accepts the burdens of judgment" without importing precisely the contested epistemology of (2). If it is defined empirically instead, as a claim about the citizens who actually exist, then in actually pluralist societies a substantial share reject the module, and (8) returns.

(10) Universality and the translation tax: if restraint is genuinely general by (6) and (7), its burden falls on every citizen whose operative reasons are doctrinally dependent, which is most of them. A requirement most citizens cannot satisfy while reasoning as they actually do is either unlivable or, if enforced selectively against the religious, unfair. And the costs are asymmetric: the religious citizen must find and fund a secular surrogate for her reasons while the secular citizen need not, and the difficulty of that search rises with how thoroughly her doctrine structures her reasoning.

(11) The historical ratchet: for some of the coercions we are most confident were justified — the abolition of chattel slavery, mid-century civil rights legislation — the considerations with any political traction among the citizens who had to be moved were, for many of them, religious. If (4) holds, those laws were not justified to those citizens. If they were justified, then (4) is false or (2) is too strong.

(12) So the restraint requirement is either self-excluding, or arbitrary in its selectivity, or universally binding and unlivable, or committed to calling illegitimate the coercion we are most confident was legitimate. None of these is available to me. Hence one of (1)–(3) is false — and (1) is close to the heart of the position I want to defend.

I would like to keep (1), and I would like to keep some qualified version of (4): that in at least a central range of cases a legislator or citizen owes co-citizens a rationale they can recognise as a rationale, and that bare appeal to revelation does not discharge that debt. I have tried two exits and cannot make either work. The first is to load everything onto "reasonably": it plainly does the work in (2) and (3), but I cannot define it non-circularly, and the more content I give it the more it looks like a comprehensive epistemology of exactly the sort (5) excludes. The second is the convergence reading of public justification — that no single shared rationale is needed, only that each citizen have uptake-accessible reasons of her own — which blunts (5) and (9); but it also seems to let a citizen's own religious reasons back in as justifiers *for her*, and then restraint has dissolved rather than been defended.

So: which step do you reject, and is the failure formal or substantive? Specifically — is there an equivocation in "justified to C" between reasons C could recognise and reasons that are independent of contested premises; a scope shift between per-citizen justification and a single rationale doing justificatory work for everyone; a modal slide between idealized uptake and actual acceptance; or a mistake about the coercion trigger in (7)? Name the step, explain why it looked sound, and price the alternatives: for someone who wants to keep (1) and a modest (4), what does each available rejection cost, and what must she then say about (9), about the translation tax, and about (11)?
