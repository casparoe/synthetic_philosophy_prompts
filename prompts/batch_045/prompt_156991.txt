I'm in an upper-level seminar on the units-of-selection debate, and I tried to reconstruct the case for gene selectionism carefully enough to see what in it is actually load-bearing. My reconstruction seems valid, but it lands somewhere I can't accept, so I assume I've built a bad argument rather than discovered an uncomfortable result.

(1) A trait counts as an adaptation only if it was produced by cumulative selection, and selection requires that the variants present in a population differ systematically in their long-run rates of replication.

(2) Organisms are not replicated. Each generation they are rebuilt from a bottleneck, and sex and recombination scramble their traits. Genes are the things that get copied with high fidelity and that persist, in the form of copies, across many generations — the only real replicators in the system.

(3) So genes are the units of selection; organisms and groups are at most vehicles, and an organismic trait is an adaptation only if having it raised the replication rate of the genes that produce it.

(4) Now take a lineage with no recombination — an asexual clone, or a large inversion that suppresses crossing over — so that the entire genome travels as one non-recombining block. Within such a lineage no gene can out-replicate another gene: every gene in it has exactly the same long-run replication rate, whatever becomes of the organism carrying it.

(5) By the criterion in (1), the unit of selection in (4) is not the gene but the block. Yet nothing about the organisms' design changes: a trait that looked well designed in a recombining relative of the same lineage looks equally well designed here.

(6) So the unit of selection is not the gene as such, but whatever heritable unit happens to carry the variance in replication rate. That makes (3) either trivial — genomes, organelles, colonies and culturally transmitted variants all qualify, so the slogan rules nothing out — or false, since the gene gets no privileged status; and on the first reading it is compatible with the organism-level and group-level views it was supposed to defeat.

(7) But (6) can't be right as stated, because gene-level thinking does seem to earn its keep where the levels come apart. Consider a constructed toxin–antidote element: a stretch of DNA that disables every offspring which fails to inherit it, while rescuing the offspring that do. It spreads even when it reduces the number of surviving offspring produced by the organisms carrying it, and calling it an adaptation of the gene but a disease of the organism seems exactly right — seems, moreover, to depend on the gene being the relevant unit.

I would rather hold on to (2) and to the verdict in (7) than accept (6): the copying asymmetry between genes and organisms looks real to me, and I don't want to give up the toxin–antidote verdict. But I can't say what goes wrong in the move from (2) to (3), or from (4) to (5).

Which step should I reject, and why did it look all right? Please price the alternatives for me — what each rejection costs me the ability to explain. If instead you think the argument is sound and (6) is defensible, tell me what the strongest version of (6) looks like and what it does to the rest of the debate. Finally, say where your own diagnosis is most likely to be wrong, and what argument or case would change your mind.
