Aristotle defines the natural slave as someone who shares in reason only far enough to take it in from someone else, but not to possess it himself. But Aristotle also holds that practical wisdom isn't present at birth — it's built up through habituation and experience — so anyone able to take in reason should be able, given the right upbringing, to come to possess it; and he says the slave is a partner in his master's life. So a good master, on Aristotle's own terms, would be training his slaves out of slavery, and "natural slave" can't name a fixed natural kind — it names whoever never got the chance to deliberate. Since Aristotle himself concedes that enslavement in war is a merely conventional arrangement that many people regard as monstrous, my conclusion is that his principles make natural slavery a curable condition, that his theory is quietly abolitionist, and that the natural/conventional distinction collapses into whatever a slave-owner finds convenient.
