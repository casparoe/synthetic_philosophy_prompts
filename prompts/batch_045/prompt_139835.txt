(a) State Harsanyi's aggregation theorem. Be explicit about: what kind of thing is being ranked; what is assumed about each individual's preferences; what is assumed about social preferences; and what the conclusion says about the weights. (10 marks)

And here is one student's answer, more or less verbatim (including their notation):

There is a fixed finite set of n people and a fixed set of social outcomes. The objects being ranked are lotteries (prospects) over those outcomes. Assume (i) each person's preferences over lotteries satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, so each person i is represented by an expected-utility function u_i, which is unique only up to positive affine transformation — u_i can be replaced by a_i·u_i + b_i with a_i > 0; (ii) society's preferences over the same lotteries also satisfy the vNM axioms; (iii) Pareto: if every individual prefers p to q, then society prefers p to q. The conclusion is that there are numbers w_i ≥ 0, not all zero, such that society's ranking is given by the weighted sum Σ w_i·EU_i(·). So society ranks lotteries by a weighted sum of individual expected utilities — utilitarianism in form.

The boast is that this is utilitarianism without interpersonal comparisons. It makes no sense to compare utility levels or utility gains across people, because each u_i is only defined up to positive affine transformation. But the theorem never needs such comparisons: if we replace u_i by a_i·u_i + b_i, we can just replace w_i by w_i/a_i, and the b_i terms add only a constant to the sum, which doesn't affect the ordering. So the representation is invariant to how we scale or shift anyone's utility function, and the interpersonal comparison question never arises.

It also follows that the weights are not determined by the individuals' utility functions. Since the individual scales are arbitrary, any vector of weights can be turned into any other by rescaling, so on their own the weights carry no information: the theorem pins down the functional form of the social welfare function but takes no stand on distribution. To get a determinate ranking we need something extra. Harsanyi's own version of the argument supplies it: imagine yourself behind a veil of ignorance, equally likely to end up as any one of the n people. A rational, impartial observer maximizing their own expected utility from behind that veil ranks prospects by their unweighted average, and that is the social ranking. Equivalently, add the anonymity condition: permuting people's utility profiles leaves the social ordering unchanged, which forces all the weights to be equal, so society maximizes average utility.

For (c): the worry about indeterminate weights is dealt with by the normalization. Once every person's scale runs from 0 at the common worst outcome to 1 at the common best outcome, the scales are genuinely comparable, and then anonymity really does force equal weights and gives a fully determinate social ordering. And because all we used was a rescaling of each person's own utility function, we haven't smuggled in any interpersonal comparison — that is exactly what the "no interpersonal comparisons" boast consists in. With n fixed it makes no difference whether you say sum or average. The theorem tells us to maximize total welfare, so if the population isn't fixed it tells us to prefer a larger population at the same average — i.e. the repugnant conclusion, which friends of the theorem just have to swallow.

The main objection is that assumption (ii) is doing a lot of work: requiring society's preferences to satisfy independence rules out caring about how the final outcome is actually distributed, and Diamond's ex ante / ex post argument is supposed to show that this conflicts with Pareto applied to realized outcomes.
