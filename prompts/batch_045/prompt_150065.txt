I've been working my way, on my own and without any training in this, through the old paratrooper puzzle in which a soldier is told a *conditional* probability rather than a plain fact — "if you're in Red territory, there's a three-in-four chance you're in the Second Company area" (I've seen it called the Judy Benjamin problem, though I've only read about it secondhand, so please don't lean on the name). I want a case engineered to specifications that forces a disagreement I can't get straight.

The two rules I have in mind: rule A treats the message as ordinary evidence — you imagine a protocol saying how likely the messenger was to send it from each location, then conditionalize on it; rule B keeps your new probabilities as close to your old ones as possible in the standard information-distance sense (minimum cross-entropy, or KL divergence), subject to the reported conditional coming out exactly right.

Please build me a civilian version — a courier, a delivery driver, a lost hiker, anything ordinary, no soldiers or spies — one paragraph, four cells (two towns, each split into two zones), meeting all of this:

1. Nothing distinguishes the towns: each town has the same internal split between its zones, and the messenger's protocol is explicitly town-blind, so that the chance of that message depends only on which zone you're in. Intuitively the message should carry no information at all about which town you're in.
2. Despite that, the two rules must disagree about the probability of the town named in the report by at least fifteen percentage points.
3. Both posteriors must satisfy the reported conditional exactly.
4. Keep it human: ordinary numbers (say, roughly a fifth of each town is riverside; the report is three-in-four), and the implied likelihood ratio in the messenger's protocol should be stated explicitly and stay under twenty — I don't want the case to work only because of a preposterous protocol.

Then: give me the arithmetic for each rule; decompose rule B's adjustment into how much went into shrinking the probability of the antecedent town versus raising the probability of the consequent zone, since my suspicion is that it "satisfies" the report partly by making the antecedent improbable rather than by making the consequent likely; name the single feature of the case doing the work and show what happens if you remove it (for instance, if the prior conditional already equals the reported one); tell me honestly what a defender of whichever rule comes out worse would say — my guess is that rule B looks bad here, since it moves the town probability under a town-blind protocol — and whether you think that reply works. Finally, one line: if the same report arrives twice from independent sources, does the gap widen, hold, or vanish?

I want this for a piece arguing that the divergence is a real feature of the rules and not an artifact of a rigged setup, so the case has to generate the verdicts from the arithmetic rather than stipulate them. If the numbers I've suggested can't do all of this, change as little as you can and tell me what you changed.
