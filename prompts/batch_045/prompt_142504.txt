A guy in my reading group picked this pop-econ book about fairness for us and one chapter is on bargaining: it spends a few pages dunking on the Nash bargaining solution and then pitches the Kalai-Smorodinsky solution as the fix. I copied the key passage out verbatim:

"Nash asked what a fair split looks like and proved there is exactly one answer: pick the deal that makes the product of the two sides' gains as big as possible. His argument rested on a principle called independence of irrelevant alternatives: if you would choose x out of a set of options, then after new options get added you can't switch your choice to some other y. But that principle gives crazy results, and in the seventies Kalai and Smorodinsky fixed it by swapping in a monotonicity requirement instead: enlarging the set of feasible deals, in the sense of giving somebody more options, should never leave a player worse off than before. Their rule is simple. Work out the best payoff each side could possibly get, then pick the feasible point where both sides get the same fraction of that personal maximum -- which is to say, an equal split of the gains. Unlike Nash's solution, this one respects monotonicity, and it also still satisfies independence of irrelevant alternatives; and it has been proved to be the only rule that checks every box on the wish list. Best of all, it requires no comparison of one person's welfare against another's, because each side's share is measured against their own best case. So it works even when the bargainers' utilities are merely ordinal: stretch someone's utility function in any increasing way you like and the recommended deal doesn't move."

The chapter then concludes that "once you do the math, fairness in bargaining just is equal division, and every rival rule is a rationalization."

I'm not a game theorist but two things bugged me. One is that "the same fraction of their own maximum" and "an equal split of the gains" don't sound like the same claim to me unless the situation is symmetric -- my roommate, who took one semester of grad micro, says in the diagram in the book they come out the same so it's a harmless slip. The other is that I vaguely remember it being the other way around: Nash is the one with independence of irrelevant alternatives, and Kalai-Smorodinsky is the solution that was cooked up precisely because they were willing to give IIA up. But maybe monotonicity and IIA are compatible after all and I'm misremebering.
