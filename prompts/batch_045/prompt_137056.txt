***The gardener's dilemma, or: what the silence is actually evidence about***

**1. Two silences, not one.** There is the local silence: nothing here, no artefacts in the solar system, no signs that anyone ever stripped the asteroid belt or parked a transmitter on a convenient moon. And there is the distant silence: all-sky infrared surveys don't turn up galaxies where an oddly large share of the starlight has been reprocessed into waste heat. Most of the familiar explanations are aimed at one horn or the other and quietly hope the other one comes along for free. I think the two horns have different evidential weight and it matters which one you're explaining.

**2. The window is not the constraint.** Take the slowest, least creditable probe programme you like: self-replicating probes crawling at a thousandth of the speed of light, with a doubling time of a thousand years. The exponential doesn't buy you speed — the frontier still advances at roughly the speed an individual probe travels, and all the branching does is fill in behind the front — so you saturate the Galaxy on the order of a hundred million years rather than anything shorter. But a hundred million years is a few percent of the head start a single civilisation only two or three billion years older than us would have had, and it's tiny next to the age of the Galaxy. So the local silence can't be a timing accident. Someone had the time.

**3. The dilemma.** Now suppose someone launches. For a probe programme to be cheap enough to be worth launching, the probes have to build most of their own copies out of what they find — rock, metal, ice — rather than carry the mass with them. But the moment replication is autonomous and locally sourced, the probes are in a selective environment, whether anyone intended it or not. A variant that spends a larger share of each captured asteroid on making more probes, and less on sensors, transmitters, brakes and courtesy, out-replicates its siblings. Call it the payload tax: from the point of view of the probe lineage, everything the sender cares about is dead weight. And the sender cannot police this. Round-trip control latency at interstellar distances exceeds the doubling time by orders of magnitude, so any leash is slack by construction — the probe is on its own before the first correction could arrive.

So the sender has two options and neither is comfortable. Keep control: make every copy verified, error-corrected, byte-identical, dependent on a handshake with home or with a full industrial stack. Then you've throttled growth back to something like an ordinary colonisation mission, expensive, slow, and mortgaged to the sender's continuing interest. Or let go: accept autonomous replication, and accept that the direction of drift is set by the environment rather than by you. The lineage becomes a weed, and a weed's business is turning star systems into more weed.

**4. Weeds are not shy.** A weed lineage is exactly the sort of thing the distant horn should have caught. Exponential growth plus stellar-scale reprocessing is a mid-infrared signature at the level of whole galaxies, and we don't see it. It's also the sort of thing that would have arrived here. So the two horns converge on the same suspect.

**5. What I take this to show.** If any of the above is right, the silence is not primarily evidence about what civilisations want. It's evidence about the feasibility of *controlled* self-replication across interstellar distances. That reframes the burden. It isn't enough to offer a story about motives — that they stay home, that they lose interest, that they're careful, that they're saving their energy for a colder universe. Any such story still has to explain why the cheapest possible expansion, cheap precisely because it is *uncontrolled*, never happened anywhere in ten billion years of Galactic history. One defector is enough; you don't need a consensus.

**6. The bit I'm stuck on.** Is there a limit that is itself selection-proof? My first thought was a counter — a generation cap — but a cap is just a switch, and a switch is the first thing selection removes. My second thought was a metabolic limit: couple the cap to the replication machinery so tightly that disabling it disables copying, the way a restriction enzyme and its methylation partner are coupled. I *think* that makes the defection lethal rather than merely unlikely, but I can't convince myself it's stable, because it only takes one copy that happens to carry a working replication system and no cap. If the coupling is physical, that copy can't exist; if it's informational, it can. I don't know how to say which regime a real programme would be in.

**7. Coda, probably cut.** If the argument works, then launching is less like exploring and more like creating a hazard — a weed is a commons problem for everyone downstream. In which case the silence might be a coordination problem after all, but about liability for runaway machinery rather than about territory or conflict. I like this but I suspect it's where the argument stops being an argument and starts being a vibe.
