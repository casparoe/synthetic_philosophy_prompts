Hi — I'm a retired field hydrologist, thirty years of stream gauges, nutrient budgets and arguing with landowners, and since I retired I've drifted into philosophy. I'm currently auditing a graduate reading group on classical Indian epistemology at the local university, and we just did the Nyāya theory of the pramāṇas — the "sources or instruments of knowledge" business. As I understood the reading: Nyāya standardly recognises four — perception, inference, comparison or analogy (the stock classroom case being someone who's never seen a gavaya but is told it's like a cow, and then picks one out in the forest), and verbal testimony, which the Nyāya authors apparently treat as a genuine source in its own right rather than inference from "she said so, and she's usually right" dressed up. I also gather that rival schools add one or two more, notably some kind of cognition of absence — knowing that the cow is *not* in the barn — which Nyāya supposedly tried to absorb into perception rather than grant separately. I may be garbling that last bit, my notes are a mess, but please take it as roughly what I'm working from and correct me gently if I've got it wrong.

Here's why I care. I volunteer as data-quality coordinator for our state Rare Species Atlas: ordinary people submit sightings of rare plants, dragonflies, mussels, breeding birds, and we vet maybe eight thousand records a month with a rota of six volunteer reviewers, none of whom is a taxonomist, and realistically about two minutes per record because the queue never stops. The output goes into the state's species-of-concern list and gets cited in land-use and conservation-easement hearings, so a bad record genuinely can trigger survey obligations on someone's property, and a missed good record can mean a population nobody knew about gets ploughed under. So the epistemic stakes are weirdly asymmetric and I've been making it up as I go along. What I want from you is a triage protocol my reviewers could actually run: the questions in order, and crucially a step that classifies *what kind of warrant* each record is offering — direct observation, something inferred from habitat/signs/phenology, something recognised by similarity to a field-guide image or a remembered specimen, or just somebody's word — with a rule for what counts as a sufficient instance of each, when a record needs a second, independent source of a different kind before it can be accepted, where the protocol terminates in "unverifiable, set aside" rather than either accepting or rejecting, and where it has to stop and hand the call to a person because the real question is about how much false-positive versus false-negative risk we're willing to run. I'd also like your view on where an automated image or sound identification app belongs in that scheme, since in practice a lot of records are now "the app said so," and it feels like neither testimony nor inference nor analogy quite fits.

Please act as someone who is genuinely expert both in classical Indian epistemology and in how evidential standards are actually written down for real-world data, and think this through carefully rather than giving me a generic data-quality checklist with Sanskrit words glued onto the headings. Before you write the protocol itself, do a short brainstorm — five or six bullets — on which of the Nyāya distinctions actually do work in this setting and which are idle wheels that should be dropped, because I'd rather have a protocol that's faithful to the important part of the theory than one that cargo-cults all four categories. Then write the protocol, as something I could hand to a volunteer with no philosophy and no taxonomy, and run it on three of my real cases: (1) a very sharp, clearly identifiable photograph of a rare moth submitted with no date and no locality, well outside its known range; (2) a beginner's record of a rare marsh bird in the wrong month backed only by a phone app's audio identification; (3) a first-state-record sedge from Dorothy, who has been our single most reliable contributor for twenty years and who lost her phone in the swamp, so all we have is a very careful written description of the plant in her own words. I'll warn you that I have a strong instinct that nothing without a photo should ever count, and I also have a strong instinct that Dorothy is basically never wrong, so I want at least one of these three to come out the way I *don't* want it to, and I want you to say plainly which of my two instincts the protocol forces me to give up, and why. Finally, be honest about where the protocol can't settle things — where a human has to decide, and where running any such protocol will systematically misfire.
