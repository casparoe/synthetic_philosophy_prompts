I am applying for a nine-month MPhil research place (thesis length around 15,000 words), and the application requires a 1,000-word research proposal. I want to work on Bertrand's paradox and the principle of indifference, and I need help turning a half-formed idea into something a selection committee would fund.

The idea, as far as I have it. The familiar dialectic: three apparently indifferent ways of choosing a chord at random yield 1/2, 1/3 and 1/4 for the probability that the chord is longer than the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle, so the principle of indifference seems to give three answers to one question. The two standard reactions are that the problem is simply ill-posed, so that the principle never applied in the first place, and the invariance-based treatment, which holds that a stronger symmetry requirement singles out one of the three. My suspicion is that both reactions share an assumption: they take the principle to range over outcomes — the chords, or the regions of the circle — whereas choosing a chord at random is elliptical for some chance setup's producing a chord. On that reading the natural object of indifference is the mechanism rather than the chord, and the question is whether relocating the principle to mechanisms dissolves the arbitrariness or merely reproduces it one level up.

What makes the relocation worth taking seriously is a small unifying observation. As far as I can tell, all three classical mechanisms are rotationally symmetric, and they differ only in how they distribute the chord's midpoint along the radius: uniform with respect to area, uniform in the radial coordinate itself, and — from my own calculation — a density proportional to (1 − r²)^{−1/2}, induced by averaging two independent uniform points on the circumference. So the symmetry that everyone agrees on does no discriminating work; the whole disagreement reduces to a one-dimensional choice of radial density, and the radius has no canonical measure either. My tentative plan is therefore to treat the space of mechanisms as the object of indifference and to ask whether any non-question-begging higher-order constraint — invariance under a larger transformation group, coherence across concentric circles or across different threshold lengths, closure under composition of mechanisms, insensitivity to how the setup is embedded in the plane — selects a single mechanism, a narrow family of them, or nothing at all. My guess is nothing, and that if so the upshot is stronger than the usual claim that the problem was ill-posed: the failure would attach to the principle itself, because there is no distinguished measure over a space of possible chance setups.

What I need is the proposal document. State the question so that it has a determinate answer and so that it is visibly not the original paradox re-described; explain why the two standard reactions leave it open; set out what I would actually do, phase by phase, over nine months, and why that approach rather than the obvious alternatives (surveying the existing solutions, or developing the measure theory for its own sake); distinguish the possible outcomes — a unique mechanism selected, a narrow admissible family, or a clean negative result — and say what would decide between them; and give an honest account of what would count as failure, including the two ways I can see this going wrong, namely that it collapses back into the familiar invariance debate or that it expands into a technical project I cannot finish in nine months. Please include no bibliography and no references: I will supply those, and I would rather the proposal stand on the argument. Write it for a committee that is not specialised in the area, and keep the formalism to the minimum the argument needs.
