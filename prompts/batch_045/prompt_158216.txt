Been chewing on the dreams-as-experiences thing for a while and I think I've built an argument that probly proves too much, but I can't find the joint. I know the usual Malcolm/Dennett setup so pls skip the recap — here's the chain:

(1) Malcolm's point, which I'm fairly confident about: the only criterion for "she dreamt that p" is her disposition to tell that story on waking. There is no independent check, nothing the waking report could be compared against to see if it got the night right.

(2) Dennett's extension, which I also like: there's no fact of the matter about how many dreams you had last night, when any of them started, or whether what you report on being shaken awake at 4am is the same dream as what you report at 6am. The individuation and the dating are just indeterminate.

(3) If there's nothing that makes it true that the thing happened at t rather than t*, or that there was one rather than two, or that it lasted a minute rather than an hour, then there's no event there to be an experience of. A disposition to produce a narrative has exactly that sort of indeterminacy. So dreams aren't experiences — the dream gets composed at or around the awakening, there's no playback happening during the night. (The cassette-ish picture.)

(4) Generalize: call it the Determinacy Requirement. A state counts as an experience only if there's a fact of the matter about when it occurred, how long it lasted, and how many of them there were.

(5) But ordinary waking experience flunks (4) too. There's no fact of the matter how many conscious visual episodes I had on my walk this morning, or exactly when my perception of the church started, or whether that was one thought or three. Which is roughly the multiple-drafts-flavored point that there's no determinate stream.

(6) So by parity, nothing is ever an experience, waking or dreaming. I'm not willing to pay that, and I'd much rather keep (3) than (4) if I have to choose one.

Two wrinkles I keep getting stuck on, because they're what makes me think the real work in (3) is being done by something other than determinacy:

Wrinkle A. Suppose a lucid dreamer, still in REM, executes a deliberate eye-movement code on request — so the content is contemporaneously available to her report and action systems. If what's really doing the work is "no contemporaneous uptake, so nothing was there for her at the time," then lucid dreams are experiences and her non-lucid dreams from the same night aren't. That looks like a distinction with no difference, but I can't say exactly why it's wrong.

Wrinkle B. A patient under a paralytic during surgery: no uptake at all at the time, no report, no action, no belief updating, and afterwards she reports pain and awareness. Same test says that wasn't an experience either — so nothing happened to her, no harm, nothing to be horrified about. I find that harder to swallow than the conclusion about dreams.

So: which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? If you think (3) survives, tell me what it's really resting on once the determinacy framing is gone. Be explicit about how confident you are in each move you make, and single out the one thing you're least sure of — I'd rather have your actual uncertainty than a clean story.
