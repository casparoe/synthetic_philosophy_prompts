I'm in my school's philosophy club, and two of our members have spent a whole term arguing about Daniel Dennett's three stances, the physical, the design, and the intentional. They have finally agreed to stop arguing past each other and to produce one joint document instead, and I volunteered to draft it. I'd be grateful for your help, because the document is going in our end-of-term zine and will be read by other students, so it needs to be exact without being unreadable.

Here is the dispute as I understand it. We agree on what the stances are: the physical stance predicts what a system will do from its physical makeup and the laws of nature; the design stance predicts from the assumption that it was built to do something; the intentional stance predicts by treating it as roughly rational, attributing beliefs and desires and working out what it should do. The fight is about whether patterns are more than a useful habit of ours. Call one of them the Pattern Realist. She says that when a short description of what a system believes and wants lets you predict a very long stretch of its behaviour, with nothing of interest left over, then that pattern is a real feature of the world and not something we project onto it, so a belief-ascription can be simply true and can sometimes explain the behaviour better than any story about its microphysical parts. Call the other the Instrumentalist. He says that compression is always compression by some scheme, that any stretch of data can be made orderly by one scheme and left disorderly by another, and that a prediction from the physical stance would in principle do all the work, so patterns are instruments for creatures with our computational limits and belief-talk is a convenient fiction rather than a discovery.

Please write the document. Before you do, I'd like you to build in three cases of our own, since the examples in the books are worn out. First, the rebuilt glider: on a grid of the kind used for cellular automata, a shape travels from one side to the other while every cell that makes it up is replaced along the way. Both the physical description and the pattern description track it. Is the pattern the same thing over time, and does the pattern story pick out something the physical story misses, or does it just summarise it? Second, twin compressors: two schemes of equal simplicity compress the same stretch of behaviour equally well, but carve it into incompatible units, one into a single agent with beliefs about locations, the other into two sub-agents negotiating, or into attractions and aversions without beliefs at all. Is there a fact about which carving is the real one, or does pattern-hood become hostage to the choice of compressor, especially given that for any finite amount of data different schemes disagree? Third, the matched-budget prediction match: two predictors get the same computing budget and the same training data, one allowed to use the intentional stance and one restricted to physics, and we score them on held-out behaviour. Both sides should record in advance what result would count for them and what against them, including what to say if the physical predictor wins only after being given vastly more computing power.

I'd like the document to have a numbered section of claims both of them can sign, then a table of the disagreements where each disagreement is stated in a wording its own side would accept, then for each crux the case or argument both agree would bear on it with each side's prediction written down. Please also say plainly which parts of their quarrel you think are really about the word real, and why, and finish by saying what would actually move each of them. Two constraints: no reading lists, citations, or suggestions for further reading, since our library has nothing on this and we can't check anything; and please define any technical term as you go, including what you mean by compression.
