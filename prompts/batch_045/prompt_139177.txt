I teach an upper-level seminar on concepts and analogy, and I am assembling the fourth-week problem set. We have just come through the letter-string material — the copycat-style microdomains where a concept slips its moorings, so that the c which was simply the last letter of a three-letter string becomes, in a different context, the last group of a run — and the students have absorbed exactly one lesson from it: relational structure wins, surface similarity loses. I want a case that makes them unable to repeat that slogan comfortably, and I want it in a form I can hand out on a single page.

The case I have never been able to build myself is the reverse of the usual direction. Design a microdomain and a set of at most four items in it, engineered so that a surface-similarity account — the kind that ranks candidate answers by how closely the change from target to candidate matches the change from source to given answer in surface respects: same length, same number of edited symbols, the edit in the same position, the same position-wise overlap profile, no representation of successor, group, first or last — delivers, on the target item, the answer that most naive solvers would actually give; while a relational account with slippage — predicates like successor, same, first, last and group-of allowed to migrate across levels and across roles — over-reaches into a reading that is structurally elegant and that essentially nobody in the room would produce. Constraints: (i) there must be at least one control item on which both accounts return the same answer, and that answer is the intuitive one, so that the pair isolates a single variable; (ii) no real-world knowledge may be needed — no English orthography, no arithmetic beyond counting to five, and if you use letters, nothing about the alphabet beyond its order and no wraparound from z to a; (iii) if you would rather invent a microdomain than use the letter-string one, define its alphabet and the relations it supports in a single sentence — I have been toying with one myself, patterns of chimes on three pitches (low, mid, high) grouped into runs by beats of silence with nothing above the highest pitch, so feel free to start there; (iv) the disagreement must be generated by one feature of the case rather than by the two accounts being handed different similarity metrics, and you have to name that feature; (v) each item must come with three or four candidate answers, and both accounts must return a determinate verdict on every item, since a shrug or a tie from either one is a failure.

Then show your work. Walk through what each account says about the control item and about the target and why, identify the feature doing the work, and run the removal test: alter or delete that feature and tell me whether the case collapses into an item where the two accounts agree, and if it does collapse, in which direction. Give me the strongest attack available to the defender of whichever account comes out worse — the maneuver that makes the case look rigged rather than merely unfriendly — and say whether you think it succeeds. Finally, tell me what you would print on the answer key and what you would accept as partial credit from a student who saw the other reading and defended it. If you conclude that my requested direction is not jointly satisfiable with (i) through (v), say which constraint has to give and build the best case you can in the opposite direction, flagging clearly that you have reversed it.
