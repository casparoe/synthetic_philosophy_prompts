Hi — I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher who's spent your career on Marx, the philosophy of history and the structure of social explanation. Please take your time with this: do a bit of unstructured brainstorming first about how the options hang together, sketch two or three candidate answers, and only then commit. And please write in British English throughout — I'm a British novelist and American phrasing pulls me right out of it.

Background, briefly: I write historical fiction set mostly in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, and I've got a bit obssessed with a question I can't settle. My characters have ideas — about property, God, the nation, marriage, what a person is — and I keep finding myself saying "yes, the economic base explains that" about some of them and "no, definitely not" about others. I want to know whether there's one principle behind my verdicts or whether I'm just saying yes whenever the thing happens to feel material.

So here are my verdicts, more or less from the gut. For each: does the economic base (the way production is organised, who owns what, who has to sell their labour) explain the item in question?

1. Enclosure. When common and customary use-rights in land give way to wage labour and absolute ownership, my characters really do start thinking about property differently — as a thing you hold outright rather than as a place in a village's arrangements. Yes, the base explains this.

2. Calvinist dissent. No. I don't think the base explains why my weaver is a Calvinist or what he believes about predestination. At a real stretch it explains why Dissent spread where and when it did, but not its content.

3. The clockwork universe. Sort of yes. My natural philosopher describes the world as a machine partly because there are now actual machines — clocks, pumps, mills — and there weren't before. But I don't want to say the base explains whether the physics is true.

4. Contract and legal equality. Yes, and more strongly than anything else on my list. The idea that people are abstract equals who make free agreements with each other looks like a direct export from a society where everyone meets as a buyer and seller.

5. Roman slavery. Yes for the stagnation of technique — no incentive to invest in labour-saving machinery when your workforce is enslaved. No for the fall of Rome; that feels like it has too many other causes.

6. Nationalism. No — or only this much: the base explains why loyalty takes the form of "a nation" rather than "a dynasty", but not which nation, and not the emotional charge.

7. Serialised fiction. Hmm. My 1840s novelist writes in monthly parts with cliffhangers because there's a market for periodicals and cheap print. That's obviously economically explained, but is it the base? Isn't that just "the economy" in a boring sense, and not what anyone means by a mode of production? Feels like cheating to count it.

8. The subordination of women. No, or not fully. It seems to survive big changes in how production is organised, so at most the base explains its current shape, not its existence.

9. Malthus feeding into Darwin. My naturalist reads a gloomy book about population and competition and gets the idea for natural selection. My verdict: no for the biology, yes for why that particular book was lying around to be read.

10. The limits of the thinkable. Yes, and this is the one I care about most as a novelist. None of my characters can properly imagine a world without wage labour, or without a monarch. The base seems to set what's available to think, which matters more to me than whether it decides what they actually end up thinking.

11. What happened after the 1970s — the turn to markets, privatisation, home ownership, that whole package. Yes that something changed; no that the base explains the moral language it arrived in. Though writing that down, I'm less sure than I was.

My own working rule has been: the base explains the menu, not the choice — it determines which forms of thought are live options and which are off the table, but not which one gets picked. The trouble is I also want to say it sometimes does explain the pick (cases 1 and 4 especially), and I can't see what separates those from case 2.

What I'd like from you:

- Two or three candidate principles, stated precisely enough that I could apply them myself, with the clause of each tied to the case that motivates it. If any of them is an established position with a name, tell me the name, but I don't want a reading list or citations — I can't look anything up and wouldn't know what to do with them anyway.
- An honest account of which of my verdicts each principle can't accommodate. I'd rather give up a verdict than have a rule that fudges everything, so say plainly which one has to go.
- I suspect at least one pair of my verdicts is quietly inconsistent with another — probably 6 against 10, or 2 against 9. Find the pair and tell me which way the pressure runs.
- What your preferred principle predicts for cases I haven't decided: (a) whether the base explains why my characters experience time as progress rather than cycle; (b) whether it explains why they think of themselves as having an inner life at all; (c) whether it explains the *style* I write in — free indirect style, all that interiority.
- Finally, the practical bit: as a novelist, am I allowed to read a character's beliefs as an expression of their economic position, or does that cheapen them? What does your answer commit me to about how I write?

Don't just tell me it depends. Give me the strongest single rule you can defend, and tell me what it costs me.
