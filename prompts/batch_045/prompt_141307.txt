After a long argument with a friend about assisted dying, I'd rather get a second opinion on the shape of actual medical practice than on either of our theories.

In nearly every jurisdiction a competent patient may refuse any life-sustaining treatment — a ventilator, dialysis, a transfusion — and the clinician must comply even when the patient is not terminally ill and even when others think the reason inadequate; the patient dies, and this is described as letting the disease take its course, although of course someone has to do something physical, like removing the tube. In those same jurisdictions a clinician who supplies a lethal dose at that patient's request and for those same reasons commits a serious crime; where assisted dying is lawful at all, it carries conditions that withdrawal of treatment does not — a diagnosis with a prognosis, repeated and witnessed requests, waiting periods, exclusion of cases where the suffering is psychiatric rather than somatic, and in several regimes a requirement that the patient perform the final act himself. Meanwhile palliative sedation to unconsciousness until death, which foreseeably shortens life and looks on the surface like the withdrawal case, is treated as ordinary good medicine.

My friend says one principle holds the whole pattern together and I say none does. He argues that the law is only protecting a negative right not to be touched or treated without consent, and that every apparent inconsistency dissolves once you see that a right to be left alone cannot generate a duty on anyone else to help. I think that covers the refusal cases and nothing else, and that the rest is a pile of unrelated things: the acts/omissions distinction, intention versus foresight, some half-belief that a death caused by the disease is categorically different from a death caused by the drug, and an unstated worry about what it does to a profession once a lethal prescription is one of the things a doctor can offer.

So: reconstruct the implicit view — about killing versus letting die, doing versus allowing, intention versus foresight, causation, and the scope of consent — that this pattern of practice would have to presuppose in order to be coherent, as opposed to merely convenient. Say which elements it strictly requires, which merely sit well with it, and which are philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival theories would license the same practice; tell me whether the self-administration requirement and the exclusion of psychiatric suffering are independently defensible or are welded onto the rest; and spell out concretely what would have to change in the practice if my friend's single principle really were the one being applied.
