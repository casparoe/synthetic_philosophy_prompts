You are a professional philosopher who specialises in epistemology and who has spent years designing classroom simulations for upper-level seminars. Work step by step. Begin by brainstorming, in terse note form, at least three different ways one could stage an activity on the material below; for each, write one line on what participants would run into and one line on its principal weakness. Then choose the strongest design and write it up in full. Do not look anything up or reach for the literature: everything you need is characterised in this prompt, and I want a design, not a reading list.

The seminar: fourteen third-year undergraduates, one ninety-minute session, a seminar room with movable chairs, a whiteboard, index cards and nothing else. They have met the Gettier problem and the basic 'S knows that p' idiom, but nothing about stakes, context-sensitivity or pragmatic encroachment.

The distinction I want them to feel is this. Two families of view both explain the familiar bank-queue style of case, and so those cases cannot tell them apart.

- Epistemic contextualism: the standards that 'S knows that p' has to meet shift with the context of the person ascribing knowledge. Make a remote error possibility salient in the conversation and the standards rise; the very same subject, with the very same evidence, counts as knowing in a relaxed context and as not knowing in a demanding one.
- Pragmatic encroachment, or subject-sensitive invariantism: what matters is how much is at stake for the subject herself. The truth of the ascription does not move with the ascriber's conversational context, but it does move with the practical situation of the person who is supposed to know.

The two views come apart only where the ascriber's context and the subject's stakes come apart, which is exactly what I want the group to construct for themselves.

I also want you to build in a third, deliberately unfamiliar position, which you should invent rather than recall. Call it reliance-relative invariantism: what fixes whether 'S knows that p' is true is not the subject's stakes and not the ascriber's context, but the stakes of whoever is going to act on the ascription. Give it the most sympathetic formulation you can, and give the participants a case that discriminates it cleanly from the other two. I am interested in seeing whether a well-designed activity can make an invented view look initially plausible.

Design requirements:

1. Hold the evidence absolutely fixed across every round. Use a single ordinary scenario — I suggest something like working out whether a particular train runs on a Saturday, on the basis of a timetable checked some weeks ago plus a colleague's recollection — and vary only one factor at a time: the subject's practical stakes; the demandingness of the ascriber's context (an error possibility made salient or not); and, for the third view, the size of the stakes borne by a third party who must rely on whatever the ascriber says.

2. Give participants roles and real choices, not a questionnaire. At minimum there should be a Subject, an Ascriber and an Operator. The Operator never hears the scenario: she receives one short handwritten sentence from the Ascriber — something like 'Mira knows the train runs on Saturdays' — and must decide, on the strength of that sentence alone, whether to commit a resource (time, effort, a favour owed, a token stake). Ban hedging: the Ascriber may not write qualifications, probabilities or recommendations. This is what forces the semantics of 'knows' to have practical consequences inside the room.

3. Specify materials, the exact sequence of rounds, a payoff scheme that needs no money (chocolate, or points toward something trivial), and a timeline in five-minute blocks. No lecture up front, and the three views must not be named until the debrief.

4. Describe how the verdicts get collected into a grid on the whiteboard, and what pattern of results would favour each of the three views. Say explicitly which single round carries the most diagnostic weight and why.

5. Write the debrief: how to map specific moments in the activity onto specific features of each view; how to handle the student who says it all just depends on what you mean by 'knows'; how to handle the student who keeps insisting that the subject should simply go and check; and which of the three views you expect the group to find most attractive once the grid is up.

6. Two closing notes. First, an honest list of what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run, with a remedy for each. Second, an honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce: be frank about the fact that it elicits verdicts about a word and about willingness to act, that it cannot by itself distinguish a genuinely semantic shift from a pragmatic or conversational explanation of the same pattern, and that the facilitator will have to supply that distinction in closing. Be equally frank about any feature of the third view that the staging cannot capture.

Write in British English, in a polished but practical register, addressed to me as the instructor who has to run this on Thursday. Aim for roughly 900 to 1,200 words.
