My flatmate did a PhD in some corner of plasma physics, and every time the reading group I'm in — a very informal AI-policy thing, six of us, half engineers — gets onto takeoff speeds, he says the same thing: look, this is just a chain reaction. There's a multiplication factor, call it k: how much more effective research effort one generation of systems lets you bring to bear on building the next one, per unit of human input. Below k = 1 you have a reactor, something that burns but needs tending; above it you have a bomb, and the only thing left to argue about is the doubling time. He uses it pretty aggressively, too: he says the slow-takeoff people are confusing the rate at which a capability gets invented with the rate at which the world adopts it, and that diffusion lag — institutions, procurement, regulation, the sheer time it takes to rebuild anything physical — is a fact about the economy rather than about the explosion, so it tells you nothing about whether the explosion itself is fast or slow.

The problem is that someone else in the group has a competing picture I also can't shake. She says capability isn't a scalar you can hang a doubling time on, it's a vector, and progress in it is gated by whichever complementary input is scarcest: fab capacity, advanced packaging, grid interconnect queues, how long it takes a regulated domain to accept a model's output at all. So the right picture isn't a bomb but a pipeline whose throughput is set by its slowest stage — pour in ten times more compute and you get the same number of finished systems out if the slowest stage didn't move. She has a quantity she keeps using for this, something like the elasticity of useful research output with respect to automated research labour, and says the chain-reaction picture quietly assumes that elasticity is above one everywhere at once, rather than in the narrow slice of work that actually feeds back into building the next generation. When I try to think about it myself I get stuck at the level of: both of these feel like claims about a functional form wearing a metaphor, and I can't tell which features are load-bearing.

So I want an audit of both, and I'd like you to actually do the mapping rather than gesture at it — a correspondence table would be ideal. On his side: what plays the role of critical mass, of the neutron, of the doubling time, of the reactor/bomb distinction. On hers: what plays the role of the stages, the bottleneck, throughput, the queue. Then where each breaks, and specifically what each one imports from its source domain that the target lacks: does the chain-reaction picture smuggle in a single conserved quantity being multiplied, a medium that doesn't deplete, an absence of negative feedback? Does the pipeline picture smuggle in stages that are independent, or a fixed capacity per stage, when in fact the stages are themselves candidates for automation? Which of those disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion each analogy is being used to carry — "timelines are short and diffusion lag is beside the point" versus "growth can't outrun the slowest gated input, so there's no discontinuity coming" — and wich are just decoration? And is there a third picture that does better, or is the honest answer that we don't know whether the relevant quantity is multiplicative at all?

One thing I'd ask you not to do: don't just say both are metaphors and split the difference down the middle. Tell me which one you think is closer on the evidence as it stands, and say plainly how confident you are. Then tell me what would change your mind — what observation, or what number, would actually move you — and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail. My guess is it's whatever you're assuming about how much of the bottleneck set is itself automatable, but you tell me where the weak joint is.
