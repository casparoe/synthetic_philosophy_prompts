My roommate built this argument over dinner and I cant find the hole in it, even though the endpoint is one neither of us accepts.

(1) A system counts as doing ethical reasoning only if it's reflexively revisable: there has to be some criticism it could run into such that it would revise even its most basic ethical starting points, not just its applications of them.

(2) In any trained system — an RLHF'd assistant, a planner tuned against a reward model, whatever — every candidate revision gets scored against the training objective, and the training objective is what fixed those starting points in the first place.

(3) A revision can't be assessed by the very thing it's a revision of.

(4) So no trained system can revise its own starting points.

(5) So no trained system does ethical reasoning, no matter how good it's answers sound.

(6) But our starting points are also fixed by something we can't get outside of — enculturation, plus the intuitions it left us with — and every revision we make gets judged against those intuitions.

(7) So we don't do ethical reasoning either. Which is ridiculous.

I'd like to keep (1), because being able to get your own foundations wrong feels like the whole point of moral reasoning rather than an optional extra, and I definitely want to keep the denial of (7). So I think I have to drop (2) or (3), and I can't tell which, because (3) sounds like it's just true and (2) sounds like a plain description of how training works.

One thing that makes me doubt (2): imagine the system is trained partly on its own earlier outputs, with later versions of the model scoring earlier ones, and you just keep iterating. Then there's no single fixed objective doing the scoring. Does that get out of the argument, or does the regress just move somewhere harder to see?

Which step do you reject, and what do you end up having to accept once you do?
