I would be grateful if you would construct a decision scenario to the specification set out below, and then demonstrate that it meets that specification. Please put your bottom line in the first sentence — whether the specification is jointly satisfiable as stated, and, if so, what the case is — and then give the scenario and the demonstration, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.

Background, so that you can see what would count as cheating. Having retired from a career in experimental chemistry, I now sit in on a philosophy reading group, and we have arrived at bounded rationality. One member maintains that there is no substantive dispute between satisficing and maximizing: search costs are simply costs, an expected-utility maximizer that prices its own deliberation will stop searching once the marginal expected gain falls below the marginal cost, and will therefore follow a threshold rule of precisely the kind Simon described. On his account satisficing is optimizing under honest bookkeeping, and the interest of the literature is sociology. I want a case to put to him, and I do not want one that merely stipulates the result: the verdict has to fall out of the structure of the case.

The specification.

Set the scenario in ordinary professional life — a purchasing decision, a hiring shortlist, a survey of field sites, the allocation of instrument time. No oracles, no idealized urns of numbered balls, nothing that makes sense only inside a textbook. One paragraph of prose. Any quantities you rely on must be of a sort the agent could have looked up or estimated before acting.

One agent under two procedures (or two agents with identical priors, information, cost per evaluation and total budget) faces the same option stream in the same order. They differ only in rule: the maximizer may adopt any stopping rule it can compute within the budget, including an explicitly optimized one; the satisficer evaluates in order and stops at the first option that meets an aspiration level. The satisficer must come out strictly better — a better final outcome, not merely an equal outcome bought more cheaply — and by a margin worth caring about relative to the stakes.

Constraints, which are what make the exercise difficult rather than rhetorical:

1. The maximizer may not be made stupid. It may not be handed a fixed sample size that a competent optimizer would never choose, nor forbidden from doing anything the satisficer does.
2. The aspiration level may not be tuned to the realized stream. It must be settable in advance from information both agents have: an institutional standard, a population statistic, last year's figures.
3. The satisficer gets no extra information, time, or budget.
4. The verdict may not be narrated into existence: no "as it happens, the third option was the best one."

The feature I want carrying the case is what I have started calling measurement drag: sampling an option degrades the reliability of subsequent evaluations — fatigue, sensory adaptation, a consumed sample, a drifting instrument, an interviewer who is sharpest at the start of the day — so that further search is not merely expensive but corrupting. And the drag must apply to deliberation itself, so that the maximizer cannot costlessly compute the optimal stopping rule either; reasoning about how long to reason is itself a degraded, budget-consuming activity.

After the scenario, in order: give the quantities; show what each procedure delivers and why; isolate the single feature doing the work and say what becomes of the verdict when that feature is removed; then give the strongest version of his reply — that a maximizer granted free meta-deliberation simply re-derives the satisficer's rule — with your honest assessment of whether my case survives it, or whether it only ever showed satisficing to be cheap rather than rival. Close with one sentence on which blade of Simon's scissors your case leans on.
