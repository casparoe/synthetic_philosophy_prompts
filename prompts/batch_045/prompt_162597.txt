You are a philosopher of action and decision theory who is also fluent in combinatorial game theory. Work carefully and in stages: before you answer, write out the premises the strategy-stealing argument for Hex actually rests on, and only then test the analogy against them.

In an AI-governance reading group I keep meeting the following move. Someone reasons: strategy-stealing shows that going first with a free stone on the board cannot hurt you — the first player makes an arbitrary move, then follows the winning strategy a hypothetical second player would have had, treating the extra stone as a bonus that is never a liability. So more capability is like more stones on a Hex board: a system with strictly more capabilities than another can always run the weaker system's strategy and use the extras only where they help. Two conclusions are drawn from this: (i) achieved outcome is monotone in capability, so only how much capability a system has matters, not which capabilities; and (ii) alignment can be secured by imitation — let a weak, well-behaved system find a good strategy, then let stronger systems steal it, since they can only do better.

I think the analogy is carrying almost all of the argument, and I want it audited rather than accepted. Please give me the correspondence table — what plays the board, the stones, the extra stone, the opponent, the winning strategy, the absence of draws — and then the places where the mapping to capability, and to settings with other agents who react to it, breaks down. Two worries of my own, which you should not let dictate the structure if the deeper problems are elsewhere: the strategy being "stolen" is a hypothetical one posited for a reductio, and the argument is non-constructive; and the extra stone is a difference of position inside one fixed game, whereas an extra capability can amount to a difference in which game is being played.

Separate the disanalogies that matter for the two conclusions from the ones that do not, say whether (i), (ii), both, or neither survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if the Hex picture cannot carry them, propose a better analogy.
