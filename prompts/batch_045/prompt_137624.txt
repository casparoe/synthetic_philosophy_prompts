I want to ask about something that began, somewhat embarrassingly, with an internet meme. There is a strain of writing in the rationalist corner of the internet that treats returning a shopping cart to the corral as the purest available test of cooperative character: nobody is watching, nobody sanctions you, the store has deliberately placed the corral where using it costs you something, and you will almost certainly never again meet any of the other people in that parking lot. The usual framing is that it is a test of self-governance, which I find thin. What I want is the reconstruction that framing is gesturing at and not delivering.

Take the practice as it actually exists. Supermarkets build corrals, do not monitor them, and do not sanction non-returners; at the same time they pay people to round up abandoned carts, and some chains install wheel-locking systems that stop carts at the lot boundary. One chain I shop at attaches a refundable coin deposit to the cart instead, which is a small bond rather than a norm. So we have, side by side, a norm with no enforcement, a tiny financial incentive, and a hard physical constraint — and, from what I can see in my own parking lot, a compliance rate somewhere between most people doing it and clearly too few for the store to stop hiring someone to clean up after them.

Here is what I cannot make clean. The machinery that does the explanatory work in the evolution-of-cooperation literature — the shadow of the future, reciprocal altruism, indirect reciprocity and reputation, the folk-theorem picture of cooperation as an equilibrium held up by the threat of future defection — seems to have almost nothing to bite on here, because the cart case is close to a one-shot, anonymous, sanction-free prisoner's dilemma. And yet cooperation is common. My own guess is that the practice presupposes something closer to a disposition than an equilibrium: that the compliance is produced by an internalized norm, or by self-signaling, or by an audience that is not really there, rather than by the payoff structure of the game the store built. If that is right, it relocates the explanation away from the game and into norm psychology, and it makes the evolution-of-cooperation story less triumphant than it is usually presented as being: cooperative hardware being run on a game it was not selected for.

So: what does the practice of returning the cart presuppose about cooperation? Reconstruct the implicit theory; separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with; and say which of the rival accounts — internalized norm, strong reciprocity, costly or self-signaling, indirect reciprocity with a phantom audience, evolutionary mismatch, or something duller like inertia or confusion about how little returning costs — would license the behaviour equally well, and which the practice actually rules out. Tell me too what evidence would discriminate among them, since I can generate the just-so version myself.

Finally, what would change your mind: which single finding or argument would most move you off your reconstruction, and where is your reasoning most likely to fail? I am especially suspicious of two moves, and I want you to check yourself on both — assuming an anonymity and a one-shot structure that the situation does not really have, and inferring a cooperative disposition from behaviour that a much thinner mechanism would produce.
