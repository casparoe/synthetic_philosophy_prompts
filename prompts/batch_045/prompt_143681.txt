My grandad landed in Normandy at nineteen and had a small bust of Churchill on the mantel for as long as I can remember. My brother now wants it in a box in the garage. He's been reading about the Bengal famine and about the things Churchill said about Indians and Arabs, and his line is that keeping the bust out isn't nostalgia, it's a claim about who we think was a good man. I'm the one who has to referee this at christmas and I definately don't want to end up just saying it's all very complicated, so I've been trying to get clear on the man-of-his-time defense instead of just feeling about it.

Here's the ask. Take the June 2020 row over the Churchill statue in Parliament Square — rough sketch, and please just treat it as given rather than fact-checking me: during the George Floyd protests someone sprayed graffiti on it saying he was a racist, the government had it covered up, the prime minister said it stays, and the whole thing turned into a general argument about whether statues come down or stay up — and run it through the man-of-his-time defense properly. Not a list of Churchill's worst remarks; I've read those. I want the mapping: what is the defense actually claiming when it gets wheeled out here, which of those claims is load-bearing, and what survives once we know what we know.

The bit I keep tripping over is that it looks like four different arguments in one coat: (1) nobody reasonable back then thought otherwise, so blame is off the table; (2) he was better than the available alternatives and better than his peers, so the comparative verdict is fine; (3) we'd have done the same in his shoes, so we've got no standing; (4) our words just don't apply to him, it's anachronism all the way down. Those come apart — a figure can flunk the first and pass the second, or pass the first and still be someone you wouldn't put on a plinth — so I want to know which one is doing the real work in the Churchill case, and whether the people defending the statue are even running the same argument as each other.

Two things I'd especially like you to chew on. First: the people he was governing obviously thought the things he said were wrong, and plenty of his own contemporaries in parliament said so out loud. Doesn't that kill version 1 stone dead while leaving 2 and 3 completely untouched? And if so, is 2 or 3 actually strong enough to justify keeping a statue up, or are they only good enough to stop us calling him a villain? Second: the Colston statue got toppled by protesters in Bristol the same month and ended up in the harbour, while Churchill got defended from the top of government. Both were men of their time by any normal standard. Does the man-of-his-time defense explain that difference in treatment, or is it just the thing we say about the ones we still want to keep?

Also, I think my brother's real point might be one level up: a mantelpiece or a plinth isn't a verdict on somebody's soul, it's an ongoing decision about what a family or a city asserts in public. So maybe the defense is answering a question nobody asked — it might get Churchill off the hook personally and still leave completely open whether the bust goes in the garage. Is that right? Is there any version of it that touches the commemoration question rather than the blame question? And while you're there: can honoring even be scoped to one part of a life — plaque with the bad bits on it, retain and explain, that sort of thing — or is honoring inherently all-or-nothing? And whose time is his time, anyway: 1940, the 1930s, the 1890s?

Be honest about where the defense goes quiet. If there's a question here it can't answer — what to do, rather than what to think — say that rather than forcing a verdict out of it, and if you think some other frame handles the case better, say which. I'd like to come away with something I can actually say out loud on the 25th that neither starts a fight nor just caves.
