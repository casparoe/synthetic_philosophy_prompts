I'm putting together next term's first-year module on "when does a legend become history", and on day one I want to put my own numbers on the board next to the students' before we've read anything — so I need yours, not anyone else's. Three cases: the Trojan War, King Arthur, the Exodus. No literature survey, no citations, no "scholars disagree": I want bare credences with the machinery showing.

To save you the lookup, stipulate the evidence profile as roughly this. Troy: a destruction layer at the right site in roughly the right period, a city-name in Hittite diplomatic archives that philologists connect to the Greek word for Troy, and no contemporaneous narrative of the war — the Greek texts that tell the story come several centuries later. Arthur: nothing contemporary at all, first clearly datable appearances some centuries after the Roman withdrawal, and no material anchor attached to a person of that name. Exodus: no Egyptian record of the events themselves; the nearest datum is the Merneptah stele (late thirteenth century BCE), which has Israel already in Canaan; the narrative as we have it is later still.

Separate three grades of historicity, because conflating them is what makes this argument go in circles: (1) something of roughly that kind happened at roughly that time and place — call it the event-core; (2) an individual existed who occupied the role the tradition assigns them; (3) the story as we have it is broadly accurate in its main claims. Give me nine numbers, one per case per grade, and say which grade you think people are usually arguing about without noticing.

Then decompose. I want the general terms the nine numbers are built from — some base rate for oral or semi-literate traditions preserving a core across a gap of that length, a prior on the genre, how much weight you give to silence in a well-documented period, whether corroboration of the setting counts as corroboration of the event — with a number or rough range for each and how they combine.

Two things I most want you to get explicit about. First: a Kripke-flavoured one. Suppose the real happenings behind the Trojan tradition turn out to be an Anatolian civil war, or a revolt against a Hittite-backed ruler, rather than a Greek expedition across the Aegean. Does "the Trojan War happened" come out true or false for you? Say which way you're settling it, since a rigid-designator reading and a descriptive reading pull teh number in opposite directions, and it's doing more work here than any of the evidence. Second: how much should the three cases move together at all? Is there one shared term — a general view about how much traditions preserve — that dominates, or are these effectively independent? Give me the common factor and roughly how much weight it carries.

I've also been toying with a rule of thumb I call the two-anchor rule: a tradition only earns a historicity credence above 0.5 if it has two independent anchors, one material and one textual, with an attestation gap under about three centuries; applied strictly it would put all three cases below the line. Tell me how much of your spread across the three cases is really that rule doing the work rather than case-specific judgement, and be honest if you think it's just a prior in a lab coat.

Finish by naming the single sub-question that would move your numbers most if it went the other way, and which of the three cases you're least confident in.

Hard limit: 650 words. A table for the nine numbers is fine and I'd prefer it — and if you have to cut, cut the prose, not the numbers. British English, please.
