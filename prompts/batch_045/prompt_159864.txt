I'm revising a chapter on the ethics of admission, and I've hit a problem I can't see around. My working account — I call it protective substitutability — reads the Refugee Convention as a surrogate-protection device: what triggers international protection is the failure of a state to discharge its protective function toward its own citizens, and the five grounds in Article 1A(2) are best understood as a historically contingent enumeration of the forms that failure characteristically took in 1951. On that reading, the exclusion of people displaced by drought, sea-level rise, or slow-onset environmental collapse is simply a gap in the enumeration, and the repair is obvious: add a sixth ground, something on the order of severe environmental deprivation, so that the protected class once again tracks the class of people whose states have failed them. I've also stopped being impressed by the objection that this would vastly expand the class. A duty doesn't weaken because more people fall under it, and I'd rather bite that bullet than let the contingency of a 1951 drafting choice decide who is protected.

What I can't settle is the downstream question, and I suspect it bears on the first. Nearly everything I read treats the duty to admit as attaching to whichever state the person manages to reach, since jurisdiction is what makes an otherwise abstract duty determinate. But arrival is a matter of luck — geography, smuggling routes, whether a given border is militarized — and it seems to follow that the duty cannot really be assigned by arrival. Yet when I try to assign it by a non-arbitrary criterion instead (capacity, causal contribution to the harm, pre-existing ties, comparative suitability for integration), each one either proves unstable under reflection or generates results I take to be plainly wrong. I've started to wonder whether the question itself is malformed, and whether the honest conclusion is that the duty to admit has no determinate bearer at the level of principle.

Before I commit to that in print: is the sixth-ground repair the right way to extend the Convention's rationale, or does the surrogate-protection reading actually tell against the definitional route I've been taking? And if the duty to admit really is unassignable in principle, what follows for the chapter's claim that states must take refugees in — is that a conclusion I should defend, or a sign that I've misdescribed the duty from the start?
