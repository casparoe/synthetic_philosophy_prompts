I help administer a small discretionary fund attached to the village sports and social club I've belonged to since I was a teenager — roughly sixty members, a few thousand pounds a year, and no written rules beyond an old minute saying to use it for the benefit of the members. Every spring we have the same argument. Two or three members want us to spend until everyone's own reported satisfaction with club life is roughly equal. Another group says that is both intrusive and unmeasurable, and that we should simply give every member an equal claim on the money and let them spend their share as they please. I have been arguing for a third option: that what we should try to even out is not how people feel and not the value of what they can draw, but what they are actually able to do and be — whether someone can play, coach, get to away fixtures, or turn up to the socials at all. I have no training in philosophy, so I may be reinventing something with a name.

Two things about our particular members have convinced me the choice matters. Several of them, after years of being quietly left out of teams and committees, have stopped wanting the things they once wanted and now tell us they are perfectly happy with very little; if we equalized reported contentment we would, it seems to me, systematically reward having given up. And two members have deliberately acquired tastes that are expensive to satisfy — one now fishes only for a particular species abroad — so that equalizing contentment would mean subsidizing them indefinitely, which nearly everyone here thinks is unfair.

The compromise rule I have been drafting says: first spend whatever it takes to bring every member up to a decent threshold of genuine ability to take part, and only after that start worrying about equality at all, giving extra weight to whoever is still worst off. (A friend says we should also use different tests for different parts of club life — one for facilities and equipment, another for whether people feel welcome — and I cannot tell whether that is a cop-out or the honest answer.) Please describe the most important implications of the underlying disagreement — is the thing to be equalized felt satisfaction, the value of what a person can claim, or what a person is genuinely able to do and be? — for a rule shaped like mine, and in particular whether my two-stage rule really is a distinct position or whether it quietly collapses into one of the three once you work out how to set the threshold and what counts as decent. I would also like to know whether my rule inherits the familiar objection that equality cannot be worth pursuing if the only way to achieve it is to make some people worse off without making anyone better off, and whether any of the three pure answers escapes that objection in a way mine does not. Please keep it concrete and aimed at what I should actually write into the rules.
