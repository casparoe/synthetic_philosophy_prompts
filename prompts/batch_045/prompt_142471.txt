I sit, as the technical member, on the Clinical Algorithm Oversight Committee of a large NHS teaching hospital trust, and I should say at the outset both that I am a software engineer by background rather than a philosopher — my only formal exposure to the subject was a first-year logic course — and that I designed and still maintain the monitoring stack wrapped around the model whose fate we decide on Thursday, so I have an interest in the outcome that the rest of the committee does not. I need a short written opinion I can circulate to the committee before that meeting.

The case. Since 2022 the trust has run a sepsis early-warning model over structured electronic health record data — vital signs, lactate, urine output, a few laboratory values — producing a risk score every fifteen minutes. Around it runs an input monitor of the kind we use everywhere in this field: a density model that flags patient-hours whose feature vectors sit far from the training distribution, plus a second check on the distribution of the scores themselves. Flags historically ran around 0.4 per cent of patient-hours and nearly always turned out to be mundane — a new documentation field, a change of units. Since March, flags on two surgical wards have run near 7 per cent, and two things changed on those wards in February.

First, new bedside monitors arrived whose firmware smooths the heart-rate and temperature traces over a rolling two-minute window and clamps artefact spikes. The vendor's position is that nothing about the patients has changed; the physiology is identical, only the measurement of it is different, and the fix is recalibration.

Second, those wards moved to an enhanced-recovery pathway in which patients routinely receive a drug I will call drug X, which attenuates fever and tachycardia. The clinical team's position is that deterioration in these patients now presents differently and is caught later: by the time anyone acts, hypotension and lactate are already abnormal, while the early signs the model was fitted to are simply absent. They call this a different mechanism of deterioration and want the model switched off for these patients. The vendor replies that the terminal pathway — tissue hypoperfusion through to organ dysfunction — is the same one the model was always predicting, that only the upstream route into it has changed, and that recalibration plus a small retrain will suffice.

A small audit of forty-odd flagged cases suggests sensitivity has fallen from roughly 70 per cent to roughly 45 per cent in the flagged subgroup, and that the misses are not scattered at random but cluster in patients on drug X. The numbers are weak and I would not want the memo to lean on them. Complicating everything, our terms of reference permit continued use of a model only where that use is supported by evidence of clinical effectiveness in the population to which it is applied — a phrase the committee has never had to define, and which may or may not be the same question as the one about mechanisms.

What I want is a memo with an actionable bottom line — continue unrestricted, continue with defined restrictions, or suspend for the affected patients — with a time frame and a trigger for review, and with the reasoning that gets there set out so that a clinician or a lay member who disagrees can see exactly which step to attack.

Three things I specifically want handled, because I think our arguments so far have been talking past one another.

I suspect we are conflating three different candidates for the phrase 'the mechanism': the mechanism producing deterioration in the patient; the mechanism the model actually reads, which is a statistical signature in a feature space rather than anything physiological; and the mechanism the monitor watches, which is the data pipeline. Does that distinction do the work I think it does, or is it a muddle?

I keep reaching for an analogy from my own work. In software we distinguish a regression that changes behaviour from one that only changes the input distribution, and we would never call the second a change in what the code does. But a colleague pointed out that behaviour is defined by the tests we chose to write, and the tests encode what we cared about, so on that analogy whether the mechanism is 'the same' would be partly a decision rather than a discovery. Use that if it is sound; dismantle it if it is not.

And I want the memo to say what would settle the question and what could not settle it. If the honest answer is that the committee cannot settle mechanism identity at all and should decide on some weaker surrogate — a decision-theoretic rule, a precautionary default, a definition of population — then say that plainly and give me the weaker thing.

Constraints on the answer. Write it in British English. No citations, references, bibliographies or suggested reading: I cannot follow them up and neither can the committee, and a recommendation that rests on authority none of us can check is useless to us. If you use a term of art, define it in a clause that the two lay members and the nurse on the committee will follow, and if you are tempted to appeal to something ontological, say what practical difference it makes to Thursday's decision or leave it out. Give the losing side at its strongest, not as a foil. Add a short closing section stating what would change the recommendation — the specific finding or argument that would move it — where its reasoning is most likely to fail, and any point at which it rests on a philosophical assumption a reasonable member could reject, together with what we ought to decide if we do reject it. Finally, say plainly what we cannot determine before Thursday. Two pages is the absolute ceiling.
