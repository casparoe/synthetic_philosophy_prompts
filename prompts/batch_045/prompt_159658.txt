Hey — I have to circulate a decision memo before Thursday's board meeting and I'd rather not write it from scratch.

Context: I'm on the board of the Ledger Street Arts Center, a tiny community arts center (six staff, budget around $400k) in a mostly Black neighborhood of a midsize rust-belt city. Last year an alumna of our Saturday youth program died and left us $62,000 with a restriction attached: the money has to go to art that functions as propaganda for Black freedom. Her letter makes clear she got this from Du Bois's argument that all art is propaganda and the only real question is what it's propaganda for — but also, in his version, that it still has to be genuinely beautiful or it isnt art at all, just a leaflet with paint on it.

Two hitches. Our lawyer says that as a 501(c)(3) we can't fund anything that's advocacy for a candidate, a party, or a specific ballot measure. And the city added $18,000 in matching funds, and the city's public-art rules say selections have to be made on artistic merit assessed by a peer panel — which is basically the rival criterion that was floating around Du Bois's own moment, the view that art has to be judged as art and that loading it with a message corrupts it.

We're down to three finalists out of 22:

- The Red Line — a 40-foot exterior mural: a hand-painted 1930s redlining map of our neighborhood with current eviction-filing addresses overprinted in white, plus a QR code linking to a tenants-union hotline. The muralist is good; the design honestly reads like an infographic with a wall around it.
- Nine Portraits — nine six-foot photographic portraits of people who grew up on the block, with headphones and oral histories attached, by a photographer whose work is in a couple of museum collections. Explicit politics: almost none, except that two of the recorded stories are about being pushed out.
- The Rent Chorus — 200 residents each record one sentence answering what does this street owe you; the artist builds a lobby sound installation out of it and prints a broadsheet that gets door-dropped across the zip code. The tenants' union is a partner and wants the participant contact list handed over at the end. There is nothing to look at yet; whether it's any good is unknowable until it's made.

The board is split in a way that feels like it goes straight back to the 1920s: Marva says under Du Bois Nine Portraits can't be propaganda because it never tells anyone what to think, and Theo says The Red Line is the only one that would reach somebody who doesn't already agree, so the other two are just the choir singing to itself.

I've been trying out a distinction to break the deadlock: conversion propaganda, aimed at people who don't yet agree, versus armoring propaganda, aimed at stiffening the resolve and the sense of legitimacy of a community that already agrees. It seems to dissolve some of the fight, but I can't tell whether it's a fair reading of Du Bois or just a way of rigging the outcome I already want.

What I need from you: the memo itself, addressed to the board, ~600 words, that (a) recommends one finalist — or a split of the money, if that's really the better call — with conditions attached where you think conditions are needed; (b) commits to dates: we vote Thursday the 16th, contract signed by the 30th, work installed by October, plus whatever review point you'd insist on; (c) says what to do about the QR code and about the tenant-union data request; and (d) gives the strongest version of the option you're rejecting, stated better than its advocates have managed to state it. My guess is the strongest case is for The Rent Chorus, mostly because it's the only one where the propaganda and the art can't be pulled apart, so please don't wave that one away. Also please say something about how you'd apply a beauty requirement to a work whose aesthetic properties don't exist yet — that one has me stuck.

Write it as something I could actually send: bottom line up front, no philosophy-seminare tone, and definitely no citations or reading lists since none of us are going to look anything up.
