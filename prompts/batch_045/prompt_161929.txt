I'm retired now — I spent most of my working life running a prenatal screening service, and since I stopped I've been reading philosophy in a fairly disorganised way, which is how I stumbled into what I think people call the non-identity problem. Something about our own counselling practice has been bugging me ever since, mostly because I wrote some of the scripts myself.

Two bits of the same appointment, ten minutes apart, same woman, same pregnancy:

- The scan or the test turns up something serious. We offer the option of ending the pregnancy, and we were very careful never to say "if you continue, you will be harming your child". The line we actually used was closer to "this isn't a life we'd wish on anyone" — impersonal, nobody is named as the victim.
- Then: stop drinking, take the folate, get your blood sugar under control. And here we had no hesitation whatsoever — "this will harm your baby." A victim, and someone doing something to them.

What I can't see is how both halves run on the same theory of harm. If the reason we can't say the child is harmed in the first case is that continuing the pregnancy is what makes this child exist at all, then the folate-and-drinking advice looks like it's in the same boat, because changing the timing or the conditions changes which egg and which sperm meet, so it arguably swaps one child for another. And if the reply is "no, that's a different child getting a better start," then I want to know what settles which comparison is the right one — because in the IVF side of the clinic there isn't even a natural "what would otherwise have happened" to measure against; you're choosing among embryos in a dish.

The case that really gets me is the one with no identity-preserving option at all: a woman every one of whose eggs carries the same serious condition, so her options are donor egg, adoption, or no child. Every branch replaces who exists. And that was exactly when our language got most confident that the child shouldn't be brought into it — firmer there than in the ordinary case, which feels backwards to me. I might be misremebering, or defintely missing something.

So: what theory of harm — or of who counts — does this practice presuppose?

Give me exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no preamble and no wrapping-up paragraph:

1. the implicit theory: reconstruct what the two halves of the script are committed to about harm, identity, and who there is to be harmed
2. what the practice strictly has to presuppose versus what it merely fits comfortably with
3. whether there's a principled line between the halves — does it track whether the intervention fixes which child exists or fixes the condition, and what happens in IVF where there's no default outcome to compare against
4. which rival theories would license the very same practice — I want to know where it's underdetermined rather than where it's committed
5. what would concretely have to change in the counselling room if we swapped to the rival: would we have to stop saying "this will harm your baby", and what would we say instead
