I write a science and ideas column for a British newspaper, and I recently sat in on a two-hour session at a workshop on consciousness and fundamental physics where two of the participants argued about the ethical upshot of panpsychism. I have reconstructed the exchange below from my notes, keeping the order of the moves but tightening the phrasing. I am not a philosopher, I could not tell who was getting the better of it, and I do not want to print a garbled version of the argument, so I would be grateful for a careful adjudication.

The dispute in outline. Dr Naomi Ferreira holds that if panpsychism is anything like as plausible as its recent defenders claim, then the amount of experience-bearing stuff in the universe is so vast that even a small chance of unpleasant experience at the fundamental level should make fundamental physics a first-order moral priority, not a curiosity. Dr Tobias Lindqvist holds that the argument never gets off the ground, and that even where it does it gives us no reason to act.

1. Ferreira. Give panpsychism three per cent. Grant that, if it is true, maybe one in twenty versions of it has it that the experiences of fundamental entities carry a negative tone rather than none. There are of the order of ten to the eighty fundamental particles in the observable universe, and each would be a separate centre of experience on the standard constitutive picture. Multiply a small credence by a small conditional probability by an astronomical headcount and the expected disvalue swamps every terrestrial cause put together. So: fundamental physics should be taking a serious share of research funding aimed at reducing suffering, and any result that bears on micro-valence should be treated as urgent.

2. Lindqvist. That calculation has no well-defined input. Under constitutive panpsychism there is no canonical answer to how many subjects a given chunk of matter contains. In you, is there one subject, one per cell, one per particle, or a nested hierarchy that includes all of them? Pick a decomposition and you can make the number whatever you like: the expected value is decomposition-relative. And supplying a counting rule is exactly what constitutive panpsychism has failed to do — that failure is the combination problem, and it is the most serious objection to the view. Until there is a principled measure, this is not an argument with a huge result; it is not an argument at all.

3. Ferreira. I do not need a point estimate, only a floor that survives every decomposition either of us would take seriously. Count only subjects whose experiences are not wholly determined by those of any subject containing them — call them non-subsumed subjects. Even on the meanest such count, the total is astronomical. And notice that the combination problem cuts against you: if micro-subjects fail to fuse into macro-subjects, that means more separate centres of experience rather than fewer. The alarming case is the default case on your own view, not an exotic variant of it.

4. Lindqvist. Suppose I concede you the headcount and a non-zero probability of negative micro-valence. Your argument still only guides action if micro-valence is sensitive to what we do — and the standard motivation for panpsychism suggests it may not be. The motivating thought is that physics characterises matter only by what it does, by its dispositional structure, and leaves the intrinsic nature of the properties unspecified; the panpsychist proposal is that this intrinsic nature is experiential. But if valence attaches to the intrinsic state of a fundamental entity, then the total quantity of valence is fixed by which state-types are instantiated, and physics conserves the bearers: we rearrange matter, we do not create or annihilate the entities or the states available to them. Valence would then be very nearly invariant under anything we could possibly do, and mitigation would be impossible in principle. Call this the invariance objection. Motivation and mitigation pull in opposite directions: the better the argument for panpsychism, the worse the argument for doing anything about it.

5. Ferreira. Two replies. First, a fixed inventory is not a fixed profile: which state-types get occupied, and how often, is precisely the sort of thing intervention changes — heating, compressing, binding and exciting matter all shift the occupancy distribution, and the aggregate at any time is a function of that distribution, not of the inventory. So intrinsic-state supervenience buys you no invariance in the totals. Second, even if you were right about strict invariance, my practical conclusion survives: if the total is fixed and merely unknown in sign, then a measurement that reveals the sign is worth an astronomical amount, because it tells us whether the universe is, in moral terms, a very good place or a very bad one — and that is worth having before we commit resources anywhere else. Call this the diagnostic fallback.

6. Lindqvist. I am willing to concede a modest version of the diagnostic fallback; it is much smaller than the priority claim, and it is not what you opened with. The priority claim needs a negative skew, and you have not earned one. On the intrinsic-state picture the sensible prior is that valence is either absent or thinly and symmetrically spread, not that it is intensely negative. Meanwhile, every account of valence we have any real grip on makes valence an achievement of organisation. In a human being, the states that are intensely unpleasant occupy a vanishingly small and highly structured region of the space of possible states; the generic state is neutral or bland. If that is the right model, then the generic micro-experience is overwhelmingly likely to be neutral, and the astronomical multiplication has nothing to multiply. Call it the rarity of valence.

7. Ferreira. I can break the symmetry, and I do not need organisation to do it. Take a frustration theory of valence: a state is unpleasant to the extent that the system in it is disposed to leave it, and pleasant to the extent that it is disposed to remain in it — which is roughly why pleasure and pain have the functional profiles they do in creatures like us. Now take cosmology: we are in a universe still far from equilibrium, whose contents are overwhelmingly in states they are on their way out of, radiating, decaying, equilibrating. If micro-experience tracks that structure at all, then the aggregate over cosmic time skews mildly but persistently negative — and mildly negative, multiplied by an astronomical headcount, is not mild. That is the asymmetry you asked for, and it costs me nothing I was not already committed to.

8. Lindqvist. It costs you the thing you need. The frustration theory is a relational, functional, organisation-involving theory of valence — that is the same shape as the theory I used to argue for rarity. You cannot lean on its structure to generate a negative skew and then deny that valence depends on how things are arranged. And if you are right, the total is set by cosmological processes we cannot touch; the fraction of it that any human action could in principle affect is negligible. So at most you have an argument for finding out, not for acting — and for finding out only for as long as the answer might be used.

For each of the eight moves, say how strong it is on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and say why — distinguishing moves that are doing real work from moves that only restate a commitment or shift the burden of proof. Then say who, if anyone, is more right overall, and where the real crux lies: which single question, if it were settled, would do most to settle the dispute. Finally, tell me what both of them are missing. I have a suspicion that they are sharing a frame they should both be questioning, and I would like to know whether that is right before I commit it to print. It would also help to know which claims in the exchange a reasonably sceptical reader would accept as uncontested, since I have limited space and cannot footnote everything.
