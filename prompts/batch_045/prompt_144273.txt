I'm a journalist writing a profile, and I have a forty-minute interview on Thursday with a senior metaphysician who has spent much of her career adjudicating proposed solutions to Bradley's regress — the argument from Bradley's Appearance and Reality (1893) to the effect that if a complex such as a's being R to b is built out of a, R, and b, then some further item must connect them, and then something must connect that item to them, and so on without end. Over the years, in talks, in a long survey essay, and in published replies to critics, she has given a verdict on every major family of response. Her verdicts, as I've collected them from her own mouth:

1. The Fregean line: the predicate is unsaturated and the object is saturated, so there is no third thing to be glued on; the concept is a gap that the object fills. Verdict: this works.
2. The tie or nexus line: add one more constituent — a primitive, non-relational tie or nexus of instantiation — whose job is to bind the others, and stipulate that it is not itself in need of binding. Verdict: this fails. Her stock line is that a tie that still needs tying is not an answer but another item on the list.
3. The unstructured line: propositions are not structured entities at all but sets of possible worlds, or functions from worlds to truth values; no constituents, nothing to unify. Verdict: this changes the subject rather than answering the question, and she sets it aside.
4. Bradley's own way out: relations are unreal, the regress shows that relational thought is appearance, and reality is a single non-relational whole. Verdict: this fails; the cure is worse than the disease.
5. The bruteness line: some complexes — facts or states of affairs — are simply primitive unities, not built up out of independently given constituents, and unity is not further explicable. Verdict: this fails; in her words, declaring unity primitive relocates the puzzle rather than solving it.
6. The act-based line: propositions are types of cognitive acts, and the content of a judgement is unified because it is the content of a single act of predicating R of a and of b. Verdict: this works, and she has called it the most promising family of responses.
7. The syntactic-semantic line: propositions are the facts that sentences express, and what distinguishes a proposition from a mere list is the syntactic relation among the parts of the sentence together with the semantic rules that assign values to those parts — rules, not items. Verdict: this works, though she has said the details need to be earned.

The new case is a proposal that postdates her survey and that she has not, as far as I can tell, ruled on. Call it the loop proposal. It holds that propositions are not tree-structured but loop-structured: the unity of a, R, and b is secured by a constituent that is identical with the whole proposition. More exactly, let P be the unique fixed point of the operation that takes a structure and adjoins to it a node binding that structure's elements; on this view P is the structure containing a, R, b, and P itself — a self-containing structure of the kind studied in non-well-founded set theories, where a set is permitted to be a member of itself. The regress, the proposal says, is not endless but circular: at every level the next binder is the very proposition you began with, so nothing new is ever adjoined, and the question what binds the binder to the bound receives an answer rather than a stipulation, namely the structure that the question was about. The proposer adds two further claims: first, that because the relevant fixed point is unique, the view explains why a proposition is one thing rather than a heap — uniqueness does the work that a primitive tie was supposed to do; and second, as a welcome consequence, that unity comes in degrees, since different fixed points can be approached at different rates of convergence, so that some propositions can be more tightly unified than others.
