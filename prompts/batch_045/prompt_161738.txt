I run a tiny reading group on modality — we did Lewis-style counterpart theory a couple of weeks ago, then the Kripke/Plantinga transworld-identity picture — and I got into an argument with a friend afterwards. She says the whole dispute is metaphysical bookkeeping with no cash value: the two accounts only ever disagree on exotic questions like whether I could have had different parents, and on those they just reduce to who's willing to accept essentiality of origin. I think that's too quick, and I want a case to bring back next week.

So: build me a case where the two frameworks come apart, to these specs. One realistic scenario, a single paragraph, ordinary human setting — a fertility clinic's records, a closed adoption file, an immigration paperwork error, a hospital mix-up, whatever you like — but no science fiction: no duplication or fission machines, no time travel, no branching people, no merely possible individuals. In that scenario there should be two claims about the same actual person: a simple de re modal claim (roughly of the form "she could have grown up elsewhere / been someone else's child / ended up with a different career") on which both theories give the SAME verdict, and a second claim — iterated, comparative, or counterfactual-looking — on which they genuinely DISAGREE. The agreement on the first claim is the point of the exercise: I don't want another re-run of the "could I have had different parents" argument where all the work is being done by essentiality of origin, and I don't want a case where one theory shrugs and returns no verdict at all.

The disagreement on the second claim has to be generated by the machinery rather than stipulated, and I'd prefer it to come from a structural feature: e.g. that Lewis's counterpart relation isn't required to be symmetric or transitive, so a counterpart of a counterpart of me need not be a counterpart of me, whereas the transworld-identity theorist is tracking the very same individual across worlds and can chain worlds freely. If the only way you can manufacture a disagreement is by exploiting the context-relativity of which counterpart relation is salient — the move where the counterpart theorist just renegotiates the relation and the disagreement evaporates — then tell me that and say the case is weak. I'd rather have that honestly than a scenario that bakes in the verdicts.

Then do the work: state both claims precisely, give the verdict each theory returns on each and the reason, name the single feature of the case that is doing the work, show what happens to the verdicts if you remove that feature (say, stipulate the counterpart relation is transitive, or impose a uniqueness constraint on counterparts), and finish with both lines of attack — how a counterpart theorist would try to wriggle out, and how a transworld-identity theorist would push back. And if no such case exists under my constraints, tell me which constraint to relax and why; that's a fine answer too.
