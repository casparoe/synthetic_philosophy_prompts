I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm finally writing the long read I've been putting off for a year: whether the whole behavioural-science-in-government thing has a legitimacy problem it hasn't owned up to. I've got a folder of interviews with people who've worked in or alongside government nudge units in three countries, plus one very long argument with a friend who used to work in one of them and is now, in her words, allergic to the word. She made two claims I can't shake. The first: practically nobody in the field actually defends nudging on the grounds that the state knows your interests better than you do — the standard defence is always that the default would have been set by somebody anyway, so the only question is which default. I take the point, but it feels like it proves too much. There's a difference between an ordering that is just what's left over after a procurement contract, a legacy IT system and thirty years of institutional habit, and an ordering that somebody sat down and designed in order to move a number. Call the first residual architecture and the second authored architecture. If the someone-was-going-to-choose argument is doing the work, it looks like it can only license residual architecture and not authored architecture — in which case it licenses almost nothing, since basically every real intervention is authored.

The second thing she said is that their interventions had a shelf life. The ones that depended on the target not quite noticing the mechanism tended to stop working once the mechanism got written up in a newspaper, and her team measured this informally, like a half-life: how many rounds of publicity before the effect was down by half. I want to build something on that, because it connects to a hunch I have about what the real test for a permissible nudge should be. The debate (don't send me citations or a reading list, I'll do my own reporting on that part) spends most of its energy on transparency — whether it's OK if the nudge survives being made public, whether it's OK if people could in principle work out what's happening. But transparency feels like a proxy for the thing that matters. What I want to try out is a mutual exposure condition: a steer is permissible when the two parties are symmetrically exposed to being wrong — the person being steered can get out cheaply, repeatedly, and without having to become an expert, and the institution doing the steering carries a real cost when the steer turns out to be bad for the people it was aimed at (lost enrolment, a complaint route with teeth, a public number that goes down). On this view a long publicity half-life isn't a nice extra feature of a good nudge, it's evidence the condition is met: an intervention that still works once everyone knows about it is one you can keep declining forever, and one whose effect evaporates in daylight was always borrowing its force from the targets not knowing.

So write me the essay I keep not writing. 1200–1600 words, in a register I could actually publish: argue it out in prose, no literature review, no footnotes, no named bibliography. Take the mutual-exposure condition seriously as a candidate answer to when nudging is legitimate, if ever, and weigh it against the main alternatives I can see — the transparency test, the straight does-it-make-people-better-off-by-their-own-lights welfare test, and the flat objection that any intentional steering of another adult's choice is disrespectful whatever the outcome. Two things I specifically want grappled with rather than glided past. One: does my authored/residual distinction actually do any work, or does it collapse on inspection — isn't every residual default secretly authored by whoever declined to change it, and if so does that rescue my friend's first claim or destroy it? Two: the condition looks like it will condemn some interventions nearly everyone thinks are fine and permit some that feel creepy, and I'd rather find that out than be reassured. Automatic enrolment in workplace pensions and default organ-donation rules are the two cases everyone reaches for, so use them — but also apply the test to at least one case I haven't seen discussed much, say a government quietly requiring a benefits or job-centre site to put the apply-now button first and the check-what-you're-entitled-to page three clicks deeper, which helps people who freeze up in front of a form and quietly hurts people who'd have discovered they qualified for more. Reach a verdict: is mutual exposure the right test, a useful but incomplete part of the right test, or a seductive wrong turn — and if it's incomplete, say what exactly has to be added. No fence-sitting at the end, and don't just restate the options.
