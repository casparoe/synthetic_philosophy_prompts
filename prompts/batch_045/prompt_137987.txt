I'm a third-year PhD student in experimental psychology and I've somehow ended up on our department's research integrity committee, which in practice means I'm the one who has to write the paragraph explaining why we're adopting whatever method reform we're adopting this year. I keep tripping over the same thing, so I thought I'd ask people who do this for a living rather than keep guessing.

Here's the bit I can't line up. Take two studies: identical design, identical n, identical measurements, identical p-value, say p = .21 in both. Study A was pre-registered with a fixed sample size and a stated smallest effect size of interest, analysed exactly as declared. Study B is the same study run before pre-registration was mandatory here; the analysis was settled after people had looked at the data, and as far as anyone can tell there was no outright fishing, just no pre-commitment. Under our new norms A counts as a genuine finding, evidence of absence, publishable, and it can be cited in a review as settling the question. B is treated as uninformative, file-drawer material, we can't conclude anything from it. Same number on the page, opposite verdicts.

Then there's the part where I'm not sure we're consistent with ourselves. We're strict about fixing n in advance, and people get told off for peeking at the data and stopping early; the line we give them is that if you'd have carried on past n = 40, you don't get to use the p = .03 you happened to have at n = 40. At the same time, we treat a single pre-registered replication that comes back at p = .40 as a failed replication that puts the original effect in doubt, even though we'd never dream of accepting a single p = .03 as establishing anything. And our ethics committee has now started requiring a smallest effect size of interest in every application, so in principle you can have a confidence interval that includes zero but excludes anything you'd have cared about, and reviewers will say no effect there — but apparently only if the study was pre-registered, and only if someone is willing to say it out loud. Quite a few of my colleagues resist going further and just using equivalence tests with a pre-registered bound, and I genuinely can't tell whether that's methodological conservatism or an inconsistency they haven't noticed.

So: what view of evidence does this whole package presuppose? More specifically,

- What has to be true of evidence for pre-registration to change what a given p-value is worth, given that the data are identical in A and B? Is that a commitment to a procedural or reliabilist picture, on which evidence is a property of the method rather than of the numbers, or is there a way to get the same verdict out of a broadly Bayesian picture, e.g. by conditioning on the fact that this result was reported at all rather than just on the test statistic? If there is, does the practice really pick out the first view, or does it fit several?
- Does the ban on optional stopping presuppose the same thing as the pre-registration bit, or a different thing? It feels like it presupposes that error rates across hypothetical repetitions matter in their own right, whereas the pre-registration asymmetry felt like it was about what the data support. If those are two different commitments, are they even compatible, and which of our rules actually follows from which?
- How much of this is forced? Which parts of our practice would survive intact under a likelihoodist or Bayesian picture, and which would have to be abandoned? If we went properly Bayesian, priors, Bayes factors, decisions by expected loss, what exactly would we stop doing that we currently do, and what would we have to start doing that we don't?
- Is there a genuine inconsistency between treating non-significance as we can't say in a single study and treating it as damning in a replication, or is there a single story from which both fall out?

I don't want a reading list or citations, I can find those myself. I want the implicit epistemology reconstructed, and I want to be told which bits of it we're actually stuck with and which bits we've merely inherited from people arguing in the 1930s. If it helps: we mostly run small-N within-subjects experiments on reaction times and accuracy, nobody has any real idea what our effect sizes are, and half the lab thinks p-values should be abolished outright.
