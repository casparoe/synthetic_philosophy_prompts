In Section X of *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (1748), David Hume defined a miracle as "a transgression of a law of nature by a particular volition of the Deity, or by the interposition of some invisible agent," and proposed what he took to be a standing test for any report of one: when someone tells you of such an event, you must ask "whether it be more probable, that this person should either deceive or be deceived, or that the fact, which he relates, should really have happened," and that "if the falsehood of his testimony would be more miraculous, than the event which he relates; then, and not till then, can he pretend to command my belief or opinion." He hoped the argument would prove "an everlasting check to all kinds of superstitious delusion."

Suppose Hume himself — the Hume of 1748, with his actual views about testimony, causation, and probability, and with no knowledge of anything after his own century — is asked to rule on three evidential situations that his text does not explicitly consider. In each case the question is not merely what verdict he would reach, but *which part of his argument* he would reach it with: Part I's general maxim about balancing contrary evidence, or Part II's empirical claims about the kind of people who report miracles and the circumstances in which such reports arise.

1. **Recorded evidence in an age of cheap fabrication.** A high-resolution video, with synchronized audio and continuous unedited takes, circulates showing a recovery from a documented terminal illness at a large public religious gathering. Thousands were present; several named physicians are on record. But it is now common knowledge that indistinguishable audio and video can be manufactured cheaply by machine, and that this is done routinely for entertainment and fraud. Does such a recording count, for Hume, as testimony at all, or as something closer to the deliverances of sense that he (borrowing Tillotson) treats as the stronger of the two evidences? And does the availability of a mundane causal route to a false record help him — because the falsehood of the report is no longer even mildly miraculous — or does it cut against him, because his whole calculus rests on experience of the usual conformity of reports to facts, a conformity that new technology may be destabilizing for *every* kind of second-hand evidence, miracle reports and ordinary ones alike?

2. **Aggregated statistical evidence.** Instead of eyewitnesses, consider a preregistered, multi-site study comparing recovery rates between patients who are prayed for and patients who are not, which reports a small but statistically significant difference, together with a meta-analysis of a dozen similar studies pointing the same way. Nothing here is a singular marvellous event; the evidence is an aggregate of individually unremarkable outcomes. Hume's Part I is explicitly a calculus of "opposite experiments," of deducting the smaller number from the greater, and of degrees of assurance; would he accept that many individually weak observations can be pooled until they outweigh the uniform experience behind a law? Or does his Part II psychology — the contagious passion of surprise and wonder, the tendency of reports to be correlated rather than independent — give him a principled reason to distrust aggregation in this specific domain? And is a small statistical deviation even the right sort of thing to be a *transgression* of a law of nature, rather than a merely unusual regularity?

3. **Educated contemporary witnesses and rival traditions.** Hume leaned heavily on the claim that miracle stories originate among "ignorant and barbarous nations," at a distance in time or place. But many well-documented modern claims are attested by numerous living, literate, apparently sincere and professionally competent people, and they are documented alongside equally well-documented miracle claims belonging to rival traditions, each of which is used to certify a different revelation. Would Hume shift the weight of his case from the sociology of credulity toward something like a base-rate argument across traditions? And how would he answer the objection, pressed by later readers, that on his own account a law of nature is nothing but a summary of observed constant conjunctions, so that "uniform experience" carries no necessity with it and a violation is not impossible but merely unprecedented?

Write a formal essay of roughly 1,000–1,300 words that does the following. For each of the three cases, say what Hume would most plausibly conclude and, more importantly, reconstruct the reasoning he would use to get there, citing the relevant features of his actual position rather than restating the text. Then reach an overall verdict: which of Hume's conclusions survive contact with these cases essentially unchanged, which would have to be restated in terms he did not have available, and whether he would regard the modern evidential environment as vindicating his "everlasting check" or as exposing a soft spot in it. Where you think Hume's own commitments pull in two directions, say so and explain which pull is stronger and why. Do not pad the essay with summaries of scholarship or with textual exegesis for its own sake, and do not appeal to sources beyond the Section X material quoted above; the interest lies in how far that argument can be made to travel.
