I am teaching a seventy-five-minute session on informational cascades and rational herding to the second-year undergraduates in my Philosophy and Public Policy course: roughly forty of them, drawn from philosophy, economics and politics, who have had a term of probability — they can read a likelihood ratio and move between odds and probabilities without flinching — but no economics and nothing in social epistemology, and who will have been assigned nothing for this session. I do not need the explanation itself; I need a plan for delivering it.

What I want the session to turn on is a claim I have become convinced of but have never managed to teach cleanly: that the engine of a cascade is not irrationality, deference or social pressure but the compression of a private signal into a publicly legible action, so that what is lost downstream is not anyone's reasoning but the evidential strength, and the remedy is therefore structural rather than exhortatory — aggregate likelihood ratios, not recommendations. I want that claim to be the spine of the session, not a moral I announce at the end.

So, first: in what order must the pieces come, and what exactly depends on what? Must they have the notion of an independent private signal of fixed and moderate accuracy before they watch anyone move in sequence? Is it better to let a cascade form in front of them, and only then give them the vocabulary for what they have just seen, or does that guarantee they will describe it as copying? Where does the contrast between the simultaneous case, where many independent signals really do aggregate, and the sequential case, where observed actions are all anyone has, properly belong — before or after they have felt how compelling the simultaneous inference is?

Second, the case that carries the session. I want one example that does the whole job, and I want to know what it must contain and what it must not. My instinct is that a binary decision under a genuinely common value, with anonymous agents rewarded only for accuracy, is essential, and that anything with reputational stakes, career incentives or a visibly corrupt majority has to be excluded from the carrying case — but I am not certain, and I want the reasoning. My usual candidate is a hiring committee that interviews in sequence and votes by show of hands; tell me whether to keep it and, if not, what replaces it. And tell me why I should or should not build the session around the examples my students will already half-know from elsewhere: restaurant queues, urns with red and blue balls, fashions and fads, review scores.

Third, the misconceptions, and where in the sequence each should be headed off. I can predict several: that a cascade is simply conformity or peer pressure under a technical name (which is why I am unsure whether to introduce reputational herding first as a contrast or to withhold it until the pure informational case has landed); that if every agent updates correctly on everything she observes, the group's outcome must be rational, or must at least converge on the truth as numbers grow; that crowds are usually right, so a cascade is a marginal inefficiency rather than the foreclosure of all further evidence; that a cascade can always be broken by one well-informed dissenter, when the visible unanimity overstates the evidence and an agent who has simply arrived late cannot tell whether the three votes ahead of her were three independent signals or one signal followed by two acts of copying; and that the remedy is to tell people to think independently or to reward accuracy, neither of which touches the information structure. Which of these must be defused before the machinery is in place and which only after the students have felt their pull, and which others should I expect and have not listed?

Finally: what should I openly decline to explain in seventy-five minutes, and say aloud that I am declining it — I am inclined to leave out the general question of whether more public information can make a group worse off, and everything that would require strategic complementarities — and what single question should I put to them at the end that would show me the compression point has landed, as opposed to the slogan that a cascade is when people copy each other?
