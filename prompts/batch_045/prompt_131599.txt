My friend Sam and I spent most of last night at a cabin with no cell signal arguing about Wigner's unreasonable-effectiveness thing, and we got exactly nowhere, which is what's bugging me. He's doing a postdoc in condensed matter and his line is the deflationary one: it's selection effect plus hindsight. Physics only ever used a tiny slice of the mathematical corpus, the cases everyone remembers are the ones where it happened to work, and most of the pure math that later got applied was developed with some physical problem sitting in the background anyway, so the miracle is us noticing the hits and not the misses. I kept pushing back that this doesn't touch the shape of the good cases: a theory gets pinned down almost uniquely by internal constraints — consistency, symmetry, the refusal to be arbitrary — and then hands you a quantity or an entity nobody had gone out looking for; and separately, the same piece of structure (the same group, the same invariant, the same algebra of operators) turns up in two domains with no causal contact with each other. I've started splitting the thesis into retail effectiveness (this particular structure, built for internal reasons, ended up fitting a phenomenon that wasn't built into it) and wholesale effectiveness (the world is mathematically describable at all), because I think Sam's deflation handles wholesale decently and handles retail badly. I also think his reply needs what I'd call pre-selection rather than post-selection: hindsight cherry-picking is only half of it, and the stronger claim is that the mathematical options that ever got developed, and that strike mathematicians as natural or elegant, were already filtered by centuries of physical usefulness — the corpus is contaminated before the fact, not just mined after it. Nobody knows how strong that filtering actually was, and it feels to me like an empirical question masquerading as a philosophical one.

Two more things came up that I dont know what to do with. One: our best theories seem to contain more mathematics than the phenomena need — there's formal machinery in them that does no representational work, redundant descriptions of one and the same physical situation. I took that as evidence against the metaphysical version of the thesis (the map has more structure than the territory, so the territory isn't really made of the math), and Sam took it as irrelevant, because on his view the puzzle was never about what the world is made of. Two: I have a nagging worry that wholesale effectiveness is close to trivial — if having mathematical structure just means being compressible, or being describable by a small set of rules with lots of consequences, then of course the world is mathematical and we'd probably never call a domain a science otherwise, so all the real content is in retail. Sam half-agreed and then said something like "then you're just asking why the world is intelligible", and we both sort of trailed off. From what I half-remember of the philosophy-of-science side, the published debate has been in roughly this shape for decades too, which is part of why I think it's stuck rather than just hard.

So here's my hypothesis, which I'd rather you take apart than nod along with: this is three disagreements under one name. Partly it's verbal — effectiveness is doing different work on each side, and so is the thing to be explained (why the world is mathematizable at all versus why mathematical reasoning is a reliable guide to conclusions we haven't tested, one metaphysical and one methodological). But partly there's a hidden empirical question doing the work — how filtered was the mathematical corpus in advance, and what's the base rate at which natural-looking structures find physical employment — and partly I suspect a bedrock disagreement about whether elegance or naturalness is any evidence at all, which neither of us can argue for, only assert. What I want from you is a diagnosis of the stalemate rather than a verdict or a survey of positions: which parts are verbal, which are hidden empirical, which are bedrock, and how you can tell from what each side is actually committed to. Then, given your diagnosis, tell me honestly what would count as progress — a distinction that dissolves part of it, something someone could in principle measure about the mathematical corpus, a description of what a case would have to look like for it to count as a real prediction from untouched pure math to measurable physics (not the name of such a case, I don't want a reading list), some formal result about how constrained the space of usable theories was in advance — or an argument that nothing would move this and why that's the right expectation.
