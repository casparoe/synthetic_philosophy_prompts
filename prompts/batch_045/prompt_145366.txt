Last night I argued with a friend — a career engine boss — about a near-miss I have to write up, and I am no longer confident I was right. He says the two crews simply never got the same message, and the fix is a better radio and a longer briefing. I said that a message arriving is not the same as everyone knowing that everyone got it, and that adding confirmation steps cannot close that gap. He called that sophistry, and I could not give him a sentence he would accept.

I chair the after-action review board for a regional wildfire coordination centre: roughly thirty incidents a season, ninety minutes each, findings written on a two-page form, signed off by a district ranger, and later read by lawyers if anyone was hurt. Legal review will not accept a finding that turns on what a person privately believed, only on what is in the radio log and the timeline.

Give me a decision procedure my board can run on any coordination failure, grounded in the epistemic game theory of common knowledge and common belief in rationality, but usable by people with no training in it. It must, in order: sort payoff or preference divergence that merely looks like a knowledge failure from the genuine knowledge failures; separate first-order mutual knowledge of a fact or plan from common knowledge of it; separate common knowledge of the plan from common belief in the other crew's rationality, competence and adherence; and handle uncertainty about whether the channel delivered, including the result that no finite chain of confirmations over an unreliable link ever produces common knowledge. It should say when more communication genuinely helps, when it cannot and the only move is to change the payoffs — a safe standing fallback, a pre-committed trigger, a single designated decision-maker, a genuinely public announcement — and it should stop with a no-change verdict when nothing in the protocol explains the outcome. Every question must be answerable from logs, a timeline and two interviews, with an instruction for what to record when a number cannot be had; and mark explicitly where judgment cannot be eliminated and the form must hand the decision to a person. Say which distinctions had to survive the simplification and which you dropped.

Apply it to three cases: two engine crews, one above and one below a ridge, each having radioed that they were holding, after which the lower crew pulled out; a hand crew that never redeployed because the acknowledgment never came back; and one where the verdict is unwelcome — where the procedure clears a crew boss the ranger wants to blame, or says a better radio would in fact have helped. End by stating plainly what the procedure cannot do.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — what the procedure is, and where its authority stops — then give the reasoning. No preamble and no restating my question.
