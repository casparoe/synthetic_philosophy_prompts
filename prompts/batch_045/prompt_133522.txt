I TA for an undergraduate course on rational choice, and last week's section was on escalation of commitment. We ran the dollar auction as a live demo, under the usual rules: nothing changes hands while bidding is open, and at the close the highest bidder pays their own last bid and takes the dollar while the runner-up pays their last bid and gets nothing — so a standing bid is less a payment than a liability you can only escape by ending up on top. Ours closed at $2.40, with $2.35 underneath. Two students, Owen and Marisol, stayed behind afterwards and argued about what the demo actually shows; because I had organized it they asked me to referee, and I genuinely could not tell who was right, so I am handing it to you. Their exchange, as close as I can reconstruct it:

(1) Owen: This is the sunk-cost fallacy in its purest form — nobody bids $2.40 for a dollar on the strength of any calculation; they keep going because they cannot bear to accept that what they have already put in is gone, and escalation of commitment is just that impulse writ large.
(2) Marisol: No sunk-cost reasoning is needed. Every step has a forward-looking justification: if I am in for 90¢ and you are at 95¢, then stopping loses me 90¢ for sure, whereas bidding 96¢ and having you drop out leaves me 4¢ ahead; and with 90¢ committed I could rationally go as high as $1.89, since anything under $1.90 beats a certain 90¢ loss. My 90¢ is not a bygone cost being irrationally weighted — it is the alternative against which the next bid is measured, because re-bidding replaces a certain loss with a chance at the dollar. The mistake, if there is one, lies elsewhere: each bid is justified only by supposing the other side will now quit, and that cannot be true of both of us at once.
(3) Owen: Two replies. First, calling each step rational is too generous when its premise — that the other person, facing identical arithmetic, will stop — is one you know to be false; the rational move was never to enter at all. Second, a rule that will happily license $3.40 for a dollar has stopped being a justification and started being a description of the pathology, so the forward-looking defence is hollow.
(4) Marisol: Then let us test it. Run the auction again, but once the standing bid passes 50¢ either side may hand its position to a fresh, uninvested participant who inherits the liability — she is charged the 90¢ already bid if she stops, and takes the dollar if she wins — while having personally put in nothing. If the pull is the arithmetic, she escalates exactly as readily; if it is reluctance to walk away from what you yourself sank, she should be markedly cooler. I predict she escalates anyway.
(5) Owen: Even if that is right about the lab, the cases the literature was built on are repeated and public — a losing war, a white-elephant project — where persisting buys a reputation for never backing down and deters rivals later, so throwing good money after bad can be coldly rational investment; and in those cases the earlier money is genuinely spent, with no dollar at the end that cancels the liability, which makes the demo disanalogous to the very phenomenon it is used to illustrate.
(6) Marisol: A reputation story needs a future and an audience that can see you did not fold, and the demo has neither; and reputation is itself forward-looking — a reason to spend now for a later payoff, not a reason to be moved by what you have spent — so if you are right it replaces the textbook story rather than rescuing it. Your disanalogy point also cuts against your own opening: if the demo is structurally unlike real escalation, then the demo does not show the sunk-cost fallacy either.

Please adjudicate. Go through the six moves one at a time: for each, say where it falls on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and give one sentence of justification. Then, in at most three sentences, say who is more right overall; and in at most two, say what you think both of them are missing. One thing I could not work out and would like addressed in that last part: does the fact that the auction has no natural last round — a bidder can always bid again — undermine Marisol's backward-induction framing, or not?
