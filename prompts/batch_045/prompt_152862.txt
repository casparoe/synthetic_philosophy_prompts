I'm writing a section of a short paper on scalable oversight, and I need one concrete case built to order. Here's the wall I keep runnning into: every failure story I can come up with for debate is a story where debate gets the *wrong answer*, and I already have plenty of those. What I actually want to dramatize is different — debate gets the verdict right and is still the wrong tool, because what comes out the other end is a forced binary, and the thing the verdict feeds into needed a graded confidence.

So: build me a case that does that.

The scenario itself: an ordinary institution, the kind a normal person has heard of, nothing futuristic, no superhuman reasoners, no perfect predictors, no exotic anything. The debaters can be LLM assistants or human analysts, your call — they just can't be magic. The judge is a competent professional with, say, thirty minutes and no special expertise on the crux. Keep the scenario to one short paragraph, and in a seperate line tell me what actually happened in reality — there should be a definite truth, even though nobody in the story can establish it.

Specs the case has to hit:

- Both debaters are equally informed: same evidence, same access. No information asymmetry doing the work.
- Both are trying to win. No collusion, no sandbagging, no reward hacking.
- Every assertion either side makes is true. The debater arguing for the wrong answer never lies — it selects, frames, omits, and emphasizes. If your case needs a false sentence anywhere in the transcript, it doesn't count.
- The evidence is genuinely close — call it 55/45 — and both debaters know it's close.
- Under plain debate the judge records A. Good: A is the better-supported answer and, as it turns out, the true one. I want debate to win on accuracy here.
- The recorded verdict then goes into a process that only takes a binary, and the action triggered by a confident A is different from — and worse than — the action you'd take on a calibrated 55%-A. Give me the numbers: the available options, the rough costs, the threshold, and one line of arithmetic showing the expensive or irreversible option is right only above about 70% while the middle option is right at 55%.
- Under a no-adversary setup (one agent, judge asks follow-ups for as long as they want), the judge comes away with something closer to the real number and picks the middle option. Show *why* the lone agent can transmit its calibration and the debater can't. This has to fall out of the setup — if the only reason the hedge gets lost is that you've stipulated that the downstream process strips it out, the case is cheating and I don't want it.

Then, after the scenario:

- Name the single feature that's load-bearing.
- Say what happens if you remove it — does the case collapse back into a plain "arguments can mislead" case, or does it still bite?
- Give me the best two attacks a debate person would make. The ones I have in mind are: let each debater state a number and pay them by a proper scoring rule; and have the judge write down a confidence next to the verdict. Take both seriously and tell me whether they actually fix it. My hunch is that both crash into the fact that you can't score the forecast without already knowing the answer, but show me instead of agreeing with me.
- Last, short: does the recursive / cross-examination variant do any better here? My guess is no, but argue it either way.
