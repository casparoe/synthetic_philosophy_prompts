So here's my situation. I got long covid in 2022 after a genuinely mild infection, then spent the better part of a year being told every test came back normal, and somewhere in the middle of that I got talked onto the board of a small foundation that funds conceptual work on health and disease — philosophy, history, STS-ish stuff, no bench science. We've got room for about six two-year postdocs in the next round and I've been asked to draft the call for proposals. It's not my field at all, but my own diagnostic mess is exactly why I care about it.

The thing that keeps bugging me is that the naturalism-versus-normativism fight feels frozen: Boorse-style biostatistical views on one side, the "disease is irreducibly value-laden" people on the other, Wakefield's harmful-dysfunction hybrid parked in the middle, everyone else writing variations. I do not want to fund six more papers on whether the biostatistical account can pick a reference class. I want to fund whatever would actually change how people reason in about ten years — guideline panels, insurers, disability-rights lawyers, clinicians, maybe me arguing with my own doctor.

Three things I've been chewing on, none of which I can tell are real research programs or just my confusion:

One: the baseline looks unstable in a way that might matter more than the usual objections. Statistical normality is measured on populations we've already medicated, screened, and reshaped, and it's environment-relative in a boring but possibly devastating way — if nearly every kid in a high-myopia population ends up needing glasses, or if everyone breathing this city's air has chronic airway inflammation, is that normal species function or not? If you build the environment into the reference class you seem to land on a situated view of health by the back door; if you don't, the naturalist's anchor drifts with our own treatment practices. Is there something to fund here, or is this just the reference-class problem wearing a hat?

Two: maybe we should stop looking for one concept. Disease talk seems to do at least three quite different institutional jobs — deciding what gets covered or reimbursed, deciding what accommodations a person is owed, and deciding questions of capacity and criminal responsibility — and my naive guess is that the honest move is to design separate institution-specific concepts and stop pretending they must coincide. Is that a genuine advance or just capitulation dressed up as pluralism?

Three: nobody seems to work on the epistemics of moving a diagnostic boundary. Given that handing someone a diagnosis changes the person and changes the population the next baseline is drawn from, what would count as evidence that a category revision was correct, and who should get to decide?

Which of these, or what I'm not thinking of, should six postdocs spend two years each on, if the success condition is that in a decade some actual institution reasons differently? Rank them and defend the ranking — what's upstream of what, what's tractable in two years for someone with philosophy or STS tools and no lab, what's just plain neglected, and what evidence in the next couple of years would move something up or down. Also tell me which of the alleged practical stakes are overrated or don't follow: everyone repeats that naturalism protects us from medicalization and normativism opens the door to disease-mongering, and I'm not convinced either one follows. I don't need names of people to hire or papers to read — I need the argument.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give me the reasoning. No preamble and don't restate my question back at me.
