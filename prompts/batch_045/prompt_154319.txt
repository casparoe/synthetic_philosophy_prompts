Me and two friends from my epistemology seminar got into this for like two hours on Friday and I still can't tell what I actually believe, so I'm writing down my gut verdicts and hoping someone can tell me what principle they add up to — or, more likely, which of them I have to give up. The setup is the boring bank case we keep getting: you drove past the branch two weeks ago on a Saturday and saw it open, and now the question is whether you know it's open Saturdays. My verdicts, going case by case, with the stakes varied: (1) low stakes, you're mailing a cheque that can wait until Monday — you know. (2) high stakes, the cheque has to clear today or you lose the apartment — you don't know, and I'm fine with that, it's the textbook thing. (3) here's the one I invented, and I've started calling it stakes laundering, which is probably not a phrase anyone else uses: a stranger you've never met, who you half suspect is messing with you, announces that he'll pay you an enormous sum if the bank is open Saturday and take an enormous sum from you if it isn't. You never agreed to the bet. My verdict is that you still know — stakes dumped on you by somebody else, that you didn't take on, don't seem to touch it. (4) the aggregation case: you've queued up two hundred tiny automatic payments over the next fortnight, each of which bounces with a small fee if the bank isn't open, and the total exposure is bigger than in (2). Here I want to say you don't know, even though you never made a single decision with big stakes, you just made a lot of small ones — which sits badly with (3), where the stakes were larger and imposed from outside and I said knowledge survived. (5) the asymmetry case, which is the one I keep going back and forth on: a kid is coming to the party, and if he's allergic to nuts and eats the cake it's really bad, whereas if he isn't and we serve nut-free cake it's just mildly annoying. On pretty thin evidence — his mum once mentioned he got a bit itchy after a cereal bar — I find myself saying I know he's allergic, and the bar feels *lower* than normal, not higher; but I'd never say I know he's *not* allergic on that evidence. So in (2) stakes raise the bar and in (5) they seem to lower it for the belief whose error is cheap, and I can't see how one threshold rule does both. (6) maximal stakes on near-certain evidence: the light right in front of me at noon is red, and someone will shoot me if I'm wrong. I know it's red. So whatever stakes do, there's a ceiling. (7) third-person version: Sam has the same two-week-old memory and zero stakes, but *I* am deciding whether to risk something expensive on Sam's say-so. I want to say both "Sam knows, I just shouldn't rely on him" and "from where I'm sitting, he doesn't know," and I can't tell which is my real verdict. (8) inquiry stakes: I know the manuscript is by so-and-so on modest evidence and nothing is at stake practically, but the attribution matters enormously for a book I'm writing so I'm spending a year verifying it — and here I don't want to say I don't know, since investigating feels compatible with knowing in a way that acting against high stakes doesn't.

What I'd like is a principle, or two or three candidates, stated precisely enough that I can run cases through it: something like "you know that p iff your evidence for p is strong enough that the expected cost of the worse error, given the stakes you have yourself taken on, is below such-and-such, capped at such-and-such" — but with the clause that does the work in each of my eight verdicts named, and the case that motivates it. I especially want to know what has to go: is (3) or (4) the one I should drop, and is there any version of the view where (2) and (5) both come out right, or does the asymmetry case show that what's tracking stakes isn't knowledge at all but something like being warranted enough to act? Please also say what the principle predicts for a few cases I haven't listed — stakes that are huge but where no action is possible because the matter is already settled, stakes the subject doesn't know about, and stakes that come from a moral obligation rather than from anything that would be bad for the subject. And if it's not too much, tell me whether whatever comes out is closer to the view where the standard for "knows" shifts with whoever's assessing, or the interest-relative invariantist one where the subject's practical stakes are part of whether she knows — because I keep thinking I want a hybrid and then not being able to say what the hybrid is.
