I work in interpretability at a small lab — mostly toy grid-world agents and small language models, nothing at frontier scale — and a few of us have been running an informal reading group on what people are starting to call model welfare. We mean something deliberately modest by that: not whether any current system is conscious, but which of the computations going on inside a trained system, if any, would deserve to figure in a moral deliberation about it, and what would have to be true of a computation for it to be the kind that counts. I hold no settled view on the big question. What I keep getting stuck on, and what I want help with, is a pattern in my own judgments that I cannot name, let alone defend.

The pattern shows up in three of my own experiments. (i) Grown versus grafted: a grid-world agent, after a few million steps, develops a direction in activation space that tracks being about to be damaged; lesioning it destroys the avoidance behaviour. Wanting a control, I hand-wrote a small circuit that reads the same quantity off the state and writes it into the same place, and then trained the rest of the network around it. From the moment it is switched on, the two systems' state-to-state maps are, by every probe I can build, the same: same ablation result, same generalisation to held-out mazes, same behaviour under distribution shift. Yet I cannot take the grafted version seriously as a candidate for moral concern, and the trained one I can at least argue about. (ii) Unrolled versus collapsed: a small language model trained to do multi-digit addition with explicit intermediate steps; I then distilled the intermediate steps away, and confirmed that the carry-like features are still linearly decodable at the same layers and still causally efficacious on the answer. The intermediate quantity is present and doing causal work, but it is, as I want to put it, written without being arrived at. (iii) Sole versus backed-up: two systems with identical behaviour; in one, ablating a circuit destroys a discrimination; in the other, ablating any one of three circuits changes nothing, because all three compute it. Here my intuition runs the opposite way from case (ii): the backed-up system feels more robustly real, not less.

So in each pair every state I would have nominated as the morally relevant computation is present and playing its causal role, and what differs is something about how the system got there, or about what would happen only under interventions that never occur in the training distribution. My question is whether there is an established concept for this. I am not after a bare label: I want the carving — what exactly the thing is, what it is not, where its edges are. I half-remember, from an undergraduate class, stock thought experiments about a room with a rulebook and about a gigantic lookup table, but those seem aimed at whether a system understands, and they are set up so that behaviour is preserved while something inner is intuitively missing; my problem is that I cannot say what is missing without circularity, since every candidate I nominate — counterfactual sensitivity, causal efficacy on output, robustness under ablation — is satisfied by both members of each pair. If the nearest established concepts fit only partially, say which they are, what each covers, and precisely where each breaks on my three cases; if nothing fits, coin a term, define it tightly enough that it has borderline cases, and give me a diagnostic test for whether a given internal state is arrived at or merely present that does not presuppose the answer. I have no library access where I am writing this, so please do not lean on references — a concept's standard name and a statement of what it means are plenty.

Give me the discussion first, and then, after it, a single JSON object with exactly these fields: "nearest_concepts" (an array of three or four objects, each with "name", "what_it_covers", "why_it_fits_my_cases", and "where_it_breaks"); "coined_term" (a string, or null if an existing concept fits); "definition" (one or two sentences, written so that it would be checkable on a system I have not described); "case_verdicts" (an array of four objects, each with "case", "verdict" — one of "in", "out", "borderline" — and "reason": one object for each of my three pairs, plus one for a case of your own choosing on which your definition yields a clear answer); "one_phenomenon_or_three" (a short string saying whether my three cases are one thing or several, and why); and "could_it_do_moral_work" (a short string on whether, if the thing is real, it is the sort of property that could bear on moral status at all, or whether my discomfort is best explained away).
