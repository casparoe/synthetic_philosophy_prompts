I am in the upper sixth and writing my extended project on decision-making under cognitive limits. I have built an argument that I think proves far too much, and I cannot locate the fault. I would be grateful for a diagnosis.

Set-up. You face a set of options; inspecting one costs time and effort; you care about ending up with a good one. Call a rule satisficing if you fix an acceptance level in advance, inspect options in some order, and take the first one that clears the level. Call it optimizing if you try to rank the options by weighing how likely each outcome is against how much you care about it, and then take the best.

(1) A decision rule is worth using only if what it gains you, compared with the other rules open to you, exceeds what it costs you to run it — where costs include time, effort, and everything you forgo while you are deliberating.

(2) Optimizing always costs more to run than satisficing, and often a great deal more.

(3) So optimizing is very rarely worth running: for nearly every decision, satisficing is the rule you ought to use. This is, roughly, what the case for bounded rationality is supposed to establish.

(4) But satisficing leaves its own choices unsettled: how high to set the acceptance level, how long to search before giving up, in what order to inspect. Each can be settled carefully or carelessly, and settling any of them carefully costs time.

(5) So the test in (1) applies one level up: you ought not to deliberate about how to deliberate either. By the same reasoning at the level above that, and the level above that, the recommendation at every level is to deliberate as little as you can get away with.

(6) None of this turns on the stakes. Another five minutes of thinking buys only a small improvement in your chances; small improvements count for little; and the cost of the five minutes is what it is whatever the decision is about.

(7) So the rational procedure, for any decision whatsoever, is to think about it as briefly as possible: take the first option that clears a modest bar, and where even that is too expensive, choose arbitrarily. Anyone who spends an afternoon choosing between two degree courses, two surgeons, or two jobs is thereby reasoning less well than someone who spends ten seconds.

(8) That cannot be right. It also eats itself: if (7) is true, these eight steps were a wasted afternoon, and I should stop now and keep whichever belief I happened to arrive at first.

Two cases I cannot fit into the argument, which makes me more suspicious of it. First, a ratchet: suppose the rule is not a fixed acceptance level but one that drops by a fixed notch every time you reject an option, so that search becomes cheaper the longer it continues — people seem to do something like this when they get tired or discouraged. By the test in (1) the ratchet looks strictly better than a fixed level, because it costs less to run. Yet if you search long enough it will accept an option worse than anything a fixed level would have accepted, with no floor beneath you at all. Second, an inverted case: take a decision where the expensive rule and the cheap rule differ only in that the expensive one is certain to find the single best option, and where the stakes are enormous. My argument appears to rank them the same way here as it does when the stakes are trivial, which makes me think that somewhere between (3) and (7) it has stopped comparing gains and started comparing only costs.

I would rather keep (1) and (3) than (6) and (7): I still want to say that satisficing is the right account of how to choose which courses to apply for, and I do not want to retreat to the claim that everyone is failing to maximize. But I want to know what keeping them costs me.

Please give me your diagnosis in prose first — which step you reject, why that step looked fine when I wrote it down, and what I commit myself to by giving it up, including what happens to the ratchet and to the inverted case. - step: the step number, or argument as a whole for the final row
- verdict: one of keep, reject, or keep under one reading only
- flaw: the single move that fails, in one sentence, named as a type of error (equivocation, scope shift, level confusion, double counting, and so on) where a type applies; write none where the step is sound
- why_it_looked_fine: one sentence on what made the step plausible
- price: one sentence on what is conceded or committed to by rejecting the step; write none where the step is kept
