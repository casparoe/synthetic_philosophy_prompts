Next Thursday I am presenting, and then defending for forty minutes, the draft apology for the 1948 removals — the compulsory transfer of some nine thousand people from the Kalva valley to the eastern districts under a cabinet decree that was entirely lawful when it was issued, and whose last surviving signatory died in 1979. I wrote the draft. Six months ago I found my grandmother's name on the transport lists, having grown up being told the family had left the valley for work, so I am not approaching this as a neutral technician and I would rather know now where my position is thin than discover it in the room.

The thesis I am defending is that the primary function of a state apology is not compensation, not expiation, and not acknowledgement understood as an end in itself, but the foreclosure of future denial: the apology puts a sentence into the permanent official record, in the state's own voice, so that afterwards the moves "it never happened," "the numbers were inflated," "no one in authority ordered it" are unavailable to officials, to the press and to the state itself without visible self-contradiction. My argument has four steps: that the state is the sort of thing that can persist across changes of government and so can speak for its own earlier acts; that this wrong was partly constituted by being authorised, so only the authorising authority can fittingly withdraw the authorisation; that the apology's value is therefore forward-looking and evidentiary rather than expressive; and hence that the draft must include what my colleagues have started calling the no-context clause — a sentence stating that nothing in the circumstances of 1948 mitigates the removal — because in my experience denial does not die when the facts are conceded, it relocates, migrating from "it did not happen" to "it happened but was normal for the time," and a purely factual apology leaves that second wave untouched.

The room is about thirty people: roughly half transitional-justice and reconciliation practitioners, several of them truth-commission veterans, plus two political theorists who work on collective responsibility and speech acts; on the other side my own ministry's lawyers, a government archivist who thinks the figure of nine thousand is not defensible and should not appear in a state text, and four representatives of the descendant associations, who have not asked for an apology and have said so publicly.

What I want is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defence of the view: eight objections, ordered by how much damage they would do to the draft's chances rather than by how likely they are, each phrased as the person in question would actually phrase it and attributed to which block in the room; for each, a reply I can deliver in under a minute, and one short clause on where that reply is weakest. Tell me separately which two objections are near-certain, what I should concede in my first sentence before anyone asks, and which single reply has to land if the no-context clause is going to survive the meeting. End with the one question I am hoping nobody asks and what I should say if they do. Assume I have read the general literature on political apology and do not restate it. Keep the whole thing under 800 words.
