I do public engagement and participant-facing writing for a national genomics programme — consent forms, school worksheets, exhibit panels, the FAQ. Our house analogy is that DNA is "the instruction manual for your body," and a few of us have started saying "source code" instead, because manual sounds like somebody has to read it and code sounds like it runs itself. We use this language in maybe a dozen documents and it gets quoted back at us in the press, so I want to get it right before the next print run.

Three things have come up that I can't sort out on my own.

One of our bioinformatics people keeps saying it isn't an analogy anymore: research groups have been writing actual digital files into synthesised DNA and reading them back out, and if you can store a file in a strand of DNA then DNA is literally a code and the rest of us are being precious about a metaphor. Something feels off in that argument but I can't say what, and he's the one with the pipeline so he tends to win.

The analogy is also quietly doing political work in both directions. Participants read "instruction manual" and come away thinking their risks are written into them and basically fixed — we get a lot of "so my genome is my fate" questions at the desk. Meanwhile the legal and data people use the same analogy the other way round: DNA is just information, so holding it is like holding any other file, and the special handling rules for genomic data are superstition. I don't think both of those can survive, but I also don't know which one the analogy is actually carrying.

And a wrinkle from our own pipeline that might be relevant or might be a red herring: what we report back to a participant is not a sequence, it's a list of differences from a reference genome, and the reference is something the community picked and occasionally revises. So the string that's supposedly "their" code depends on a convention that isn't in their cells at all. Relatedly, I've noticed we slide between "coding" in the narrow molecular-biology sense (protein-coding stretches, a small slice of the genome) and "code" in the big metaphorical sense, sometimes in the same sentence.

Can you audit this the way you'd audit any analogy someone is using as an argument: what maps onto what — what plays the part of the source code, the compiler, the hardware, the runtime, the input, the bug, the version history — which parts of the code idea are doing real work and which are decoration, where the mapping breaks, and whether each break matters for the conclusions people are drawing from it. I'd especially like to know what the analogy smuggles in from the software side that the genome doesn't have, whether the DNA-storage argument settles anything or is a different claim wearing the same words, and whether the reference-genome point is a genuine problem for "code" talk or just a detail about how we happen to report. If code is the wrong word, give me something better — but it has to be a phrase I can put in a consent form without a lawyer or a participant coming back at me.
