I am trying to decide what to do with a cast-brass shrine figure that has been in my family since 1901, when my great-grandfather, serving as a district officer in a colonial administration in West Africa, bought it from a village elder for cash. His diary notes that he paid above the price asked, because the sellers seemed reluctant and he did not want to appear stingy. The national museum of the country it came from has written to ask that it be returned, adding that the figure is one of a pair whose twin they already hold, that the pair was split at the time of the sale, and that the two were never meant to be separated. A large museum here has offered to take it as a gift and display it, and has proposed what it calls a partnership with the source museum: a long-term renewable loan, joint curatorship, high-resolution scans made available to the community, and permanent transfer once the source museum's storage meets international climate and security standards.

That museum's argument for keeping it has three parts: the sale was legal when it happened; figures of this kind were being melted down for scrap and sold on to itinerant dealers in the same decade, so this one probably owes its survival to having been removed; and if everything acquired under comparable circumstances had to be handed back, its African galleries would have to close.

I feel that something is wrong with each of those three arguments, and with the loan offer itself, but I cannot say what. I also cannot tell whether permanent return is really what is being asked of me, or whether the partnership would answer the request. Please lay out what is at issue here and advise me on what I should do.
