So here's my situation and I'd really like some help thinking through it.

My dad died last spring. A few months later my brother signed us all up for one of those "legacy avatar" services — you hand over a dead person's texts, emails, voicemails, a pile of old home videos, and it builds something you can text with. It's been going since about November. My brother's youngest, who's nine, uses it to say goodnight to grandpa. I hate the whole thing and I can't cleanly say why, which is annoying because I'm usually the articulate one in the family.

I went looking for a framework and landed on Dennett's stuff about counterfeit people — roughly, the idea that these systems are built to trigger all our person-detection machinery while having none of what's supposed to be behind it, so the warmth is the product and also the problem. That clicked hard for me. But then my brother's wife said something I can't shake: we already do this. There's a voicemail from Dad on my phone from 2021 that I've listened to maybe forty times. I talk out loud to him in the car. My sister writes him letters she never sends. None of those are him either, and nobody calls those counterfeit.

What actually gets under my skin isn't that it talks like him. It's that it makes things up. Last month it told my nephew a story about Dad taking him fishing at a lake near their place, and it never happened — we checked with my mum, and Dad hadn't driven by then. The company's help page basically admits this: the model fills gaps in the source material rather than going silent, and there's a setting, I think it's called something like "continuity," that trades off sticking to the archive against improvising to keep the conversation flowing. Turned down, it's stiff and dodges everything; turned up, it's warmer and more like him and lies more.

Could you write me a dialogue between two people arguing this out? One of them takes roughly the Dennett-flavoured line: that this is a counterfeit person, that the fluency and the warmth are exactly what make it dangerous rather than what redeems it, and that the invented fishing trip is the whole case in miniature — a machine tuned to manufacture the feeling of Dad with no stake in whether a single thing it says is true. The other one argues back that the counterfeit framing is doing less work than it appears to: that remembering someone is already partly reconstruction, that portrait painters and biographers and novelists invent and still tell us true things about real people, and that the question isn't whether it passes some metaphysical test for being Dad but what it's doing to my nephew's relationship with him and to ours.

Don't make either of them an idiot, and please don't let them just trade past each other. I want the counterfeit side to actually have to deal with the voicemail on my phone, and the other side to actually have to deal with the fishing trip and with the fact that the slider exists at all. A couple of places where one of them concedes something real would help me more than a tidy resolution — but by the end I want to be able to tell what each of them thinks I should do about the setting, and about my nephew using it at bedtime. Somewhere in the 900-1200 word range, in ordinary speech, no stage directions beyond who's talking.
