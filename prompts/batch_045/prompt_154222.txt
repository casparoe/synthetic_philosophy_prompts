i'm taking a seminar this term on the ethics of influence — we did a lot of nudging stuff early on and now we're on interpersonal cases — and two people in my section got into a long argument after class that I've been turning over ever since. The case they were fighting about involves a mutual friend, Sam, whose roommate talked him into signing a lease on an apartment that was about $300 a month more than Sam had said he wanted to spend. Nothing the roommate said was false: bigger windows, shorter commute, a neighbourhood Sam already liked. But he raised the whole thing on the specific night Sam found out he'd lost a sublet he'd been counting on, and Sam admits he was pretty panicked about having nowhere to live. Fast forward a few months: Sam loves the apartment, says he'd sign again, and just shrugs when you point out the timing.

Ana's opening move was that this is manipulation rather than persuasion, because the roommate got Sam to act on a psychological state — panic — that Sam himself would refuse to count as a reason if he were calm; her line was something like "manipulation is getting someone to treat as a reason something they'd reject as a reason in a cooler hour." Ben pushed back that every bit of persuasion leans on timing, tone, framing, catching someone when they're receptive, and that it isn't even true that calm deliberation is always better — people make plenty of good calls while keyed up and plenty of bad ones while serene. Ana then revised: forget emotion, the real mark is that the roommate engineered which considerations were on the table and which weren't, so Sam's reasoning was genuinely exercised but on a rigged menu; persuasion adds a consideration to somebody's deliberation, manipulation designs the deliberation itself. Ben said that can't be the line either, since any finite conversation selects some considerations and not others, and whenever you help someone who can't weigh twelve things at once you're managing their attention — doctors recommending a surgery do it constantly. So Ana tried a third test, which she called the transparency condition: an influence is legitimate only if, told exactly how and why it was being brought to bear, including the influencer's own stake, the target would still count it as a reason; the roommate fails, because Sam would have balked at "I'm asking you tonight because you're scared." Ben answered that people are definitly terrible at predicting their own counterfactual reactions, that the roommate's stake wasn't exactly hidden (he obviously wanted Sam to stay), and that some perfectly fine influence — a surprise party, a staged intervention, a teacher arranging things so a kid discovers something for herself — only works because it isn't announced up front. Then they got stuck on Sam's own endorsement: Ana said the shrug settles nothing, because people routinely talk themselves into wanting whatever they ended up with, and Ben said that if you set aside the target's own verdict you're just substituting your judgement for his, which is its own kind of paternalism. The last thing tossed around was an afterthought from someone else in the room: the roommate claims he hadn't planned any of it, he just happened to bring it up that night — Ana said intent doesn't matter because the mechanism is identical, Ben said without strategy there's no manipulation, there's just luck.
