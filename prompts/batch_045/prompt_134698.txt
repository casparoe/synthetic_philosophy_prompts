I am writing a chapter for a collection on philosophy of religion in contemporary cinema, and my assignment is Spielberg's Minority Report. I want to read it as a film about middle knowledge rather than about foreknowledge, and I want that claim earned rather than asserted.

The reason the distinction matters is that Precrime's warrant cannot be simple foreknowledge of what will happen. On the programme's own logic, what the precogs see is precisely what is then made not to happen, so the content of a prevision is structurally a counterfactual: a truth about what this agent would do in circumstances the system is about to prevent. That is the Molinist's middle knowledge almost exactly, and the apparatus built on top of it — arrest for a murder that is not-yet, indefinite confinement of people who have done nothing, and the six-year boast that premeditated murder has been eliminated — looks like a state that believes such counterfactuals are determinately true in advance, stably enough to punish on, and knowable by a non-divine observer. Set that reconstruction out carefully, and distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with.

Then I want three things pressed.

First, a taxonomy of what a prevision is taken to track. Call these the actualist reading (it sees the one real timeline), the counterfactual reading (it sees what this agent would freely do in these circumstances, whether or not the act is ever performed), and the staged reading (it is true because someone has arranged the circumstances so that the agent will do the thing, so its truthmaker is a hidden causal setup rather than the agent's free disposition). My suspicion is that the film needs the second for its opening, slips into the third for its plot, and needs the first to make its ending land; I want that argued scene by scene rather than declared. In particular, does the staged reading turn the film into an informal version of the grounding objection, supplying by conspiracy the truthmaker that critics say Molinism cannot supply?

Second, the scenes. The hotel-room confrontation, where Anderton is shown what he is about to do, relents, and the killing the precogs saw comes about anyway by a route that no longer looks like Anderton freely doing the thing he was shown doing. The minority report, and the fact that dissenting visions are purged to preserve the reputation of infallibility. Agatha's suppressed vision of a woman drowning, on which no one acted. Burgess's murder of the Justice department auditor, timed to the absence of the one precog who could have foreseen it. And the closing confrontation, in which the man who has spent the film using other people's futures as instruments is exposed and kills himself rather than be arrested. For each, say what in the Molinist apparatus it illuminates and what it breaks. I am especially interested in two failures of fit: the prevision shown to Anderton is causally implicated in producing what it depicts, whereas middle knowledge is supposed to be knowledge that does not make its object true; and the previsions are satisfied even when the depicted agent does not do the depicted act, which suggests that what is known is an outcome rather than a free choice. I also want you to take up the system's blindness to murders of passion, since that is a distinction the counterfactual reading can explain and the actualist reading, as far as I can see, cannot.

Third, the use of the knowledge rather than its content. Burgess both prevents a murder and permits one, and he places Anderton in circumstances where he knows what Anderton will do there. That is providence by middle knowledge in its preventive and permissive forms, staged by a villain, so part of what I need to know is whether the film criticises middle knowledge or a certain providential use of it, and whether the Molinist can simply grant the criticism. Test the minority report device against the standard picture, on which counterfactuals of creaturely freedom have a unique truth value: can divergent previsions from the same circumstances be modelled by anything in the actual debate — restrictions to feasible worlds, for instance — or is the device merely a mechanism? Say plainly if it is merely a mechanism.

Finally, I do not want a reading that reduces the film to an illustration. Tell me which stretches of it are doing other work — the surveillance and the transplanted eyes, the abducted son, the noir machinery generally — and what, if anything, the film knows that a journal article does not: perhaps something about what an institution becomes once it is licensed to treat a counterfactual as a crime, or about what it does to an agent to be shown his own future.

Practical constraints: aim at 1,200 to 1,500 words; no plot summary beyond what your argument needs, and assume a reader who has seen the film; no secondary literature and no citations, since I want the argument to rest on the film as it is commonly remembered and on the structure of the position rather than on who said what. Where the reading fails, say so instead of smoothing it over.
