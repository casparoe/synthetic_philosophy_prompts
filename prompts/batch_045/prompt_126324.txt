I convene a graduate reading group on moral responsibility, and over the last few weeks I have assembled an argument whose individual steps I trust and whose conclusion I cannot accept. I am asking for a diagnosis rather than a survey: I already know the standard menu of positions (compatibilist, source-incompatibilist, reactive-attitudes, skeptical-abolitionist), and having them laid out again will not help me. What I need is the step to give up, why it looked safe, and what giving it up commits me to. I also don't need anything attributed to particular authors or papers — the reasoning is what I am after.

The argument:

(1) Control principle, comparative form: two agents may deserve different moral assessments only if they differ in some respect that was within their control.

(2) An agent has no control over her constitutive base — temperament, native capacities, the desires and aversions she simply finds herself with — nor over the circumstances into which she is thrown. Call these constitutive and circumstantial luck.

(3) What an agent does on any occasion is a function of her constitutive base as it stands at that time, together with her circumstances.

(4) So any difference between what two agents do traces back, along the chain described in (3), to something neither of them controlled. In the sense relevant to (1), then, no two agents ever differ in a respect that was within their control.

(5) Hence no two agents ever deserve different moral assessments: everyone deserves the same.

(6) Notice that (1) constrains differences, not levels. It forbids unequal verdicts but says nothing about which uniform verdict is correct. Levelling up — everyone deserves what the torturer deserves — is grotesque. Levelling down — no one deserves anything — is the only option left. I think of this as the baseline problem, by analogy with the one that afflicts luck egalitarianism: neutralizing luck tells you to equalize without telling you where to equalize.

(7) Therefore no one ever deserves blame or praise. Blame, resentment, gratitude and retributive punishment have no desert basis at all, and the honest response is to dismantle them in favour of a purely forward-looking regime of deterrence, quarantine and therapy.

(8) The argument also generalizes. Run the same chain with epistemic credit in place of blame and it shows that no one deserves credit for reasoning carefully, or for the true beliefs that careful inquiry produces. So nobody — I included — could deserve credit for reaching this conclusion, or for accepting it, or for rejecting it.

(9) Which means the debate the argument grew out of has evaporated as well: if there is no desert, there is nothing for luck to undermine, and the thesis that moral luck is a problem for responsibility is unavailable even to someone who thinks resultant luck is entirely real.

Steps (7) to (9) are where my confidence collapses, but (1) to (5) each look airtight to me, and (6) looks like a piece of honesty rather than a premise.

So, concretely, three things:

- Which step do you reject, and what made it look safe enough to pass without comment?
- Price the alternatives. If I give up (1), what replaces the intuition I would least like to lose — that two drivers who take the same risk, one of whom arrives home and one of whom kills a child, do not differ in blameworthiness by virtue of that difference alone? If I hold (1) and (2) and reject (4), what account of control survives, and does it require the agent to be the ultimate source of her own character in a way no causal story could deliver? If I accept (5) and resist (6), is there a uniform verdict I have not considered, and can it be anything other than an arbitrary stipulation?
- Finally: which of these repairs leaves the phenomenon in (9) intact, and which quietly concedes that the whole problem was never really about luck?
