My old boss Ray is retiring in three weeks after 41 years at the same place, a manufacturing company with about seventy people, and he asked me to give the send-off speech. He said specifically: no plaque talk, tell them something true. The problem is that the true thing I'd want to tell is also an accusation, and I'm not sure I'm allowed to make it.

For 41 years Ray ran the place on one rule: everything is a handshake, nothing goes in writing, nobody discusses pay. We take care of our own, he'd say, whenever someone asked what the person at the next desk was making. Sometimes that was actually loyalty. It also meant that for roughly fifteen years the three women in the front office were paid less than the men doing the same job and had no way to find out, and that when Marla finally asked him about it in 2009 he said we take care of our own, don't worry about what other people make, and I sat there taking the minutes and said nothing. She was gone eight months later. She's not coming to the party; I told her I was giving this speech and she said say whatever you want, just don't say it for me.

I don't think I have the standing. Not only because I stayed quiet that day, but because in 2013, when I was office manager, I used the exact same sentence on a new hire. So I'm not a clean-handed bystander, I'm somebody who operated the rule. My brother in law, who is insufferable, thinks that settles it: I used it, I benefited from it, and now I want the microphone so I can come out as the good guy, which is worse than silence. My sister says the opposite thing for a different reason: Marla's the one it was done to, Marla has told me not to speak for her, so the only person with the right to name it is the person it happened to.

Four other things I keep turning over and can't settle. Whether him asking for the truth makes this not an ambush or just makes me his instrument. Whether there's a version where I describe what happened to the room in the third person, so I'm reporting rather than blaming him, and why that feels more cowardly rather than less. Whether standing is something you can buy back by confessing your own part first, or whether that's worthless precisely because a room full of people rewards a confession, so you'd be profiting from the whole mess twice. And whether blame even has an addressee after tonight — he's retiring to another state, so this might be the last moment there's anyone to blame, which feels like it should count for something and maybe counts against me instead.

One more wrinkle: his daughter speaks right before me and her whole speech is about how he taught her that we take care of our own. So the sentence I'd be indicting is the sentence she's celebrating. A third of the room are family and church people who will hear any of this as a man getting heckled at his own party; the rest are people who worked there and are either like me or like Marla.

Write me the speech — eight minutes, last of the night. Not a trial, not a confessional, and I need to be able to shake his hand afterwards. Then give me a short paragraph on what you had the speech refuse to do and why.
