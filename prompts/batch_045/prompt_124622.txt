After a long and unresolved argument with a friend last night about Thomson's lamp, I would like an independent reading of the material we were both reacting to — not a verdict on who won the argument, but an accuracy audit of the passage itself, which neither of us is in a position to check.

The passage comes from a chapter on infinity in a popular book on mathematics; the author seems careful elsewhere, but some of the moves struck me as quicker than they should be. I have copied it out:

"It is switched off. Half a minute later you switch it on; a quarter of a minute after that, off; an eighth after that, on; and so on, each switching taking half as long as the one before. Because 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + ... sums to 1, the entire infinite sequence of switchings is finished by the end of the minute. Now ask what state the lamp is in at the end of the minute. It cannot be on, because every time it was switched on it was switched off again afterwards. It cannot be off either, because once it has been switched on, every subsequent switching-off is followed by a switching-on. But a lamp with a two-position switch must be either on or off. So the story requires it to be both on and off — a flat contradiction. Nothing in the story depends on anything special about lamps or electricity: the contradiction is purely logical, and no empirical discovery could overturn it. The only premise that could be responsible is the supertask itself, that is, the completion of an infinite sequence of tasks in a finite time. Thomson's lamp therefore shows that supertasks are logically impossible, that Zeno's paradoxes have real force after all, and that anyone who wants to take infinity seriously in physics should accept that time comes in indivisible minima — the lamp is, in effect, an argument for the discreteness of time."

Where my friend and I split: he said the whole thing is a physics point in disguise, since the switch has to be thrown at unbounded speed and no mechanism can do that, so the real content is about the limits of machinery rather than about logic or the structure of time. I said that could not be all of it, because the argument never mentions the mechanism — it says the lamp can be neither on nor off, which looks like a claim about what states are available to a two-state system. Neither of us could say which sentences in the passage are doing the work and which are the author's gloss.

Trying to get traction, we improvised two variants and disagreed about both. First: replace the switch with a dimmer. At the nth switching, set the brightness to 1/2 + (−1)ⁿ · 2^−(n+1), so the brightness alternates between above and below 1/2 while converging on 1/2, and say the lamp is on when its brightness is at least 1/2. Here the underlying continuous quantity has a limit even though the on/off predicate flips forever with no final change, so that — if the brightness at the limit instant equals the limit of the brightnesses — the lamp comes out on. My friend said the variant simply smuggles the answer in by assuming continuity at the limit point. Second: keep the two-position switch but arrange the switchings so that there is no first one, with infinitely many of them crowded into the interval and none earliest. Here, we thought, half of Thomson's argument seems to fall away.

Concretely, what I would like:

1. Go through the passage sentence by sentence and separate what a philosopher who works on this material would accept as a fair statement of the argument, what is a permissible simplification, what is a substantive misstep, and what is merely not entailed. In particular, I would like to know whether the slide from "the series sums to 1" to "the sequence of switchings is finished by the end of the minute" is a real step or a restatement of what is at issue, and whether "both on and off" is the conclusion the argument actually supports.

2. Say whether logical impossibility is the right category here at all, as opposed to the description's failing to determine an answer, or to physical ornomological impossibility — and whether the argument as stated has the resources to tell these apart.

3. Locate the disagreement about the switch: is the unbounded-speed requirement a premise of the argument, an artifact of this particular lamp, or something the argument is neutral about? Relatedly, if the contradiction can be blamed on any of several premises, say which ones a defender of supertasks would give up and what it would cost them.

4. Assess the two variants. Does the dimmer version dissolve the puzzle, relocate it to a question about continuity, or simply change the subject? Does the no-first-switching version show that one half of the argument was carrying all the weight, and does that tell us anything about how much the conclusion depends on the order structure of the task sequence?

5. Say whether anything in the argument supports the closing moral about the discreteness of time, or whether that is an inference the passage is not entitled to.

6. Flag any point at which my friend and I were disagreing only about words.

Where you are unsure, or where people who work on this genuinely disagree, say so rather than smoothing it over.
