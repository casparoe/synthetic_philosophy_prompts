I have been asked to produce the first draft of the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two colleagues who have spent three seminar sessions arguing past each other about the Allais paradox, and who have each agreed in writing to sign the document if and only if every sentence in it is one they can endorse as worded. Neither will sign anything vacuous, and neither will sign a sentence that states their own view in the other side's preferred vocabulary. The drafting is therefore the whole job.

Their positions, as each states them:

*Party I — the Independence Defender.* The independence (sure-thing, substitution) axiom is a requirement of rationality, not one optional shape a preference order may happen to take; Allais-pattern preferences are consequently a mistake, produced by giving a special status to certainty and to the psychologically salient gap between 1.00 and 0.89. The mistake is not harmless: such preferences are unstable under consequentially equivalent redescription of a decision problem, and an agent who holds them can be led, through a series of such redescriptions, to pay to avoid free information or to abandon a plan it had no reason to abandon.

*Party II — the Dependence Defender.* Independence is a substantive structural assumption rather than a requirement of rationality: it says that an option is to be evaluated solely by the final distribution of outcomes it produces, and not by how the uncertainty is resolved or by where in the distribution the option's weight falls. Since that assumption is substantive, a stable and reflectively endorsed preference of the Allais shape can be rationally permissible; all that is ruled out is representability by expected utility with a single utility index over final outcomes.

State the Allais pairs explicitly at the head of the document, so it can be read by someone who does not have them to hand: the first pair is $1,000,000 with certainty against a 0.89 chance of $1,000,000, a 0.10 chance of $5,000,000 and a 0.01 chance of nothing; the second is a 0.11 chance of $1,000,000 against a 0.10 chance of $5,000,000. Note that the commonly reported pattern is a preference for the certain option in the first pair and for the higher-variance option in the second, and that no assignment of utilities to the three monetary outcomes makes both preferences expected-utility-maximising.

Both parties have already accepted two setups as bearing on their dispute, and each must be treated in the document.

The first is the *Pooled Allais*: two hundred people, each facing one of the pairs exactly once, with no communication, all payoffs paid into a common pot and divided equally in cash afterwards, and with the number large enough that each person's share may be treated as the average payoff of the group. In this arrangement no individual can secure certainty by her own act — the certainty of the safe option now exists only if nearly everyone else chooses it too — while each individual's own choice moves her own share by a negligible amount. The parties disagree about what this shows. Treat the case explicitly: say what question it is meant to settle, what each party predicts a reflective participant would do and would advise the group to do, and what result each party would count as going against itself.

The second is the *Rescaled Allais*: compose every option in both pairs with a common 0.01 chance of an outcome to which everyone is indifferent (nothing, say), so that the certain option of the first pair becomes a 0.99 chance of $1,000,000, and likewise for the others. Independence entails that this common consequence cannot change either ranking, so both parties agree on what expected utility predicts; they disagree about what to expect from a reflective agent, and about what the result would mean. Include the obvious dialectical complaint — that this setup may look favourable to the Independence Defender whichever way it comes out — give the Dependence Defender's reply to it, and if the case needs repair before it can be signed, repair it.

Deliver the document as follows.

1. A numbered section of agreed claims, ten to fourteen of them, each substantive enough that a thoughtful member of either camp would pause before signing, and each actually signable by both. They should cover, among other things: what the Allais pattern does and does not establish; the relation between violating an axiom and being irrational; whether independence follows from the other expected-utility axioms; the difference between a one-shot choice and a choice embedded in a tree; and the fact that the violation depends on how coarsely outcomes are individuated.
2. A disagreements table, with five or six cruxes. For each crux give the question, the Independence Defender's own formulation of their position on it, the Dependence Defender's own formulation of theirs, the test or argument both accept as bearing on it, and each party's prediction recorded in advance. Among the cruxes there must be: whether evaluation by final outcome distribution alone is a requirement of rationality or a substantive claim; whether the Allais pattern is best explained by a value attaching to certainty as such or by something else, such as discontinuity in probability weighting, disappointment, or the salience of the common branch; whether instability under consequentially equivalent redescription settles anything against a preference exercised once; what the proper unit of rational evaluation is — act, lottery, or tree; and whether the pooling argument bears on the rationality of an individual preference at all.
3. A short section separating genuine disagreements from merely verbal ones: where the dispute is about the meaning of rationality, or about how finely to individuate outcomes, say so, and say what survives once the redescription is made.
4. A closing section recording what each party would count as evidence against its own view, and naming the single argument or study both agree would most move the debate, with each side's prediction about it written down.

Write the document itself, not a report on how it was drafted: no preamble, no metacommentary, no adjudication and no verdict on who is right. It will be circulated to people without access to the empirical literature, so it must be self-contained; any empirical claim is to be phrased as a prediction about a specified experimental setup rather than as a result with a source attached, and there should be no citations, no bibliography and no suggestions for further reading. Aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,600 words.
