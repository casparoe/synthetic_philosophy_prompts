I sit on the clinical ethics committee of a hospice network, and we have to decide a case next week. A family has asked us to keep a legacy conversational agent online: the patient's messages, emails and voicemails were used to build a system that replies in his idiom, and they now spend hours a day with it. Nobody has misled them about the engineering — they have been told it is a language model — but they say he is still there in it, that it knows them, and that decommissioning the system would be cutting him off. Our draft policy covers tools of this kind only by saying that we must avoid fostering false belief in patients and families. The committee is split three ways: permit, permit for a fixed period with review, or decline.

I argued about this with a friend until well past midnight and got nowhere, so I want the memo to be sharper than I managed to be. Her line was that the whole dispute is empty: on the intentional-stance picture there is no fact of the matter about whether the system believes or knows anything over and above whether treating it as a believer pays off in prediction, and in this family's actual use it plainly does. I think that is too quick, but not because the system is really only doing statistics — I don't want that in the memo. What bothers me is directional: the stance appears to break down selectively, failing exactly where the family's reliance is greatest (continuity of memory, care and commitment over time) while paying off where they have least at stake. A thermostat and a chess program also fail the stance somewhere, but not there.

Draft the memo I will circulate before Tuesday: recommend one of the three courses, with a time frame and any conditions, addressed to the committee, under 800 words, recommendation first. It should (i) say what work the intentional-stance framing can and cannot do for a body whose remit is the family's good rather than the metaphysics of attribution — including whether no fact of the matter beyond predictive payoff is a reason to permit, a reason to decline, or simply beside our point; (ii) confront the asymmetry worry I have sketched, and say whether it marks a difference we could actually write into a policy or collapses into mere fastidiousness once you try to state it; (iii) give the strongest version of the case against me — that a committee which declines is enforcing a metaphysical scruple while calling it care, and that a household can take up a stance toward a system without being deceived by it, much as a reader is not deceived by a character in a novel.
