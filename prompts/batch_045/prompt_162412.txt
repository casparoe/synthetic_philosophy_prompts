My group evaluates language models, and we have been trying to convert what Dennett calls the strange inversion of reasoning into an operational test. The rule we adopted is the substitution rule: if we can exhibit a mechanism that would generate a system's outputs without drawing on comprehension at any point in its operation, then the attribution of comprehension to that system is refuted, and any residual appearance of understanding is our own anthropomorphism. This looks like the inversion applied straightforwardly — Darwin showed that apparent design needs no designer and Turing that apparent thinking needs no thinker, so the burden of proof always sits with the comprehension claim, and a mechanistic account discharges it. We accept the consequence: since our own neurons are equally mindless, the rule seems to imply that our comprehension is illusory too.

What unsettled us is a result we call the scaffolding reversal. A small model that fails our comprehension probes when answering directly passes them when it is allowed to write intermediate notes and read them back before answering, even though those notes are, by the rule, nothing but further mindless token production, and the composite system's success is not shallow. So: is the substitution rule a faithful operationalization of the strange inversion, or does it misdescribe the explanatory order, treating comprehension as a posit that a mechanism can crowd out rather than as itself a competence that stands in need of mechanistic explanation? If it misdescribes it, what should we be measuring instead when we want to know whether a system that has bare competence on a probe also comprehends?
