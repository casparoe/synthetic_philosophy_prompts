For the past three years I have been keeping a small real-money prediction market — modest stakes — for about forty people, mostly colleagues and friends from a cycling club. It began as a way of settling arguments about race outcomes and the weather, and for a long time I assumed that if a member's prices are mutually consistent, that member cannot be systematically relieved of their money. Two episodes this spring have convinced me otherwise, and a third has left me unsure what consistency is even supposed to buy you.

In the first, a scrupulous member whose prices never contradict one another had for years treated certain outcomes as not worth pricing at all — "that won't happen" — and would happily write a ticket paying a fixed sum if such an outcome occurred, for a penny, or for nothing, since by his own reckoning he was taking money for nothing. Another member quietly bought several hundred of these tickets over eighteen months. Then one of the "impossible" outcomes occurred — a stage cancelled hours before the start because of a protest on the route — and the seller had to pay out many times what he had taken in. What I cannot get out of my head is that, at the time of each sale, nothing bad was happening by his own lights: the region of possibility from which the money eventually came was one he had simply closed off, so no outcome he took seriously ever left him worse off. In the second episode, a different member — equally coherent, and perfectly willing to revise his odds — accepts any bet he himself prices as fair, and even a slightly unfavourable one if the stake is small, for the pleasure of the wager. A third member who is simply better informed about cycling took the other side of a long series of these bets and has steadily drained him. Nothing here turns on inconsistency, yet the money moves in one direction. The two cases feel different in a way I want made precise: the first scheme could have been run by a bookie who knew nothing about cycling at all, working from a table of the victim's own published prices, and it pays no matter how the world turns out; the second requires the bookie to know something the victim does not, and it is not guaranteed at all — it pays in expectation and, over many bets, with something approaching certainty, while any individual bet could go the other way. (There is a wrinkle I find myself returning to: I put it to the victim that the mere fact of being offered a bet by a better-informed person ought to have told him something, and he found that unpersuasive.) A third pattern may or may not belong with these: several schemes people have shown me work only if the victim will stake arbitrarily large sums, or accept an arbitrarily long sequence of bets, with the guaranteed gain per round fixed and small while the stake needed to secure it grows; with a finite bankroll the bookie might well be ruined before the "sure" profit arrives. I cannot tell whether that is a separate problem or the first problem in a costume.

I have started using my own labels — "closed-book" for extraction that follows from the victim's own prices alone, and "open-book" for extraction that requires the bookie to know more — but I would rather use established vocabulary if there is any. My nephew, who is reading philosophy, said "Dutch book" when I described the first case; but what I found under that name seemed to concern people whose odds clash with each other, so that some package of bets wins against them in every outcome, and that is not what is happening in any of my three cases, or so I believe. So: is there an established concept, or a family of concepts, for extracting money from someone who is internally consistent? If these are really three different things, I want the joints — which of them is a defect in the victim's thinking, which is merely a defect in their information, and how one could tell the two apart in practice; and I especially want to know where the line falls between a loss that is guaranteed and a loss that is guaranteed only relative to what the victim admits as possible. If there is no established term, I would be grateful for a name together with a definition sharp enough to settle borderline cases. A bare label would not help me, because what I actually need is to explain to a club committee whether any of this counts as cheating.
