I run a casual philosophy club at my local library: about 15 adults, one 90-minute session, and nobody there has any formal philosophy training (including me). I've been reading on my own and I definately don't have a philosophy background. I've been reading about a couple of thought experiments and a weird decision rule that goes with them, and I want the group to feel why it pulls people in rather than just hear me explain it. From what I gather, one setup is called the Platonia dilemma: a rich person contacts 20 strangers and says each of them may send one telegram (or email) within 48 hours; if exactly one person sends it, that person gets a billion dollars, but if zero people send or more than one sends, nobody gets anything. They can't communicate, and everyone wants the money. The other setup is something called a Luring Lottery, which as I understand it is a lottery where the prize is divided by the number of people who enter: if only one person enters, that person gets the whole thing, but if a thousand enter, everyone gets a tiny slice. I might be getting the origianl details wrong on both, so if it's easier to run the activity with a cleaned-up version of the rules, please do that and just tell me what version you're using.

The decision rule I keep bumping into — I think it's called superrationality — says roughly this: you should decide by asking what strategy would be best if everyone else, being just as rational as you, ended up choosing the same strategy, and then you commit to that strategy. In a one-shot prisoner's dilemma this apparently means cooperate, whereas regular game theory says defect. In the 20-person Platonia case, the regular rational move seems to be to send the telegram for sure, because sending is never worse than not sending; but if all 20 do that, nobody wins. The superrational move is supposed to be something like: roll a 20-sided die (or otherwise randomize) and send only with a 1-in-20 chance, so that the probability exactly one person sends is as high as possible. I'm fuzzy on the Luring Lottery part, but my sense is that the lure is that entering feels individually smart even though it wrecks the prize for everyone, and I want people to feel that too.

Can you design a runnable activity or game for my group that makes them feel the force of this idea? The rules, incentives, or roles should be set up so that participants run into the coordination failure — and then the pull of the superrational response — themselves, before anyone names superrationality or the Platonia dilemma or the Luring Lottery. No lecture up front, and they shouldn't hear the name until afterwards. It can be a game with points, play money, candy, or a single small prize (we can use a 20-dollar bill or a bag of chocolates as the prize if a real stake helps, but I'd rather not need real money for payouts). Include the materials, the rules, a payoff scheme, a timeline for the 90 minutes, and the debrief.

What I want them to come away seeing is the difference between two questions: first, given what everyone else is likely to do, what is my best individual move? And second, given that we are all trying to figure out the same problem and will all land on the same answer, what should that answer be? I want them to see why the second question can feel almost magical or spooky — like your rolling a die somehow controls what the others do — and also why it might actually be justified when the group really is full of people reasoning the same way. I also want them to see where it breaks: if even one person is a jerk who always sends the telegram, or if you aren't sure how many others are superrational, the whole thing changes. And in the Luring Lottery part, I want them to feel whatever the real temptation is, even if my summary of the rules is off.

The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto parts of the idea: the moment everyone sends and nobody wins; the moment they get dice and can randomize and the expected payoff jumps; the moment they realize the assumption that everyone is alike is doing all the work; and the Luring Lottery moment. It should also be honest about what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — people may not get the payoff math, someone may try to communicate or collude, the randomization may not produce a winner in a small group, etc. — and how I should handle each. Finally, be frank about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and which part I, as facilitator, will just have to explain in the debrief.
