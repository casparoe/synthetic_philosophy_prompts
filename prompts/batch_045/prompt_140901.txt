hey — every take I read about Dutch bluntness vs Japanese indirection, or about whether white lies are ever ok, seems to be answering a different question than mine. So before I read more of them, I want the spec.

What I want it for: I'm a particular person about to say a particular sentence to a particular person, and I want to know whether to soften it and by how much. Not a scheme for classifying national character, not a ranking of cultures, not a general vibe about authenticity. If a candidate can't be cashed out as guidance for a speaker mid-conversation, that counts against it — but tell me if you think that's a real requirement or just my own prejudice, since maybe I'm asking for a user's manual where what's on offer is a moral theory.

So: write me the desiderata. What any adequate account of when to be blunt and when to soften has to deliver, with the case behind each. Here are the cases I think it has to get right — tell me which of mine are decoys and what I've left out.

- the no-stakes ritual: how are you / good, you? on a really awful week. Nobody wants the truth and nobody is taken in. Does a ban on saying what you believe to be false also cover this, and if not, what's the line between this and a lie?
- volunteered specificity: nobody asked, but you add the unflattering detail anyway. Is that different from giving the same information when asked?
- the mismatch: a speaker from a blunt milieu and a hearer from an indirect one, both trying to show respect. Whose convention sets the standard? And if it's the hearer's, then what about someone who talks one way to their Dutch colleague and another to their Japanese one — respect, hypocrisy, or something else?
- the transparency test: could you say out loud that you're softening things without the softening failing? I genuinely can't tell if this is the most important item on the list or a puritan hang-up, and I want your read.
- cheap for me, expensive for you: blunt feedback in front of the team, the referee report, the doctor, the teacher and the kid's terrible poem. Does it matter who bears the cost?
- the retrospective complaint: two years later, why didnt anyone tell me.
- protective pretense: someone with dementia asking for a relative who died. Is the pretense/deception distinction real, and does it carry weight, or is it a dodge?
- a stopping rule: thirty seconds of ritual is one thing, someone losing their chance to act is another. Where's the cutoff and what sets it?
- act or agent: is the theory grading the sentence or grading the person?
- universalization: if everyone were maximally blunt, compliments would carry no information and the habitually blunt person would stop being believed at all — bluntness would destroy the thing it's supposed to serve. Does a theory have to survive everyone adopting it?

Two things I care about more than the cases. First: which of these are genuine desiderata and which are the hang-ups of one camp, and which single requirement knocks out the most candidates? Second: flag any subset you think can't be jointly satisfied. My guess is there's a trilemma in there between getting people the information they need to act, deferring to the conventions the hearer actually expects, and being the same person with everyone — but I might have set that up wrong.

Then briefly, two lines each, or skip it if you think the spec already settles things: how do the usual contenders do? The absolute ban on saying what you think is false, act-consequentialism about honesty, the golden-mean/character-trait version, and the polite fictions are fine because they fool nobody line.

Last: say how confident you are overall and single out the one claim you're least sure of. I'll use that to calibrate you, so don't spread the hedging evenly — plant a flag somewhere.
