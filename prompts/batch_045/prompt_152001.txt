Hi — hoping you can help with something for the job I do alongside the PhD (I'm in STS, writing up on workplace AI and the labour process). Two days a week I'm with the new-technology team at a federation of trade unions. When an employer brings in some new bit of kit — warehouse pick-rate monitoring, an "AI assistant" for clinical coders, shift-scheduling software, automated marking, predictive-maintenance diagnostics — I write a one-page assessment that our reps then use in bargaining. I get about half a day per tech, I almost never get anything useful out of the vendor, and the people reading it are lay reps who will glaze over the second it turns theoretical. We do maybe fifteen a year, and the output has to be signed off by a committee before it goes anywhere near an employer.

What I want is a procedure, not another ethics checklist. And I want it actually grounded in Marx's account of machinery rather than a generic "is it fair, transparent and accountable" grid. The bits I keep coming back to: the difference between formal and real subsumption (capital taking over a labour process more or less as it finds it, versus capital revolutionising the labour process itself); the machinery chapter in Capital vol 1, including that bit where the worker in manufacture uses the tool while in the factory the machine uses the worker; the stuff in the Grundrisse fragment on machines about social knowledge and the general intellect becoming a direct productive force; relative surplus value (that machinery is a way of cheapening labour-power, not just a way of sacking people); and the reserve army — how far the technology widens or narrows the pool of people who could be hired to do the job. I don't want it to collapse into the deskilling thesis either — my supervisor thinks the Braverman version of that is too crude and I half agree.

Two things I've been messing about with in my own chapters that I'd like built in, if they survive contact with what Marx actually says:

- discretionary residue: after the kit goes in, how much judgement is still left with the worker — and, the bit that matters, whether that residue is codifiable, i.e. whether the calls workers make are being logged in a form that the next version of the system can learn from and eventually take over. Contrast with residue that's genuinely tacit and walks out the door when the worker does.
- replaceability gradient: how much the technology widens or narrows the set of people who could do the work, and how fast that happens.

So: give me a decision procedure I can actually run. Questions in a fixed order; what counts as a good enough answer to each when the vendor stonewalls or I can't get a number; where the procedure just stops with a no; and where it has to hand the decision to the reps because it's a question about what workers want rather than a question of fact. It must not reduce to a predicted headcount of jobs lost — half the time the headcount barely moves and the job is still wrecked, and I need it to say why in language a rep can use in a meeting.

Then run it on three cases: (1) a handheld scanner plus pick-rate monitor with a public league table in a distribution centre; (2) an AI clinical coding tool where a human coder signs off every code, which the employer is calling "augmentation" and which our reps have already publicly welcomed; (3) predictive-maintenance diagnostics on the railway, where the engineers genuinely like the tool because it kills the paperwork, but which logs every diagnostic judgement the engineer makes. I especially want to see it bite on (2), because I suspect the answer it gives is one we won't like and have already gone out on a limb against.

Finally, be straight with me about where it misfires: public-sector employers with no profit motive, co-ops and worker-owned outfits, kit that's announced with a big press release and then quietly never switched on, and the case where one tool upskills one grade while disciplining another. And if either of my two concepts above doesn't survive being turned into a checklist, say so and say why.

Cheers.
