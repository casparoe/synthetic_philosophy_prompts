I teach the Nyāya section of a first-year course on classical Indian epistemology, and for our reading week I am trying to replace the usual hill, smoke and fire illustrations with something that puts real weight on the apparatus: I want to run John Snow's investigation of the 1854 cholera outbreak in Soho as a case study in the pramāṇas, and see whether the machinery earns its keep. Please treat the following capsule as stipulated — it is from memory, so flag anything you are fairly confident I have misremembered, but do not stall on the history. Late in August 1854 an appalling outbreak hit the streets around Broad Street (now Broadwick Street); over a hundred people died within the first few days and some four hundred in total. Snow mapped the cases, spoke to residents with the help of the local curate Henry Whitehead, and identified a public pump as the source; his own chemical and microscopic examination of the water proved nothing conclusive. The parish authorities were eventually persuaded to remove the pump handle, though the epidemic may already have been in rapid decline, as Snow himself conceded. Among his striking data points: a brewery on the same street whose workers mostly drank beer or used their own well and largely escaped; a nearby workhouse with its own well that likewise escaped; and an elderly woman in Hampstead who had water carted to her from the Broad Street pump because she liked the taste, and who died of cholera with no local outbreak around her. In the second edition of his book he added what he called the grand experiment: in south London two water companies supplied interleaved streets, one drawing from the polluted central stretch of the Thames and one from an intake that had been moved upriver, and mortality in the houses supplied by the former was several times higher, in otherwise comparable streets. The reigning alternative was miasma from the filthy river; if I remember right, William Farr's own favoured regularity tied mortality to elevation above the river rather than to water supply. Snow died in 1858, and the organism was not isolated and generally accepted until the 1880s.

What I want first is a disciplined mapping rather than a general essay. Set out Snow's central argument as a parārthānumāna in the five-membered form — what exactly are the pakṣa, the hetu and the sādhya — and then test the hetu against the standard requirements (pakṣa-dharmatā, presence in the sapakṣa, absence from the vipakṣa, non-stultification, absence of a counterbalancing reason). My suspicion is that the honest hetu is not the pump as such but something like water contaminated by cholera evacuations, which nobody in 1854 could observe, so I would like your view on which hetvābhāsa the miasmatists were in fact entitled to charge: asiddha, because the supposed poison is unestablished; savyabhicāra, because the correlation is deviant; or satpratipakṣa, because they had a rival inference to a different conclusion. Say what the brewery, workhouse and Hampstead cases contribute — which of them is anvaya and which vyatireka, and whether Farr's regularity is best described, in Nyāya terms, as an upādhi vitiating the concomitance or as something the vocabulary does not reach. Then allot the evidential work among the other pramāṇas: how much of Snow's case is really śabda (neighbours, parish officers, death registers, the Hampstead family), whether lay reporters satisfy whatever conditions Nyāya places on an āpta, and whether the theory has any way of handling testimony that is only credible in the aggregate and individually weak. Is any step of the argument genuinely upamāna, or does this episode suggest that upamāna is idle in a case like this? And where does Snow's eliminative reasoning belong — can tarka carry as much of the load as it is being asked to carry here, given that Nyāya denies it the status of a pramāṇa?

Then I want the traffic in the other direction, and this is the part I care about most, because I think the case may be an anomaly and not an illustration. First: epidemiology is irreducibly contrastive and statistical, and as far as I can see Nyāya has no pramāṇa whose object is a rate difference between two populations. I have been toying with the claim that Snow's real hetu is not a property of the water at all but a relation between two populations — a contrast class — and that the five-membered syllogism has no slot for a hetu of that shape; please take that proposal seriously enough to say precisely where it fails, if it does. Second: there is a general worry about aggregation. Snow's conclusion seems to me to rest on a concatenation of pramāṇas, no one of which yields certainty on its own; so does Nyāya permit corroboration to accumulate across distinct pramāṇas, or is each pramāṇa supposed to deliver niścaya by itself, in which case Snow's cognition never rises above saṃśaya? Third: given parataḥ prāmāṇya, the natural Nyāya route to vindicating a cognition is confirmation by successful practice, and yet here the practical test is corrupted — the outbreak was already declining when the handle came off — so we have a true conclusion whose pragmatic confirmation misfires. Does that leave the theory able to credit Snow at all, does it force it into a retrospective Whiggism in which whoever later wins is deemed to have had knowledge, or is there a better Nyāya resource I am overlooking? Finally, and importantly for teaching purposes: identify what the theory simply does not speak to here, and say so plainly, rather than stretching it to cover the case. If, for example, the decision to remove the handle under uncertainty is a question about acting on a less-than-ascertained cognition that Nyāya does not address, I would rather have that stated than have a pramāṇa invented for it.
