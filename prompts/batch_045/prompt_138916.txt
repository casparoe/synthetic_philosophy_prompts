I'm about two-thirds of the way through a novel set in Thalwend, a river port whose Grain Court meets every spring to decide how much of the common store to sow in the silt lands below the flood wall, and I have written myself into a scene I can't make work. It is the ninth sitting after the Great Shift, when the river Sarn took a new channel and the old flood tables stopped meaning anything. Present are Warden Halia Merrow, who has fallen in with the new geometric doctrine — that the thing to maximize is not the average harvest but the long-run growth rate of the store, which for an even-money bet means staking one's edge, the difference between the chance of winning and the chance of losing, as a fraction of the whole; Keeper Oss Barrow, who is answerable for the Floor, the twelve barge-loads below which the city is legally dissolved and its people dispersed to the uplands; and a junior clerk, Jento, who has found something in the pre-Shift ledgers that he cannot get anyone to look at. The bet on the table: a barge-load sown in the silt comes back as two in a good year and as nothing at all in a bad one, and the Gauge readers say the odds of a good year are five seasons in nine — from a record of nine seasons, which everyone in the room knows is thin.

What I want is the scene itself: dialogue-heavy, a thousand to fifteen hundred words, self-contained, with the arithmetic right enough that a careful reader could check it in the margin. The arithmetic I have in mind is that the same doctrine yields three different answers depending on what you count as the store: the Court's discretionary surplus above the Floor is four barge-loads and the silt is worked in whole strips, so a ninth of the surplus is less than one strip, which is to say "don't sow"; a ninth of the Floor plus the surplus is nearer two strips; and if the upland city of Marrowgate's treaty promise — grain in a famine year at four times the going rate, if honored, worth perhaps twenty loads of relief — is counted as part of what the city has, the same ninth lands on the entire surplus. So "sow nothing," "sow two," and "sow everything" all come out of one rule. Neither of my two seniors should be a fool: I want the Warden to have the strong version of her case, including that halving the recommended stake costs her only about a quarter of the growth rate while insuring her against exactly the error the nine-season record invites, and that betting past twice the optimal fraction cancels the growth rate altogether; and I want the Keeper to have the strong version of his, which is that the Floor is not a low level of wealth but a boundary the city does not come back from, that log-arithmetic has no kink at that boundary, and that a city has no well-defined bankroll in the first place, since its fields, its people and its future harvests are not in the granary. Jento's discovery is this: in the old ledgers, the near terrace and the far terrace each returned exactly what was put into them over any two-year cycle — one doubling in a wet year and halving in a dry one, the other doing the opposite — and yet the clerks who moved half the grain from one to the other at the end of every year finished each cycle with about a quarter more than they began. Let him be unsure whether that is real grain or an error in the bookkeeping, and let the Warden and the Keeper fight over what it would mean if it were real, since it seems to bear on the very same question of where the boundary of a single store is drawn. Keep them in their own idioms — the Warden in ratios and seasons, the Keeper in mouths and winters, the clerk in ledger entries — and don't let anyone stop the scene to explain.

Afterwards, add a brief note, no more than a couple of hundred words, telling me as honestly as you can three things: first, which moves in that argument are mere arithmetic that anyone competent would grant once shown, and which rest on something genuinely unsettled — whether maximizing a growth rate is even the right target for a city with a dozen seasons left before the new channel chokes and a floor below which it ceases to exist, whether an uncertain edge is best handled by shrinking toward a fraction of the recommended stake or by some other rule, whether the terrace gain is grain or accounting; second, which of the three characters is in fact right about this sowing, and which single sub-question your verdict turns on; and third, what would change your mind — the one argument or consideration that would lead you to write the scene the other way round, with a different character vindicated.
