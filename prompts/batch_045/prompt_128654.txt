Been going in circles on the torture versus dust specks thing for literally years — you know the setup: either one person is tortured for fifty years, or an absurdly astronomical number of people (call it 3^^^3, or just "a number so big it makes your head hurt") each get one dust speck in the eye. I've never managed to settle it by arguing about it, so I want to try it from the other direction: here are my actual gut verdicts on a bunch of cases, including some I havn't seen anyone discuss, and I want to know whether anything I believe adds up to a principle.

(a) The standard one. I take the specks. Not cheerfully — annoying an enormous number of people for no reason feels like a genuine cost — but I take them.

(b) Same, except each person's "speck" is a discomfort of one thousandth of a just-noticeable-difference: nobody detects anything, ever, and it's not like one person gets a thousand of them, it's one each, though a thousand landing in the same person at once would be a barely noticeable pinch. Also, if you asked each of them beforehand, they'd rather have it than give up one second of their favourite song, and they wouldn't cross the street to avoid it. Now I take the specks even more readily than in (a). Two things bug me: it feels like I dissolved the problem by making the harm invisible rather than solving it; and taking each person's own valuation seriously seems to commit me to adding up astronomical numbers of tiny preferences, which is exactly the move I refused in (a).

(c) Bump the severity instead of the number: the same astronomical number of people each get a broken arm (hurts like hell for a few weeks, heals fine), versus one person tortured for fifty years. I flip. I take the torture. I think I also flip for one day of migraine each, and probably for one bad hour each.

(d) So fill in the middle: one speck each, then one minute of real stinging each, then one bad hour, then a migraine day, then a broken arm. Somewhere along there my verdict flips and I cannot say where. I also can't tell whether that's just ordinary vagueness on my part or a sign that I've been aggregating the whole time and laundering it through a fudge factor for tiny stuff.

(e) Hold the total fixed and change the spread. Assume for the sake of the argument that there's an additive scale of discomfort, so I can say "the same total, arranged differently" — I know that assumption begs part of the question, I want to see what follows from it anyway. A billion units: a billion people with one each, I take the specks. A thousand people with a million each, spread across their lifetimes, I genuinely don't know. One person with all billion, as constant low-grade agony for decades, I take the torture easily. I have no idea where between the first and the last it turns, and I don't trust any answer that has it turning at some precise number of units per person.

(f) I don't get told how many people there are. Maybe a billion, maybe astronomical, and I have to choose now. My verdict depends on a number I will never know, and I freeze. What should I pick, and what does it say about a principle that leaves me stuck like that?

(g) The mirror image with benefits: either the astronomical number of people each get one tiny pleasure (a single whiff of something lovely, one extra tasty crumb) or one person gets pulled out of fifty years of torture. I lean toward the tiny pleasures, which is not the mirror of (a), and I can't tell whether that's me being inconsistent or whether harms and benefits really come apart here — something about imposing versus helping, or about leaving someone in agony, or about ruined lives. It's my least trustworthy verdict, so throw it out if you need to, but say why.

My best stab so far is: add up the badness within a single life, never add across lives, then rank options by whoever ends up worst off, maybe letting the number of people at each level break ties when the worst-off lives are close in badness. I think that gets (a), (b) and the easy end of (e), and I think it loses (c) and (d), and I've no idea what it says about (f) or (g). Test it against my cases, repair it if it can be repaired, replace it if it can't, and if you give me more than one candidate, tell me which of my verdicts each one has to overturn. Where a clause is doing real work, say which case forced it. And give me its verdicts on two or three cases I haven't listed — your pick, especially anything where the harms differ in kind rather than in degree, or where the same small harm lands on the same person over and over instead of once on each of many people. No citations or reading lists please, I've read enough of the threads; I just want the shape of the thing.
