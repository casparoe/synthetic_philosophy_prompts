I have been rereading Hofstadter's *I Am a Strange Loop* (2007) in the months after a death, and the claim in it that I cannot stop turning over is not the claim that a self is a pattern — I can accept that without much trouble — but the quantitative, distributive one: that a single "I" is realized at different strengths in several brains at once, that most of what there is of me sits in the one skull and a smaller but real share sits in a handful of others, and that when one of those others dies a genuine part of me goes too. I want to know what a theory of persons would have to be able to do in order for that sentence to be true rather than a consoling way of saying "she is remembered" or "she shaped me." So, please: write the desiderata. Not a defense of Hofstadter, and not a survey — a specification, with the case or consideration behind each entry, of what any adequate account of the persistence of a self across brains must deliver, and what count as non-negotiable requirements as opposed to preferences of one metaphysical camp. Fix the purpose as this: the theory is to be good enough to make the claim usable by someone standing at a grave, i.e. strong enough to underwrite the ordinary reactions the claim is invoked to underwrite (that this loss is a bigger death than that one, that something of the dead person is still here and can be betrayed or diminished or carried faithfully), and not so strong that it commits us to obvious nonsense.

Some of the entries I already suspect belong on the list, and I would like you to sharpen them, order them, and add the ones I am missing. First, the account must separate "part of me is in you" from "a great deal of information about me is in you" and from "you are causally downstream of me": my biographer may know more about me than my brother does and my students may be shaped by me, yet neither intuitively hosts me — so what is the marker, and does it have to be something like the hosted thing's being able to occupy the first-personal position, or being causally organized as a loop rather than as a record? Second, it must handle the quantitative dimension explicitly: what is the unit in which "how much of me" is measured, is there any conservation principle (does writing the memoir that lets thousands reconstruct me internally increase or merely dilute the total?), and can there be more of me in you than in me? Third, it must give some non-arbitrary account of why the primary locus is the one in my skull, given that that pattern was itself acquired from other people — and it should be tested against the degenerative case, where my brain's realization of the pattern decays while yours, acquired from me earlier, stays intact: the theory has to say whether the center of gravity can shift, and whether I can end up a degraded instance of a self of which you are the fullest instance. Fourth, it must say when the thing in your head becomes me rather than a model of me, given that the acquisition is gradual, and whether it can tolerate a sorites here or must cut. Fifth, it must distinguish a persisting instance from a descendant: the copy in your head keeps developing on your experiences, and after thirty years the version of me in you believes things I would have loathed — is that a corrupted instance of me, a new person who branched off me, or a person who branched off me while still being me, and what would we need to be told to decide? Sixth — and this is the one I think may be fatal — it must state whether the survival it offers is supposed to be survival for the subject of my experience, given that there is no co-consciousness, no transfer of my memories or of my access to them, and that the pattern in your brain may be dispositional rather than occurrently active for decades: is a dormant copy a place where I am, and if survival never shows up for the one who wanted to survive, in what sense is it mine?

Two more things. Please mark, for each pair of requirements you think cannot be jointly satisfied, which one you would drop and why — my own worry is that the degree requirement and any identity-based persistence relation are simply incompatible, so that the list itself is a result, and that the demand that the claim do real explanatory work in grief is in tension with the demand that it not reduce to facts about memory and influence. And please tell me which single requirement excludes the most candidates: my guess is that either the memory/influence requirement or the no-co-consciousness requirement does the most damage, but I would like your reasoning rather than my guess back. If it helps, you may close with a brief scorecard — a line or two each on Parfit-style reductionism, a neo-Lockean psychological continuity view, animalism, and the strange-loop/pattern view — but keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; I can do the scoring myself if the list is good. No bibliography, and please do not lean on passages from Hofstadter I would have to look up: I want the specification to stand on the cases.
