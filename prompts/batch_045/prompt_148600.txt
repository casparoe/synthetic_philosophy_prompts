So this is one of those annoying situations where a genuinely practical problem turned out to be a philosophy thing. I got drafted onto the deployment-review committee at the place I work (five of us: two ML people, a red-team lead, a policy person, and me, and Im pretty sure I'm only there as the token person who says "but what if" a lot). Last month we hit something I still cant get straight. Two people were recused, so it was three of us voting on three propositions for a model we'll call Atlas-3: P = Atlas-3 can autonomously copy its own weights out of our staging environment given the access it currently has; Q = if a model can do that, it must not ship behind the current API-only tier; R = Atlas-3 must not ship behind the current API-only tier, which is just P and Q together. I voted yes/yes/yes. Priya (red team) voted yes on P, no on Q, no on R, because she thinks a capability like that can be mitigated by monitoring so the conditional is too strong. Dev voted no on P, yes on Q, no on R. So: majority yes on P, majority yes on Q, majority no on R. Depending on whether you add up the votes on the premises or on the conclusion you get opposite decisions, and we went with ship, mostly because nobody could say out loud why the other way was the right one. I've since been poking around and it looks like this has a name — something like the discursive dilemma or doctrinal paradox — and that there are a lot more possible rules than the two we were fumbling between. I can't get at any of the actual papers from my work laptop, so please don't give me a reading list or citations, I literally can't open them; I need something that works from first principles.

What I actually want is a decision tree / quiz I can put in front of the committee before the next release rather than after it, so we pick a rule in advance and stop reinventing it under time pressure. Think ten questions, each a concrete mini-case in the same deployment-review setting with three answer options, where one of the options is always something like "no decision, send it back to staff." No question should name a procedure or ask anyone which label they like — I want them sorted by what they think the board should do in the case, and then told which rule they've been defending. By a show of hands I think we'd land all over the place, and the two engineers will probably want to just talk until we agree instead of voting at all, so that has to be somewhere in the tree too.

A few things I want you to build in, because they're the parts I keep going in circles on:

- Please build the tree from the logical space and not from a list of rule names: start with the branching questions and the combinations of answers they actually permit, then locate the named rules inside that space, and mark the combinations nobody occupies with one line on why — incoherent, unattractive, or just unoccupied because nobody bothered.
- Leaves I want to be able to reach, at minimum: vote on the conclusion only; vote on the premises with one uniform threshold; vote on the premises with a different threshold per proposition; weighted or deferential voting where the specialist's judgment counts for more on the factual premise but not on the normative one; pooling what everyone knows and deciding on the evidence rather than aggregating the votes; minimal-distance style merging (pick the consistent bundle closest to what people actually said); a sequential or priority-ordered rule where you settle the most important proposition first and fill in the rest; deliberate-until-unanimous with some default if we run out of time; and some version of "this whole exercise is illegitimate, let the chair decide."
- The twists that have to show up as branch nodes, because they're exactly what our argument was about: (a) stakes asymmetry — a missed real capability on P is, by our own charter, much worse than attributing one that isn't there, so maybe P should need a different threshold than Q; (b) specialist deference — Priya's yes on P is arguably better evidence than mine and Dev's put together; (c) what happens if R only follows from P and Q probabilistically rather than deductively; (d) agenda gaming, where someone notices that adding a fourth proposition to the ballot flips the outcome and proposes it; (e) a member whose own three votes are internally inconsistent, so the premise and conclusion counts both look fishy; (f) whether the answer changes if we split P into two finer-grained propositions and vote on those instead.
- Flag any pair of leaves your instrument genuinely can't separate rather than pretending it can. My hunch is that in the plain three-person, three-proposition case, "vote on the conclusion" and any minimal-distance rule give the same answer every time, so nothing I've described so far tells them apart — tell me if that's right and what extra case would pry them apart.
- End with a scoring key: answer patterns mapped to leaves, plus a note on which patterns are unstable, i.e. where changing one answer flips the result to a totally different rule, so I know which questions are actually carrying the weight and which are decoration.

One more constraint that comes from real life: whatever we output gets handed to a higher committee, so each leaf should also carry one line on its most awkward practical commitment — the thing that will make somebody on the board go "wait, we'd be on record as believing that?" For instance the awkward bit with premise voting here is that the board ends up officially blocking release on the strength of a conclusion two of the three of us voted against, and I want that kind of sentence for every branch we might end up on.
