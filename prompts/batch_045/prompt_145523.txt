I'm a third-year undergraduate in a joint history-and-philosophy seminar that has spent about half the term on the Great Divergence, and I'm writing my term paper on whether "why Britain and not China?" is even a well-posed question. What I keep running into is that the debate looks stalled in a very specific way. On one side you have accounts that treat divergence as late and contingent: England and the Yangzi Delta looked broadly comparable on commercialization, living standards and market institutions well into the eighteenth century, and what broke the tie was a bundle of accidents — accessible cheap coal sitting near navigable water, and the windfall of New World land and slave-grown staples that let one region escape the resource constraints the other hit. On another side you have accounts built on factor prices: high wages and cheap energy in Britain made labour-saving machinery profitable to invent and adopt, so the direction of technical change was an equilibrium response to prices rather than a fluke. On a third you have institutions and politics: secure property rights, constraints on expropriation by the crown, and the fiscal–military state. On a fourth, a story about useful knowledge — a culture of tinkering, measurement and public experimental demonstration that made invention cumulative. And then there's the real-wage and GDP-reconstruction work that keeps relocating the divergence in time, sometimes early (a slow widening from the sixteenth or seventeenth century), sometimes dramatically late, which quietly changes which of the other stories is even in the business of explaining anything. Everyone in the seminar has read enough to know all of this; the same four or five explanations get asserted, each camp produces a case the others can't handle, nobody concedes, and after weeks we've moved zero. So I don't want another survey of the explanations.

My own hypothesis, which I'd like tested rather than flattered, is that the impasse isn't really about which factor mattered — it's about three things nobody fixes before arguing. First, the contrast class: the question slides between why Britain rather than this one other civilization, why northwestern Europe rather than the rest of Eurasia, and why sustained growth happened anywhere at all, which is a question about a rare event rather than about a comparison. Second, the baseline: it's never settled whether China is the deviant case that needs explaining or Britain is, and I suspect a lot of the heat comes from that unargued choice, because "why didn't China industrialize" and "why did Britain, uniquely, industrialize" invite different answers and different standards of success. Third, the unit of comparison: China, the Yangzi Delta, a delta county, England, Britain, Europe — and each shift moves the divergence date and therefore the set of admissible causes. I also tried a design idea on my professor, borrowed from a friend in econometrics: a within-Europe holdout test, where you take the factors the literature proposes and ask what each predicts about regions that had some of them and still didn't industrialize first — the Netherlands with its capital markets and high wages, Bohemia with coal and iron, Catalonia with proto-industry and coal nearby, plus Tokugawa Japan and Ottoman Egypt as non-European near misses — on the theory that a factor which doesn't discriminate within Europe isn't doing the work it's credited with. My professor thought this was naive and that the answer would just be that causes are conjunctural and probabilistic, which struck me as a way of making the literature unfalsifiable, but I may be wrong about that.

So here's what I actually want. Diagnose the structure of this disagreement using what each camp is really committed to: is it a substantive dispute about historical fact and causal magnitude; a verbal or methodological dispute about the contrast class, the unit of comparison and the baseline; a disagreement about what would count as evidence for a singular, conjunctural historical outcome; or several different questions travelling under one title? Where my hypothesis is wrong, say why. And then, given whatever diagnosis you give, tell me honestly what would count as progress — a distinction that gets drawn, a design like the holdout test done properly, a shift to a better-posed question such as asking about rates of growth rather than first-mover status — and whether that progress is realistically available, or whether a one-off outcome with a handful of candidate conjunctures is just underdetermined by any evidence we could in principle have, in which case I'd rather write a paper arguing that than one pretending to adjudicate.
