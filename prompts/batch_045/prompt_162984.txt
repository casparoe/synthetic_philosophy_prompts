I run a tiny reading group — six of us, none of us academics — and this month we read one of those brisk popular-history books arguing that nations are a modern invention, and I need to know how much of its version of things to trust before I lead the discussion on Thursday. Its capsule goes roughly like this: before about 1800 nobody had a national identity at all, people thought of themselves as villagers, or as subjects of a crown, or as members of a religion; then Hobsbawm showed that most of what looks immemorial — Highland tartanry, royal pageantry, flag rituals, anthems — was stitched together in the late nineteenth century by states scrambling to manage new mass electorates, which makes it essentially propaganda with fake antiquity painted on; and Anderson supplied the mechanism, print capitalism, where profit-driven printers and newspapers started churning out books and papers in vernacular languages so that strangers reading the same things on the same morning could picture themselves as one people, which is what he means by imagined community, i.e. a fiction held together by the media. The book then draws a moral: national attachments are manufactured, so whoever controls the presses can dial them up or down more or less at will, and since the whole thing is only a couple of centuries old and rests on a made-up story, we should expect it to fade and build political loyalty on something with real substance instead — class, or cosmopolitan institutions.

Two things make me suspicious. First, one member of the group whose family is Kurdish, and another who grew up in Catalonia, both got annoyed and said the book keeps sliding between invented, fake and recent, and that Hobsbawm himself apparently spends time on popular patriotic feeling that is not elite-managed and not modern in the same way; they also pointed out that plenty of state-run national projects fail while some stateless ones hold together for generations, and I couldnt tell whether that is a genuine problem for the account or something it already has an answer to (I half remember something in Anderson about official nationalism and about later nationalisms copying ready-made models, but I may be mixing that up with something else). Second, I tried to sort it out myself and came up with what might be a fake distinction: inventing a tradition (a practice you claim is ancient), inventing a nation (a people you claim was always there), and inventing the legitimacy of a state — and I suspect Hobsbawm's mechanism and Anderson's are really doing work on different ones of these rather than on the same thing, which would mean the book is leaning on a single account for three separate claims.

So: can you go through the capsule more or less point by point and tell me what a historian or political theorist of nationalism would accept as fair, what is a routine simplification you'd tolerate in a popular book, and what is the kind of distortion the two of them explicitly guard against? I have a feeling Anderson says somewhere that communities shouldnt be judged by whether they are genuine or fabricated but by the style in which they are imagined, and that the book's imagined-means-imaginary reading gets him backwards, but I'm relying on half-memory, so correct me if I've got that wrong. More specifically: does invented amount to fake, or does an invention have to work with materials that are already there and become genuinely binding once it takes? Is the plasticity claim — that elites can manufacture national feeling pretty much where and when they like — something either of them is actually committed to, or is it an overstatement of a thesis about social engineering from above? Do the Kurdish- and Catalan-style cases cause real trouble, or does the standard account absorb them, and if so at what cost? And does the book's political conclusion — debunk nationalism's origins and you undermine its claims on us, so shift loyalty to class or cosmopolitan institutions — really follow from the corrected version, or is that a non-sequitur from a genealogy? I need something I can say out loud to a group where two people are already irritated, so a clean way of stating what X is invented does and does not imply would recieved very gratefully.
