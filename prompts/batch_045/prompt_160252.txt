hey — I'm giving a talk in two weeks at the interpretability reading group where I'm doing a stint this summer, and what I actually need is Q&A prep, not slide feedback.

The room: about twelve people. Most are mechanistic interpretability people — CS PhDs and research engineers who spend their days training sparse autoencoders, doing attribution patching, moving activations around with steering vectors, arguing about whether a given direction is one feature or two (the feature-splitting thing). They care whether a claim cashes out in something you can go do in a notebook, they get impatient fast with anything that sounds like it could have been written before 2015, and their default attitude toward content talk is somewhere between "useful shorthand" and "shut up and compute." Two exceptions. One senior person reads philosophy of mind and is basically a Dennett-style instrumentalist about content. One postdoc likes structural-realism-flavoured talk about the network tracking causal structure in the world. Both will end up agreeing with different halves of what I say, which is its own problem.

My thesis, roughly: I want to argue that ascriptions like this direction in activation space represents P are claims we should be realists about, but that what they're claims about is not what either side in the usual fight thinks. The usual fight: does the state carry information about P (tracking, correlation, teleosemantic stories), or is content talk just instrumental shorthand for predicting behaviour. I say the load-bearing condition is a third thing — whether the downstream computation consumes the state in a P-sensitive way, i.e. whether swapping in a state that differs from the original only in P would make the downstream computation do the P-appropriate thing. Call it the consumption condition.

Then the part I'm actually nervous about: I want to add that the consumption condition is only well-defined relative to a contrast class — the set of foil states you could have swapped in. Same direction, two contrast classes (one where P varies together with its usual correlates, one where the correlates are held fixed and P varies alone), two different verdicts on whether the condition is met, so arguably two different features. Feature identity gets indexed to a contrast class; I've been calling them contrast-relative features. The conclusion is meant to be that realism survives but the realist has to swallow a relativity that looks a lot like the instrumentalist's relativity, and that the relativity is benign because the contrast class gets fixed by whichever intervention you actually care about.

What I want from you: the eight-ish objections I'll really get, ordered by how much damage they'd do, phrased the way this audience would phrase them — in terms of steering, SAE reconstruction, off-target effects of patching, feature splitting, whats the baseline, not in terms of tracking or teleosemantics or Fodor. For each: how much force it actually has versus how much of it is just vocabulary friction, a two-minute reply, and where that reply is soft. Also: what I should concede in the first five minutes before anyone asks anything (I think there's something about the train-an-SAE-then-probe-it-for-P pipeline that I should just hand over), what I have to hold or the whole thing collapses, the weakest joint in my own argument that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss, and the one question I'm praying nobody asks.

Format is 45 minutes with maybe 15 for questions, and the senior instrumentalist is going to be polite about it, which somehow makes it worse.
