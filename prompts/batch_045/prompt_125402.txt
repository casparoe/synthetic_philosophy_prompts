I wrote the following argument for a seminar paper on Leibniz, and it now proves considerably more than I want it to, which makes me suspect I have smuggled something in. I cannot locate where.

(1) Optimism: the actual world W is the best of all possible worlds — no possible world is better than W, all things considered.

(2) Call W′ a *surgical variant* of W with respect to an event E if W′ is exactly like W except that E does not occur, every event causally downstream of E being replaced by whatever would have happened had E not occurred. (I invented this notion because the phrase "a world just like this one except that…" seemed to be doing all the work in the arguments I had read, and I wanted it out in the open.)

(3) Take an actual evil E — a child dying untreated of malaria, say — and suppose its causal aftermath is itself bad or neutral: she dies unobserved, and the only things downstream of her death are further suffering. Then the surgical variant W−E is worse than W in no respect and better in one, since it contains strictly less suffering and loses nothing that is good.

(4) If a world is worse in no respect and better in some respect, it is better all things considered.

(5) So W−E is better than W, and optimism is false.

(6) Nothing in (2)–(4) depends on which evil we take. For an evil that a theodicy calls necessary to a greater good — the suffering that makes compassion possible, the wrongdoing that makes forgiveness possible — the surgical variant retains the downstream good and deletes only the evil, so it is better by exactly the argument above. The reasoning therefore refutes not optimism alone but every greater-good theodicy, and it entails that no world containing any evil at all could be the best: the best possible world would have to be entirely free of evil.

That last consequence is stronger than anything I believe, and the argument seems to impale itself on either horn. If the Leibnizian replies that surgical variants are never available, because nothing can genuinely be held fixed — on his own view every substance mirrors the whole world, so no event can be peeled off — then no world has any better variant, "best of all possible worlds" comes out true of every world indifferently, and optimism says nothing.

A friend tells me step (4) fails, on the ground that values form organic unities and the value of a whole is not fixed by the values of its parts. That sounds to me like a label for the conclusion rather than an answer to it.

Which step should I reject, why does that step look sound when it is not, and what does each of the candidate rejections cost me? If I must choose, I would rather keep (4) and (6) than rescue optimism by making it vacuous, and I would rather not surrender the claim that God had a reason to create this world rather than a worse one — but I do not trust that preference, so tell me if it is the wrong thing to protect.
