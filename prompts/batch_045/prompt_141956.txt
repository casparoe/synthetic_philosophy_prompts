I chair a committee that, for the first time in our institution's history, has to write down in public how much weight our selection processes may give to an applicant's membership in a group that is under-represented or historically disadvantaged. I did not seek the role. I took it after a complaint from a rejected applicant forced us to concede that what we actually do is an unwritten set of reflexes: it differs from committee to committee, it changes with whoever happens to be in the room, and no one can explain it to the person it disappoints. The rules we produce will govern several thousand decisions a year, made by rotating panels of faculty, clinicians and alumni readers who receive a single two-hour training session and who are neither philosophers nor lawyers. Individual files get minutes, not hours. The finished document has to fit on one page a reader can follow at a desk, has to survive a legal complaint from someone who was not chosen, and has to be defensible at a senate meeting where a substantial minority believes any group-conscious selection is simply wrong and another substantial minority believes our present practice is timid to the point of dishonesty. Sign-off requires both our general counsel and the senate.

This is not an abstract assignment for me. I was admitted to this university through a regional access scheme that was later abolished, and I have never been able to decide whether what happened to me was a correction or a mistake. I would rather not write a document whose real function is to let me feel better about that.

The constraints I have been handed, which I do not want re-litigated, are these. For our own admissions and hiring, counsel says we may not allocate fixed numbers of places to groups, and that every candidate must be assessed as an individual rather than as a representative. Separately, a national rule requires that neither sex hold fewer than forty per cent of the seats on the board of the teaching hospital we operate, with a real sanction for non-compliance; there, a number is required rather than forbidden. So the procedure has to work in both directions.

What I want from you is the procedure itself, written so that a committee could run it and so that a reader could follow it. As I see it, that means five things.

First, the ordered questions a committee must work through before adopting, retaining or discarding any group-conscious element of a selection process; for each question, what would count as a sufficient answer; the point at which the answer is no and the procedure simply stops; and the points at which it has to stop and hand the matter to a named person, because what is left is a disagreement about values rather than a question of fact. I care a great deal about that last category being marked honestly rather than disguised as a further factual test.

Second, a set of distinctions I think the procedure has to carry, and which I would like you either to vindicate or to replace with better ones. Does the element reorder candidates for a fixed number of places, or does it change how many places there are or who is in the pool at all? Under-representation relative to what denominator: the population, the applicants, the people holding the relevant credentials, or the people who would have applied and qualified had the practice never existed? Who gains from a selection and who bears the cost of a bad one, and are they the same people? Can the identical mechanism be defensible as a guaranteed minimum yet indefensible as a ceiling, and if so what does the difference consist in? And since group membership is almost always justified as a proxy for something else — disadvantage suffered, a perspective brought, legitimacy conferred on the institution — what would have to be re-checked, and how often, for that proxy still to hold, particularly where the practice has been running for two or three generations and some families have now used it repeatedly?

Third, a distinction I have not listed but should have, if you think one matters more than any of mine.

Fourth, the procedure applied end to end to three cases from our own institution: first-year undergraduate admission, roughly thirty thousand applications for two thousand places, read holistically, no numbers permitted; selection for the forty clinical training places attached to our teaching hospital, where the costs of a bad choice fall largely on future patients rather than on the successful or unsuccessful applicant; and appointment to the hospital board, where the legal percentage requirement applies, the sanction is real, and the pool of people with the relevant experience is thin enough that the same handful of names keeps recurring. Run them honestly, including whatever the procedure says about the second case that I would rather not hear. I would rather have one case come out against what we currently do than three tidy confirmations.

Fifth, what the procedure cannot do. Which of the disagreements in my senate are genuine conflicts of value that no procedure will settle? What will this procedure systematically misclassify or get wrong? And how might a committee that has already decided what it wants use the procedure to launder that decision — what would I look for, as chair, to tell the difference between a committee that followed the procedure and one that reverse-engineered it?
