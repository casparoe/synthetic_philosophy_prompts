I'm in a final-year philosophy of mind seminar, and our last block is on the meta-problem of consciousness: the project of explaining, without helping oneself to phenomenal notions, why we find the hard problem compelling in the first place — why we deploy the concepts we do, why we issue the introspective judgments we issue, and why those judgments strike us as peculiarly authoritative and hard to correct from the third-person standpoint. The seminar split almost evenly: one half argued that a completed solution to the meta-problem would leave the hard problem entirely intact, the other that it would quietly dissolve it, and by the end I wasn't convinced the two halves were trying to explain the same phenomenon.

To force the issue I invented a case, and I've been trying it out on friends. Call it the grafted monitor. Neuroengineers can build a synthetic introspective monitor: a device that reads a subject's first-order sensory and motor states and issues reports in the first-person present tense ("it now looks thus-and-so", "there is something it is like, and it is like this"). It can be grafted in place of the subject's native introspective system. Three findings: (i) reports after grafting are fluent, confident and behaviourally indistinguishable from native ones, and subjects cannot tell, even when told, whether their monitor is native or grafted; (ii) the grafted monitor can be systematically miscalibrated so that its outputs drift away from the first-order states, and the subject's downstream judgements, choices and emotional responses track the monitor rather than the first-order states; (iii) when asked whether it really seems that way or whether the device merely says so, subjects report no gap — the seeming just is whatever the monitor delivers.

Could you write me a dialogue of roughly 900–1100 words between two philosophers, Mara and Dev, who meet for coffee after a conference panel to argue about it: Mara holds that solving the meta-problem dissolves the hard problem, Dev holds that the meta-problem is itself hard. Requirements: (1) they must be equally competent — no concessions that a real defender would refuse, no cheap moves like "you're just denying the obvious"; (2) they must actually engage the graft case, and in particular disagree explicitly about whether the subject in finding (iii) is a partial zombie and about whether that question is answerable even in principle; (3) Dev should press the specification problem — what, exactly, does the introspective system represent states as having, and can that be specified in functional or structural terms without covertly drawing on phenomenal concepts — and Mara should have a substantive reply rather than a shrug; (4) they should also isolate a distinction I think the dispute turns on: explaining the occurrence of a judgement versus explaining its apparent rational credentials from the subject's own point of view, and take opposed views on which the meta-problem is answerable for; (5) somewhere near the end, they should each say, as part of an adversarial-collaboration protocol, what development — empirical or conceptual — would count as evidence against their own view, without either of them conceding.

Please identify positions by description rather than by author or paper title, and don't include any bibliography, references or footnotes — I only need the argumentative structure, since I'm writing this up as a seminar response and will attach the reading myself.
