I write literary fiction, and the final third of the novel I'm finishing turns on a document my narrator's mother leaves behind: a long letter, written in the months before her death, explaining why she did something she was never caught for and never punished for — a betrayal of a friend who trusted her, in exchange for something she badly wanted and got. She is not meant to be a villain or a barroom sophist; she is meant to be the most intelligent person in the book, and her defense is supposed to be genuinely hard to answer rather than merely clever. The intellectual spine of the letter is Glaucon's challenge in Plato's Republic, and I've been working from an account of it that a philosopher friend gave me over dinner, which he has since declined to elaborate on, saying only that I should be careful which parts I put in her mouth. His version, as I noted it down the next morning, ran roughly as follows: Glaucon picks up where Thrasymachus left off and presses the claim that morality is a convention with no basis in nature — justice, on this telling, is a set of rules invented and imposed by the strong, which the weak internalize and obey because they lack the power to break them with impunity. The ring of Gyges is his proof: a shepherd who finds a ring that makes him invisible promptly uses it to seduce the queen, kill the king, and take the throne, and the point is that any one of us would do the same, since fear of being seen and punished is the only thing that has ever restrained anybody. So no one is just willingly; being just is simply the price of being weak; and the whole question of why one should be moral reduces to the question of whether one can get away with it. Plato's answer, which supposedly occupies the rest of the Republic, is that justice is the better long-run wager after all: the unjust person's soul is disordered and his appetites never satisfied, so injustice is the worse bargain, and a clear-eyed person should choose justice because it pays out more in happiness.

The part I'm most suspicious of is that last sentence, because it makes Plato sound as though he is simply offering a better calculation of self-interest than the cynics, and my friend did concede that this was rough before changing the subject. But I don't trust my own instincts on the rest, and I'd rather find out now than after a reviewer with a classics degree points at a page. So: please go through the characterization more or less line by line and tell me what someone who actually works on the Republic would accept as right, what they'd call a tolerable simplification, and what they'd say is just wrong. In particular I want to know whether it's fair to describe the challenge as Glaucon's own view rather than something he is ventriloquizing and asking to have refuted; whether the little genealogy of law has the direction of power right about who makes the compact and why; whether the ring story and the other thought experiment I half-remember — the perfectly unjust man with a reputation for justice, set against the perfectly just man with a reputation for injustice — are doing the same work or two different jobs, and whether I lose anything by only using the ring; and whether it matters that the Greek term in question is closer to justice or righteousness than to what we now call morality.

What I ultimately need from this is the strongest and most charitable version of the challenge that an intelligent non-philosopher could credibly put in a letter: which of the claims above I should cut so that the argument is honest rather than conveniently cynical, which I should keep, and whether the conclusion my character wants to draw — that in her circumstances she was rationally permitted to do what she did — follows from anything true in the story as my friend told it, or only from the parts of it that a defender of Plato's position would reject.
