Me and my friend Priya ended up having this out for about two hours after our reading group last night, and I went home more confused than when I started. Shes half way through a PhD in political theory, I'm a software engineer who reads this stuff for fun, so assume I roughly know Berlins negative/positive liberty distinction and the neorepublican idea that freedom is really freedom from arbitrary power, but not the secondary literature. She wrote out an argument on her phone that seems to show nobody is ever free, and I couldn't find the hole in it. Here it is as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) To be free is not to be dominated: I am unfree when some other agent has the power to interfere in my choices on terms they can set for themselves, whether or not they ever actually interfere.
(2) A power stops dominating when whoever holds it is required to track my avowed interests — that is supposed to be the whole difference between living under a decent law and living at the pleasure of a master.
(3) A requirement like that only really binds if someone has the standing power to enforce it: to hear my complaint, to demand reasons, to reverse the outcome. Call it contestation.
(4) But the reviewers power over me is unconstrained in exactly the way that matters. Whether my complaint succeeds depends on how well I argue, how confident I am with forms and procedures, whether I can afford to keep pressing, what mood the reviewer is in, how they read an ambiguous clause — none of that tracks my interests. And nobody reviews the reviewer; if somebody does, the same problem just repeats one level up.
(5) So the mechanism that is meant to drain the arbitrariness out of power is itself arbitrary power over me, and the regress never bottoms out.
(6) So in any society that has any procedure at all for settling disputes — i.e. any society — everyone is dominated and no one is free.
(7) A theory of freedom that can't tell a constitutional order apart from a tyranny isn't reporting a discovery about the world, it has gone wrong somewhere in (1) to (5).

I want to know which step to give up and what giving it up costs me. If I have to choose I'd rather keep (1) and the claim that a decent legal order doesn't dominate its citizens than keep (5), but I want to know what I'm paying for that. Three things I tried that she brushed off: (i) its not arbitrary if the reviewer has to give public reasons — she says that just relocates the question, because someone has to judge whether the reasons are any good; (ii) contestation can be mutual, A reviews B reviews A, so there is no top of the chain — she says a ring of mutually arbitrary powers is just distributed domination, not non-domination, and I honestly can't tell if shes right; (iii) I tried the case where the rule is enforced by a machine with zero discretion left in it, and she said that's the same problem one level down because somebody writes and maintains the code. On (ii) especially: is there a name for the difference between a chain of reviewers and a loop of reviewers, and does it actually make a difference here?

The other thing I can't get straight is whether step (4) is doing something sneaky. That sounds too strong to me but I can't say why.
