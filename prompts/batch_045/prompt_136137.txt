I'm a high school senior editing a small philosophy-and-humor zine for my school's humanities club, and I'd like to commission one final piece from you. Please take on the role of a philosopher of language and mind who has also worked professionally as a type designer and writes occasional humor for magazines — someone who knows the literature on concepts, categories, and essences, and who also knows why the 'a' in a humanist serif face looks nothing like the 'a' in a geometric sans.

Here is the background, so you can see what I'm after. I've been reading about researchers who tried to build a program that could design an entire alphabet in a coherent style, and about the puzzle those projects run into: the 'a' of a Renaissance serif face, the 'a' of a geometric sans, the single-story 'a' of a beginner's reading book, and the 'a' of a blackletter face share almost no visible feature, and yet nobody has any difficulty seeing that they are all the same letter. What fascinates me is the tempting inference from that — that there must be some hidden essence, some single property that all and only a-glyphs possess, which explains why they are all 'a's.

What I want is a satirical piece aimed at that inference: essentialism about letterforms. The piece should dramatize the view, never name or explain it.

Form and constraints:

- An internal memorandum from a fictional government standards office — call it the Bureau of Glyph Identity — that certifies whether a submitted glyph is or is not an instance of the letter 'a.' Think case rulings, form numbers, an appeals process, and interoffice squabbling.
- 800–900 words, laid out as numbered sections, plus a short appendix headed "Summary of Doctrine."
- A deadpan bureaucratic voice. The piece must never wink: no character steps forward to state the philosophical moral, and there should be no closing paragraph tipping the reader off. A reader should be able to tell what is being mocked from the content alone.
- The humor has to come from getting the philosophy right. Every visual criterion the Bureau tries out as necessary and sufficient should be dismantled by a real feature of typefaces: bowl shape, double versus single story, serifs, stroke contrast, slant, weight, the fact that nearly the same shape can count as different letters in different writing systems, and the fact that a glyph can be deformed bit by bit until no one can say at what point it stopped being an 'a.'
- Please invent one further doctrine for the Bureau to fall back on once every visual test has failed: that what makes a glyph an 'a' is purely relational — its place in a system of contrasts that distinguishes it from the 'o,' the 'd,' and the 'g' of the same alphabet. Then push that doctrine somewhere it doesn't want to go: a foundry submits a typeface in which every glyph is the same plain circle, and by the Bureau's own reasoning each circle is a flawless instance of its letter. The Bureau should find that it cannot reject the submission without appealing to exactly the sort of consideration its doctrine has excluded.
- Include at least one quote from a self-important Bureau official and one from an exasperated outside consultant.
- Where you mention real typefaces, scripts, or historical letterforms, say only what you are confident is true about them; otherwise invent plausible ones. Don't stop to lecture the reader about typography.

Before you start drafting, please brainstorm for a few paragraphs: list several candidate comedic premises, then list the real features of the category 'a' you could exploit, then choose the strongest combination and check that the satire is fair — that it exaggerates something a defender of hidden essences would actually say, rather than a straw man. Then write the piece. Take your time with it; I'd much rather have one sharp, internally consistent document than a long, bland one.
