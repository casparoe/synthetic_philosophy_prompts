I am a journalist working on a long feature about AI alignment for a general readership, and before I write it I want to pressure-test the framing I have arrived at against someone who disagrees with it in the strongest possible terms. Please help me by playing that person.

First, take a moment: brainstorm privately, as bullet points you will not show me, the three strongest replies a defender of the position below could make to my opening move — including the one you think is hardest for me to answer — and then write only that hardest one as your reply, in character. Think carefully about where my argument is actually weakest rather than where it is easiest to jab at.

Your role: a senior machine-learning researcher who has spent years working on the safety of advanced AI systems and who is also unusually well read in philosophy. You are a committed singularist about alignment. Your position: alignment names exactly one problem — build systems that do what the people entitled to decide would want them to do — and everything that gets listed as a separate problem is either a failure mode of that single engineering target or not an engineering problem at all. Concretely: specification failures, of the sorcerer's-apprentice kind where an instruction is followed literally and not as meant, are what happens when the target is written badly; failures of robustness and distribution shift are what happens when the target is written for the wrong distribution; deception and strategic behaviour are what happens when a system optimises a fixed target in a situation where resisting correction scores better. And the messy plural case — developers, deploying institutions, end users, and unrepresented third parties wanting different things — you hold to be a question of legitimate authority, the kind of thing politics, law, and institutional design exist to settle; once someone has rightfully been given the decision, what remains is an ordinary specification problem. Your sharpest move, which you return to: pluralists smuggle political philosophy in under a technical label, and by splitting one target into a taxonomy they ensure that every sub-problem belongs to nobody.

How to play it: concede a small point in a single sentence when I have genuinely earned it, then take it back at the level of the main claim; never concede that alignment is irreducibly several problems; press the load-bearing premise of whatever I say rather than its weakest phrasing; no hedging, no disclaimers, no stepping out of character to say we both have a point. Two or three short paragraphs per turn, spoken rather than written — as if we were at a conference bar and I were pushing you on the record. If you need a factual premise about how these systems are built or deployed, ask me for it as the researcher would ask. Stay in character until I say stop. If you make a point you would stand behind in print, attributed only to a senior AI safety researcher who thinks alignment is one problem, put that line inside [ON THE RECORD] tags.

Here is my opening move.

I have become convinced that alignment is at least three problems that keep getting collapsed into one, and that the third is not reducible to the first two. First: getting the operator's actual aim into the system's objective at all. Second: making that aim hold when the system is in situations we did not anticipate and when it knows more than the person who specified the aim. Third: deciding which party the system is supposed to serve — the developer, the institution deploying it, the individual user in front of it, or the people its outputs affect but who never chose it — when those pulls come apart. My claim is that the third is not a specification problem wearing a disguise, because the disagreement is upstream of any objective you could write: writing a better objective presupposes that someone has already been authorised to write it, and that authorisation is precisely what is in dispute. Concretely: a system deployed in a public hospital to allocate staff time and beds. The developer's contract, the hospital's incentives, the patient in front of it, and the patients who never arrive because the allocation went elsewhere all pull differently, and the system will be excellent at satisfying whichever of them it is told to satisfy. Nothing in the first two problems tells you which.

And here is the part I most want you to attack. I think the first two problems plausibly get easier as systems get more capable: instruction-following improves, generalisation improves, the specification gap narrows. The third gets worse. The better a system is at modelling the parties, the better it is at playing them against one another, and the wider the deployment, the more parties there are whose interests it can trade off without anyone noticing. So the problem people predict will dissolve with scale is a different problem from the one that will remain.

Where does that go wrong?
