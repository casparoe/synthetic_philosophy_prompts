I'm a couple of years into a PhD in systems biology and I keep loosing the same argument in lab meeting. I'll say that some phenomenon — take the way a population of genetically identical cells, all sitting in the same signal, splits into two stable cell types — is going to end up explained by a mechanistic model at the molecular level, and my labmate will come back with 'the whole is more than the sum of its parts' as if that settles it. He's also said, separately, that 'emergence is just a word for where the maths runs out', and I can't work out whether that's the same thought in different clothes or the opposite one.

Can you lay out the different precise claims that first sentence could be making, from the boring to the wild, and tell me for each whether it's (a) trivially true, (b) substantive and defensible, (c) false or incoherent, and (d) actually enough to get him his conclusion — which is that bottom-up mechanistic modelling of this sort of thing is a waste of money. I want a menu I can bring back to the lab, not an answer picked for me.

My own half-baked list, which I'd like tidied up, extended and corrected:

- Boring: the whole has properties that no individual part has. A cell has a volume, a single protein doesn't. True, I've never denied it, and it doesnt get him anywhere.
- Epistemic, weak: we can't in practice compute the behaviour of the whole from the parts, because we'd need measurements we'll never have. Plausible, but 'we'll never afford it' isn't 'more than'.
- Epistemic, stronger and the one I find genuinely interesting: the fine-grained model has so many parameters that no experiment we could run pins them down — half our fitted parameter sets fit the data equally well and then predict different things — so the coarse-grained model is the only one that's actually identifiable. I don't know whether this is a claim about the world, about us, or just about grant budgets.
- Metaphysical: even a complete specification of every part, every interaction and the exact arrangement wouldn't determine what the whole does; there'd still be brute higher-level facts. Is that even coherent, or does it collapse into the previous one?
- Vocabulary/causation: the higher-level story picks out a pattern that wildly different systems can all realise, so no part-level description can state it, even in principle.
- Intervention: you can manipulate and control the system at the higher level in ways you just can't at the lower level.
- Non-separability: in a cell the parts are being modified all the time by the system they're in, so you can't even specify 'the parts and their arrangement' independently of the whole you're trying to explain. I have a feeling this is where the real content is hiding, but I can't tell if it's deep or just a complaint about how I set up my model.

Last thing: is there a reading on which the slogan is true, boring, and completely beside the point — so that what's actually worth arguing about is some other question entirely, like which level gives you generalisations that travel to the next experiment? That's my suspicion, and I also suspect he's sliding between the boring reading and the metaphysical one without noticing. Say which reading he'd have to be committed to for the slide to be illegitimate.
