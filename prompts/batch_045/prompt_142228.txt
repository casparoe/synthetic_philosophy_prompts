I'm trying to work out what I think about embryo selection, going purely on gut. Here's my tentative principle, which I suspect is wrong somewhere: of the children you could actually have, pick the one whose own life will go best, judged by that child's expected wellbeing and nobody else's; widening the menu is allowed but not required; and risk is handled by expected value. Test it against my verdicts and repair it.

1. Two embryos, one will be blind from birth, the other won't. Picking the sighted one isn't just permitted, it's what you should do — picking the other is a real moral failing.
2. Two embryos, one will be deaf; both parents are deaf, they sign, the whole extended family and neighbourhood signs, so the child's expected wellbeing looks about the same either way. My verdict: no failing either way, but I also feel a faint pull toward "the hearing one has more options open" that I can't quite justify.
3. Selecting for higher expected school achievement. Fine, not required, slightly icky, not wrong.
4. Selecting for height. I feel this is considerably worse than (3) even though the structure looks identical — both are positional goods that only pay off relative to other kids. I can't say why and I suspect the feeling is indefensible.
5. Niche one I made up: there's a temperament variant that makes a child miserable in loud, chaotic households and unusually content in quiet, structured ones. The parents are definitely the quiet, structured type. Selecting for it seems to me fine and genuinely counts as giving the child a better life — even though the identical variant would be a harm in a different family, and even though the parents could in principle become chaotic people.
6. Embryo A: 55% chance of an outstanding life, 45% chance of a life with chronic pain. Embryo B: near-certainty of a solidly good life, lower expected value. I lean B, and I know that's inconsistent with my expected-value clause.
7. The clinic can generate more embryos with another stimulation round — costly, unpleasant, small health risk — and that would raise the ceiling on the best available child. I don't think anyone is obligated to do it. But if you're already doing IVF for infertility and there are five embryos on the tray, I do think you should pick the best of them. I want to know whether that asymmetry is principled or just squeamishness.
8. Embryo X will have a somewhat less happy life than Y, but will grow up to save several strangers' lives. Picking X is, I think, fine and maybe admirable; picking Y is also not a failing. Nothing here is required either way.
9. The swap case: two couples at the clinic, each with two embryos. Couple 1's second-best is better than couple 2's best, and vice versa — swapping gives both couples a better-off child than either could get alone, and nobody is made worse off. I think swapping is permitted and it makes me uneasy, and I have no idea whether it's required.
10. Whatever you pick, a different child exists, and the one you didn't pick isn't harmed because they never exist. I still think there's something to choose in (1) and (7) — so my principle can't be harm-based, but I'd like that made explicit rather than assumed.
