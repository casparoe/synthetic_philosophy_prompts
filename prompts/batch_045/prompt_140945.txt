My partner and I have been going round on this for years and I've finally written down my gut verdicts on a pile of cases, and they dont line up. The topic is compromise between people who have different values. Sometimes splitting the difference seems like the best thing two people can do, sometimes it seems like corruption or cowardice, and I can't tell the difference in a way I trust.

1. My flatmate eats meat. I think eating meat is seriously wrong. We settle on: meat gets cooked at home twice a week, everything else vegetarian. Fine. Actually better than fine; it feels like the mature answer.

2. Two of us are writing a public health guidance document. I'm the consequentialist, she has informed-consent scruples. The final recommendation is neither of our first choices. We both sign. Fine, maybe even good.

3. City council: I vote for your bike lane, you vote for my library. Each of us thinks the other's project is a waste of money. Fine — that's just how the place works.

4. Two journal editors: I accept your buddy's weak paper, you accept mine. Not fine at all. That's corruption. (This is the pair I can't reconcile with 3. They look like the same move to me — I do something I judge bad so you'll do the same — and I have opposite reactions.)

5. You're negotiating with a dictator to release political prisoners. You get him down from zero to a hundred released. Fine. Close to heroic.

6. A mob says hand over five people or they burn the street. You talk them down to one, and hand over one. Not fine. Not even less-bad-in-a-good-way. (And this is the pair I really can't reconcile with 5: both are bargaining with people who are doing wrong, to reduce the wrong they'll do. One is admirable, one is monstrous.)

7. School board. Compromise with anti-vaccine parents: they can opt out if they sign a form. Not fine — it feels like buying peace by buying an error. But compromising with religious parents by letting them opt their kids out of parts of sex ed: fine. Those look parallel and I have opposite verdicts again.

8. My lab rival and I each think our own project matters more; we split the grant 50/50 and co-apply. Fine, mildly annoying, fine.

9. Two parents, one wants a competitive school, one wants a low-pressure one; the kid is 11, hates homework, and gets bound by the outcome. I think fine.

10. Moral trade with a stranger online: I give 500 to their animal charity, they give 500 to my malaria charity, and each of us thinks the other's cause is close to worthless. I flip-flop on this one weekly. Some weeks it's obvious gains from trade; some weeks it's two people paying each other to do something pointless.

11. The one I actually care about: I stop arguing with my dad about politics and he stops bringing it up. Fine, I think — but is it? On my own account I'm not sure, because there's something odd about it: our silence makes the household look more agreed than it is, and nobody involved pays for that.

Here's the principle I keep trying out and it keeps breaking: a compromise is in good order when (a) everyone bound by it prefers it to no deal, (b) the costs land only on people party to it or represented by someone who is, and (c) nobody has to act against a conviction they hold with real confidence. (c) is wrecked by 1 and 2, which I think are fine. (a) and (b) are wrecked by 9, which I think is fine. And (a) plus (b) are satisfied by 6, which I think is not fine. So either my principle is wrong or some verdicts have to go, and I don't know which.

What I want: give me a principle, or two or three candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it cold. Say which case motivates each clause. Say what it predicts about 10 and 11, and about at least one case I haven't listed — I'd especially like to know what it says about a holdout: the person who refuses to compromise gets their way, so any principle built on mutual gain seems to reward intransigence, and I don't know whether to count that as a bug, a sad fact, or a clause I'm missing. And be honest about which of my verdicts your principle can't accommodate and which one you'd overturn. If you think 3 and 4 really are the same move and I should drop one, say so and say which. Same for 5 and 6 — I have a vague idea that the difference is about doing something wrong yourself versus merely failing to prevent it, but then case 1 is a counterexample, and I want to know whether that idea is rescuable or whether I should throw it out.
