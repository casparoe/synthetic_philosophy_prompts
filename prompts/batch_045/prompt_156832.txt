I'm a third-year PhD student in political psychology — I work mostly on affective polarization — and I keep running into the same shape of thing in my own data, and I don't have a word for it. Does it have one?

Here's version one. In a pilot I showed people short op-eds (lightly trimmed) and asked whether the author was arguing in good faith. When the author is on the participant's side and makes a concession — the equivalent of "yes, the other side has a point about X" — participants call it honesty, intellectual humility, good faith. When the very same sentence is attributed to an out-party author, participants say things like "they only threw that in so they'd look reasonable" or "they conceded the cheap point so they could keep the real one." And when the out-party author concedes *nothing*, that's proof of bad faith and closed-mindedness; whereas the in-party author who concedes nothing is "principled," "not giving ground where none is due." So both conceding and not conceding land on good faith for us and bad faith for them. Nothing the out-group person could have done would have counted as evidence either way.

Second version, from interviews: when I point this out to people — literally, "look, whatever they do, you read it as bad faith" — a decent share of them read the pointing-out itself as a bad-faith move ("so you're defending them"). The immunity seems to operate one level up, on the accusation as well as on the original behaviour.

I definately don't think it's just one of the standard things. Naive realism is about taking your own view to be just how the world is, so disagreement must be explained by bias or ignorance; the hostile media thing is about judging identical content as slanted against you. Both feel close, but neither quite captures the no-possible-evidence structure, where the *content* of what the out-group person does is irrelevant and every behaviour routes to the same verdict. And the motive asymmetry finding — people describe their own side as moved by principle or concern and the other side as moved by self-interest or hostility — is about *which motive* gets attributed, not about why no evidence could ever shift the attribution.

One thing makes me think it isn't a flat "out-group equals bad faith" rule: it seems to break when the out-group person visibly pays a cost inside their own camp for the conciliatory thing, e.g. gets attacked by their own side for conceding. Then people do credit them, at least sometimes. So maybe the asymmetry is really in how costliness gets read: our concessions cost something (they went against their own team, so they mean something), their concessions cost nothing (they were forced, or it was tactical), and only their costly concessions register at all.

So: is there an established name for this? I want more than a label, obviously. I want to know what exactly the phenomenon is, whether what I've described is one thing or two or three things I've mashed together, what the nearest established concepts are and how mine differs from each, and where the edges are. If there's nothing established, coin something, define it, and give me a case that would *not* count. Also practically useful: what would experimentally separate "this person genuinely believes the other side argues in bad faith" from "this person is running an evidence-immune attribution rule"? My supervisor just says "that's the attribution literature, go read it," which isn't helping.
