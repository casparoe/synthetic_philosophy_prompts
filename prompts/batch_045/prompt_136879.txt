I'm a PhD student in political science, and over the past year I've been serving on a small departmental committee that hands out seed grants, which has made me keep noticing a pattern I don't have a word for. The advice I was given about my own dissertation timeline — and that we were given on the committee — is the familiar one: distrust the story you can tell about why your case is special, and instead ask how often things of that kind work out. Not "my chapters will take four months each, and here is what each one involves," but "how long do dissertations here actually take." I'm fully convinced by that move in the ordinary case. What I keep running into is that the number you get from the outside is itself built out of people who reasoned from the inside. Everyone who shows up in our dissertation-completion figures formed their expectations by listing what they had left to do and estimating each piece, so the distribution I'm told to defer to is largely an aggregate of the very bias it is supposed to correct; the correction seems not to remove the optimism but to relocate it into the reference class and launder it. The committee case works the same way: the track record of seed grants looks like a fact about the projects, but it is equally a fact about how much we gave them, when we gave it, and whether the applicant, having confidently predicted a smooth path, had made any provision for the path not being smooth. I don't think this is survivorship bias, since the failures do get counted, and it isn't a self-fulfilling or self-defeating prophecy either, because the distortion seems to be present whether or not anyone has ever seen the base rate.

There is a second thing I can't name, which feels like a companion to the first. Even when the reference class is clean, applying the outside view seems to require a judgment I can't see how to outsource: whether my case is relevantly similar to the cases in the class. My topic is one nobody in the department has worked on; does it belong in the class of dissertations generally, or dissertations where the student had to learn the method from scratch, or dissertations where the data had to be collected in the field? Picking among those is itself the kind of from-the-inside, story-telling judgment the outside view was meant to replace — so the inside view looks not expelled but pushed up one level, and I can't tell whether that regress bottoms out somewhere or whether it just shows that the two views were never really separable. I've been informally sorting the first phenomenon into three varieties — the class being composed of inside-view-generated cases; the class shifting once the people being predicted learn the number and start deferring to it; and the class being defined by a self-description, like "high-risk, high-reward," that is produced by the same judgment being corrected — but I have no idea whether that cut is at the joints or whether the three are really one thing.

What I'd like to know is whether either phenomenon has an established name and literature — in the forecasting and decision-theory literatures, in epistemology, or in the social-studies-of-economics end of things, wherever it lives — and whether the two are instances of one underlying problem or genuinely different ones. If they already have names, I want to understand how each differs from its nearest neighbours: how the first is not just the ordinary problem of choosing among several available reference classes, and not just the econometric point about relationships breaking down once policy is made in reliance on them, and not just a self-fulfilling prophecy; how the second relates to what people talk about under the heading of epistemic modesty and to worries about trusting your own judgment when you have evidence that your judgment is unreliable. If they don't have names, I'd like you to coin them and define them precisely enough to settle these boundary questions: whether the contamination can ever push the outside view in the pessimistic direction rather than the optimistic one, what happens in the limit when everyone in the reference class took the outside view, and whether a case counts if no agent ever knew the base rate was being computed. A bare label won't do what I need here — I'm trying to get clear on what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie, because I want to use the distinction in a methods section and not have it collapse under the first objection.
