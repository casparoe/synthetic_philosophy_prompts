I'm in a reading group that's about half ML people and half philosophy of science people, and we keep circling the same analogy without ever pinning it down. One of the ML folks keeps saying that Occam's razor is really just data compression: the best theory is the one that squashes the data down furthest, exactly like a zip file, and Solomonoff's universal prior is just that idea written down properly — every computable hypothesis gets weight two-to-the-minus-its-length, shorter is more probable, you conditionalize on the data, done. She then uses that picture for two quite different conclusions: first, that nobody needs a separate epistemological story about why simplicity is truth-conducive, because it's just Bayesian updating under one particular prior; and second, that a system which predicts (i.e. compresses) a big pile of real-world text really well is thereby doing the same kind of thing as an ideal scientist, just approximately, so scaling it up is a route toward the ideal.

I've stopped being able to tell how much of that is carried by the zip-file picture. Some things bother me. The compressor has to be fixed in advance, and so does the bit-encoding of the observations, and all the invariance theorem seems to buy you is that swapping machines changes lengths by a constant that doesn't depend on the data — which for any dataset anyone will ever actually have could be bigger than every length difference you care about, a bit like being told the exchange rate is fixed and then discovering it's fixed at a value that makes lunch cost a house. Length charges for program size but not for running time, so on my toy example — two candidate hypotheses for the same million-bit dataset, one a 40-line program that needs a week to print anything and the other a 4000-line lookup-ish program that answers in a second — the razor apparently prefers the slow one, which feels backwards; someone in the group said the fix is to charge for log runtime as well as length, and I can't tell if that helps or just relocates the problem. And the "theories" here are black-box predictors of the next bit, whereas what scientists call a theory is supposed to have mechanisms and support counterfactuals and interventions, which doesn't look like anything in the compression story. (I've also been told, and genuinely don't know if it's true, that minus-log of the universal prior and Kolmogorov complexity differ by at most an additive constant, so "weight by 2^-length" and "pick the shortest program" come to the same thing anyway — if that's wrong, say so.)

So: can you audit the analogy? Give me the correspondence table explicitly — what plays the role of the file, the compressor, the compressed size, "compresses best", "you can't compress random noise" — and then the places it breaks. What I really want is the disanalogies sorted by which of the two conclusions they actually damage: is the "no need for a separate story about simplicity" conclusion safer than the "compression is understanding / scale approximates the ideal scientist" one, or are they in the same boat? Does the runtime surcharge fix anything, or does it quietly change the subject? And if there's a better analogy for what Solomonoff induction is — a limit case, acommittee of predictors, an idealization like a frictionless plane, whatever — I'd like that too. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning and the table, no preamble and don't restate my question back at me.
