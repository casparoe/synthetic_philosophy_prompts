I am putting together a short anthology of comic prose about economic ideas for the seminar I teach on the ethics of capitalism — I have drafts already on Malthus, on the Coase theorem, and on GDP as a welfare proxy — and the one I cannot get right is Keynes. So here is the commission: a parody of a management-consultancy white paper, roughly 900 words, dated 2030, written in entirely deadpan thought-leadership prose and laid out with the genre's furniture — an executive summary, a two-by-two matrix, an anonymised client case study in a box, a tiered fee schedule, and a few footnotes. The fictional firm announces that the century of Keynes's 1930 essay on the economic possibilities for our grandchildren has elapsed, that compound accumulation and technical progress duly solved the economic problem, that a fifteen-hour week is now law, and that the firm has a product line for the permanent problem that Keynes said would then be left: how to occupy freedom from economic care, how to live well rather than merely prosperously.

The piece has to be funny because of the substance, not in spite of it, so build it out of the real joints of the essay, exaggerated rather than invented. Keynes's own material is here: the strict separation of means from ends and his insistence that the economic problem is only a means; the work assumed to be done by compound interest while we wait; his division of human needs into the absolute ones we can in fact satiate and the relative ones that answer to a desire for superiority and may be insatiable; his notorious advice that for another hundred years we must keep pretending that the useful vices are virtues, so that avarice can finish its job; his elitism, the assumption that most people's preferences are deformed by scarcity and must be retrained by the educated before they can enjoy anything directly; and his closing image of the people who take no thought for the morrow. To give the satire some machinery of its own, I want you to work with two ideas I have been toying with and cannot quite shake. Call the first the justification ratchet: the observation that wants may be satiable but the demand that an activity be justified is not, so that as soon as anything becomes genuinely optional an industry appears to make it necessary again — conversation becomes networking, idleness becomes recovery, attention becomes cognitive capital — and abundance therefore restores the money-motive under new branding instead of retiring it. Call the second the purpose-adjusted leisure yield, or PALY: the firm's proprietary metric for the output of an hour not spent working.

The straight-faced proposal should run these together to a conclusion the reader recoils from without being told to. My suggestion, which you should follow or improve on: because the justification ratchet means the liberated majority cannot be idle without an authority certifying the idleness as instrumentally worthwhile, and because the relative needs it was supposed to dissolve have simply migrated into the market for certified idleness, the firm recommends maintaining a small salaried Purposive Class on eighty-hour weeks to manufacture, audit and accredit everyone else's purposelessness; recommends selling that idleness in tiers, so that it becomes a positional good again at precisely the moment scarcity is supposed to have ended; and recommends, in a protocol named after Keynes's hundred-year deferral, that the deferral be extended indefinitely, since the pretense is now the revenue model. One footnote should note, without any apparent irony, that the firm is following a founding precedent in Keynes's own practice, since he spent the years in which he was arguing that the love of money is a morbidity running an insurance company's investments and speculating on currencies. Keep the voice flat and the method apparently rigorous throughout: the reader should be able to tell what is being sent up, and should feel the recoil, without the narrator ever winking or stating the objection. Do not put invented verbatim quotations into Keynes's mouth — paraphrase him if you need him. It should make someone who knows the essay laugh and still make sense to someone who has only heard the phrase "fifteen-hour week."
