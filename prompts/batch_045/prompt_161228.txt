You are a philosopher who writes occasional satire for a magazine of ideas — think of the register as *The Onion* crossed with a civil-service procurement portal. Before drafting, brainstorm the real mechanisms by which states manufacture national traditions and by which publics come to imagine themselves as nations: not the straw version in which a committee simply tells lies, but the actual machinery — standardization of a language, the census category, the museum, the map, ritual repetition, the telescoping of antiquity, the crowding-out of genuinely old local custom by nationally framed substitutes. Choose six or seven of these to carry the piece and discard the rest.

Write a parody government solicitation: a Request for Proposals issued by the Ministry of National Continuity of a newly independent state (invent the state; do not model it on any real country), inviting contractors to bid for the design, prototyping, and delivery of the nation's traditions. About 700 words, American English, in a flat, unironic bureaucratic voice, formatted as three parts: (1) a scope of work in numbered clauses, covering at minimum a national costume, a national epic composed in the newly standardized official language, a founding date, a census category, a museum wing, and a border that must appear inevitable on maps; (2) a weighted evaluation rubric; (3) a short FAQ for prospective bidders.

The comedy has to come from the mechanics rather than from the word "fake," and it should stay deadpan enough that a reader can tell what is being ironized without you winking. Build it around, among others: the requirement that delivered traditions be no less than three hundred years old and also delivered within eighteen months; a provenance dossier that must be filed in full with the Ministry and simultaneously classified so that no citizen may ever learn its contents; a scoring penalty for submissions whose author can be identified; the awkwardness that genuinely surviving old customs score poorly because they are local rather than national in form; and a clause specifying that the traditions need only be rehearsed, not believed. A reader who knows the literature on invented traditions and imagined communities should recognize which real features are being exaggerated, and a reader who does not should still laugh.

Close with a 150-word production note, in your own voice, on where the piece takes liberties: which reading of invented traditions and of nations as imagined communities it commits to, which feature it exaggerates most, and whether it is taking the contested position that public exposure of a tradition as manufactured does or does not disable that tradition. Commit to one answer on each rather than laying out the alternatives.
