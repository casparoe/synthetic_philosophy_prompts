Two members of a human-rights reading group I sit in on spent an entire session arguing over a hypothetical I had circulated, and at the end they asked me to referee. I couldn't do it, so I want yours.

The case was built to sit on a seam. The Semberi are a minority of roughly four hundred thousand in the state of Ardan. Over fifteen years Ardan removes the Semberi language from schools and official use, redraws districts so that Semberi are a local minority everywhere they live, pays substantial cash bonuses to anyone who re-registers as Ardani and to families who send children to Ardani-language boarding schools in another province, and demolishes a Semberi historic quarter and cemetery for a highway and a reservoir. A small separatist militia is crushed, with a few hundred deaths. There is no mass killing. Officials say on the record that in a generation there will be no Semberi and that Ardan will be better for it; they also say that nobody has to leave, that Semberi are welcome to stay as Ardani citizens, and that the state wishes them no harm — it wants them to stop being a separate people. The definition the group was working from is the 1948 genocide convention's: acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such, where the listed acts are killing members, causing serious bodily or mental harm, inflicting conditions of life calculated to bring about the group's physical destruction, preventing births, and forcibly transferring children out of the group.

Malik opened: this is not genocide, and the concept is working properly when it says so. Every listed act points at physical or biological destruction; what Ardan is running is cultural and demographic engineering — coercive, shameful, probably unlawful under human rights law, but not the crime of crimes. Stretch destruction to mean ceasing to exist as a distinct people and every assimilationist state becomes a perpetrator; a word that covers both the Holocaust and nineteenth-century language policy cannot tell anyone what to do, and this word is supposed to trigger a duty to prevent and punish.

Ines replied that categorically worse is the very thing in dispute, and you cannot settle it by pointing at the paradigm case — of course killing looks worse from the outside, but ask the last Semberi speaker. And the text is less clean than he says: transferring children out of the group is already a non-lethal technology for ending a group, so the drafters built in the idea that a people can be destroyed without corpses; they simply covered the one practice they had in front of them. The wrong here is the destruction of a collective good — the group's continuing existence — inflicted on people as Semberi. Killing is one means among others. And the narrow reading creates a perverse gradient: the more efficiently you end a people without bodies, the safer you are.

Malik: the child-transfer clause is about children, and it shows the drafters knew how to cover non-lethal destruction when they wanted to, which is a reason not to imply the rest. Targeting people as a group is not distinctive either — expulsion, ethnic cleansing, apartheid all do that. So here is the choice he wanted to force: either she says this is genocide and owes an account of why a state that leaves Semberi culture alone but lets their region rot in poverty has done something less bad, or she admits that what is really doing the work for her is intent, and then she must say why intent matters so much when the outcome is identical.

Ines took the second horn: intent matters because of what it does to the people it targets — being flooded on purpose is not being rained on, even at identical water levels. And he should not lean on the cash bonuses: each family's choice can be voluntary while the aggregate is the disappearance of something no individual owned or was entitled to sell, which is the ordinary reason collective goods are under-protected by contract. Yes, intent matters, and the object of that intent is the group's existence, with killing incidental to it.

Malik then tried a cleaner probe, the Archivist case: suppose Ardan does none of this, only stops counting Semberi in the census, funds no Semberi-language schools, and lets market integration take its course; three generations later the Semberi are gone and no one aimed at that — it is a byproduct of policies adopted for other reasons. On her account this looks as bad or worse: more Semberi gone, no compensation. So which is it — the death of the group, or the aiming? If the death, she has stopped talking about a crime and started doing demographics. If the aiming, then she concedes his point.

Ines answered that the test is rigged: a state that closes a language's schools and lets it die has not failed to aim at anything, and no one intended is doing the same work in his example that calculated to bring about physical destruction does in the text. But she would take the horn anyway: the Archivist case is a different wrong — a structural injustice with no agent who wrongs anyone in particular — and she is happy to say it is not genocide.

Please go through the exchange in the order it happened and rate each move — right, partly right, or wrong — with a brief justification, rather than just declaring a winner. Then three specific questions. First: can the phrase destroy a group as such be read to cover Ardan's campaign at all without producing the collapse Malik predicts, and what would have to be true for that reading to be stable? Second: is the Archivist variant a fair test of Ines's position or a rigged one, and what should each of them have said about it? Third: what is this argument really about — is there a distinction both of them are ignoring that would dissolve part of it, for instance between the work the word does as a legal trigger, as a moral verdict, and as a mobilizing label? Finally, say who is more right overall, and what, if anything, both sides are missing.
