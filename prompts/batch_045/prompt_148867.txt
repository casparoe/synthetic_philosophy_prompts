You are a professional philosopher who works on philosophy of religion and on the logic of modality, and who is experienced at leading advanced undergraduate seminars. I run a small philosophy of religion reading group and I have to lead next session, so I need something I can put in front of people. Please think carefully and work in two stages. First, brainstorm freely: map the whole logical space of precise claims the sentence could be making, varying the kind of possibility involved, whose power is in question, at what time that power is had, what the word all is ranging over (states of affairs, outcomes, tasks or action-types, objects or creatures), and whether the sentence is really about power at all rather than about sovereignty, freedom, goodness, or faith. Do not settle on a favourite reading during this stage. Only then organise the results into the numbered menu I ask for at the end.

The sentence is with God all things are possible. I know it comes from a gospel passage about whether a rich man can be saved, right after the remark about a camel and a needle's eye, and that a nearby line runs all things are possible to him who believes; my group keeps using it as a general slogan about divine power, which is the use I care about.

Three people used it to do three different jobs. One member used it to shut down the old stone question: make a stone so heavy that its maker cannot lift it, and so on. He said the phrase describes no genuine task at all, so there is nothing here that God fails to do, and the slogan simply records that whatever is genuinely doable is doable by God. A second member used the same sentence for the opposite conclusion: God is not answerable to logic, so of course he both makes the stone and lifts it, and treating coherence as a limit is just importing a constraint from outside. A third member used it in a completely separate discussion, about whether God could have created a world containing free creatures who never in fact sin: all things are possible, so such a world was available, so the amount of moral evil we actually have stands in need of an explanation. I strongly suspect these three people are not asserting the same proposition, and that at most one of them has a reading that does the work they want.

There is also a case we invented ourselves, which I could not find anything about and which I think separates the readings sharply. Call it the self-emptying case, our own label. Suppose that at one time God does something whose whole point is to bring it about that at a later time he cannot do some specific thing: he binds himself by an unconditional promise he will not go back on, or creates a creature whose choices he cannot determine, or takes on a nature that cannot do various things the divine nature can do. At the later time he cannot do the thing. Does the slogan fail then? Does it matter whether the inability is self-imposed and rescindable, or whether rescinding it would just be another exercise of the same power? Does it matter that the God at the earlier time could foresee and prevent the later inability? I want to know which precise readings of the slogan this case refutes, which it leaves untouched, and which it forces into a distinction the slogan cannot express in one sentence.

Please give me a numbered menu of the distinct precise theses the slogan could be asserting, ordered roughly from the most deflationary to the most extravagant. For each one: state it as precisely as you can, in a sentence that could be true or false; say whether it is a trivial platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, a substantive claim that is probably false, or something strictly incoherent or empty; note any reading that is really about salvation or faith rather than power, since that one may be what the original passage meant and is almost certainly not what my group means. Then, in a separate short section, go through the three uses and the self-emptying case in turn and say, for each, which reading or readings on your menu would be needed to make the use work, whether that reading survives your verdict on it, and where the use simply smuggles in a premise the slogan does not supply. Where the slogan is underdetermined between readings that would behave differently, say so explicitly rather than choosing for me. Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations, and do not rest anything on what some named author is supposed to have held; my group wants the distinctions themselves and I cannot check sources anyway.
