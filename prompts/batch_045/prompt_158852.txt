I've been lurking on rationalist blogs since the Sequences were still going up, and I still don't have a rule for the thing I actually do most at work. Hoping someone here does.

I'm the only quant on a four-person team at a specialty insurer. We price weird, thin risks — political risk in a single country, cyber for one narrow industry, satellite launch, a couple of kidnap-and-ransom books. Most of the time there is no data: either nothing has happened yet, or we have three observations and none of them are events. Twice a month I have to put a number — a probability, a rate, a severity distribution — into a model that produces a price, and the price gets filed. If I'm off by 3x we lose the account or we eat a loss. I've got maybe half a day per number, teammates who can drive a spreadsheet but don't know what a conjugate prior is, and everything goes past a chief actuary who trained in the nineties, thinks "prior" means "what we assumed last year," will sign anything that arrives with a documented rule attached, and will bounce anything that says judgment call.

The rule I need is for choosing a default prior when we genuinely have nothing, and I need it to survive Bertrand's paradox rather than just gesture at it. You know the setup: an equilateral triangle inscribed in a circle, a chord chosen at random, how often is it longer than a side of the triangle — and depending on how you spell out "random," three applications of the principle of indifference give you 1/3 (two random points on the circumference), 1/2 (random point on a fixed radius, chord perpendicular to it), or 1/4 (random midpoint anywhere in the disk). I've read the Jaynes maximum-ignorance argument secondhand and I take the point that the paradox is really about an ill-posed question, but "go find the actual generator of the randomness" is not advice I can use at 4pm on a Thursday when the quantity is "share of claims in this class that exceed $5m."

So: write me a procedure my team can actually run, in order, in under two hours, on a new quantity. Specifically:

- the questions to ask about the quantity, in the order to ask them, and what counts as a sufficient answer to each — where sufficient means checkable by someone who can't derive a posterior by hand.
- a decision-sensitivity check near the front: if every candidate prior I could defensibly pick gives the same decision, the procedure should say stop and file a note rather than let us argue about priors.
- an explicit invariance step: which symmetries the quantity ought to have (rescaling the units, regraining the time window, aggregating or disaggregating the exposure), a reparameterization test I can run in the spreadsheet, and a rule for what to do when the answer moves under reparameterization.
- where the procedure hard-stops with "no number comes out of this" and we tell the underwriter we can't price it, as against where it hands the question to a human because the remaining question is about our risk appetite or our solvency rather than about the world.
- an honest marking of the steps that are judgment calls wearing a lab coat.

Run it on three cases. (1) The chord problem itself, twice: once exactly as stated with no generator given, and once where I tell you the chord is produced by a machine that picks a uniformly random point in the disk and draws the chord perpendicular to the radius through that point. I want to see it return "ill-posed" the first time and a number the second, and I want to know what it did differently. (2) A component that has flown 200 times with zero failures; I need a defensible 90% upper bound on the per-flight failure probability, and I want to see what the procedure does with the three priors that all look uninformative here and all give different bounds. (3) The one I actually care about, which I suspect your procedure is going to be useless on: probability that a named sovereign imposes capital controls within twelve months. No symmetry, no exchangeable sequence, nothing to be indifferent between.

There's one homebrew move I've been using that I want you to adopt, kill, or show is a special case of your procedure. Instead of being indifferent over the parameter, I write down my uncertainty as the ten deciles of the outcome distribution and treat those as exchangeable — indifference over the quantile function rather than over some coordinate I picked to describe it. It feels like it sidesteps the parameterization problem, because I'm being indifferent over the thing the decision actually touches. My guess is that its either deep or complete bullshit, and if it's bullshit I want to know what it was doing that made it look like it worked.

Finally, tell me plainly what the procedure cannot do, so I know which numbers to keep apologizing for.
