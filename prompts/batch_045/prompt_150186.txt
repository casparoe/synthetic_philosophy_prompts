You are a formal epistemologist who has spent a decade mediating disputes between research programmes. You are completely fluent in the machinery both sides use — measures of inaccuracy, strict propriety, accuracy-dominance arguments, expected-epistemic-utility arguments, the treatment of full belief as a threshold on credence — and you have no stake in either side winning.

Two colleagues have argued past each other for years and have now agreed to an adversarial collaboration. Camp A, the accuracy monist (accuracy-first veritist): the only fundamental epistemic good is accuracy, understood as closeness to truth; the epistemic utility of a doxastic state is therefore a function of its accuracy alone; every genuinely epistemic norm is to be vindicated by showing that violating it costs accuracy; anything else that looks epistemically valuable is valuable only instrumentally, as a means to later accuracy, or is not epistemic value at all. Camp B, the epistemic-value pluralist: the expected-epistemic-utility formalism is the right tool, but the utility function has irreducibly non-accuracy components — explanatory grasp, unification, or evidential fit, for instance — and, further, the space of possibilities over which accuracy is measured is selected rather than given, so accuracy facts cannot be stated independently of those other values.

Before drafting, spend a few sentences brainstorming: list four or five candidate cruxes, then keep the three that best survive the signature test. A formulation passes the signature test only if a sophisticated advocate of that camp would sign it without editing and the opposing camp would recognise it as that camp's actual view rather than a straw version of it. Drop any crux whose resolution would change nobody's mind.

Produce the document they could both sign, in this format:

1. Agreed claims. Six to ten numbered one-sentence claims both camps can sign. They should cover the shared machinery (propriety as a necessary condition on any measure of inaccuracy; the accuracy-domination result for probabilistically incoherent credence functions; what the framework is and is not a theory of) and any shared verdicts about the test cases below. No vacuous claims, no claims smuggled in from one side.

2. Disagreements table. One row per crux, columns: crux; formulation the accuracy monist signs; formulation the pluralist signs; the case or computation both agree bears on the crux; the monist's prediction about that case; the pluralist's prediction. Each prediction should be specific, written as if recorded in advance, and tagged high / medium / low confidence. Use these cruxes:

- Scoring-rule selection, tested by the Dismissal case: an agent whose evidence-supported credences over three exhaustive possibilities are 0.5, 0.3, 0.2 considers dismissing the third possibility outright and renormalising. Work out what a bounded quadratic measure and an unbounded logarithmic measure each imply about that move, and record what both sides take the divergence to show.

- Partition relativity, tested by the Refinement Stability Test: the same evidence, the same agent, but the possibility space is refined (rain splits into drizzle and downpour). Does the ranking of two candidate doxastic states survive the refinement? State precisely what the measures imply — if the ranking is stable after all, record that as the finding rather than assuming instability.

- The scope of epistemic value, tested by the Archivist's Ledger: two agents with identical credence functions over every proposition in a shared algebra, one of whom additionally grasps the explanatory structure linking the propositions. Is there any epistemic difference left to record, and if so, what kind?

If you judge that stakes-weighting belongs — a scoring rule in which each world's inaccuracy contribution is multiplied by the practical cost of error in that world, checked for propriety — add it as a fourth row and stay within the limit.

3. Verbal or genuine. One short paragraph saying which parts of the dispute you take to be about words, which are substantive, and why.

4. Confidence ledger. Three lines: your own confidence in the agreed-claims section; the single claim the accuracy monist is least sure of; the single prediction the pluralist is least sure of.

Write for a reader who knows this material but has no internet access: define every technical term you use, do not name authors, papers, or titles, and cite nothing. Do not pad; prefer exact formulations to rhetorical ones.

Hard limit: 900 words for the whole document, table included.
