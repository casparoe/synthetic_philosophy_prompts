hey — below are rough notes for a short essay on whether LLMs are agents, simulators, or something else, aimed at people who've read a bit of the debate but aren't specialists. I'd like comments before I write it up properly. Please answer in British English.

THE NOTES

The agent/simulator dispute is usually run as a fight about what's inside the system. I want to argue it's badly posed in two ways.

First, both sides assume there's a single thing with a determinate answer to "what is it?". But the candidate units are different objects: the base model, the tuned assistant, the sampler around it, the scaffolded system with memory and tools, and the whole deployment. Agency might be something scaffolding confers rather than something you discover, and then the question "is the model an agent?" is like asking whether a transistor is a memory.

Second — the bit I actually care about — ordinary agency talk, in ethics and in economics, embeds an agent in a principal–agent structure. An agent acts for someone: there's a party whose interests it represents, who authorised it, and to whom it's accountable. That structure is part of what makes "what does she want, and why?" a question with a satisfying answer. A trained system has goal-directed, context-sensitive behaviour but no principal at all. No one authorised it, it represents no one's interests, and there's no one it answers to. I want to call this unowned purposiveness, and claim that it's the reason "what does the system want?" keeps feeling empty even for people who are happy to call it goal-directed and planful.

If that's right, the missing ingredient isn't consciousness, unity over time, or a self-model. It's authorisation — a normative relation, not a psychological one. So the agent/simulator debate is partly a normative argument wearing a metaphysical costume, which would explain why it tracks the stakes: we reach for "agent" when a system acts on our behalf or when we might hold it responsible, and for "simulator" when we want to keep it in the tool box. That makes the disagreement interest-relative in a way neither side admits.

What I'm least sure about: whether "unowned purposiveness" is a stable category or whether it collapses — either into "tool" (the purposiveness is just borrowed from the user's prompt) or into "simulator" (the purposiveness is only simulated, so of course there's no principal).
