I am a graduate student in philosophy writing a short commentary on Part I of Section X of Hume's Enquiry concerning Human Understanding, the section on miracles, and I have read the central passage perhaps five times now without settling on how it is supposed to work. I would be grateful for help understanding it. I am only commenting on Part I, so please answer without leaning on the later parts of the section that argue about the historical record — though do tell me if the passage simply cannot be understood in isolation. Here is the passage, from the Selby-Bigge edition (pp. 114–16), reproducing the source's own ellipses:

A miracle is a violation of the laws of nature; and as a firm and unalterable experience has established these laws, the proof against a miracle, from the very nature of the fact, is as entire as any argument from experience can possibly be imagined. Why is it more than probable, that all men must die; that lead cannot, of itself, remain suspended in the air; that fire consumes wood, and is extinguished by water; unless it be, that these events are found agreeable to the laws of nature, and there is required a violation of these laws, or in other words, a miracle to prevent them? Nothing is esteemed a miracle, if it ever happen in the common course of nature. It is no miracle that a man, seemingly in good health, should die on a sudden: because such a kind of death, though more unusual than any other, has yet been frequently observed to happen. But it is a miracle, that a dead man should come to life; because that has never been observed in any age or country. There must, therefore, be a uniform experience against every miraculous event, otherwise the event would not merit that appellation.... The plain consequence is (and it is a general maxim worthy of our attention), 'That no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such a kind, that its falsehood would be more miraculous, than the fact, which it endeavours to establish....' When anyone tells me, that he saw a dead man restored to life, I immediately consider with myself, whether it be more probable, that this person should either deceive or be deceived, or that the fact, which he relates, should really have happened. I weigh the one miracle against the other; and according to the superiority, which I discover, I pronounce my decision, and always reject the greater miracle. If the falsehood of his testimony would be more miraculous, than the event which he relates; then, and not till then, can he pretend to command my belief or opinion. In the foregoing reasoning we have supposed, that the testimony, upon which a miracle is founded, may possibly amount to an entire proof, and that the falsehood of that testimony would be a real prodigy: But it is easy to shew, that we have been a great deal too liberal in our concession, and that there never was a miraculous event established on so full an evidence.

What I want, concretely, is the following. First, take the passage apart: separate the argument that runs from "a firm and unalterable experience" to there being a proof against any miracle from the later balancing rule about weighing one improbability against another, and say whether the second is independent of the first, an application of it, or something the first is needed to motivate. Second, the comparison itself troubles me. On one side of the balance is a violation of a law of nature; on the other is the falsehood of a piece of testimony, which is at most a very unusual fact about human psychology. A friend of mine who works in probability says the two sides are not the same kind of quantity and that weighing them is a category mistake unless some common currency is supplied. Is that fair to Hume, and if not, what exactly does the currency consist in? Third, I do not know what to do with the closing paragraph, where Hume says he has been "a great deal too liberal in our concession." Does that retroactively turn the maxim into a rhetorical device — a standard conceded only so that it can be shown unsatisfiable — or does it introduce a separate, empirical claim about what has actually been attested, leaving the maxim standing as a principle? Fourth, here are two difficulties I invented, which I would like tested against the passage rather than against anything you remember: the vanishing-miracle problem, which is that since there must be uniform experience against an event for it to count as a miracle at all, two well-attested instances would disqualify the event from miraculous status, so that evidence for a miracle apparently cannot accumulate in the ordinary way no matter how many independent witnesses are added — does the passage really commit Hume to this, and is it a feature of the argument or a defect? And the institutional-echo case, in which the alleged event was public and a commemorative practice was instituted at the time and observed continuously thereafter, so that anyone who denies the event owes an explanation of the practice as well as of the testimony — by Hume's own maxim, does that make any difference to the weighing, and should it? Fifth, a check on a reconstruction I keep hearing: if the definitional claim pins the probability of a law-violation at or near zero, then reading the maxim as a comparison of likelihoods seems to divide by zero. My sense is that this reconstruction distorts what Hume is doing, but I cannot say why in a form that would survive a seminar. Please say what precisely is gained and lost in the translation.

Please argue in your own terms rather than by appeal to secondary literature, since I cannot follow up references. Where the passage is genuinely ambiguous, tell me what the ambiguity is and what sort of consideration would settle it; and tell me plainly if any of my questions rests on a misreading of the text.
