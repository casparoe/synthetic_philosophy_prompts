Hey — I teach a second-year ethics seminar, 18 students, one 75-minute slot, and most of them aren't philosophy majors (a bit of utilitarianism, one session on Kant, that's it). I want an activity where they first feel the pull of the idea that some actions are genuinely good to do but not required, and that omitting them isn't blameworthy — and then, more importantly, feel how unstable that category is once you start pushing on it. I'd rather they not hear the word "supererogation" until the last ten minutes, and I don't want to lecture up front at all.

Here's the half-formed thing I keep failing to make work: a cost ladder. Fixed benefit (a stranger's life), ten rungs of cost to the agent, each rung only a hair worse than the one below — stubbed toe at the bottom, near-certain death at the top. Everyone privately marks where "you'd have to do this" ends, where "you'd be amazing but you don't have to" begins, and where "nobody could possibly demand this" starts. Then I want them to run into the collapse themselves: the person whose line is at rung 4 and the person whose line is at rung 5 have to explain to each other why a two-percent difference in risk flips a moral category. Can you design that as something they do to each other, with rules and roles, rather than something I announce?

The part I actually care most about is a wrinkle they won't have seen before. My idea: the ratchet. Once you learn what people in the room actually did (or say they'd do), your sense of where the line sits moves — the person who goes furthest raises the bar for everyone else, which means the category partly eats itself, and now the other seventeen have a kind of complaint against the hero for making them look bad. Can you build a round where that happens to them for real, with small actual stakes (no money — chocolate, poker chips, a raffle for a silly prize, whatever), and where they notice it happening on their own? I also want a round where they try, and mostly fail, to rescue a non-arbitrary cutoff by finding a qualitative break somewhere on the continuous scale, and then have to say out loud what they ended up appealing to instead.

Deliverable: rules, materials, a timeline with the reveals, and a debrief that maps specific moments onto the moving parts — how the blame/praise asymmetry behaves, what happens to the line when the group's behaviour is known, and what the failed rescue attempt shows. Real constraints: whiteboard and a projector, no prep outside class, and it has to still work if only 12 show up.

Also two things: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'm guessing one student goes full act-utilitarian and refuses to play, and another tries to dissolve the whole thing into "well, it depends on the person"), and an honest note on which feature of the idea the activity structurally cannot reproduce and I'll just have to say in words. My guess is it can't separate "not required" from "positively permitted to skip" from "worth praising," but you tell me — and tell me if there's something bigger it leaves out.
