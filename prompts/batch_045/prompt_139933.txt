I've been going to a small reading group on early Buddhist philosophy, and we've spent the last several weeks on the no-self teaching: the analysis of a person into the five aggregates, the claim that what we call a person is a causally connected stream with no owner standing behind the experiences, and the thought that identity talk is at best a useful convention rather than a description of how things really are. I find the view attractive in the abstract. But when I try to actually use it, I notice I still have quite firm verdicts about particular cases, and I cannot get them to line up with each other. So I would like to treat this as an exercise in reflective equilibrium: here are my verdicts, and I want to know what principle, if any, captures them — or whether I have smuggled back in exactly the kind of deep fact the view was supposed to get rid of.

My verdicts:

1. Dreamless sleep, and also half an hour under general anesthesia: obviously the same person on waking. I feel no pull at all to hedge here, or to say that the question is merely conventional.
2. A man who at seventy shares no memory, desire, or settled trait with his twenty-year-old self, but is linked to him by long chains of overlapping connections: same person, and I would say so without hedging in an ordinary or legal context — though I also want to add that nothing deep makes this true, only the existence of the chain.
3. The textbook fission case, where each hemisphere is transplanted into a different body: there is no fact of the matter about which one is the original, and the original did not thereby die. Now a variant I invented and cannot stop thinking about: instead of a single operation, a team migrates neural tissue gradually over ten years, so that two fully functional brains with two streams of consciousness emerge, with each stream continuously connected to the original's throughout and no moment at which a division occurs. Here I feel even less inclined than in the sudden version to think there is a fact of the matter — yet, oddly, more inclined to say that each of the two owes the whole of a debt the pre-split person incurred, rather than half of it.
4. A woman in the late stages of dementia: I want to say that there is much less of her now than there used to be, and I resist being forced into a yes-or-no answer. I do not want to say she has ceased to exist either. It is a matter of degree.
5. A clinic overwrites, in a single session, a patient's entire episodic memory with a coherent but fabricated set. He wakes with no sense of discontinuity and behaves exactly as he would have; only the causal provenance of his memories differs. I lean strongly toward saying this is not the same person — or at least, that the person who wakes up is not the one who signed the consent form, and that the consent is therefore void.
6. Without any external intervention, someone comes over twenty years to believe a largely fictional account of her own life; a large share of her apparent memories are confabulated. Obviously the same person. This case does not move me at all, which sits badly with case 5, since the resulting states look alike and differ only in how they were produced.
7. Every neuron replaced, one at a time, by a functionally identical artificial unit over thirty years: the same person throughout. And I would say the same if the whole replacement were done in a single night under anesthesia, provided function is perfectly preserved — sudden substrate change bothers me far less than sudden content change.

The best principle I can come up with is this: a later stage of a stream is the same person as an earlier stage to the extent that it is linked to that stage by overlapping chains of memory, intention, and character, where the links must arise from the stream's own ongoing operation rather than being imposed from outside; the relation comes in degrees, and we round it off to a binary only when some decision forces us to.

Please test that principle against the cases and repair it where it fails. State each clause and say which case motivates it. Tell me what the repaired version predicts about two cases I have not settled: first, the artificial autobiography of case 5 spread out over twenty years, one memory replaced per week, with the patient never noticing; second, a soldier whose brain is stripped of its fine structure in an injury and then rebuilt from a connectome scan taken a year earlier, so that he recovers his old memories and traits — is he the person who enlisted? And tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, particularly my sensitivity to provenance in cases 5 and 6. I do not need a survey of the literature or suggestions for further reading; I want the principle, its clauses, its verdicts on the two unlisted cases, and a straight answer about what I would have to give up.
