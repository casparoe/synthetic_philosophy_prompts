You are a philosopher of probability who also has a working command of the mathematical background — invariant measures, Haar measure and uniqueness up to scale, improper priors, maximum-entropy methods — and of the epistemology of underdetermined or ill-posed questions. Before you start writing, spend some time in unstructured brainstorming: list the candidate defeaters you can think of, including ones you are inclined to reject, and ask yourself in each case whether it is really a defeater or just an unclarified question. Only then organize. Where you rely on a contested assumption of your own, say so explicitly. The position I want stress-tested — call it invariantism about indifference — is this:

The Bertrand-style paradoxes refute indifference over a naive partition, not indifference itself. Choosing "at random" among endpoints, or among points on a radius, or among midpoints in the disk, are three different experiments, and it is no surprise that they yield 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4. The lesson is that indifference must be applied to the symmetries of a problem rather than to a parametrization of it. On the corrected statement, indifference is a demand: the prior must be invariant under the transformation group that carries the problem into itself. Specify the experiment properly — a circle drawn on a large floor, straws tossed onto it — and the symmetry group is fixed (translations and rotations of the plane, plus scale), there is at most one invariant measure on the relevant space, and the paradox dissolves. Apparent ill-posedness is always a defect of the questioner's specification, never of the principle.

Deliverable: an unsentimental map of this position's exposure — what it risks, and where.

1. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each say whether a committed invariantist would concede it as counting against the view or dismiss it as resting on an incompletely specified problem. I want your own verdict, not just the critics'.

2. Take seriously the objection I find most troubling myself: symmetry fixes marginals, not dependence structure. Two points are placed on the circumference of a circle "at random." Rotation invariance forces each point's marginal to be uniform on the circle, but it does not force independence: the distribution supported on diametrically opposite pairs is invariant under joint rotation and has uniform marginals too. So the probability that the chord between the two points exceeds √3 R is 2/3 on one reading and 1 on the other, with nothing in the symmetry of the problem to choose between them. Does invariantism therefore need a supplementary rule — declare the parameters independent absent a reason otherwise, maximize entropy subject to the symmetry constraints? And if it adds one, does the view collapse into a rival (maximum-entropy objective Bayesianism, say) in which indifference as such does no independent work? Does the supplementary rule then generate a regress of its own, since independence is independence of some chosen coordinates and the choice of coordinates is exactly what the view disavows? Does the same worry reach the classic inscribed-triangle case?

3. Assess the empirical exposure. Does the invariant answer ever actually bind a real experiment, or is it recovered only in a limit — an unbounded plane, straws long relative to the circle, boundary effects removed — so that in practice one must specify the physical mechanism anyway? If the latter, what has indifference bought? Is there a testable prediction in the vicinity, e.g. that no mechanism whose setup genuinely respects the symmetry group can reproduce 1/3 or 1/4? Would a careful, repeatable experiment that yielded 1/3 from a setup one could not fault for asymmetry count against invariantism, or would the invariantist rightly reply that the symmetry was broken somewhere unseen?

4. Assess the scope limitation. There are natural indifference problems where the symmetry group gives nothing usable: indifference over the positive reals under scaling yields only an improper measure, and over the integers there is no countably additive uniform distribution at all, only finitely additive natural density, which answers some questions and not others. These are close to the cases for which indifference was historically wanted — priors over "how many," cosmological and anthropic magnitudes. Is the invariantist's silence here honest modesty about the method's reach, or a symptom that the method has content only where the answer was already implicit in the specification?

5. Give me your honest verdict on insulation. The reply "that problem was ill-posed" is in principle available for every counterexample, so the view risks being qualified into vacuity. Is it? Is there any case where invariantism, subjectivism, maximum-entropy objective Bayesianism, and mechanism-first views of probability come apart in what they predict, such that some finding or argument could favor one over the others? Or is the correct conclusion the friendly amendment that invariantism is best read as a constraint on how to specify a problem rather than as a generator of probabilities — and if so, is that amendment or capitulation?

6. Finally, the thing that would move me most: a case in which the symmetries really are fixed by the statement of the problem, the group is well behaved, the resulting measure is normalizable, and the answer still looks wrong or still fails to command agreement. Produce the best such case you can, and then tell me whether the invariantist's "you have not finished specifying the experiment" is, in that case, a genuine answer or a refusal.
