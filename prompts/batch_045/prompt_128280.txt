The thesis, so you know what's being attacked. I'm arguing that informational cascades are a smaller problem than the social learning literature makes them out to be, because a cascade is partly self-announcing: it leaves a signature in the sequence of actions that you can read without knowing anyone's private signal. Call it a stall. The signature isn't the agreement, it's the absence of defections: once a cascade is on, the defection rate is exactly zero, whereas genuine consensus at the same signal precision would almost certainly have thrown up at least one contrarian somewhere in a long run. So a run long enough and flat enough is evidence of a stall rather than of agreement, an outside observer who sees the run and knows the rough signal precision can tell that the tail of the sequence is carrying no information, and the information loss from the cascade is bounded rather than growing with how many people are already in it. Second half of the talk is the part I actually care about: if that's right, the standard policy levers in this literature — pay the early movers, force disclosure of private signals, randomize the speaking order so the smartest people go first — are aimed at the wrong end of the sequence. You should be watching for stalls and intervening at onset, not subsidizing the first three actors. I also want to claim, more loosely, that a lot of what gets called herding in the wild is phantom consensus, flat runs produced by correlated private signals rather than by anybody ignoring their own.

My argument in four steps, roughly: define the stall as a run length at which the no-defection probability under consensus drops below some threshold; show the likelihood ratio for stall-versus-consensus is monotone in run length; argue the observer therefore recovers most of the information the cascade appeared to destroy; note that in the field the correlation story gives you flatness without any cascade at all, so the naive diagnostic has been over-counting cascades the whole time.
