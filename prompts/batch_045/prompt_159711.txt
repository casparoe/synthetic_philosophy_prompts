Hey — I'm in a tiny reading group that's been working through Dennett's four kinds of minds (the Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian and Gregorian creatures), and we're putting together a silly end-of-term slideshow where everyone has to contribute one joke as a kind of palette cleanser between the serious talks. Mine is supposed to be the one that actually lands with people who know the taxonomy, so I really don't want a generic nerd joke with animal names swapped in. The punchline has to turn on the real differences: the hard-wired creature that just does what its genes say, the one that can only learn by actually trying things and getting burned or rewarded, the one that lets candidate actions get eliminated inside its own head first so it doesn't have to die to find out, and then the Gregorian one that doesn't evolve or figure out the tricks at all but inherits mind-tools somebody else already built and thinks thoughts it could never have thought on its own.

Here's the extra wrinkle, which is the part I'm stuck on. One person in the group half-jokingly proposed a fifth kind she calls the Baldwinian creature: the idea is that whatever a creature manages to learn the hard way during its own lifetime gradually stops being learned and starts being built in for its descendants, so a skill that cost somebody enormous effort ends up free and automatic a few generations later — her example was a rat that figures out a maze and whose great-grandchildren somehow just know it. I thought that was a weirdly funny thing to hang a joke on, but every version I try just turns into a list of five creatures doing five predictable things, which isn't a joke.

So: can you write me a short joke — something I could read out in about twenty seconds — that would work for people who know these four kinds, and that somehow makes the Baldwinian creature the punchline rather than a fifth item on a list? I was vaguely imagining an escape room as the setting: four creatures locked in a room, each getting out (or not) in its own characteristic way, and then the Baldwinian creature somehow turning the whole thing on its head — but if a better setting occurs to you, use that instead. Please don't explain the taxonomy back to me, and don't pad it with footnotes.

After the joke, give me exactly one sentence on why the punchline is supposed to be funny, and then finish with a single line naming the two kinds of mind the joke really hinges on, in this form: \answer{Popperian, Baldwinian} — with whatever's right for your version substituted in.
