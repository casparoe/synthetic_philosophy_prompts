You are a political theorist who works on the definition and measurement of corruption in comparative perspective, with a working knowledge of public-administration ethics and of patronage and clientelist politics in societies where gift-exchange is a normal part of social life. Please think this through carefully: before committing yourself, lay out two or three candidate principles, run each of them past every case below, and say plainly which of my verdicts each one would force me to give up.

I'm trying to work out what I actually mean by "corruption," going on gut reactions to cases rather than on any official definition — the standard anti-corruption formula about abuse of entrusted power for private gain is precisely what I want to put pressure on, so please don't stop there. My verdicts:

1. A citizen pays a clerk twenty dollars to get her already-complete passport application processed inside the statutory five days; without it the file will mysteriously go missing. The clerk is corrupt. The citizen is not — she's being robbed.
2. A minister takes an envelope from the firm that won a contract, after the award, where that firm really was the best bid and he would have picked them anyway. Corrupt, obviously — which I take to show that corruption can't just be about distorting outcomes.
3. A village elder who is the recognized local authority mediates a land dispute and afterwards receives two goats from the family that won. Not corrupt — that's a gift, not a bribe.
4. A hospital administrator gives a scarce ICU bed to a patient whose family donated to the hospital last year. Corrupt.
5. A company pays officials to get a safety regulation repealed, knowing the resulting product will kill people. Corrupt, and much worse than case 1.
6. A company pays the same amount in the same kind of market, but only because all its competitors pay and it will otherwise lose the contract. I can't call that corrupt, or at least not in the same way — yet it's the same act as case 5.
7. An admissions officer admits a big donor's child over a slightly better-qualified applicant: corrupt. The same officer admits a legacy child — an alum's daughter — over a slightly better-qualified applicant: fine, not corrupt. These two feel completely different to me and I can't say why they should.
8. A procurement minister awards a contract to his cousin's firm, which is genuinely the lowest bidder and best qualified; no money changes hands, he discloses the relationship but doesn't recuse himself, and he's partly motivated by wanting people from his region to have something. Not corrupt, I think — but I dislike it in a way I dislike none of the clear cases.

The principle I keep coming back to is: an official act is corrupt when a private benefit — to the agent, or to someone the agent has a special relationship with — is allowed to do the work that the reasons constitutive of the office were supposed to do. Test that against the list and repair it, or replace it with something better.

Then tell me what your account says about the case I go back and forth on: a city data scientist designing an algorithm to rank applicants for public housing adds a feature, "length of residence in current neighborhood," which he privately knows will advantage applicants from his own ethnic community. He takes nothing, discloses nothing, and the feature has a respectable policy rationale in community stability. There's no payment, no deal, and no personal gain — and yet I want to call it corrupt.

Finally, give me two further cases your principle settles that I haven't thought of, at least one where its verdict is one I'd find unwelcome, and say honestly which of my verdicts above it cannot accommodate.
