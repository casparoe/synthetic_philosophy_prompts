I run a small reading group in epistemology, and two of its members have spent an entire term disagreeing about what one should do on discovering that someone one regards as an epistemic peer disagrees with one. The first holds roughly an equal-weight conciliationism: the discovery of peer disagreement is itself a reason to move, and in the cleanest case one should end up near the midpoint between one's own confidence and the peer's, because the fact that she landed where she did is evidence one cannot simply set aside. The second holds a steadfast view: if one has already folded her competence, her evidence and her care into one's own assessment, then the bare fact that she arrived at a different answer adds nothing further, and splitting the difference is a way of discarding the reasoning one did the first time. They have both stopped learning anything from arguing at each other, so I want to convene them as an adversarial collaboration and put a draft joint document on the table for them to negotiate over. Please write that draft, working from the arguments themselves and not from the literature: no citations, no bibliography, no appeal to what any named author has said.

Two distinctions of my own that I want the document organized around. First, I think they keep sliding between three different claims: that disagreement is evidence bearing on the truth of the disputed proposition; that it is evidence bearing on the reliability of the method by which each party reached her view; and that it is evidence bearing on whether the other person was a peer in the first place. I want the joint statement to keep these apart and to record what each side says about each. Second, call a proposition method-entangled when settling it requires deploying the very method that is in dispute — a disagreement about whether a given argument is a good one, between someone whose test of goodness is intuitive plausibility and someone whose test is fit with a formal framework, or a disagreement between someone who trusts a heuristic and someone who distrusts it about a conclusion that heuristic generates. Both sides should say what their view implies about method-entangled propositions, and in particular whether it is even coherent there to treat the peer's verdict as independent evidence. I also want each side to state whether disagreement is meant to be an evidential defeater or a matter of doxastic risk management — a reason to hedge because being wrong is costly — since I suspect they mean different things by "should move." Finally, both are attracted to the thought that across a long run of disagreements with acknowledged peers one ought to come out calibrated, so the document should record in advance what each predicts about who turns out to be right more often in such a run.

Format it as exactly five agreed claims, numbered, one or two sentences each, that both could sign — substantive enough that a third party would learn something from them rather than pieties about disagreement being common — with no introduction and no concluding paragraph. Follow them with a table of exactly four cruxes, one row per crux, with columns for: the crux; each side's position on it phrased in wording that side would accept; the case or argument both would accept as bearing on it; each side's prediction about that case; and whether the residual disagreement is factual, normative, or merely verbal. Mark explicitly any crux where you conclude the two are talking past each other rather than disagreeing.
