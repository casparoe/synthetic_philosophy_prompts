hi — I work on a small team that builds an automated budgeting tool for a regional flood agency. Every cycle it has to pick between things like build the levee now for sure, versus spending the same money on a portfolio of smaller projects that only pay off if a flood of a certain size hits a certain sub-basin, where the probabilities come out of a model nobody on the team fully trusts. We keep arguing past each other and I've realised the arguments are all really about which of the coherence requirements behind maximise-a-weighted-average-over-outcomes we're willing to let the tool violate. So I want a decision tree that sorts you into a position on that question by what you'd actually choose, not by which label you like.

Hard constraints on how you write it:

Every internal node has to be a concrete choice between two (occasionally three) options with numbers in it. No theory words at the nodes — nothing like independence, transitivity, reduction of compound gambles, sure-thing, ambiguity, expected utility. A reader should be able to answer each node without having any idea which position it leads to. All the naming goes at the leaves. Give it to me as a nested list (node, then the answers, then where each answer sends you), plus a short legend paragraph at the end.

Leaves that need to be reachable: the full-strength weighted-average view; the one that weights the probabilities themselves according to where they sit in the ranking of outcomes; the one that weights them differently depending on whether the outcomes are gains or losses relative to where we already are; the one that keeps a set of probability estimates and ranks by the worst case over that set; the one that keeps a set and averages over it while having it's own separate attitude to how much it dislikes not knowing; the one that says some pairs of options simply aren't comparable and refuses to rank them; the one with a hard threshold such that no probability of gain is worth crossing it; and, if there's room, the one that knowingly has cyclic rankings but plans around them. Each leaf gets the standard label if it has one, or unnamed if nobody holds it, plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Aim for about seven branch nodes and eight to ten leaves.

Four cases I want built into the nodes. Two of them I made up precisely so nobody can recite an answer:

One, the provenance test. Three tickets, each of them stated as paying ten million with probability 0.5. The first is a coin flip. The second is a draw from a bag you've been told holds 50 red and 50 blue but which you have never looked inside. The third comes from a model that says 0.5 and then, when asked, says it won't tell you how confident it is. Would you pay anything to swap any of these for another, how much, and in which direction.

Two, the escalator. Nine options arranged so that you prefer each one to the one before it and you also prefer the first one to the last. You get to walk up the escalator one step at a time at a dollar a step, and from the bottom you can see the whole thing including where it ends. How many steps do you take, and what makes you stop.

Three, a menu test: the ranking flips depending on whether an option that will never be picked is sitting on the list.

Four, a packaging test: the very same gamble offered either as a single draw or as two draws in a row.

Then two follow-ups I care about more than the tree itself. First, tell me honestly which pairs of leaves your tree can't separate — I suspect the two weight-the-probabilities views collapse into each other under everything except the gains-versus-losses test, and I suspect worst-case-over-a-set and weight-the-probabilities-in-the-pessimistic-direction look identical if you only ever probe with two-outcome gambles. Say what extra case would pry them apart, or say that nothing will. Second, walk through the combinations of answers your seven nodes permit that no actual position occupies, and for each one say whether it's empty because it's incoherent, because it's merely unattractive, or because nobody has bothered to defend it.

Please don't give me citations, papers, or a reading list — I havent got library access and would just have to take your word for it. Labels at the leaves are plenty; skip author names and dates entirely. And write the nodes for an audience of four engineers plus one philosopher who is going to be insufferable about the labels, so keep the nodes in plain language and let the leaves do the technical work.
