Imagine that Kurt Gödel, who died in 1978, could be brought up to date on a few developments in physics and philosophy that occurred after his death, and then asked whether he still holds the view about time that he defended in connection with his 1949 rotating solution of Einstein's field equations. Write the essay that answers this question on his behalf.

Take the historical position to be roughly this. In that solution, the matter of the universe rotates in such a way that its light cones tip over far enough to admit closed timelike curves: an observer could, in principle, travel along a world-line that returns to her own past. Gödel read this as showing that an objective lapse of time, or becoming, cannot be a feature of reality, since a universe in which one can revisit one's past leaves no room for events to genuinely come into being. He connected the conclusion with the relativity of simultaneity in special relativity, and he appears to have regarded it as a genuinely paradoxical result: the correct physical theory seemed to contradict the most immediate deliverance of experience. Because he was a Platonist about mathematical and conceptual truth, and because he was prepared to draw idealist conclusions from a physical theory rather than distrust the theory, he was willing to say that intuitive time is in some sense ideal or mind-dependent.

Suppose now that he is told the following.

First: observational cosmology has established that our own universe is not rotating, is expanding, and shows no trace of closed timelike curves. Moreover, physicists have produced a body of arguments and results, associated with Stephen Hawking and going under the label of chronology protection, which suggest that the laws of physics actively prevent closed timelike curves from being realized: any attempt to engineer one in a bounded region appears to require matter with negative energy densities of a kind not observed, or to destroy the would-be time machine before it can operate. Gödel's model, on this telling, remains a mathematical solution but describes no physically attainable world.

Second: notwithstanding this, eternalism — the view that past, present, and future events are all equally real, and that nothing about the present is objectively special — has become the dominant position among physicists and philosophers of physics. Its grounds have little to do with rotating universes: they include the relativity of simultaneity, the apparent time-symmetry of fundamental dynamics, and the habit of treating the entire history of the cosmos as a single four-dimensional object.

Third: it is now widely argued that our sense of a directed, flowing time can be explained entirely by a very low-entropy condition at the big bang together with statistical mechanics, without positing any objective flow or privileged present.

Fourth, and this is a stipulated hypothetical rather than a fact: suppose he is shown a research programme that claims to derive, from within eternalism, why any subsystem of a block universe that has memory, acts on incomplete information about itself, and is temporally asymmetric in its information-processing will, as a matter of physical necessity, represent time as flowing and represent its own present as privileged. Call this the internal-view programme. If it succeeded, it would explain the experience of passage rather than explain it away.

Your essay should do the following. Separate the strands in Gödel's thinking: an argument from the mere possibility of a universe with closed timelike curves, an argument from the relativity of simultaneity, and an argument from the block-universe picture of physical theory. Ask which of these, if any, his conclusion actually depended on, and say whether the loss of physically realizable closed timelike curves removes a load-bearing premise or only removes a vivid illustration. Ask, given his Platonism and his willingness to countenance idealist conclusions, whether he would instead have been tempted to read his own result as a reductio of the physical theory rather than of experience, and whether chronology protection changes which side of that paradox he should reject. Ask how he should respond to the third and fourth items — in particular, whether an explanation of why we experience passage is compatible with, or in tension with, his claim that passage is unreal, and whether he should distinguish the claim that there is no objective present from the claim that the experience of becoming is ideal. Finally, commit to a verdict: state, and justify, whether Gödel would uphold, reformulate, or abandon his conclusion, and if he would reformulate it, set out the reconstructed argument you think he would offer. Reason from the information given above rather than from outside sources, and do not merely survey the options: defend one answer.
