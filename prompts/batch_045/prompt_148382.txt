I'm on the grants committee of a small private foundation (about $6M a year to give away; historically we've funded farmed-animal welfare stuff — cage-free corporate campaigns, slaughter and stunning enforcement, a bit of fish welfare). This year we got a strange application and I've been asked to draft the decision memo, and I'm honestly torn, so I'd rather you wrote the thing and I argued with it.

The application is from a two-person group calling themselves the Affective Substrate Project, asking for $1.8M over three years. Their core idea, as I understand it: what matters morally isn't the number of minds or subjects but the amount of valence-bearing processing — they call it "affective density" — per unit of compute. On that picture you don't need distinct, bounded, persistent subjects for there to be enormous moral weight; a system with nobody "in" it, just an enormous throughput of something suffering-like running on cheap hardware, could dominate everything we do about chickens. They want to (a) build a rough measurement scheme for affective density, (b) turn it into a kind of welfare-footprint label that a lab could attach to a training run so it can pick "valence-sparse" architectures, and (c) get that written into procurement standards. They're upfront that their own probability of any of this being morally relevant by 2050 is low — they say 3 to 5 percent — but that conditional on it mattering the numbers are astronomical, so the expected value swamps everything else, and that at that ratio the burden of proof actually flips onto those of us funding the measurable near-term work.

Two things I need the memo to settle. First, our charter restricts grants to "the alleviation of suffering among sentient beings now living," and one faction on the committee reads that as ruling out anything about minds (or whatever they are) that don't exist yet, while another points out that we routinely fund work whose beneficiaries are future animals — animals that only exist because of the intervention we funded — so the precedent arguably cuts the other way. Second, our trustee Dana says this is Pascal's mugging with a spreadsheet: you can't trust a 3-to-5-percent number enough to multiply it by astronomical magnitudes, and the same $1.8M put into stunning-at-slaughter enforcement in a handful of countries buys effects we can actually audit. I half agree with her and half don't, and I don't want the memo to just assert that expected-value reasoning wins by construction.

So: draft the committee's decision memo — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with a concrete dollar figure and a time frame, conditions the applicants could actually respond to, and a straight answer on the charter question. Give Dana's objection in its strongest form and then say honestly why it loses or why it doesn't. One more wrinkle, use it or ignore it: I worry that publishing a welfare-footprint standard either hands labs a cheap moral fig leaf ("our cluster is valence-sparse") without changing anything, or pushes toward architectures that are less capable and so slows down safety work that the same people would want. And please don't pad the memo with citations or suggested reading — we can't check them and nobody on this committee will read them.
