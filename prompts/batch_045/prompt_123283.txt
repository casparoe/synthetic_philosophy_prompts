I work at a mid-sized foundation that is about to commit a roughly forty-million-dollar, five-year program budget, and I want a scripted dialogue to circulate to our program team as a discussion document before the final vote. The team is divided between two ways of thinking about cause prioritization, and the division is not merely temperamental: applied to our three finalists, the two approaches yield different rankings. I would like the disagreement written up as a conversation rather than as two memos, because what I want the team to see is where exactly the two approaches come apart.

The three finalists, as our internal dossier describes them:

- **Cause A — mass drug administration for soil-transmitted helminths** across eight endemic countries. Large affected population, cheap and well-understood delivery mechanism, plausibly steeply diminishing returns as coverage rises, and several large funders already active. Our in-house evidence review has also recently and substantially downgraded the long-run developmental benefits that used to carry much of the case for it.
- **Cause B — a treaty campaign**: a coalition effort to secure a binding international instrument restricting a class of autonomous weapons, pursued alongside parallel national legislation in six jurisdictions. Our political consultants model it as effectively all-or-nothing. Below some threshold of sustained funding and staffing across the coalition, the campaign cannot maintain the personnel, litigation, and drafting capacity it needs and produces nothing durable; above it, the probability of success rises sharply, and success is durable in the sense that an instrument in force keeps constraining behaviour long after we stop paying for it. At present very few funders are involved.
- **Cause C — measurement and registry infrastructure** for a broad class of neglected conditions: baseline burden estimation, incidence surveillance, and a public registry of cost-effectiveness estimates. The direct effects inside the cause are modest. Most of its value, if it has any, comes from redirecting *other* funders' money over the next decade and from preserving options that we cannot presently describe.

The two characters: **Priya Anand**, who defends the importance–tractability–neglectedness framework as the right way to think about this choice, and **Tobias Lund**, who argues that the framework should be demoted in favour of an explicit estimate of the counterfactual value of the marginal dollar. Both are senior, both have read the same dossier, and neither should be written as the other's caricature.

Please have the dialogue work through, at minimum, the following four cruxes, with both characters forced to take a position on each rather than talking past one another:

1. **The shape of the production function, and the sign of neglectedness.** Under diminishing returns, neglectedness really is a reason to prioritize: few prior resources means the marginal dollar goes further. But where the marginal returns are *increasing* over some range — tipping points, coalition thresholds, minimum-viable-capacity effects — the reverse holds, and a dollar is worth more where others are already committed. On that reading neglectedness has no fixed sign; it is a proxy for where a cause sits on its production function, and reading it as a uniform multiplier is a category mistake. The characters should ask, concretely, which of A, B, and C plausibly have convex regions and what evidence would tell us.
2. **A related point about aggregation.** For a threshold cause the quantity that matters is the probability of crossing the threshold, not expected importance; multiplying an expected-importance figure by a tractability score computes the wrong thing. Variance is not neutral here.
3. **The arithmetic of the multiplication itself.** The three factors as ordinarily scored have no fixed units, and their product is not invariant to reparametrizations that look harmless: measuring importance in lives rather than in DALYs, or on a log rather than a linear scale, can reorder the ranking. Worse, if neglectedness is scored as resources devoted *relative to* the size of the problem, then the importance factor is either cancelled out or counted twice, depending on the exact definition. The clean version of the framework, I take it, treats it as a first-order approximation to something like a production function with exponents on importance, tractability, and cumulative resources, with the neglectedness term entering through the returns-to-scale exponent. If that is right, the exponents rather than the factors carry the content, and they would have to be estimated rather than guessed. Priya should be given the strongest available replies: that the framework was always a screening device for a wide funnel rather than a decision rule, with cost-effectiveness analysis doing the fine-grained work; that estimating the exponents for novel causes is precisely what cannot be done, so a transparent qualitative score beats spurious precision; and that the same complaint about scale-dependence can be turned against Tobias's own numbers.
4. **Counterfactual rather than correlational accounting.** What we are choosing is the difference our particular grant makes, which depends on replaceability (if we step back from A, does another funder plausibly step in?), on absorption capacity (whether the binding constraint is money or scarce talent), and on flow-through effects of the kind that dominate C, where the benefits accrue to other people's budgets and to options we cannot yet name. Neglectedness measured as resources currently devoted is a poor proxy for all three and may be inversely related to some of them.

Requirements of form. The dialogue should run roughly 1,400 to 1,800 words. Each character must respond to what the other has actually said, including conceding at least one substantive point along the way — I want the team to see movement, not two monologues. Neither character should win by introducing machinery the other is not permitted to understand: if a distinction or result is invoked, the other character must be able to restate it and push back on it. It should not end in the comfortable thought that judgment and frameworks are both needed. It should end with a precisely stated residual disagreement about how to rank A, B, and C, together with each character saying what single empirical finding or conceptual argument would settle it — even if they disagree about what would settle it. Please write it in a professional register suitable for circulation to colleagues, and avoid citations, footnotes, and bibliography entirely: refer to positions and arguments descriptively, and do not attribute any of the frameworks or slogans to named authors or invent sources for them.
