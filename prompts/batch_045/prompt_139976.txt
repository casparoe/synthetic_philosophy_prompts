I host a podcast on explanation and evidence in the historical and social sciences. My next guest works on path dependence and defends a strong version of it, which she formulates in three parts. First: in a real range of episodes, an outcome was produced by a cascade in which some early event — one no better suited to producing that outcome than its available rivals — was amplified by positive feedback of the familiar kinds (learning effects, coordination effects, adaptive expectations). Second: the proper test for such a claim is an erasability test — ask whether deleting or reversing the early event, while leaving the amplifying mechanism itself in place, would have changed the outcome. Third: for the episodes that pass that test, the answer to the question would something functionally equivalent have arrived anyway is often no, at any rate at the level of description at which the outcome mattered to the people who had to live inside it.

She has given this interview before and handles it very smoothly. I want you to draft ten to twelve questions that make the machinery do real work. For each question, add a one- or two-sentence note on the reply you expect her to give, and then a follow-up written specifically against that reply rather than alongside it.

Coverage and constraints:

She is impatient with the standard war stories about keyboard layouts and videocassette formats, and will say they have been both over-claimed and over-debunked. Do not build a question around either; if one appears at all, use it only to force her to state what a well-documented instance of lock-in would have to look like.

At least one question must attack the erasability test on the following ground: for any actual episode we possess only the sequence that occurred, so the counterfactual in which the early event is removed is not something anyone can observe. Press her on what could count as evidence for or against a lock-in claim if the tape cannot be run twice, and follow up on the move you should expect her to make — appealing either to cross-case comparisons in which a similar mechanism operated with a different initial ordering, or to the parts of the mechanism that are observable independently of the outcome.

At least one question must press on the grain of description. The same episode looks contingent when described finely and inevitable when described coarsely: this firm's format prevails versus some shared format prevails. Ask what fixes the correct grain. Anticipate her answering that it is fixed by the alternatives actually available to the agents at the time, or by what those agents had reason to care about, and prepare the follow-up that makes trouble for that answer — in particular the worry that the grain will then be chosen so as to guarantee whichever verdict she wants.

At least one question must raise the selection objection: competitive pressures, deliberate standard-setting, and the simple fact that agents can see an incumbent's defects are all mechanisms for displacing inferior arrangements, so lock-in should be rare, self-correcting, and very hard to detect after the fact. Expect her to reply that the switching costs are themselves products of the arrangement that got locked in, and have a follow-up ready on whether that reply insulates the view from ever being wrong.

At least one question should apply her own test to a case I have invented for the occasion, which you should reproduce before asking about it. In 1908 a provincial government sets up a land registry and must decide whether to index it by parcel or by owner; the clerk who opens the office indexes by parcel, because that is how the office's one filing cabinet happens to be labelled. A neighbouring province opens a registry the same year and indexes by owner for an equally incidental reason. For twenty years both registers are equally accurate and equally cheap to keep up. Then the parcel-indexed province develops a dense market in mortgage lending and title insurance, because lenders can aggregate and compare parcels; the owner-indexed province does not, and by 1940 it cannot convert, since re-indexing would interrupt every pending transaction. But the owner-indexed province has markedly fewer contested inheritances, because family claims run through persons rather than through plots. Ask her whether this is a case of lock-in on her test. In your note, name the specification you expect her to demand before she will answer — I suspect it will be either whether the divergence in lending markets was foreseeable in 1908, or whether her account requires the retained arrangement to be worse than the discarded one — and then let the follow-up ask why that specification, rather than some other, is the one that settles the matter.

At least one question must press the normative upshot without letting her treat it as someone else's problem. If lock-in is real and we generally cannot tell in advance which of several rival arrangements is better, what follows — greater willingness to intervene in early standard-setting, greater willingness to break up incumbents, or simply greater humility? Then follow up on the tension between her descriptive claim, that which arrangement wins is in a sense arbitrary, and any prescriptive claim to the effect that we should have chosen differently.

Close the sequence with a question that hands her one concrete way of turning out to be wrong, phrased so that she cannot treat it as a request for a summary.

Assume the audience already knows the framework: the questions should be intelligible to such an audience and useless to anyone wanting an introduction to it. Keep them in a natural spoken register but precise, and order them so that the last third is where the pressure is highest. Nothing may turn on looking anything up — no questions about what to read, who else holds the view, or how she came to it.
