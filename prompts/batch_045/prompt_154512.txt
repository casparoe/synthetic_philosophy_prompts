In most common-law criminal courts a jury is asked to do two things that seem to pull against each other: the twelve of them deliberate together, as a single body, until they converge — and then the verdict does not count at all unless the convergence is complete, i.e. unanimous.

Read through the Condorcet jury theorem, this looks like a badly designed institution. The theorem's guarantee needs voters who are individually better than chance and whose errors are independent; deliberation is a machine for destroying exactly that independence (shared evidence, deference to the articulate, cascades). And on the usual Condorcet assumptions unanimity is strictly less accurate than simple majority rule, and gets worse as the jury gets bigger.

So what does the actual institution presuppose? Candidates: that juror errors are already strongly correlated, so that unanimity is a hedge against a shared mistake rather than a demand for twelve independent confirmations; that the point is legitimacy or the appearance of certainty rather than accuracy; that the errors are asymmetric in the way the theorem is not, since acquitting the guilty and convicting the innocent are not equally costly. Reconstruct the implicit epistemology, say which of these the practice strictly presupposes and which it merely sits well with, and say whether the parts of the practice are consistent with each other.

Assume, for the sake of the question, the following empirical stipulation: deliberation raises each juror's individual accuracy noticeably while making their errors substantially correlated. Now consider a reform that takes the theorem seriously: each juror records a private verdict before any discussion; if nine or more of the twelve already agree, there is no deliberation and that is the verdict; otherwise they deliberate and decide by majority. What would that reform buy, what would it cost, and is the cost something the Condorcet framework can even represent?
