I built this argument for a graduate seminar on higher-order evidence and now I cannot locate its mistake, even though I cannot accept where it ends.

(1) Evidence that a belief-forming process of mine is unreliable can rationally require me to revise a belief produced by that process: if a blood panel shows I was dosed with a hallucinogen an hour ago, I should stop believing what my senses reported during that hour. Call any such evidence higher-order.

(2) The verdict that my first-order processes were unreliable during that hour is itself one of my beliefs, delivered by some route or other — the laboratory printout, my memory of the needle, my reading of the technician's note — and (1) draws no distinction between the order of the process indicted and the order of the process doing the indicting.

(3) So apply (1) to that verdict itself. Whatever route delivered it can be impugned by a further consideration of the same kind, and that further verdict by yet another, without end.

(4) At no level, then, does a requirement to revise come to rest: for every deliverance there is a higher-level consideration available that would defeat the process which produced it.

(5) So the antecedent of (1) is never cleanly met, and (1) never applies.

(6) Therefore higher-order evidence never rationally requires any revision of any belief: whatever I learn about the provenance of my beliefs — that I was drugged, deceived, or systematically misled for years — I should keep exactly the credences I already have.

(7) But (6) is unacceptable: in the hallucinogen case revising is plainly the rational response, and (6) tells a person who discovers that everything they were taught came from a decades-long propaganda campaign to change nothing at all.

I would rather give up almost anything else in the chain than (1) and (7). Which step should I reject, why did that step look sound when I wrote it, and what does rejecting it commit me to? Two further cases on which I want any diagnosis to earn its keep. First: an audit I have excellent independent reason to trust informs me that 30% of the first-order beliefs I formed in the last hour are no better than a coin flip, and declines to identify which ones — does your account tell me to lower confidence in all of them uniformly, in none of them, or in some pattern, and why is that response rational rather than arbitrary? Second: in the ordinary mild-revision case I lower my confidence in p without abandoning it, and so end up believing p while believing that my total evidence no longer supports p; if epistemic akrasia of that sort is irrational, then (1) generates incoherence whichever way I go — so say whether the way out is to permit rational akrasia, and what permitting it costs.
