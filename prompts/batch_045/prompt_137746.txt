I teach a final-year option on probability and induction, and last week's seminar on Bertrand's paradox left me and a colleague in the department — a formal epistemologist considerably more sanguine than I am about objective Bayesianism — stuck at what I take to be the real crux. We agree about the algebra: with the unit disc, and the question whether a "random" chord exceeds √3 (the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle), the three classical procedures give 1/3 (two points chosen uniformly on the circumference), 1/2 (a uniform point on a uniformly chosen radius, with the chord drawn perpendicular to it) and 1/4 (a uniform point in the disc taken as the chord's midpoint). What we disagree about is what the multiplicity shows. I should like your credence in the following proposition: Bertrand-style multiplicity is a decisive objection to the principle of indifference as a generator of priors for continuous parameters — decisive in the sense that (i) no non-arbitrary, non-question-begging selection among the admissible answers is available, and (ii) there is no determinate fact of the matter about the correct answer until a sampling procedure has been fixed. Take it to be resolved affirmatively if, thirty years hence, the settled view in formal epistemology is that indifference cannot by itself deliver a prior for a continuous parameter; resolved negatively if the invariance-and-maximum-entropy programme is by then generally held to have answered the challenge. My own credence is around 0.55; my colleague's is around 0.2; and I want to know where that gap most likely lives.

So: give me your number, but give it to me decomposed — a probability and one line of justification for each term, then an explicit combination, then a statement of which term the final figure is most sensitive to and what would move it. I have in mind at least these: (a) that the "ill-posedness" reply succeeds outright, i.e. that once a measure is specified nothing arbitrary remains, rather than the arbitrariness having merely been relocated one level up, to the choice of parameterisation or of invariance group; (b) that invariance or maximum-entropy constraints can pin down a unique prior for a physically meaningful continuous quantity without the group or the parameterisation itself being a free choice; (c) that there is a determinate fact of the matter about the chord question as originally posed, so that the question is under-described rather than genuinely incomplete; (d) that the pathology is peculiar to continuous, geometrical set-ups, given that the same under-determination appears in the wine-and-water case, where taking the ratio of wine to water as uniform gives 5/8 for the probability that the ratio is at most 2, whilst taking the proportion of wine as uniform gives 5/6; (e) whether an empirical resolution is even coherent, since actually dropping straws onto a circle would only disclose the physics of whoever or whatever drops them.

One item I should like you to work out rather than recall. Consider a fourth procedure: pick a point uniformly from the disc, then pick a direction uniformly at random and draw the chord through that point in that direction. Derive the resulting probability that the chord exceeds √3 — I make it a little over 0.6, but I want to see the derivation and I am prepared to be corrected — and then tell me whether, by mixing procedures, or by biasing the distribution of midpoints towards the centre or the rim, one can obtain any value strictly between 0 and 1. If one can, say whether the availability of a full continuum of equally symmetry-looking answers should shift your numbers in (a) and (b), and why. Please do not dissolve the exercise into the observation that everything here depends on what one means by "probability" or by "rational"; I want the numbers, the arithmetic, and then the honest identification of the weak point where a determined opponent of your conclusion would press.
