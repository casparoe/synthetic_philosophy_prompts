I've managed to argue myself into a corner about pain asymbolia and I want to know which plank to pull out.

Setup first, so we're using the same words. Pain asymbolia — I read about this a while ago and might be getting the details wrong, so correct me gently if I've mangled it — is a thing that sometimes shows up after certain kinds of brain damage. The patient will tell you the pain is there, point to where it is, say it's burning rather than stabbing, and rate it 8 out of 10 on the hospital scale. But they say it doesn't bother them. Some of them apparently laugh about it. They still flinch or pull away reflexively, they just don't seem to mind, and don't seem to care whether it stops.

The argument:

(1) If an experience is intrinsically bad, it's bad in virtue of what it's like, not in virtue of anyone's attitude toward it. So if pain is intrinsically bad, any experience with the same phenomenal character is equally bad, however the subject happens to feel about it.

(2) In asymbolia the phenomenal character of the pain is retained — the patients report the same locations, the same qualities, the same intensities — but everything bad about it has gone.

(3) So if pain is intrinsically bad, asymbolic pain is exactly as bad as ordinary pain of the same reported intensity.

(4) But it plainly isn't. These people aren't suffering. If a nurse asked whether they wanted something for it, the right answer is no, and handing them morphine wouldn't be treating anything.

(5) So pain isn't intrinsically bad. What makes a pain bad for you is the aversive bit — the minding, the wanting it to stop, the unpleasantness — which the lesion removed, leaving the bare sensation behind.

(6) And there's nothing special about pain here. If the badness of one kind of experience is constituted by the subject taking up an attitude toward it, then that's just the shape experiential badness has: nothing is bad for you except in virtue of your attitude toward it.

(7) Which means a perfect analgesic wouldn't have to touch the pain at all. A drug that left the agony entirely as it is — same intensity, same burning — and only removed the minding would be a complete treatment. And by the same token the horror of torture, of nausea, of grief, of a panic attack, is just the fact that the person minds it. Train them or drug them out of minding and nothing bad is happening to them any more.

(8) That's the bit I can't swallow. A drug that left the agony untouched and stopped you caring about it strikes me as a way of wrecking somebody, not of helping them. And I want to say there's something bad about a person being in agony even if they've somehow been made indifferent to it — that the indifference is part of the harm.

So one of those steps is wrong and I can't see which. Each of (1) through (5) sounds fine to me in isolation. If I have to pick, I'd most like to hang on to (1) and (4) together — pain's badness is built into the experience, and nothing bad is going on for the asymbolic patient — and I'm willing to bin (6) if that's what it takes. But I don't know what binning (6) actually buys me, or whether it just pushes the trouble back into (2).

Things I've tried and got stuck on:

- A friend says I'm sliding between "bad for you" and "bad, period." I don't think I am; I meant bad for the person throughout.
- Another friend says asymbolia doesn't really hold the experience fixed, so (3) never gets going. That sounds to me like just refusing to take the patients at their word, which I don't like.
- I also can't work out what to do with masochists, who report intense pain they enjoy. If (5) is right their pain isn't bad either, which feels wrong in a different direction. And then there's the mirror case I made up: someone who rates it 2 out of 10 and finds it completely unbearable. That pushes the opposite way from asymbolia, and I can't tell what the intensity ratings are even supposed to be evidence of any more.

Two things I want from you. First: name the single step where this goes wrong, explain why it looked innocent, and tell me what giving it up commits me to — in particular what it commits me to about the asymbolic patients' own reports, and about (1). Second: suppose we had a cheap, safe, short-acting drug that induced asymbolia and did nothing else, and a post-op patient in genuine agony asked for it instead of morphine. Should a doctor give it? Whatever diagnosis you offer, I want to see it produce an answer to that; if it doesn't, I'll assume you've only renamed the problem.
