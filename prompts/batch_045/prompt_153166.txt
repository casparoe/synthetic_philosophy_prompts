i've got a 90-minute workshop slot at a science-journalism conference in the spring, usually around 24 reporters in the room (six tables of four), and i want to spend it on hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy. What i definately don't want is to stand at the front and define anything — this crowd is skeptical, a good half of them already know both phrases, and they'll fold their arms the second they smell a lesson. So whatever the point is, it needs to happen to them before anybody names it.

Constraints: no money (points, chocolate, or a wall leaderboard are all fine), no pre-reading, and the raw material has to be stuff we generate in the room or stuff i can print in a folder ahead of time — i can't count on a real news event breaking during the session, and i don't want an exercise that depends on anyone being able to look something up.

The rough shape i have in my head, which you should feel free to take apart:

- something fast at the start where they commit to actual predictions with confidence numbers attached, about things we can genuinely resolve before lunch, so there's a live memory in the room of what being uncertain felt like
- then a case where they have to explain an outcome, with the twist that half the tables are quietly told the wrong outcome and only find out after they've already written the explanation
- then something about compression. My hunch is that the felt inevitability isn't mainly about memory at all, it's that writing a story short forces you to delete the forks in the road. So: same fact deck for everyone, seeded with two or three genuine near-misses, a tight word budget, and maybe vary the order different tables encounter the facts in. I want them to watch the near-misses get cut with their own hands and feel how much cleaner the story gets.

What i'd like back from you:

- rules, any roles, a materials list, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a scoring sheet i can photocopy
- the predictions i should be able to read off those sheets at the end, with actual numbers you'd expect: roughly how many people will misremember their own earlier confidence, how many near-misses survive a 120-word compression, how often the fabricated outcome draws an explanation just as confident as the real one. And tell me what counts as the thing having worked versus having flopped, so i'm not just declaring victory whatever happens.
- a debrief that maps specific moments in the room onto (a) hindsight bias as the felt symptom and (b) the narrative machinery that seems to manufacture it — i want those two kept seperate rather than blended into one blob
- how to handle the person who says at minute ten, "yeah, we know, this is hindsight bias," and thinks that means they're exempt
- one thing they can actually do differently on monday, since they're all reporters and will ask
- and be honest about what this sort of exercise can't show. I really don't want to walk out having produced a room of journalists who think causal explanation in history is just a literary effect. Where exactly does the analogy between my constructed case and real reporting break down, and what do i have to supply out loud because the activity can't?
