Hi — I'm writing a seminar paper on Spinoza and democracy for a third-year political theory module, and I've got into an argument with a friend who works on Hobbes. She says Spinoza's exclusion of women from political power in the last, unfinished political treatise is just 17th-century prejudice bolted onto the theory — an ad hoc bit you could shave off without cost to anything else he argues. I want to say it's in tension with his own principles, but every time I try to write it up I end up sneaking in modern premises about equal dignity or equal moral worth, which my supervisor will (rightly) call anachronistic. So what I actually need is a case that generates the pressure from inside the machine.

Could you build me one? Here's the spec. One scenario in a recognisably 17th-century Dutch republican commonwealth — nothing futuristic, no thought-experiment gadgets, ideally a paragraph or two of prose. The case has to make a Spinozist argument for widening the franchise to women fall out of Spinoza's own commitments: natural right coextensive with power, a sovereign's right limited by what it can actually enforce, laws that the multitude can't be brought to obey being self-defeating, nobody transferring their whole nature into the commonwealth. The hard constraint is that there has to be no moralised equality premise doing any work. The pressure must come from *power* in his sense — from the fact that the excluded group retains power the sovereign needs and hasn't got — not from fairness, dignity, rights-as-trumps, or anything a 1670s reader would first have to be argued into. If a premise wouldn't have been available to Spinoza, it doesn't go in. Then, because my friend's real objection is going to be that this at most shows the sovereign must make concessions to a powerful group and not that they get the vote, I want the case to isolate that: give me the same commonwealth twice, everything held fixed except one variable — in the first version the excluded women's labour and ongoing cooperation is dispensable to the enforcement of the laws, in the second it isn't. Same laws, same numbers, same exclusions; only how necessary their continuing cooperation is changes. Show why the Spinozist pressure to enfranchise shows up in the second and not the first, say explicitly which single feature of the case is doing the work and what happens if you remove it, and then give the strongest version of my friend's bullet-biting reply — the one where Spinoza just says: fine, buy them off with material concessions, co-opt their leaders, nothing here forces democracy. I'd rather you didn't dodge that bit.

A couple of housekeeping things: start with the scenario itself, no "here's a thought experiment" preface, and in the analysis put the verdict of each step in its first sentence rather than building up to it. Be clear about what's settled among people who actually work on Spinoza and what's genuinely contested — I get the sense that how strongly to read right = power, whether the two treatises are consistent with each other, and whether his remarks about women are meant as an empirical claim or a principled one are all live questions — and don't quietly present a contested reading as the consensus. Where you're going beyond what the texts settle, flag it as your own reading. British English please, and keep it tight enough that I can work it into a 2,000-word essay rather than a literature survey. Thanks!
