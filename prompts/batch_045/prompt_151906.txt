I'm a high school junior on my school's Model UN team, and at our last conference a committee deadlocked over a resolution about an ongoing mass atrocity: several delegates wanted to weigh the costs and risks of outside intervention against the lives at stake, and one delegate closed the argument by saying "Never again." It worked — nobody wanted to be the person arguing against the phrase — and the effect was to take cost-weighing off the table. I want to know what the phrase was actually asserting.

Could you lay out the distinct precise claims "Never again" might be making: who the "we" is supposed to be; what exactly must not recur (an event above some scale, a kind of group-targeted intent, a failure by bystanders, a failure of memory); whether it is a prediction, a promise, a command, or an expression of resolve; and what any of that obligates anyone to do — prevent, punish, intervene, record, teach. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and say which readings would really justify refusing to weigh costs and which leave that deliberation open.

Two complications I'd rather you engage with than smooth over. First, "genocide" has a strict legal use that turns on a demonstrated intent to destroy a protected group as such — hard to establish while the killing is still underway — and a looser moral use as a plain name for the mass slaughter of civilians. Tying any duty to act to the strict label may give states an incentive to contest the facts precisely because so much rides on the word, which would make the most consequential label the slowest to arrive. Call that the prevention paradox, and tell me whether it defeats the reading of the slogan that most depends on the label, or only the legal-institutional route to it. Second, the contrast class: what the slogan forbids depends on whether the forbidden thing is "no mass killing at all" or "no organised destruction of a group as such," and on whether it constrains what governments do to their own people, what bystanders fail to do, or both.

Please mark which of your verdicts you hold least confidently, and for each shaky one say what argument or kind of evidence would move you — I'd rather see where the analysis is fragile than get a tidy menu.
