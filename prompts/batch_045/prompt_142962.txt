I write literary fiction, and for each of my last three novels my publisher has sent any passage containing a slur to the same in-house language panel before copy-edit. I have kept their rulings, and read together they look less like a series of judgments of taste than like the application of a rule nobody has ever stated to me. I am about to submit a fourth manuscript and I would rather work out the rule now than negotiate it one sentence at a time. I don't need the scholarly literature on slurs; I need the rule, and I need to know what the panel would do with a passage their rule has not yet met.

The record, as I have it:

1. A boarding-school novel set in 1986, third person, protagonist a boy with cerebral palsy. His dorm-mates call him *spaz* in dialogue; in my draft nobody reacted. The panel blocked it and asked for a counter-signal in the text. I gave the boy a line of thought immediately afterwards, and it passed.
2. In the same novel, the omniscient narration named what the other boys saw when they looked at him, using the same word. The panel cut it with no discussion; as I understood their reasoning, the narrating voice is the book's voice, not the boys'.
3. Also in that novel, a doctor used *spastic* clinically, in dialogue, as part of a diagnosis. Passed without comment.
4. In a later novel, two characters with cerebral palsy used *spaz* to each other in dialogue, affectionately and reciprocally. Passed without comment — no counter-signal requested.
5. In the same novel, the non-disabled boyfriend of one of those characters used the same word to her in dialogue, affectionately; she laughed, and the scene treated her laughter as genuine. The panel cut it. Their note distinguished this from case 4 only by who was speaking.
6. An epigraph in that novel quoted a 1950s charity poster that contains the word. Passed, once I attributed the poster precisely. So mention and use are not the same thing to them.

I have tried to reconstruct the rule and I keep arriving at two that fit all six. One says the question is standing: a member of the targeted group may reclaim the word, a non-member's use survives only where the text marks it as the character's rather than the book's — that gets cases 1, 4 and 5, and treats case 2 as an unmarked non-member use. The other says the question is which textual level the word occupies: represented speech and represented thought are quotation and are permitted if the work doesn't adopt the contempt, while the narrating voice is never available for a slur — that gets case 2 directly and explains case 5 as quotation the work failed to distance itself from. A third possibility I can't eliminate is that what has really been driving them is the position of the reader: whether the word reaches someone who is not a member without a frame that licenses it. Case 5 seems to kill the crude version of that — uptake by the person addressed was not enough — but not the version on which it is the reader's position that matters.

The new manuscript is a first-person retrospective narrated by a woman of sixty-two who has mild spastic diplegia; much of it is addressed to a friend from her childhood clinic who died at nineteen and who had the same diagnosis. Three instances are at issue:

A. In the narrating voice, describing a photograph of the clinic's outing: *we were the spastics, that was the word they had for us and we took it and wore it.* The "we" is the two of them; the "you" it is addressed to is her dead friend. Affectionate, defiant, not a report of anyone's speech.
B. In dialogue, the friend, age eleven: *come on, spaz, keep up*. In-group, affectionate.
C. In dialogue, a stranger on a bus in 1979: *get out of the way, you stupid spastic*, answered by a shouted line of hers and nothing from anyone else in the scene.

There is also a transatlantic wrinkle. My American editor wants C changed to *spaz* for the US edition, on the ground that American readers hear *spastic* as clinical and *spaz* as "clumsy person," so the insult won't land otherwise. I think that flattens the history the book is about, but it also raises a question I can't answer from the record: whether the panel's rule is a rule about a word with a history, and what it would say if the same sentence had to be judged twice for two audiences who hear it differently.

So, please: predict the panel's ruling on each of A, B and C, and say which of them is the real fight. State the rule you take them to be running, state the alternative rules that fit all six precedents as well as it does and where each of those comes out on A, and give me your confidence on each verdict with the reason for it. Then tell me what I should do if I want to keep A: what is the smallest change that flips the verdict under the rule you think likeliest, and what is the price of that change in prose terms — does moving the word into dialogue, or into the friend's mouth, or into an epigraph, actually preserve what the sentence is doing, or does every route that satisfies the panel destroy the register the book is written in? Finally, name one further instance — a variation I could draft and submit, or a question I could put to them directly — that would force the panel to reveal which of the two or three rules they are running, and say what each rule predicts about it. I would rather provoke the disclosure deliberately than discover it in a revision letter.
