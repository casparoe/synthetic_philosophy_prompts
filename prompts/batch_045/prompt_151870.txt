I was discussing with some friends after a seminar on philosophy of mind, and we got stuck on Hofstadter's Careenium thought experiment. As I understand it, the Careenium is a sealed environment filled with tiny magnetised beads called simms; these bounce around and, under the right conditions, clump together into larger, semi-stable configurations called simmballs. The simmballs appear to collide, merge, and shove one another around, and Hofstadter uses this to ask whether the higher-level patterns—the symbols, as it were—push the substrate around, or whether, at bottom, only the simms ever push simms. The question has stayed with me because it seems to show up in ordinary life, not just in imagined domes.

Consider first the widespread practice, both in everyday life and in the law, of explaining, predicting, and holding people responsible for their actions by citing their reasons—their beliefs, desires, and intentions—rather than by describing their neurons, muscles, or any other microphysical goings-on. In a courtroom, for instance, we say that the defendant took the money because he wanted it and believed he would not be caught; we do not say that he took it because of a pattern of neural firing, even though the same court may also accept that every action is realised by such goings-on. There is a puzzle here: if the person is, at bottom, a careenium—a vast collection of simms—why do we treat the simmballs, the reason-level patterns, as the things that do the shoving? Does this practice strictly presuppose that symbols, or high-level semantic states, can exert downward causal power over the physical substrate? Or can a non-reductive physicalist argue that citing reasons is merely an indispensable shorthand that fits the practice perfectly well without requiring the simmballs to push the simms? And can a thoroughgoing reductionist equally license the practice, or does the practice quietly rule out the view that only the microphysical level is causally efficacious?

The same puzzle, it seems to me, is built into a design choice in a piece of technology I recently read about: engineers take a high-level symbolic specification of how a system ought to behave—a short list of English principles, for example—and use it, via feedback, to adjust the system's low-level numerical parameters; they then describe the resulting system as having been shaped by those high-level principles, even though every adjustment is just one set of numbers altering another. A friend has started calling the assumption behind this way of talking simmball primitivism: the idea that, in any careenium-like system, the coarse-grained symbolic patterns are the primary causal agents and the substrate is merely their vehicle. What, if anything, does this engineering practice presuppose about the causal power of symbols over the substrate? Is the high-level specification strictly presupposed to have causal power, or is the language merely a useful façon de parler that hides a purely substrate-level causal story? Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, so that reductionists, non-reductive physicalists, and something like simmball primitivism could all license it? Finally, if we replaced the implicit theory with a strict microphysical causal story—only simms push simms—what would have to change in how we explain human action, assign responsibility, and build or evaluate such systems?
