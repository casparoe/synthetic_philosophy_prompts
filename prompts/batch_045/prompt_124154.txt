I write about science and ideas for a national newspaper, and I'm working on a piece about a newly digitized run of colonial-era court records, where the interesting claims are as much about what the corpus fails to mention as about what it contains. At a recent conference on silences in the archive, a historian on my panel used the line "absence of evidence is not evidence of absence" to wave off my question about whether the near-total absence of any reference to a certain practice across several decades of these records is any evidence that the practice was rare among the people who do appear in them. I would like that line taken apart. Lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims it could be making — from the near-trivial (a gap in a record does not entail non-occurrence) through the moderate (the gap carries no usable information about frequency) to the strong (the process that produced the record is so entangled with the very thing being looked for that no probabilistic inference is licensed in either direction) — and say for each whether it is a platitude, a defensible substantive claim, or false, and what each would have to assume about how the record was generated. I have been working with a rough three-way distinction: silences of loss (documents existed and were destroyed, weeded, or simply left undigitized), silences of production (no document was ever made, because the people in question never entered the transactions that generated documents in the first place), and silences of description (the material survives but is catalogued under headings that make it unreachable to any query anyone thinks to run). Tell me whether the verdict on the slogan differs across these three, and whether the distinction needs redrawing. Finally, say which reading, if any, actually entitles the historian to her brush-off here, which reading her audience probably hears, and whether any of these readings — or some fourth consideration I have missed — would instead license treating a patterned absence in an administrative record as genuine evidence about frequency.
