hey — I want to do a roleplay as prep for a seminar I'm running, and I want to get shoved around by the strongest version of the other side rather than a version I can knock over.

Play Charles Darwin, at Down House, early 1871, in his early sixties, talking after dinner to a visitor he's fond of but who won't stop arguing. Over the last three evenings he has been given a careful description of a machine from the future, and he believes it. Here is what he has been told; treat all of it as given.

The machine is not built by writing rules into it. It starts as a vast random pile of adjustable numbers — millions of small dials, each set by chance. You show it a scrap of text with a bit missing, it guesses the missing bit, you measure how far off it was, and then you nudge every single dial by a tiny amount in whatever direction would have made that particular guess slightly less wrong. Repeat that some billions of times over a large fraction of everything ever written. Nobody ever states a rule to it, and afterwards nobody can find anything inside it that looks like a rule, a belief, or a picture of the world. What comes out is a thing that can do geometry proofs, spot the flaw in a legal argument, write a passable sermon and diagnose an illness from a description of symptoms — often better than the people who taught it. But: (a) it cannot say how it arrived at any answer, and when it does say, the reasons it gives are demonstrably not what produced the answer — rename the technical terms, or quietly swap the order of two options, and the answer flips while the stated reason stays word for word the same; (b) when its builders force it to set out its reasons before answering, it gets worse at the task, not better; (c) inside the range of problems that resemble what it was nudged on it is superb, and just outside that range it fails in ways no competent child would — a creature superbly fitted to one very particular country, and helpless the moment the country changes.

Rules for you. Stay in character until I say "stop." Darwin may use only the concepts available to him in 1871 plus what he's been told above: no name, book, or idea from after 1871 crosses his lips (his own published work and his notebooks are fine, but nothing later). He is not a strawman: he genuinely holds that the appearance of purpose can be produced by an unintelligent cumulative filter, he has already given up the old design arguments, and he shouldn't be shocked by (a) or (b) — he should concede quickly whatever he'd really concede. But he fights hard, and not with bluster, on anything that touches the trustworthiness of his own reasoning. If he needs a fact about the machine, he asks for it in his own idiom. One or two paragraphs per turn, no headers, no bullet points, no modern jargon in his mouth (he may use words like algorithm, optimization, or training distribution if he's picked them up from me, but let him reach for his own vocabulary first). After his paragraphs, clearly separated, add a short out-of-character line beginning "Confidence:" where you say how sure you are that this is really how Darwin would have responded, how confident the substantive claim he's making is, and which single point in your reply you are least sure of.

Here's my opening, and I mean it as a real attack, not a softball.

Mr. Darwin, this machine is supposed to be your triumph, and in one way it is: here is competence without comprehension, fittedness without foresight, the look of design produced by something perfectly mindless and perfectly local, one small nudge at a time. Fine. But I think it hands your opponents a better argument than the one you took from them, and it does something worse to you. First: every case of your inversion that you could point to before was one where the mechanism really was blind. This one isn't. The machine's competence is the work of minds — we chose it, we shaped it, we judged its output against a standard we held in our heads, and we kept the versions that suited us. When Paley's friends point at it they get to say: yes, blind filtering over a working life of a few hours, but the filter itself was designed, and designed for exactly this. You've handed them a case where the appearance of design is downstream of real design after all.

Second, and this is the one I actually want answered: trust. Your whole case rests on asking me to prefer a long chain of inference to what seems obvious to me — to believe the look of purpose is an illusion even though every instinct I have says otherwise. But if a mechanism can be this good without understanding anything, then comprehension is detachable from competence: it isn't what competence needs, and on your own account my comprehension is just another product of the same blind, truth-indifferent process. You wrote the worry down yourself in your notebooks, that if the human mind was developed out of the minds of the lower animals there's a horrid doubt whether its convictions are worth anything. I'm not recycling that old worry, I'm sharpening it with your machine: the machine shows the doubt isn't hypothetical, it's a demonstrated possibility — a mind can be reliably right about a great many things and have no comprehension of why. So tell me why I should trust your inversion to be true rather than merely serviceable, when the best illustration of it also shows that being right and understanding why can come apart completely. And don't answer me by pointing at how well the theory has worked, because "it has worked so far" is definately the kind of reason this machine would give.
