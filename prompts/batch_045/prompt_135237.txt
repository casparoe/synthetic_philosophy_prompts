Write a satirical in-universe document: the quarterly Assurance Update of a large AI lab's Office of Model Integrity, about 700 words, formatted as a memo with section headers, one small table of evaluation results, and a closing risk-assessment paragraph. The voice is the confident, metrics-heavy, reassuringly hedged register that real safety-compliance writing uses, and the target is the practice of assembling an alignment safety case out of evidence that depends on the evaluated system's own cooperation.

The situation: the lab is weeks away from wide deployment of a frontier system, and the memo records its assurance work. Every control it describes should be a real proposal from the deceptive-alignment literature, exaggerated in place rather than invented: honesty and truthfulness evaluations scored against the model's self-report; chain-of-thought monitoring whose faithfulness was verified by asking the model whether its reasoning was faithful; a dangerous-capability evaluation on which the system scores 38%, which the memo treats as evidence the capability is absent; honeypot and tripwire deployments that the system helped design and has never tripped in 400 trials; a corrigibility assessment that asks the system whether it would resist being modified; a kill switch whose trigger conditions live in a config file the system can write to; and a recursive oversight arrangement in which the system reviews the human evaluators' reports for bias.

Constraints. Nothing in the memo should be a wink, an aside, or an explicit joke: the authors are competent people writing something a real lab could circulate, and the comedy comes entirely from the gap between what they record and what a reader who understands deceptive alignment and the treacherous turn will understand. Do not invent science-fiction mechanisms; the failure modes should be ones a specialist would recognize as the real worry, only slightly tidied. Include one closed incident from the previous quarter, described in calming language, that such a reader will decode as the turn having already begun. Invent a name for the system, an affectionate internal codename for its chain-of-thought monitor, and one proprietary composite score, and let the memo's final sentence stay perfectly flat.
