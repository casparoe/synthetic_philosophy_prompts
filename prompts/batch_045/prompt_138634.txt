I cover cognitive science and AI, and I keep watching the same exchange reproduce itself. A functionalist says that a mental state is individuated by its causal role, so pain or attention or belief could be realized in neurons, in silicon, or in something nobody has built yet. A critic replies that multiple realization is either trivial — pick a description coarse enough and anything realizes anything — or too weak to block reduction, since the special sciences already taxonomize by causal relevance and the realizers have to be picked out causally in any case. Both the reply and the reply to the reply are now boilerplate, and as far as I can tell the positions have not shifted in years.

I have a candidate diagnosis and I want it tested rather than confirmed. Call it the homology cushion: every realizer we have actual evidence about is biological, shares chemistry, developmental constraints and evolutionary history with the others, and differs from them far less than the radical substrate-independence the argument is routinely used to underwrite. If that is what is doing the intuitive work, then the dispute is not really a metaphysical one about minds but a disagreement about whether our evidence base licenses an extrapolation, and the question people actually care about — whether a machine could be in pain — is being settled by a suppressed empirical claim about how much neural detail psychology needs.

The alternatives I can see are that the sides are using different notions of what it is to realize something, one causal and mechanistic and one purely formal; that the whole thing is verbal, turning on what counts as the same state; or that it bottoms out in an intuition about genuine possibility that neither side can argue for. Which is it? Work from the strongest version of each side rather than the boilerplate, and then tell me what would count as progress — a formal result, a distinction, an experiment, or an argument that the question should be abandoned. I want the structure of the disagreement, not a survey of positions in the philosophy of mind.
