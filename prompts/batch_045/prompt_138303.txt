A popular-science podcast argues for the block universe by way of an analogy: now is supposed to be an indexical exactly like here. Chicago does not cease to exist when you are in Paris, and the fact that you occupy one place does not make that place metaphysically privileged; likewise every event, past and future, simply exists, and now merely marks the one you are at. The host adds that this is the same conclusion the Rietdijk–Putnam argument reaches from the relativity of simultaneity, and draws a practical moral: because the future is already there, deliberation is a kind of theatre. My philosophy tutor deploys the identical analogy for the opposite moral: your deliberation is inside the block, and the existence of future events no more makes your choosing idle than the existence of Chicago makes your travel plans pointless.

Audit the analogy. Give the table of correspondences — what plays the role of places, of here, of travel, of distance, of the map — and then the disanalogies, keeping those that matter for the intended use separate from those that do not. I especially want two of them assessed. First: co-location is absolute while simultaneity is frame-relative, and that disanalogy is precisely what Rietdijk–Putnam exploits — so is the analogy doing any argumentative work of its own, or is it rhetoric borrowed from an intuition about space that does not transfer to time? Second: the analogy seems to have no way of representing the rival views — a moving spotlight, or a privileged cosmic present — which are the live alternatives to the block picture; is that a defect, and if so how serious? Then a variant I have not seen discussed: imagine a world in which relativity holds for every possible experiment but there is in fact an undetectable privileged foliation, a cosmic present. Would now-is-like-here be any help in deciding whether that foliation is metaphysically privileged, or does the analogy fall silent exactly where the disagreement lives? Close by saying whether either the fatalist or the compatibilist moral actually follows from the analogy, and offer a better analogy if one is available.
