I'm writing the final essay for a philosophy of language seminar on the semantics and pragmatics of pejoratives, and I've settled on a thesis that I can't quite make stick on my own. I'd be grateful if you would write the argument for it: not a survey of the literature, but one sustained case of about 700 words, followed by a structured summary.

The thesis is this. What makes an utterance of a slur derogatory is not any component of what the speaker says or expresses — not a derogatory truth-conditional constituent, and not an expressive or use-conditional attitude — but the fact that the speaker has performed an act that is conventionally prohibited in the linguistic community in which the utterance is made. What makes that utterance offensive, by contrast, is the audience's warranted uptake of the act as a flouting of the prohibition. Derogatoriness is thus a property of the act relative to a norm; offensiveness is a property of the act relative to an audience. On this view, a good deal of the stand-off between the three standard families of account — content-based views that locate derogatory content in what is said, expressivist or use-conditional views that locate it in what is expressed, and prohibition views — is really a dispute about which of these two things is being explained, and the familiar embedding data (negation, antecedents of conditionals, attitude reports) have been read as evidence for a semantic conclusion when they only ever bore on one or the other.

Please build the argument around the three cases below, which I invented so that the usual examples from the seminar can't do the work.

1. Homophonous transplant. A word-form w is, in language L1, a prohibited slur targeting group G. In an unrelated language L2, a phonologically identical form is the ordinary, unmarked, administratively standard term for G, carrying no history of oppression of G by L2 speakers. A heritage speaker, raised with that form as the neutral L2 term, uses it inside an otherwise L1 sentence, addressing fluent L1 speakers, meaning the neutral L2 term, with no derogatory attitude and no knowledge of the L1 prohibition.

2. Survival without a target. In a community in which the slur's original descriptive application condition is satisfied by no one and is not believed by anyone to be satisfied by anyone (a slur for witches, say, in a thoroughly secular community), the word survives and is used as a bare expletive after stubbing one's toe, and as a vocative addressed to a friend. Nothing is predicated of anyone, and no attitude is expressed toward the original target class.

3. Audience-free inscription. A speaker types the slur into a private diary entry, or into a search box, with no audience and no possibility of uptake.
