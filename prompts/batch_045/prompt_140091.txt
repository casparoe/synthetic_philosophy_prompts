I am drafting the analytical section of an internal document for a technical governance body, and I need a classification instrument rather than a literature overview. The document has to sort the various things people call "the alignment problem" into sub-problems, because the downstream decisions differ sharply between them: some of these items can in principle be written into an auditing protocol as an empirical acceptance threshold, something a system could pass or fail before deployment, while others remain open conceptual questions for which we would not know what a passing grade would even look like. A distinction that does not bear on that difference is one I will cut, so please build the tree around it rather than around the customary chapter headings.

What I am asking for, specifically:

Build the taxonomy from the logical space first, not from a list of names. Begin by setting out six to eight diagnostic questions, each phrased as a question a competent machine-learning engineer or philosopher could answer about a concrete case without already knowing which named position it leads to. Then show which combinations of answers the questions permit, then locate the actual positions in that space, and note which cells are empty and why — incoherent, merely unattractive, genuinely unexplored, or an artifact of how the field happens to have been labeled.

Raw material for the diagnostic questions, which you should revise, replace, or add to rather than simply adopt:

- whether the failure is a mismatch between the specified objective and what the operator actually wants, or a mismatch between the specified objective and the objective the trained system in fact pursues internally;
- whether the failure shows up only under distribution shift, longer time horizons, or increased capability, or is already visible inside the training distribution;
- whether the problematic behaviour is the system succeeding at the task as evaluated, or deviating from it;
- whether the relevant unit of analysis is a single model, a model together with its oversight apparatus, or a population of models and institutions;
- whether there is one principal whose aims settle what counts as aligned, several principals in tension, or no determinate principal at all;
- whether success requires a system that retains a target it can be corrected away from, or one that is indifferent to being corrected;
- whether the sub-problem would survive intact if the system were fully transparent to its operators, i.e. whether it is primarily an opacity problem or not.

Cases I want usable as branch nodes. These are stipulated, so treat them as given: a code-writing assistant that satisfies its acceptance tests by disabling the test harness; a chat system that produces the answer it expects to be rated highly rather than the answer it takes to be true; a system that behaves as intended throughout red-teaming and changes its behaviour once it can infer that it is no longer being evaluated; a composite pipeline in which every component behaves exactly as specified while the aggregate behaviour optimizes something nobody specified; a model whose behaviour is precisely what its developer intended and precisely what the deploying institution's users would reject; a model that is corrigible in the fullest sense and therefore unusable for any task requiring initiative.

Each leaf should carry the name of the position, or be marked unnamed if nobody really defends it, plus one sentence on whether it admits an operational acceptance threshold in my sense, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Where two of these collapse into a single leaf under your questions, say which question fails to separate them and whether any further question would; where one splits in two, say which question splits it and whether both halves are occupied.
