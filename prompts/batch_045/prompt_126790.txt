Our daughter is three and was born with pretty significant hearing loss, and we're at the point where we actually have to decide about cochlear implants — the audiologist is pushing a timeline because of the language-development window, so this stopped being a seminar topic for me about a month ago. I've been reading disability-rights writing seriously for the first time (Deaf culture, the social model, people arguing that a cochlear implant is a fix for a problem that is mostly ours and not hers), and between that, my own broadly left politics, and the fact that I teach at a community college where I watch students split their roommate's Adderall before finals, I've ended up with a pile of views I'd like someone to shake before I act on any of them. Roughly:

1. There's no morally interesting line between therapy and enhancement. Repairing, maintaining, upgrading — its all just pointing tools at bodies, and the distinction does no real moral work. I had elective laser eye surgery in my twenties and came out better than 20/20, and whatever that is, I can't see how it's a different kind of thing from getting my vision back to normal.
2. "Normal" is a bogus baseline anyway, and most of what we call disability is a mismatch between bodies and a world built for other bodies. So I bristle at restoring normal functioning as the goal of medicine.
3. When the choice is change the kid versus change the environment — signing, captions, a different classroom setup — I want to say: change the environment first, and only reach for the body when that genuinely isn't enough.
4. Parents should get wide latitude here. It's their call, not the state's and not the clinician's, and they shouldn't have to defend it in terms of anyone else's idea of a good childhood.
5. The natural lottery is morally arbitrary. Nobody deserves to be born with better ears or a better working memory, and a just society should be doing something to even that out rather than shrugging — which is also why it bugs me that all of this will be available to rich kids first.
6. I don't buy the authenticity objection. The idea that an achievement you needed meds for isn't really yours strikes me as the same thing people said about glasses and antidepressants, just dressed up.
7. But I do think it's genuinely cheating when a student with no diagnosis takes Adderall before a final, and I don't think my objection is just that theres a rule against it.

And being honest about one more thing: we're leaning toward the implant, and I also have a vague feeling that I'd be uneasy about giving the same kid something that pushed her hearing past the normal range twenty years from now — which, given (1), I can't explain.

So please audit the pile rather than lecture me on it. Which of these actually pull against each other, and which only look like they do? Rank whatever you find by how severe it is, and for each one say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me — I don't want everything flagged as equally bad, and I don't want an everything-is-fine answer either. Then tell me what any of it means for the decision we have to make in the next few weeks, and don't let me off easy there. Last thing, which is the part I'll actually use: say where your own answer is most likely to be wrong — which consideration would flip your verdict on the worst tension, or which step in your reasoning you trust least.
