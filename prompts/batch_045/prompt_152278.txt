Next month I am to give the dedicatory address at the opening of a permanent exhibition marking the 175th anniversary of Halloran College, a small liberal-arts college in the American Midwest where I have taught history for twenty-two years. The exhibition occupies the ground floor of the library and is a chronological walk-through, currently titled Becoming Ourselves, designed largely by the development office in collaboration with an outside designer whose previous work has been corporate heritage projects. It ends with a wall of screens showing present-day students and a panel whose text is something like the founders' dream, finally kept. I have twelve to fifteen minutes. Please write the address.

Four features of the situation make this harder than an ordinary celebratory speech.

First, the room. Roughly a third of the audience are alumni and donors, many of them elderly, who have given money partly because they believe the college has a noble and continuous story and who would be wounded by anything that sounds like an exposé delivered at their own party. Another third are current students who are impatient with institutional self-congratulation and who will be filming it. The rest are my colleagues in the history department together with six or seven museum and public historians from neighbouring institutions, several of whom have written against exactly this genre of institutional self-commemoration and who will hear any Whiggism — any slide from and then to therefore, any past rendered as a preparation for us — as a professional failure on my part. The speech has to be honest enough for the historians and warm enough for the donors, without ever announcing that it is steering between them.

Second, the founding. Halloran was founded in 1850 by a coalition of local ministers and merchants to train schoolteachers. The founding charter's stated rationale is explicit that advanced schooling unfitted women for the domestic and religious duties to which Providence had called them, and that a seminary producing pious, marriageable teachers was the safe compromise. The college is now coeducational, and its current mission statement speaks of intellectual independence and civic leadership. So the institution's present self-description is not the fulfilment of the founding aim; in important respects it is a repudiation of it. I therefore cannot say that the founders would be proud of what we have become, and I cannot present today's college as what the founders were really reaching for beneath the limited language of their century. But I also cannot simply unmask the founding as hypocrisy, because the college I am praising really does stand in a causal line that runs back through it, and because the unmasking gesture has its own bad rhetoric, which the historians in the room will recognise instantly.

Third, the change the exhibition most wants to celebrate. In 1949 the trustees voted to admit men. The exhibition presents this under a heading about opening our doors, and the wall text implies a widening of principle. In fact the vote followed an actuarial report showing that the college could not survive financially as a women's college; the president who pushed it through argued almost entirely from enrolment projections and wrote privately that coeducation would lower academic standards; the alumnae association opposed it bitterly; and the people who most wanted it were regional accreditors and two local employers. Yet the outcome is one that almost everyone now in the room regards as a genuine improvement, and regards as expressing a commitment the college would now defend on principle. So the speech has to honour a change whose actual causal history is narrower, more accidental, and more self-interested than the value we now assign to it — without either laundering that history into foresight or sneering at it as mere interest.

Fourth, there is a donor plaque near the entrance crediting Halloran with having always stood on the right side of history, and the college president has already used that phrase twice in publicity for the anniversary. I would like the speech to avoid the idiom entirely — the idea of history as a tribunal with a known direction, which vindicates some people and not others, is precisely the habit I am trying not to perform — while still giving the donors in the room something they can hear as praise of the college rather than as a correction administered to them.

What I want the speech to do, philosophically, is the following. It must narrate a real causal ancestry without converting that ancestry into a credential or a mandate: the fact that we are descended from these people cannot be allowed to do justificatory work. It must avoid describing past actors as having anticipated commitments they did not hold, and it must equally avoid the mirror-image error of treating them as merely benighted. It must not write their decisions as though the outcome we now know was available to them when they chose. It must be careful about the institutional we: a dedicatory address naturally says things like we have always believed or we decided in 1949, and I want you to think hard about what that we is allowed to mean when the institution's aims have changed and when many of the people who made the relevant decisions would not have recognised the institution that now claims them. And it should find some way of saying something true about why this exhibition is worth having at all, given that its framing is in several respects wrong — including, if you can manage it, something about why a flawed commemoration might still be worth walking through, rather than merely worth fixing.

Practical constraints: aim for 1,400 to 1,700 words; no footnotes, no citations, no named historians or schools of thought, and no vocabulary that would not survive being read aloud to a room of non-specialists; it must remain a dedicatory address, not a lecture on historiography, and it must end by sending people into the gallery rather than by qualifying the exhibition out of existence.
