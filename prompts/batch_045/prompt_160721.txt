You are a professional philosopher with dual expertise: David Hume's account of impressions, ideas, and the self, and the contemporary debate over whether qualia are a coherent posit at all. Think the problem through carefully before you write; begin with a page of rough, private brainstorming on which Humean commitments would be load-bearing here, then discard it. I want only the polished residue, and I want you to commit to a reading rather than hedge.

I write literary fiction, and a scene in my current novel — set in Edinburgh in the winter of 1775 — has stalled. My character, a young surgeon, has been dosing a patient's eyes with a preparation that very slowly rotates the patient's entire colour solid. After six months every colour the patient sees is a different colour from the one that used to appear there, yet every similarity and difference among the colours is exactly as it was, and because the change is gradual he notices nothing and never contradicts his own earlier colour judgments. The surgeon wants to insist that something has changed nonetheless, and changed in a way that could never in principle be found out. A second character insists there is no fact of the matter.

The scene only works if the argument is one Hume could have made in his own vocabulary, before anyone had the words "qualia" or "phenomenal concept" — and, more pointedly, if it answers the contemporary argument that qualia are a posit we cannot even formulate, the argument being that the concepts we would need in order to state a hypothesis of undetectable change drift along with the states they are supposed to pick out, so that nothing determinate is left to assert or deny. So: how would Hume have received that argument?

Answer in exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion; argue from Hume's own commitments rather than from secondary literature, and cite nothing, since I cannot follow references up:

1. Would he have taken this incoherence argument to vindicate his own account of mind, or to threaten it?
2. Which single Humean commitment does his verdict turn on?
3. Which premise of the argument would he most likely refuse to grant?
4. Does his own famous concession about a man who can form an idea of a shade of blue he has never seen push him toward the argument or away from it, and why?
5. Does my rotation case — nothing identical, all relations preserved — help the argument or hurt it, relative to an ordinary overnight red-green swap? Include, as the last line of this point, the one sentence my surgeon could say in 1775 that carries the verdict without anachronism.
