I host a philosophy podcast, and in two weeks my guest is Dr. Miriam Halloran, author of a recent book defending what she calls the two-stage answer to Molyneux's problem. Her claim, in brief: the newly sighted subject would fail at first and succeed within days, and — this is the controversial part — those two verdicts have entirely different explanations, so the centuries of "yes" and "no" answers were each half right about a different moment. The early failure, she argues, is not about the format of spatial representation at all but about the visual system's initial inability to segment an object from its background: the patient is not misperceiving shapes, he is not yet perceiving objects. The later success is then explained by rapid cross-modal calibration on top of a shared, amodal spatial format that touch and vision both feed. Her slogan is that Molyneux put two questions in one mouth, and that once you separate them the empirical record from cataract-reversal and late-blindness work looks like confirmation rather than a mess.

Draft nine to eleven interview questions that force her past restating this architecture. Requirements:

- Press on the first stage: if "failure of segmentation, not of format" is to be more than a label for the failure it explains, what independent evidence should we expect — early performance on figure-ground and border-ownership tasks, patients' spontaneous reports, performance on isolated high-contrast objects versus cluttered scenes — and what should we expect if the explanation is wrong?
- Press on the second stage from the opposite direction: the amodal-format claim looks insulated, since every success is counted as evidence for shared content and every failure as noise, degradation, or task difficulty. Build a question that makes her say what pattern of results would disconfirm it, and give the follow-up for her likeliest reply (that success after a short delay is itself the signature of a shared format).
- Include one question on task type: forced-choice cross-modal matching ("pick the one you just handled") versus open recognition or naming by sight alone, which is closer to how the original question was posed. Ask which task carries the philosophical weight, whether her view predicts a dissociation between them, and what she would say if patients pass matching for months while failing open naming.
- Include one question on which objects: cubes and spheres versus irregular three-dimensional shapes, objects identified by affordance rather than geometry, and properties that touch delivers but sight does not (weight, compliance, thermal texture). Have her commit to a predicted profile of success and failure, and ask why that profile rather than its reverse would support her view.
- Include the learning-versus-calibration fork: is post-surgical success the acquisition of a mapping between two modality-specific formats, or the unmasking of one format that was always shared? Have her name what would distinguish these — rate of improvement, transfer to shapes never felt before, savings after a delay — and then ask her to say honestly which side of the fork the current data favor.
- Include an extension she may not have addressed: a congenitally blind adult who has used a visual-to-auditory or visual-to-tactile sensory substitution device for years and then has sight restored. Ask for her prediction, then add the harder variant — a device whose encoding of shape is deliberately non-spatial, closer to a chord or a phoneme pattern than to a picture — and ask what remains of the amodal claim if long-term users of that device succeed anyway.
- Include one question on the historical stake: the original question was asked in the service of a claim about whether the idea of a figure is the same idea when it arrives through different senses, and the classic negative answer denied it. Ask whether the two-stage answer settles that question or quietly replaces it with a question about the timing of performance; anticipate her reply that the old question just is the empirical question properly posed, and write the follow-up that that reply has to survive.

She is skilled at deflecting with the phrase "that's a task artifact, not a finding about content." Order three of the questions so that this escape becomes progressively harder to use, ending with one that forces her to specify in advance which result she would refuse to explain away.

Format: for each question give the question itself in one to three sentences, a parenthetical gloss of any technical term so a listener who has not read the book can follow, and one follow-up of a single sentence keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. Close with a final question about the single finding that would make her give up the amodal claim, and note which question you intend as the short clip for the episode feed.
