Had a three-hour argument with my roommate last night about whether unelected judges should be able to toss out laws that a real legislature actually passed, and it went nowhere because we kept swapping hypotheticals. He's basicly a judicial supremacist because of the Warren Court, I kept saying the New Deal shows a Court using rights talk to protect property holders against an enormous majority, and we left it there. I want to redo it on one real episode instead.

The episode: FDR's fight with the Supreme Court in 1937. The Court had knocked out core New Deal statutes (the NRA codes, the AAA), and then in early 1937 FDR proposed adding one new justice for every sitting justice over seventy, up to six — a court-packing play dressed up as docket relief. A few weeks later the Court upheld a Washington state minimum wage law in West Coast Hotel v. Parrish, 5–4, with Justice Roberts switching sides. The bill died in the Senate that summer: the committee chair slow-walked it for months, its main sponsor in the Senate died, and the Judiciary Committee's report tore into it as a needless and dangerous abandonment of constitutional principle. One of the justices retired anyway, so FDR got his first pick regardless. I've also read that the switch-in-time story is probably wrong — that Roberts had voted in conference before the plan was announced — so please don't hang anything on the timing.

The theory I want it read through is the core-case argument against judicial review, the Waldron-style one: review is hardest to defend when (1) the legislature is genuinely democratic with real suffrage and working process, (2) the courts are in good order and reasonably independent, (3) people in the society broadly accept rights, and (4) they still disagree in good faith about what rights require. On those conditions judges have no special epistemic edge on rights questions, and letting them overrule the legislature treats the losers as less than equals.

What I actually want:

Score the episode against those four conditions and be honest about the ones that fail. FDR had just won 46 of 48 states and around 60 percent of the vote, about as clear a mandate as this system ever produces, but Black voters in the South were almost entirely disenfranchised, so condition 1 is definately not cleanly met. Does that mean the argument goes silent here, or does it still have something to say about a case like this?

Then discipline the mapping: who the players are, what their real options were (defer, strike down, pack the bench, amend, lose an election and try again), and what would count as evidence for or against a rights claim in this setting. Then say what each of them should have done by the lights of the theory.

Then the part I care most about, which is where we got stuck: what the episode does to the theory. Run two ledgers, one for who has the votes and one for who has the veto. Judicial review is normally sold as protecting minorities against majorities, but in 1937 the striking Court was the minority veto — employers, property owners, a bloc of five — and a deferring Court would have been the majority's instrument. So is the standard justification a description of what courts do, or a hope about which way they'll happen to lean? And take on the 1938 repair: the famous footnote saying courts should stop second-guessing economic regulation but get strict about groups shut out of the ordinary political process and about laws that close off future political change. Principled fix, or an ad hoc retreat that saved the institution by switching topics?

Two more things. The ratchet: once a court learns deference in 1937, does it have any way to be undeferential in 1942 or 1954, or is doctrinal humility sticky? And what I'd call mandate laundering — a 60 percent landslide is a mandate for a person and a platform, not for the constitutionality of each statute — so how much work can the 1936 election really do in the pro-deference argument? Also tell me what the theory simply cannot explain about how the whole thing ended.

Say how confident you are in the load-bearing claims and single out the one you're least sure of. I'd rather get "this framework has nothing to say about X" than a forced mapping, even if it ocasionally leaves me with less than I asked for.
