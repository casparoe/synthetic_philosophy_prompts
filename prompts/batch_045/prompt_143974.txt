I sit on a small advisory group that serves two very different clients — a constitutional-drafting commission currently rewriting its amendment rules and debating whether to entrench certain provisions against ordinary majorities, and a hospital ethics committee that is repeatedly asked whether to honour a psychiatric advance directive written during remission — and the group keeps talking past itself, because each member is working from a different picture of what makes an earlier self's constraint on a later self genuinely binding. Rather than sample more of the literature, I want the specification first. Please write the desiderata: what any adequate account of precommitment validity has to deliver, in a form that could actually be used to advise a drafter or a clinician on whether to honour a given constraint and how tightly to bind it. For each requirement give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are merely preferences of one camp (a horror of any dead hand at all, say, or the demand that a valid precommitment must be re-authorizable at will).

The stress set I want the requirements keyed to, at minimum: the plain personal case (the Ulysses contract, addiction, advance directives); the institutional case (entrenchment, unamendable clauses, delegated independence for courts or central banks); the case of a wholly renewed body that inherits the commitment after every member has turned over, with no one left who made or consented to it; the case in which the earlier self precommits not to a fixed outcome but to a procedure for periodic re-authorization — a rolling Ulysses contract — and that procedure's gatekeepers themselves drift or are captured; the case of literal compliance that defeats the point of the constraint; and the case of the guardian, since any enforcement mechanism is itself an agent that can defect, so the account owes something about what binds the enforcer rather than pushing the problem up one level forever.

Three things matter most to me. First, the account must be able to distinguish a motivated repeal from a justified one — the case where the later self or later majority wants out because the constraint is working exactly as designed, from the case where circumstances have genuinely changed — and I suspect this desideratum is in tension with the requirement that the test not defer to the judgement of the very agent whose judgement is in question; call it the diagnostic circularity, and tell me whether it is escapable or only manageable. Second, I want a desideratum about calibration: that the barrier to reversal track the expected cost of each kind of error rather than the intensity of the pressure to reverse, which is the only signal actually observable. If that pair cannot be jointly satisfied, or if any other subset of your list is jointly unsatisfiable, say so plainly — that result is the part I would take away. Third, tell me which single requirement excludes the largest share of candidate theories, and which requirement, if dropped, would make the whole exercise collapse into something trivially satisfiable. A short scorecard at the end is fine: three families only, described rather than attributed — the view that ties bindingness to the earlier self's authorization, the view that ties it to whether the constraint still serves the values that justified it, and the view that treats it as a matter of aggregating the interests of earlier and later selves across time — each graded against the list in a sentence or two, with no bibliography and no proper names I would have to look up.
