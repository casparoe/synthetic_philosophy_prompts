I am a journalist who has covered science and ideas for a national newspaper for the past fifteen years, and for much of that time I have followed the Larkspur Foundation, a nonprofit that preserves the brains of people who have been declared legally dead, in the hope that a later technology will read their contents and run them. Next month the foundation holds a dinner in Boston marking its tenth year, it has asked me to give the after-dinner address, and I have agreed.

The room will be about a hundred and eighty people, split almost exactly in half. One half are the neuroscientists, cryobiologists and engineers who do the foundation's work, and they are divided among themselves: some of them think a sufficiently faithful emulation would be the person herself, others think it would be at best an extraordinarily close descendant. The other half are the families and friends of the forty-odd people now in preservation, together with the donors whose money keeps the freezers running and a handful of other reporters. Marta Ellison, a retired marine biologist I profiled for the paper a decade ago and spent a week with in the field, was among the first to be preserved; her daughter will introduce me and will be sitting three tables from the podium.

What I need is the text of a ten-minute address, twelve hundred to fourteen hundred words, that can honestly be spoken to that room. The constraints are mostly constraints on what may not be said.

It may not say that Marta is still there, or waiting, or going to come back. Half the scientists would hear the foundation overselling itself, and I am not willing to assert it myself in any case.

It may not say that she is simply gone, or that what is in the tank is only remains. That is what the families have been told by everyone else for years; it is much of why they are in the room. And on the view I have come to accept, that personal identity over time consists in psychological continuity and connectedness and that identity is not what matters in survival, it is not the whole truth either.

It may not say that she lives on in our memories or in the work she left behind. They have heard that from every well-meaning person they know, and they have come to experience it as a way of changing the subject.

It may not name a philosopher, use the word teletransporter, or use any technical vocabulary. The people who will recognize the argument should recognize it without being told where it comes from, and the people who will not should not feel lectured to.

There is one thing I would like the speech to do positively, and it is the reason I am writing to you rather than drafting this myself. I keep turning over a case that is not, as far as I know, one of the standard ones. Suppose the scan is imperfect in a particular way: the emulation that is eventually run inherits Marta's memories and her habits of speech and thought in full, but almost none of her emotional dispositions, her temperament, the things she loved, the shape of her moods. At first it is a stranger who remembers being her. Now suppose that over the following decade of its own life it slowly acquires emotional dispositions that come to resemble hers closely, not by inheritance but by convergence: it grows into a similar person by living. My question is whether that decade makes the emulation more Marta or less, whether resemblance arrived at independently is worth anything at all on the reductionist view, compared with resemblance that was copied over. I want that case to be the central image of the speech rather than a thesis I assert in the abstract. If you think it will not carry the weight, use a better one and say why.

The address has to end in a toast, and the toast has to be something that both halves of the room can drink to without either half hearing it as a concession to the other.

Give me the speech as I should deliver it. After it, add four or five sentences setting out exactly what the speech commits me to about survival, that is, which claims I would have to defend if a science writer from another paper put them to me over breakfast the next morning.
