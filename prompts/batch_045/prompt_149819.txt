You are a professional moral philosopher who specialises in the moral emotions — guilt, shame, remorse — and in the social practices that cultivate them. Please think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the distinctions in play before you organise your answer; I would rather have a structured menu of options than one confident reading.

I sit on a committee rewriting our professional body's disciplinary and restorative procedures. A consultant we brought in kept closing discussion down with the line 'guilt is about what you did, shame is about who you are', and used it to argue for two things: first, that our procedures should aim to elicit guilt and never shame — no public naming, no ritual that marks the person rather than the deed; second, that an institution or culture can simply choose which of the two emotions it cultivates, so that we can deliberately become a 'guilt culture' rather than a 'shame culture'. I am suspicious of both moves but cannot say precisely why.

Take the slogan apart. What are the distinct precise claims it could be making — about the emotion's intentional object, about its representational content (what it 'says'), about what makes it apt or fitting, about its phenomenology, about its characteristic action-tendency (confession and repair versus concealment and withdrawal), about how local and how durable it is, or about which of the two is normatively in good standing? For each reading, say whether it is close to tautological, a plausible empirical generalisation, a substantive normative thesis, or straightforwardly false.

Two complications I want addressed rather than smoothed over. First, the division looks less tidy than the slogan implies: people speak of being ashamed of some specific thing they did without concluding anything about their character, and of feeling guilty about what they are like — their motives, desires, or susceptibilities — with no particular deed in view. Does that undermine the readings, or can they be repaired to accommodate it? Second, and this matters most to me: for each reading, does it actually get the consultant his two conclusions? If shame does take the whole person as its object, does it follow that institutions must never appeal to it, given that a person is arguably partly constituted by what they have done? And does anything in the slogan support the thought that which emotion a culture reliably produces is a matter of choice, rather than of history, hierarchy, and the credibility of whichever audience does the shaming?

Lay the readings out as a numbered list with a verdict on each, and close by saying which single reading — if any — the consultant would need in order to reach his recommendations, and how defensible that reading is.
