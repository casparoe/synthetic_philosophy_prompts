I'm in the final weeks of a philosophy-and-economics seminar, and I've drifted into defending a position I originally found unattractive, and I'd like it stress-tested before I commit it to a term paper. The position is a hard-line revealed-preference reading of utility: that utility denotes nothing over and above a numerical representation of an agent's choice behavior; that there is no further fact about how well off someone is, hidden behind or underneath what they choose, for welfare policy or interpersonal comparison to track; and that talk of a true or experienced welfare that diverges from choice is, at best, a rejection of the consumer-sovereignty premise misdescribed as a discovery about the agent. On this reading, where an agent's choices admit no coherent utility representation at all, the right conclusion is that there is no utility ordering to be had — a verdict about that agent's incoherence, not evidence of a welfare fact that choice has failed to capture.

So: what would count against it? Please don't give me the literature map or tell me who has argued what — I can assemble that myself, and I'd rather you work through the cases than through the names. What I want is a ranked set of defeaters, from fatal down to merely awkward, each with the argument or case attached and worked through rather than gestured at.

Three things I want you to take as stipulated and tell me what they do to my view, because I think the standard objections often misfire on wording rather than substance:

First, the valence monitor. Suppose we had a perfectly reliable non-invasive instrument recording moment-to-moment affective valence — reliable in the sense that it agrees with itself across sessions, is not perturbed by being observed, and is not simply re-encoding the agent's own judgments. Suppose further that in a wide, stable class of cases it ranks options inversely to how the agent chooses, that informing the agent of the readings changes nothing about subsequent choices, and that the agents themselves, shown their own readings, accept them as genuine measurements of something while declining to revise their behavior in light of them. Is that a defeater for my reading of utility, or only for a thesis about welfare I never claimed? Say exactly which of my claims it touches, if any.

Second, a result I'll stipulate rather than derive, and call the shadow-rerepresentation result: any body of choice data that admits an ordinary utility representation also admits a representation on which the agent maximizes a welfare function subject to a menu-dependent distortion, and no possible extension of the choice data — choice under risk, over time, over menus, under framing variation — distinguishes the two representations. If that were true, would my position have been shown to be empty? Is the residual dispute verbal, and if so, does that verdict help or hurt me?

Third, reverse revelation: the phenomenon of agents choosing to shrink their own opportunity sets — declining options at no cost, paying to have temptations removed, pre-committing against choices they predictably go on to make. I take these to be choices like any others and hence grist for the representation; a critic hears them as the agent's own testimony that choice is not the last word on her welfare. Which reading is more defensible, and what would settle it?

Two further requirements. For each defeater, tell me whether a committed defender of my position would concede it as counting against them, or would absorb it by a reformulation — I'm more interested in what my own side has to grant than in what its opponents allege. And tell me what my view predicts that its rivals do not, since a position that predicts nothing distinctive has arguably insulated itself out of content; if you conclude that it has been qualified into unfalsifiability, say so plainly and say whether, for a view of this kind, that is a defect. For what it's worth, the thing I personally find hardest to shrug off is not the valence monitor but the repudiated choice: the case of an agent who declines to reverse a choice at zero cost while sincerely and stably wishing she had never been offered it. I'd like you to take that case as seriously as you can, and then tell me whether taking it seriously is itself a confusion I should get over.
