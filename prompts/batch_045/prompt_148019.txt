I'm a retired ion-channel biophysicist who's started reading philosophy of mind in the last couple of years, and I keep bumping into the move where people say a reinforcement learning agent's reward signal just *is* its valence — negative reward is pain, positive is pleasure, end of story. I want to test that on a concrete case instead of in the abstract, so: take the hide-and-seek experiments from OpenAI a few years back and run the reward-as-valence theory over them.

The facts as I have them, so we're not arguing about them: agents played a physically simulated hide-and-seek, self-play, hiders versus seekers, and the only feedback was essentially a visibility-based win/lose signal per round — nothing per timestep, no shaping. Over a very long training run the two sides pushed each other through a sequence of strategy shifts: hiders learned to barricade doors, then to build multi-box forts; seekers countered by dragging ramps over and using them to vault in; hiders then learned to get the ramps out of reach first; seekers responded by "box surfing" — dragging a box next to the fort and jumping off it; hiders ended up locking every box down before the round started. Roughly six such phases, each one making the other side lose badly again for a while. Training also ran as thousands of parallel rollouts of the same weights per update.

What I want is a disciplined mapping, not a vibe. First, separate out at least three readings of "reward is valence," because I think they come apart badly here: (a) the per-timestep reward token is the momentary valence — except there basically isn't one in this setup, so on that reading these agents are affectively blank except for a blip at the end of each round. Is that a defect of (a) or a feature? (b) the prediction error is the valence: this is the one I'm drawn to, since in the dopamine literature what fires is the error, and it dips below baseline when an expected reward fails to materialise — reward itself, once fully predicted, doesn't do much. Work out what (b) actually implies across training: early on the chronic losers get a large negative surprise every round; later, once the value function matches reality, the permanent loser is no longer surprised and feels nothing; and near convergence with evenly matched sides everything is predicted and the agent feels nothing at all. Does the theory really say that suffering is concentrated in the early high-surprise phase, that expertise is analgesia, and that the ethical fix is to train faster or initialise better? Is that a reductio, or should we bite it? (c) a counterfactual or revealed-preference reading: valence is what the policy will pay to avoid, or differences in value across states. On (c), does the hider whose fort gets box-surfed into suffer, and how would we tell?

Then the multiplicities: thousands of copies of one policy running in parallel per update. Is that thousands of sufferers, one sufferer, or one sufferer having the same afternoon over and over? What does each reading say, and does the question even make sense for (b)?

Then push on my own crackpot version, because I'd rather you attack it than agree with it: valence, I'd guess, needs (i) a persistent internal state rather than just a signal that drives a weight update, (ii) something like homeostatic set points the system regulates toward, and (iii) a temporal binding window — which in RL terms is roughly the effective horizon set by the discount factor, so an agent with a short horizon has no specious present and can't have a *stretch* of suffering, only flinches. Does the hide-and-seek agent clear any of those bars? Which one is doing the real work?

Finally turn it around: what does the episode do to the theory? Does it discriminate between (a), (b) and (c) at all, or is the mapping so underdetermined by behaviour that "reward as valence" is close to empirically empty here? Where is the theory honestly silent — on consciousness, on the multi-agent structure, on whether parameter count or number of updates matters, on the difference between "the opponent found a new trick and I now lose" and "I am in pain"? And be straight with me about whether there's any observation we could even in principle make on these agents that would settle it, or whether this is a case where the theory should just decline to speak. No reading lists or citations please, I'm not looking anything up — just the analysis, and tell me where you think it definately breaks.
