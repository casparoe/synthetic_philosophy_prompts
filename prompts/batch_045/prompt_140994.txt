I'm trying to pin down what I actually think about money pump arguments before I commit it to paper, and I'd rather find out now that the pieces don't fit. Here's roughly the package I've ended up with:

(1) The money pump argument against cyclic preferences is basically right, and what makes it right is the exploitation: having A better than B better than C better than A is irrational because you'd hand over money and end up with exactly what you started with. It isn't irrational because it breaks some axiom.

(2) It's not irrational to change your preferences over time. Twenty years from now I might prefer a quite different life, and that's a defect in neither present me nor future me — it's just what time does to people.

(3) Cycles of *indifference* (I'm indifferent between A and B, and between B and C, but prefer A to C) are the more interesting case than strict cycles, and I think the money pump works better there, since each individual trade either looks like a genuine improvement or costs me nothing by my own lights.

(4) In sequential decisions I'm a sophisticated chooser, not a resolute one: backward-induct on what your later self will actually want, don't pick a plan at the start and stick to it out of pigheadedness.

(5) But Ulysses-style precommitment is sometimes rational too — paying now to take options off your future self's table isn't automatically a form of weakness.

(6) The diachronic Dutch book arguments, the money pump analogues for changing credences, show nothing at all: someone who plans to revise in the light of future evidence isn't being inconsistent.

So: does this hang together? I've got a vague feeling that something in here is unstable but I genuinely can't locate it, and I definately don't want to prejudge which claim I'd drop.
