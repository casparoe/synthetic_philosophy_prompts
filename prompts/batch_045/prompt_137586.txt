Two friends of mine got into it after a panel discussion last week and I've been going over it in my head since, mostly because I genuinely couldn't tell who was winning. They were arguing about "Moloch" — the name that's been floating around certain corners of the internet for years for situations where everybody ends up worse off through individually sensible competition, races to the bottom and so on. Jules, who did graduate work in political economy, opened by saying the whole thing is just a multiplayer prisoner's dilemma with better branding: we've had the formalism forever, and the demon talk buys you nothing except fatalism, because a named monster sounds like a cause when it's really just a summary of everyone's incentives. Bea said no, the name tracks something the payoff matrix doesn't: it isn't only that defecting pays, it's that defectors survive and get copied, so the bad outcome gets arrived at by elimination rather than by choice — nobody picks it and there it is anyway. Jules replied that that's just evolutionary game theory, also not new, and worse, Moloch-talk points people at the wrong remedy: you don't fight a demon, you change the payoffs. Bea said the rhetoric is part of the remedy, since nobody votes to change the payoffs until they can feel the trap, and besides the label covers cases a single matrix doesn't — jurisdictions competing over the rules themselves, not just players competing inside one fixed game.

Then Jules said: fine, let's be precise, because I think there are at least four different things getting lumped together here. (a) Greed traps, where defecting pays no matter what anyone else does. (b) Fear traps, where it only pays if others defect, so what's actually driving things is not wanting to be the last sucker. (c) Selection traps, where no individual faces any temptation whatsoever, but groups that restrain themselves lose out to groups that don't. (d) Phantom traps, where nothing in the structure forces a race and it happens anyway, because everyone falsely believes everyone else is already racing. Those have completely different fixes — enforcement, credible assurance, group-level institutions, and simply better information — so a single word covering all four is doing real damage. Bea came back with the point I found hardest to answer: every one of those fixes is exposed to the same dynamics again — the enforcer gets captured, the assurance game gets exploited by people who lie about their intentions, regulation turns into a race between jurisdictions, and even information about what others are doing becomes another input to the race — so the taxonomy doesn't dissolve Moloch, it just relocates it one level up. Jules said that's exactly the problem: if the label also applies to the attempts to escape it, then it isn't an explanation of anything, it's what you say when you've stopped looking for the mechanism. Bea said that's overstated, and offered a test: picture twenty firms that have all signed a voluntary six-month pause on something they'd all slightly prefer stayed paused, none of which actually wants to defect, all of which believe the others are about to defect. The pause collapses and it looks exactly like a race to the bottom, but it's type (d), and you can tell, because a cheap simultaneous-commitment device fixes it overnight, whereas a greed trap shrugs off cheap fixes. And then she added a fifth category herself that neither of them had mentioned before: ratchet traps, where nobody defects, nobody competes, and every individual step is a genuine improvement everyone involved agrees to — it's just that the endpoint is somewhere nobody would have chosen — and she said game theory doesn't even get a grip on those.
