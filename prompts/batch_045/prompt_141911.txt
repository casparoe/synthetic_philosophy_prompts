I draft the allocation recommendations for a regional health board's priorities committee, and I have a case next week that I believe our own precedents genuinely underdetermine. I would like your help before I write the memo.

The committee's five relevant decisions, each with published reasons:

1. It funded a drug for about sixty patients with advanced cancer, giving each roughly eight additional months, over a dermatology program for twelve thousand patients with mild conditions that produced roughly three times as much total benefit in the board's own units. Stated reason: a given gain matters more the worse off the patient is. The cancer patients are the worst-off group the committee has ever funded; the dermatology patients are among the least badly off it has ever considered.

2. At equal cost, it funded renal support for three hundred patients with severe kidney failure over a program that would have given twenty-five of the very gravest patients a somewhat larger gain each. Stated reason: the committee is not required to spend everything on the very worst off, and numbers count.

3. It funded a chronic-pain service for patients whom the board's own guidelines classify as above the decent minimum, over a health-promotion program with greater total benefit for people who are only mildly affected. Stated reason: these patients are still the worst off in the region.

4. It approved a uniform cap on a home-care benefit that had previously been available at a higher level in three districts. No one's health improved and total benefit fell slightly. Stated reason: it was unfair for the system itself to provide different levels in different districts.

5. It rejected a proposal to close a specialist clinic used mainly by the least-deprived quartile, which would have reduced inequality substantially, improved no one's health, and cost a large amount of total benefit. Stated reason: the committee is not in the business of making people worse off merely to improve the shape of the distribution.

The new proposal: extend intensive home respiratory nursing to about eight hundred and fifty additional patients with advanced COPD, paid for by discontinuing a free non-emergency transport and interpreter service used by roughly six thousand better-off patients. Our analysts put the gain at about 0.6 units per COPD patient and the loss at about 0.1 units per transport user, so roughly 510 units gained against roughly 600 lost: the package is net-negative in total benefit. Every gainer is worse off than every loser. The gainers sit at the bottom of the board's severity ranking and the losers in its top third. Unlike decisions 4 and 5, the gains here are not zero: eight hundred and fifty people really do improve. And the transport service is an ordinary universal service that better-off patients happen to use most; there is nothing unfair about it in the way the district tiers in decision 4 were unfair.

What I need:

The decision you would have us recommend, and the reasoning that would let the committee take it without contradicting itself. I am not asking what either of us should believe about distributive ethics; I am asking what this committee is already committed to, and what it would be adopting either way.

Set out the full set of rules that fit all five precedents, not just the ones I have thought of. My own candidates are a prioritarian rule with a concave but finite weighting plus a side-constraint about fairness in how the system treats people; a prioritarian core plus an independent concern with equality of outcome, weighted small enough to explain decision 5; and a sufficientist rule on which extra weight applies only below the decent minimum. There may be others, including rules that treat decisions 4 and 5 as turning on something other than size.

For each rule that survives, say what it implies here, and note which precedents are doing the actual work and which are consistent with everything. State the break-even priority weight, that is, how steep the weighting of the worst off must be relative to everyone else for approval to follow, and whether any weighting that fits decisions 1 through 3 permits rejection.

Give me your best guess with the reasons for preferring it, identify the closest analog among the five, and be explicit about what we would be committing ourselves to by approving and what we would be committing ourselves to by declining.

Two further things. First, someone at the meeting will say that approving a net-negative package is leveling down by the back door; tell me whether that charge has any force against a prioritarian rule as opposed to an egalitarian one, and what exactly the difference amounts to. Second, name the single further case that would most efficiently separate the readings that survive, and say what it would have to look like.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning: no preamble and no restatement of the question. A short table of the surviving rules and their verdicts would be useful. Aim for roughly 700 to 900 words.
