Dear Sir or Madam,

I am in the upper sixth, writing an extended essay of about fifteen hundred words for a school prize on the evolution of cooperation. It is meant to be readable by a bright sixth-former but to say something a specialist would not think simply wrong, and I have to revise it largely on my own, so I am writing to ask for comments on the argument rather than on the prose. Two requests first. I cannot get hold of journal articles and our school library has almost nothing on this, so please do not give me reading lists, citations, links, or the names of papers; if my idea turns out to be a version of something familiar, tell me in your own words what it amounts to rather than pointing me to a source. And please reply in continuous prose, the way you would write an email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables. Lists are easier to skim, but I find them harder to argue with.

The essay has three moves. The first is standard. In a single play of the prisoner's dilemma, with payoffs ordered T greater than R greater than P greater than S and with two R greater than T plus S, defecting pays more whatever the other player does, so two rational players both defect. When the game is repeated with a continuation probability w, reciprocity can sustain cooperation, and comparing what a tit-for-tat player gets from cooperating forever, R over one minus w, against what it gets from defecting once and being punished forever, T plus wP over one minus w, yields the condition that w must be at least the ratio of T minus R to T minus P. I would like to know whether I have that right and whether I have stated it the way it is usually stated.

The second move is my complaint. Popular accounts, I argue, show that reciprocity is stable once plenty of reciprocators are already around, but not how cooperation gets going, and these are different questions. Drop a single tit-for-tat player into a population of defectors with partners drawn at random, and it cooperates in its opening encounter, is exploited for S, then defects thereafter and gets P; the defectors around it get P from the beginning, so the newcomer is worse off by P minus S and selection removes it. I also note that a fixed and known last round unravels everything by backward induction, which is why the ending has to be genuinely uncertain rather than merely distant, and that a single misread move can lock two reciprocators into a vendetta. I set kin selection aside as too narrow to explain cooperation among strangers, and group selection as something I do not want the essay to rest on.

The third move is my own proposal, which I call the sponsorship model, and it is the part I most want comments on. Keep the repeated game and the uncertain ending, but add an outside option: any player may decline an encounter and take a solitary payoff L, where L lies between P and R, so that refusing to play a defector is worth doing while finding a partner is still worth doing. Now add sponsorship. Any player may sponsor any other; the sponsor takes no share of the client's winnings but pays a penalty each time the client defects, and sponsorship can be withdrawn at any time and at no cost. The only reason to sponsor anyone is that sponsorship is reciprocal, you vouch for me and I vouch for you, and it is that pair of sponsorships which lifts both of you into the club. Being sponsored is what makes you eligible: sponsored players meet sponsored players and earn R, everyone else falls back on L, and because a sponsor is penalised for a client's cheating, sponsors drop clients who defect, so expulsion from the club is what holds cooperation up. The point of the model, as I use it, is that it needs no critical mass: two rare cooperators who find each other can sponsor each other and from then on earn R while the defectors around them earn L, so cooperation can start from a pair rather than from a majority.

I have four worries I cannot settle, and I would be grateful for your view on each. First, when I redo the threshold for my own model, I get that a club member should refrain from defecting when R over one minus w is at least T plus wL over one minus w, which rearranges to the condition that w be at least the ratio of T minus R to T minus L; since L exceeds P, that is a stricter requirement than the ratio of T minus R to T minus P in my first section, which suggests my mechanism needs a longer shadow of the future than the plain reciprocity it was supposed to rescue. Is the algebra right, is the comparison right, and if so is it as damaging as it looks, or am I comparing two situations that are not really comparable?

Second, I think I may have helped myself to two different assumptions about matching. The threshold calculations compare a player against one partner who meets it again and again, while the invasion argument assumes partners are drawn at random from the whole population. Is that an inconsistency I have to repair, or can both hold at once?

Third, and most important: does the sponsorship model actually solve the origins problem, or has it only renamed it? Two cooperators sponsoring each other just is a way of arranging to meet each other more often than chance would arrange it, and I cannot tell whether that is a genuine mechanism for manufacturing assortment out of nothing or whether I have quietly taken the correlated interaction that the first half of my essay said needed explaining. There is a related difficulty I noticed late: if sponsorship is mutual, then the sponsor who drops a cheating client may be dropped in turn by that client and lose its own membership, and I cannot see whether that makes the threat of expulsion incredible or whether it does not matter.

Fourth, even if a founding pair works, I have no clean account of how the club grows. A member who sponsors a newcomer risks the penalty, so members should be selective, but to be selective they need a track record to be selective about, and that looks like the same chicken-and-egg problem one level down. Is there a version of the growth story that works, or does growth push me back to needing a critical mass after all?

Beyond those four, I would like to know whether the frame of the whole essay, the distinction between stability and origins and the claim that the second question has been neglected, is a real distinction or a muddle; whether the middle section is too technical for the audience I described; and which single part of the argument you would shore up or cut first if you had the draft in front of you.
