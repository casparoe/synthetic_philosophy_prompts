I'm writing a short paper for my seminar on consciousness and perception, and I want to press on something we have been treating as settled. The case is the color-phi display: a red spot is flashed at one location, a green spot at a nearby location a fraction of a second later, and viewers report a single spot gliding from the first position to the second and changing color about midway. Since the reported color change seems to occur before the system could have known the second spot would be green, some retrospective filling-in has to happen, and we discussed two accounts of it: an Orwellian one, on which the viewer first consciously undergoes some other episode and that episode is subsequently written over in the record, and a Stalinesque one, on which the visual system drafts its story and hands consciousness only the finished version, so the earlier candidate episode is never conscious at all. My suspicion is that the alleged equivalence between these two holds only because the usual ways of probing the interval are too weak.

Please design a single experiment that pries them apart. Equipment is ordinary visual psychophysics: two displays or LEDs, a button box, reaction times, forced choice, and post-decision confidence or wagers. No neuroimaging, no electrode readouts, no lesions, no drugs. Constraints:

- One paragraph of set-up, and the scenario should be something a real lab could run on human subjects.
- The two accounts must generate different, measurable behavioral outcomes — not merely different descriptions of the same data.
- The discrimination may not come from asking subjects to introspect on the timing or sequence of their own experiences, since on both accounts they will report only the finished version.
- Do not stipulate that the intermediate content is unconscious; that is exactly what is at issue. And do not let the verdict hinge on a device that reads out neural states directly.
- The divergence has to follow from what each account says is happening during the interval, not from an extra assumption bolted onto one side alone.

After the scenario, give the predicted data pattern under each account together with the mechanism that produces it, and state which single feature of the design is load-bearing. Then say what happens to the case if that feature is removed, and take on the two moves that would neutralise it: the Stalinesque theorist's claim that a pre-conscious draft can leak into the response without ever being conscious, and the Orwellian theorist's claim that revision can be fast and complete enough to leave no trace. Tell me which of those replies, if either, is doing genuine work rather than restating its own view — and if your design cannot survive one of them, say so plainly instead of papering over it.
