I run a fortnightly reading group for second-year undergraduates on Kant's ethics, and we have just finished the section of the Groundwork on the formula of universal law. What struck me is that everyone in the room now uses the same state-your-maxim-then-universalise-it-then-ask-whether-you-could-will-it vocabulary, and yet they disagree about nearly every case, because under that shared vocabulary they are quietly running four or five different tests. A friend of mine who works in decision theory sat in last week and added to the confusion by insisting that the whole procedure is just an under-specified coordination test, and that almost all of its apparent results come from choices about how the maxim is described. So rather than run another seminar on it, I want to give them a diagnostic quiz that sorts them by what they say about cases before they learn what the readings are called.

Please act as a professional philosopher who specialises in Kant's practical philosophy and who has some experience designing teaching materials. Before you write any questions, take a bit of time to brainstorm the logical space: what are the genuine choice points that any universalizability test has to settle, and which combinations of answers to them are actually live? Build the instrument out of those distinctions, and only afterwards locate the familiar readings inside the space as leaves. The deliverable is ten questions, each a concrete maxim or case with two or three answer options, phrased so that a student can answer from their instincts about the case without knowing any labels and without being able to guess which reading an option leads to. No question should ask directly which version of the test they accept, or whether the formula is empty, or whether maxims must be described at a certain level of generality. Please use at least some of these cases (and add better ones of your own): the familiar promise-breaking case; lying to a would-be murderer who asks where his intended victim is; the inefficacy maxim in which one person's flight, coffee cup or ad-blocker makes no difference and yet everyone's does; a maxim of universal deference in which everyone insists on letting the other person go first; a maxim of extraordinary self-sacrifice such as donating a kidney to a stranger; and a maxim concerning beings who cannot reciprocate, such as eating animals or discounting future people. The distinctions I most want the questions to be sensitive to are: whether failure is a matter of inconceivability, of the agent's own purpose being defeated, or of a rational will's refusing a world it can perfectly well conceive; whether the universalised world is one in which everyone acts on the maxim or one in which everyone is disposed to; how many agents must act on it for the test to bite — literally everyone, or merely a quorum large enough to destroy the practice; whether the test is about what everyone does or about what would be done to you in the reversed position; and whether the test has purchase at all when the affected parties cannot reciprocate.

The scoring key should map answer patterns, or dominant tendencies rather than exact hashes, onto five or six leaves — I want the contradiction-in-conception reader, the purpose-frustration reader, the contradiction-in-will reader, the reader who thinks the formula of humanity is doing the real work, the constructivist who relocates the action to the specification of the maxim, and the sceptic who thinks the formula delivers nothing — each leaf with one sentence on the most awkward commitment it inherits. Please also flag any pair of leaves your ten questions cannot really separate, rather than pretending they separate, and note any combination of answers that the space allows but that nobody seems to occupy, with a word on whether it is empty because incoherent, because unattractive, or merely because unexplored. I expect the rigorism-versus-latitude question (whether even the murderer case admits no exception) to cut across several leaves rather than forming one of its own; say so if that is what you find. Nothing in the quiz may depend on looking anything up: no citations, no attribution of readings to named commentators, and no requirement that a student know who defended what — the labels should appear only in the key at the end. Finally, add a short closing note on how confident you are that each of your questions really discriminates between the readings rather than merely between temperaments, name the single question you are least confident about, and say which of your leaf placements you would expect a Kant specialist to contest most sharply.
