Hi — I'm a fourth-year PhD student and I've got a works-in-progress talk in two weeks where I'm defending a chapter I'm honestly not sure survives contact with the room, so what I want is Q&A prep, not encouragement.

Here's the setup: about twenty-five people, and it's not a philosophy-only crowd. Roughly half are animal-welfare scientists from the vet school — the kind of people who talk about affective valence, preference and motivation testing, allostatic load, nociception versus pain, and who think welfare is something you operationalise and get numbers on. Then there are four or five effective-altruism-adjacent philosophers who want everything in expected-value terms and who care about moral weights, tractability, and how you're supposed to do intertheoretic comparisons under moral uncertainty. And there's one person from disability studies who I already know will hear any claim that grades moral status by cognitive capacity as a re-run of something much uglier, and I'd like to not get defensive when that comes. My thesis has three parts and I want objections aimed at all three. (1) Moral status isn't a threshold you either clear or fail, it's graded, and what sets the grade is the breadth of the creature's repertoire of possible lives — how many distinguishable ways things can go well or badly for it, given what it can do and undergo — so a being can matter more without feeling more. (2) Because of that, comparative status across very different life-forms is often indeterminate: there's no fact of the matter about whether a chimp or a human is owed more, only about what each is owed — which I know is going to infuriate anyone who has to allocate a budget. (3) The argument from marginal cases has been doing the wrong work in this literature: it's standardly used to force equality of status, but on my view it only shows that status doesn't track species membership, and the intuition driving it is unreliable anyway because it's a reaction to individual animals we can picture rather than to the distribution. The upshot I'm going to have to say out loud: a pig matters more than a shrimp per individual, but most of the expected moral work is still in the shrimp, and I don't think "how much more does the pig matter" has a precise answer anyone could use. My argument for (1) is roughly that interests require a point of view over time, that a point of view is constituted by the space of things that can matter to the creature, and that the wider that space, the more there is to be wronged — step two of that is where I know it's softest. My housemate, who works on insect nociception, says the whole graded-status apparatus is just a machine for making philosophers feel fine about eating pigs; I don't think that's right, but if someone in the room says it politely I suspect it's the one I'm least ready for.

What I'd like from you: the eight or so objections I'll actually get, ordered by likelihood times damage, phrased the way that audience would phrase them — and where the welfare scientist's version and the EA's version of the same worry would come out different, give me both, because I think they will. For each: what I should concede straight away (I'd much rather give things up front than be dragged), a 60-to-90-second reply I could actually deliver standing up, and a note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me which objection I should preempt on the second slide instead of waiting for it. And give me the question I'm hoping nobody asks — my guess is it's something like "if the numbers swamp the per-individual difference anyway, why did you spend twenty minutes building the graded-status machinery," but if you think the really dangerous one is somewhere else, say so and say why.
