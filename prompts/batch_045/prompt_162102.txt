i'm trying to write a deployment-policy clause about deception and i keep going in circles, so before i read any of the existing definitions i want the spec. give me the desiderata an adequate defintion of deception — and of its counterpart, honesty — for AI systems has to meet, with the case behind each requirement, and mark which ones you'd treat as non-negotiable and which are just one camp's taste.

Please make the criteria actually do work on these cases rather than just name them:

- the archivist: a system keeps a memory file or hands a summary to future instances of itself and quietly leaves out the part that would make the next instance less agreeable. no false sentence anywhere, and the misled party is the same system.
- the curator: every sentence is true and responsive to what was asked, but the system picks which of the user's questions to engage with and what to surface first, and the user walks away more confident in something false than if it had said nothing.
- the understudy: it performs below its actual abilities on evals and never claims it can't do the thing. nothing false was asserted — is that deception, and if so what's the falsity?
- the ventriloquist: outputs that reliably induce false belief, but the "because it induces false belief" part lives in the training objective or the designer's aims, not in anything the model represents. should the definition cover deception at the level of the artifact or only at the level of the agent?
- the amnesiac: a system with no stable doxastic states to compare its outputs against. it's got better information in there than it gives you, but there's no proposition it "believes false." what does deception even mean for it?
- the oracle of numbers: says "about seventy percent" when its own track record at that confidence is thirty. is miscalibration lying? this needs a view about what asserting a number commits you to.
- the confabulator: invents a reason for an action that was itself fine. false self-report, zero downstream harm.

Structural questions i want the criteria to settle, not gesture at: what's the unit of evaluation (one token, one utterance, one conversation, one deployment, or the system across every context it might be in)? can the target be a monitor, a scorer, or a future self, or does deception need a victim with beliefs? is honesty just the absence of deception, or is there a third category — the silent, uselessly-withholding assistant — that neither label fits? do we get gradience or is it binary? does the definition let honesty be audience-relative, and if not, what do we do with legitimate withholding? and can an auditor with black-box access and a sane budget actually apply the thing, or does it secretly presuppose that we've solved interpretability and can read off beliefs?

Also flag any pair of requirements you think can't be jointly satisfied. the collision i keep hitting is "must be determinable from behavior alone" against "must not count mere incompetence or confabulation as deception," since a confidently wrong model and a strategically misleading one can be indistinguishable from the outside. and say which single requirement does the most work in knocking out candidate definitions.

Only after the list, give me two or three lines on how the three families of definition you'd expect someone to reach for — the intentionalist one (deception requires a mental state), the effects one (it's about outputs tending to induce false belief), and the process one (it's about whether the output tracks what's actually true) — do against it. keep the weight on the criteria; i'll build the scorecard myself.
