I run a tiny reading group — nothing formal, we meet in the back room of a pub every other week, usually 12-16 of us, mostly people in their twenties and thirties with day jobs and no philosophy between us. We've got 90 minutes, no budget (I can bring index cards, dice, a coin, pens, maybe a whiteboard if I can borrow one), and I want to do a session on the thing I've been chewing on for months: whether the fact that there are *more* of them is itself a reason to save the many instead of the one. My flatmate thinks it's obvious — five deaths is worse than one, of course you save the five, anyone who hesitates is being precious. I don't think she's exactly wrong, but I also don't think the "of course" is doing the work she thinks it is, and I can't put my finger on what's missing, and I really don't want to stand up and explain it, partly because I'd tip my hand and partly because I'd probly mangle it. So I want the group to walk into it instead.

Constraints I actually care about: nobody gets told the view up front, and I don't want the side-issues doing the work either. Last time we tried to talk about this someone immediately asked whether the rescuer was a lifeguard on duty, or whose fault the danger was, or whether the one was a child, and the whole thing dissolved into that. I'd rather the setup itself fenced that off — all strangers, nobody caused it, nobody has a special duty, none of them braver or needier or more deserving — than have me say "just assume that away". And ideally they invent the equal-chance, flip-a-coin answer themselves, by being backed into a spot where splitting the difference is the only thing that feels fair, and *then* get to see what that commits them to once the numbers get big.

Two wrinkles I've been playing with that I can't get to work, which you should use or improve on. First: each person at risk also secretly draws a card saying what kind of decision procedure *they* want the rescuer to follow — some cards say "add us up, save the most", some say "give each of us our own shot, don't total us" — so the rescuer ends up having to weigh what the people themselves asked for, and it can come out that the one says save the five while the five say give him his chance, or the reverse. I like it because it seems to expose something that neither the adding-up answer nor the equal-chance answer handles cleanly, but I'm not sure what it's actually showing. Second: the five can each waive their own claim, signing a card saying "ignore me, save him", and the question is how many have to waive before the one gets saved outright — and whether that shows the numbers were never doing real work, or is just the same move with extra steps.

What I want back: actual rules, roles and a rough minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes (including how people get assigned to be at risk versus doing the rescuing, and whether they rotate), something that gives the choices a bit of bite without money — I was thinking everyone quietly gets a stake in whether "their" person makes it out, with some silly prize at the end, winner picks next month's book or whatever — and a debrief that maps what happened in the room onto the parts of the argument. Please include where it will predictably go wrong (I'm betting at least one person tries to settle it by majority vote, which I think quietly destroys the whole point, and at least one notices the stakes aren't really real) and be honest about which part of the idea the game simply can't reproduce and I'll have to say out loud myself at the end.
