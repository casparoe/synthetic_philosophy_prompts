A podcast on the history of ideas I was listening to summed up Descartes on animals roughly like this: animals are just clockwork, he said, with no soul and no thought, and the argument was basically that they can't talk — a machine can produce any behaviour you like, but no animal can use language to declare its thoughts to us, so nothing that can't talk has a mind, and once you describe animal behaviour as matter in motion you don't need to posit a soul to explain any of it. And since for Descartes the soul just is the conscious mind, having no soul means having no feelings either: a yelping dog is like a bell that has been struck. That is supposedly why he and his followers were untroubled about cutting up live animals, and why the whole doctrine was mostly a licence for cruelty dressed up as metaphysics. But it was never a serious position anyway: anyone who's lived with a dog knows animals have feelings, and we now know they share our brain structures and our pain chemistry, so the view is simply refuted.

I dont trust it, partly because the pieces fit together too neatly to all be right, and partly because I half-remember hearing that Descartes denied thought to animals but not sensation, and that the language point was a test for the presence of a mind rather than the reason he thought animals lack one — though I may well be mixing that up. Can you go through the summary and tell me what a careful reader of Descartes would grant, what's a conflation or an overstatement, and what's just polemic? In particular: is the bit about shared brain chemistry actually touching his argument, or does it change the subject, and is the vivisection stuff a fair consequence of the view or a bit of hostile anecdote-mongering?
