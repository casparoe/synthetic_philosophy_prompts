I write about science and ideas for a magazine, and over the past two years I have spent time with people who believe, variously, that a destroyed advanced civilization lies behind the world's megaliths, that extraterrestrial visitors are the real explanation for engineering that ordinary textbooks attribute to Bronze and Iron Age workmen, and that the conventional chronology of the last two millennia is an early modern fabrication of the sort associated with Fomenko. What I keep tripping over is not that their individual claims are false. Plenty of those claims are checkable and they fail. It is something about how the claims relate to the conclusion, and I would like to know whether there is an established way of describing it.

Two features, which I take to be aspects of one thing.

First, the evidence is detachable. In conversation, when one item is discredited — an out-of-place artifact explained, a corrected date, a map that does not after all show what it was said to show — the conclusion does not shift by any amount I can measure. The person concedes the item, sometimes cheerfully, and within a sentence has reattached the same conclusion to a different item. This is not quite a theory protecting itself with an auxiliary hypothesis, because nothing is revised to accommodate the anomaly; the item is simply dropped and replaced, like a bulb in a string of lights. The overall brightness is apparently supposed to come from the number of bulbs rather than from any of them in particular.

Second, the items do not cohere with one another. They share no mechanism and no causal story. A claim about the precision of a megalithic alignment, a claim about a Renaissance map, and a claim about irregularities in a king list have nothing to do with each other except that all three are being used to support the same conclusion. And yet their number is offered as cumulative support: surely not all of these can be coincidence. That is the line I hear most often, and it is the one I have the hardest time answering in a sentence.

What I think I am looking at, put as plainly as I can, is a style of argument in which an essentially fixed conclusion is supported by a large and open-ended assortment of weak, mutually unrelated considerations, none of which is load-bearing, and none of which is abandoned at any cost when it fails. My questions:

1. Does this have a name, either in the epistemology of evidence and testimony or in the literature on demarcation and pseudoscience? I have tried unfalsifiable and self-sealing, but both seem wrong: these claims get falsified one at a time, repeatedly, and it costs nothing. Confirmation bias and motivated reasoning are too broad to be useful, and they locate the problem in the psychology of the believer rather than in the structure of the argument, which is where I think the problem actually is.

2. What exactly is wrong with the inference from many weak, unrelated items to a conclusion? I gather there is a literature on whether variety of evidence is confirmatory in its own right, and on the conditions under which it is; I would like those conditions stated precisely enough that I can see why this case fails them. Is the failure that the items are not independent, that the pool they were drawn from cannot be specified well enough for any probabilistic reasoning to get a grip, that there is no shared mechanism linking them, or something else again — and if several of these are true, can they be ranked by how much work each is doing?

3. Where are the edges? I do not want to accuse everyone who reasons from scattered traces of arguing badly. Detectives, intelligence analysts, and entirely respectable historians argue from many small, individually inconclusive items, and sometimes they are right. What distinguishes the legitimate version from this one? Is the difference that the historian's clues are bound together by a causal story that says what further clues ought to look like, whereas these are bound together only by the conclusion they are supposed to support? If so, is that a test I could actually apply to a claim handed to me?

4. Relatedly, is the failure here different in kind from pseudoscience proper? The arguments I am given do not make predictions that fail. They offer reinterpretations of evidence already in hand, and history, unlike physics, cannot run the experiment that would settle the matter. I want to know whether the shrug with which my editors treat this material, compared with how they treat creationism or vaccine misinformation, has any principled basis in the epistemology, or whether it is only that the harms are less visible.

Please answer in American English. I am not after a reading list. I want the phenomenon carved — what it is, what it is not, and which of the nearby notions fits it best, and why the others do not — precisely enough that I can reproduce the distinction accurately in print without caricaturing anyone.
