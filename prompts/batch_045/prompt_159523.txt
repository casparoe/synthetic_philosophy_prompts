I work in a cognitive science lab, and at a journal club last week we got stuck on how to score a masking variant I will call staggered-probe masking. A target shape is presented for 33 ms under forward and backward masking; 150 ms after onset we force a speeded two-alternative manual response, and at 800 ms the same subject gives a verbal report of what they saw. On roughly a fifth of trials the two probes come apart: the hand discriminates the shape at well above chance, while the subject says, apparently sincerely, that nothing at all was visible on that trial. Our preregistration requires each trial to be scored as a case of conscious perception or not, so we need an answer to whether the subject consciously perceived the shape on those split trials.

A senior colleague ended the discussion by saying that there is no Cartesian theater, hence no fact of the matter about whether the subject saw the shape on a split trial, so our question is ill-posed and should be dropped from the analysis rather than answered. I suspect there is something right in this, but I genuinely cannot tell whether the slogan is doing work here or changing the subject, because the phrase seems to be used for several claims of very different strength.

As I understand its original use, Dennett introduced the term in Consciousness Explained (1991), in connection with the multiple drafts model, writing that Cartesian materialism is the view that there is a crucial finish line or boundary somewhere in the brain, marking a place where the order of arrival equals the order of "presentation" in experience because what happens there is what you are conscious of. A claim about a finish line is one thing; a claim that there is no fact of the matter is quite another, and I cannot see what takes you from the first to the second.

Please give me exactly five numbered items, ordered from the weakest reading of the slogan to the strongest, with no introduction and no conclusion. I would like the readings to differ along more than one axis — some about where, some about when, some about which of several parallel processes is privileged, some about whether there is any fact of the matter at all. Each item should be one or two sentences and should: state one precise thesis the slogan could be expressing; classify it as trivial, substantive and defensible, substantive and contested, or false; say whether, if that were all the slogan meant, it would entitle my colleague to call our question ill-posed; and say whether that is likely to be the reading people who reach for the phrase in this setting actually have in mind. Please don't give me citations or a reading list — I want the distinctions and the verdicts made out from the concepts themselves, and I especially want to know which reading, if any, has to carry the weight of the conclusion and what it would cost us to accept it.
