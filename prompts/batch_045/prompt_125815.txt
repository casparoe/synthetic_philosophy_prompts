I'm writing a paper arguing that the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem is not an artifact of bet-and-demon cases, and that the standard reply — that the demon gives you a reason to bring it about that you have the attitude, not a reason to have it — is a local fix rather than a general solution. I need one case built to order that makes the point without the devices the usual cases lean on.

The case: a single paragraph, realistic, involving ordinary people, with no demon, bet, bribe, threat, hypnotist, drug, neurointervention, or similar crude incentive device. The wrong-kind pressure must come from something with independent normative standing of its own — a promise, a role, a relationship, a joint commitment, a duty of gratitude — and it must bear on a particular occasion, not on a long-term project of managing your own psychology.

Specifications:

(i) The attitude is recalcitrant: not producible or extinguishable at will, and not the sort of thing one can simply decide to acquire. Grief, amusement, trust, being unsettled, and finding someone impressive are the kind of thing I have in mind. Name the attitude you pick and say why it qualifies.

(ii) The right-kind reason — the consideration bearing on whether the attitude fits its object — is present and undefeated, and the wrong-kind reason is moral rather than merely prudential.

(iii) The verdicts must fall out of the views rather than be stipulated. Show what a fitting-attitude theorist says (understand this as someone who analyses value in terms of what it is fitting to favour, and who holds that a reason for an attitude must bear on its fittingness) and what an inclusive theorist says (someone who holds that any consideration counting in favour of having an attitude is a reason for it, and that the right/wrong-kind distinction marks nothing real). Argue to each verdict from the view, not from the intuition you want the reader to have.

Then: explain why the reason-to-bring-it-about or cultivate-it reply cannot do its usual work here, and in particular why the fallback that a recalcitrant attitude can still be cultivated indirectly — arranging situations, enlisting others, taking it up as a project over time — does not restore that reply. Identify the single feature of the case doing the work, say what happens to both verdicts if you remove that feature and substitute the nearest thing the standard cases use (a controllable attitude in place of the recalcitrant one, or a cash offer in place of the promise), and give the strongest objection available to whichever of the two views your case leaves looking worse.
