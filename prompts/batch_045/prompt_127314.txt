I never went to university — I left school at sixteen and have been working through this material on my own in the evenings for a couple of years — and I've let myself be talked into taking one forty-minute session on Aumann's agreement theorem at the fortnightly discussion group that meets in the back room of our library. About a dozen regulars, nearly all retired: a former civil engineer who enjoys catching the speaker out, two ex-primary-school teachers, and several who come mainly for the company. Nobody has any philosophy or any formal maths.

Please don't explain the theorem to me; I've read plenty of accounts of it. Plan how I should explain it. The thing I most want to land is the reading I arrived at on my own, which none of the popular write-ups seem to stress: that the theorem is not a prediction that reasonable people will converge if they talk long enough, but a claim about where a surviving disagreement has to come from — that once two people who trust each other's honesty and reasoning have genuinely put everything relevant on the table, any remaining gap traces back to something each of them had to assume before any of the evidence arrived, and the theorem itself says nothing about whether that something is defensible. The moral, on that reading, is not that you must end up agreeing, but that you can no longer blame the facts for the gap.

So I need: the order the pieces have to be introduced in and what depends on what (I can't tell whether the common-prior assumption or the common-knowledge point has to come first, or whether the payoff can even be heard before both are in place); the one example that should carry the whole session, with what it must contain and what it must carefully avoid — I assume anything smelling of politics, religion or expertise will swamp the point, but tell me what else to keep out of it; the misconceptions this particular audience will arrive with and form, in the order they will actually surface, and where in the sequence to head each off — I'm expecting so the other person must just be irrational, so I should defer to whoever knows more, prior just means prejudice so this says anyone can believe anything, common knowledge just means we both know, and so the sensible thing is to split the difference; the things I should say outright that I'm not going to attempt at this level; and one question I can put to the group at the end whose answers would show me they've understood the claim rather than merely been reassured that reasonableness wins in the end.
