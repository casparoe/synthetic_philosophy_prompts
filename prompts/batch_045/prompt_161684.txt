So we actually pulled off a surprise 70th birthday party this afternoon for my old undergraduate logic professor, Hal — which is awkward, because Hal spent a good chunk of his career on the surprise exam paradox and was sincerely convinced, on the strength of the backward-induction argument, that surprise parties are impossible. His line was always that if you tell someone in advance that the thing falls somewhere inside a bounded window and that they won't know which day, you've said something that can't be true, and he specifically begged us never to throw him one: either he'd deduce it, or we'd have lied to him. We did it anyway. We gave him the window (sometime during the week of his birthday), we never named the day, he walked in at 2pm and said out loud to nobody in particular, I did not see this coming — and then spent twenty minutes in the corner of the room trying to work out which premise of his own argument had just broke.

I'm giving the toast in about an hour and I want it to say something genuinely true about that, and it has to land for two rooms at once: half the people there are his former students and colleagues who will catch every reference, and the other half are his four kids, his brother the plumber, and neighbours who know him as the guy who mows his lawn at 6am on Sunday. Two ideas I'd like you to build it around. First: one of his old students, a guy named Raj who now does something in formal epistemology, used to argue with him that the induction never gets started if the window isn't bounded — if you say "one day, sometime, I'm going to throw you a surprise party" and never name an end date, there's no last day to eliminate first, so the proof collapses, and yet you can still be surprised on the day. Hal thought this was a cheat and refused to take it seriously, and I'd like Raj's point to get a fair hearing in the toast without turning into a seminar, because Raj is standing right there. Second: Hal's own answer, which he never published and only ever handed out on a single mimeographed page, was that "surprise" is doing two different jobs — that the real condition was never you can't deduce it but you won't, on the night before, be attending to the question at all, and that surprise works because people stop watching, not because the deduction fails. I think that's actually why this worked on him: he'd have predicted it in March, and by November he had forgotten to keep looking.

Complication the toast has to survive: there's a second thing he still doesn't know. His estranged old friend and coauthor has flown in from another country and is standing in the kitchen, and we're bringing her out the moment I sit down. So I can't say anything that announces a surprise still to come — which is, obviously, exactly his own paradox biting — but I do need to end in a way that hands off to her walking in, and I'd rather it land as affection than as a setup. Can you write me a three-ish minute toast? Constraints: short sentences, because he has a hearing aid and the room is going to be loud; one joke that works for the neighbours as well as for the logicians; no nothing-but-lecture; and no sentiment he'd have rolled his eyes at. Also, somewhere in it, in one sentence a non-philosopher can follow, I want an actual verdict on what went wrong in his argument — I'd of asked Raj to give the toast instead but he's the one hiding in the kitchen. My sister wanted to bake a noose-shaped cake and the rest of us talked her out of it, so if you can get at the hanging version of the paradox without any gallows humour, that's a bonus.
