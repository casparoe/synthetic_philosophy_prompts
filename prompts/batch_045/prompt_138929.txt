I've been arguing with myself about asylum cases for a while and I want to know what principle, if any, I actually believe. Here are my gut verdicts:

- A journalist who'll be jailed for publishing accurate criticism of her government: obviously yes.
- A woman whose husband beats her and whose local police won't lift a finger because that's just how things are there, even though the law on paper says otherwise: yes.
- A farmer whose land has stopped producing after ten years of drought: yes, but a uneasy yes — it doesn't feel like the same kind of yes as the first two.
- A kid who joined a gang at 14 because it was the only protection going, ran errands for it, now wants out and will be killed if he stays: yes, and I don't think the gang stuff should count against him.
- A man who was a low-level guard at a prison where beatings were routine, turned informant, and is now being hunted by the people he informed on: no, he doesn't get in. Which bugs me, because he was poor too and had few options too.
- A family that fled eight years ago, is safe and settled in the country next door, and now wants to move on to a wealthier one: no. That one feels finished.
- Someone who isn't in danger at all and just can't earn a living: no — but I don't feel great about it.
- On who has to take people: the country next door does, even when it's poor and already hosting two million. But the rich country whose weapons kept the war going also has a duty, and I can't get both of those just from "whoever's able to help."
- A rich country with no historical tie and no causal involvement: some duty, but weak? Genuinely unsure.

The test I keep coming back to is something like: you have a claim if going back leaves you with no acceptable option at all. That seems to get most of the list, but it turns the prison guard into a yes and the settled family into a maybe, and I don't want either. Can it be patched, or is it the wrong shape entirely? Whichever principle you land on, state it precisely, tell me which clause each case is doing the work for, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts I'd have to give up. I also want to know what it says about people who aren't displaced yet but will be — say a village that'll be unfarmable or underwater within fifteen years: do they have a claim now, and against whom? Please answer in American English.
