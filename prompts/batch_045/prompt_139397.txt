About three months ago I got moved (not exactly promoted) into the job where I'm the one who signs off on whether a support agent "misled" a customer. It happened because a friend of mine got let go over a chat where every single sentence he typed was literally true and the customer still came away believing something false. So this isn't academic for me and I keep going back and forth on cases.

What I need is a decision procedure I can run on a transcript, not a theory. I review maybe ten cases a week, fifteen minutes each, and my write-up goes to a manager who is not going to read anything with jargon in it. The output ranges from "no action" to coaching to written warning, so it matters but it isn't court.

Please build it out of the Gricean material — the gap between what a sentence says and what the speaker means by it, the cooperativeness assumption, the four-ish clusters of expectations about truthfulness, informativeness, relevance and clarity, plus the diagnostic tests people actually use: can the problematic inference be cancelled without contradiction, could you have said the same thing differently and carried the same inference, can you reconstruct step by step how a hearer would get there from the words plus shared background. Turn those into ordered steps: the question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with "not misleading," and where it hands the decision to a person because it's a values call about what our customers are entitled to expect. Say explicitly where lying and misleading-by-implication come apart in your procedure, and what you do when the literal content is true and the customer's takeaway is false.

The hard cases I actually hit, which I want handled and not hand-waved:

1. Tickets auto-close after 48 hours of customer silence, so the customer never gets to ask a follow-up, and the tests that depend on what would have happened if they had were hard to run. Also, our silence itself gets read as "we're on it" — is silence an utterance for this purpose, or does the procedure just not apply?
2. Most of our replies are macros, and increasingly they're drafted by a language model with no intentions at all. The Gricean stuff leans hard on what the speaker meant. Whose speaker meaning is it when the drafter is a template? I'd rather the procedure say "stop, this framework doesn't reach" than manufacture an answer.
3. Our customers include people the implication will not land on at all — second-language readers, people skimming on a phone at 2am. Is reasonableness indexed to an idealised hearer or the actual one who wrote in?

Then run it on three: (a) an agent sending "Your refund has been processed" at the moment they queued it in a nightly batch that then failed for two days; (b) an agent using the "Thanks, we've received your message and someone will look into it" macro on an account that had already been suspended and was never going to be looked at — they picked it because it was the closest macro; (c) an AI-drafted reply to an angry customer that ends "I completely understand your frustration," which the customer read as a human taking responsibility.

Please give me at least one case where the procedure lands somewhere I won't like, and say so straight instead of nudging it toward the comfortable answer.

Keep the whole thing under 900 words: numbered steps first (one line each — question, sufficient answer, stop or hand off), then the three applications, then a short list of what the procedure cannot settle and which distinctions you had to drop or fudge to make it usable by someone like me. If you have to cut something, cut the applications rather than the steps.
