I'm on a university committee that's trying to draft a pre-approval policy for "high-risk" research proposals — the two live cases are a gain-of-function-ish pathogen project and a proposed field trial of gene-drive mosquitoes that a biology group wants to run with a partner institution in another country — and we've been going in circles for about two months. I think I've worked out why: one analogy is doing all the argumentative work in the room, and I can't tell how much of it is load-bearing.

The analogy is our senior ecologist's favourite move: precaution is just insurance. You pay a certain, small, known premium up front to avoid an uncertain, large, possibly ruinous loss later. Nobody calls buying fire insurance irrational just because the house probably won't burn down, so obviously the burden of proof sits with the people who want to release the thing, and obviously "we don't know yet" is a reason to wait rather than a reason to go ahead. It's extremly effective rhetorically — it makes the precaution crowd sound prudent and makes the rest of us sound like we're refusing to pay the premium on a burning building. One of the other members just snaps back that this is Pascal's mugging in a cardigan, and that if the insurance logic were right we'd have banned penicillin, and then nothing gets resolved.

Here's what I want from you, and please be concrete rather than giving me a general essay on the precautionary principle. Lay out the mapping explicitly (a table of correspondences is fine): what plays the premium, the insured, the insurer, the payout, the actuarial table, the deductible, the decision not to buy. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the use it's being put to from the ones that are noise. My own list of suspicions, which I genuinely can't rank: insurance works because risks are pooled, repeated and roughly actuarially known, whereas these are one-off and the loss is correlated with everything else (who pays out to the insurer when the whole neighbourhood burns?); insurance prices risk rather than refuses it, so the analogy might license a bond or a tax rather than a moratorium; the premium here isn't known, and it's paid by different people than the ones who benefit or the ones who bear the risk; insurance is bought voluntarily by the risk-bearer, precaution is imposed on people who might rather take the risk; there's no insurable interest in the outcome we actually care about, since you can't indemnify the last generation or a collapsed ecosystem; and the one I keep coming back to, which is that for some of these technologies you only learn the risk profile by deploying at scale, so a rule that demands demonstrated safety first can become a self-locking equilibrium of permanent prohibition rather than a premium you pay and then stop paying.

Then the two questions I actually care about. Does the conclusion the ecologist is using it for — burden of proof on the proponents, "we don't know" counts against going ahead, whenever the downside is bad enough — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the whole thing? And is there a better analogy for what a precautionary review is supposed to be: engineering safety factors, phased clinical trials with stopping rules, firebreaks, real options and the option value of waiting, something else entirely? If you think insurance is still the best available frame, say what it would force us to change about how we actually decide these two cases.
