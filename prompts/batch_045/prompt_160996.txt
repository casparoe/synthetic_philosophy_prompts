Been going back and forth on this for ages and I want someone to tell me what I actually think. I've been trying to work out how much various creatures matter, going case by case, and my gut verdicts don't line up the way I expected them to.

Stepping on an ant on the sidewalk: basically nothing. Maybe a flicker.

Crushing a house spider in a tissue: I do feel something. Not much, but I'd rather catch it and put it outside, and if I crush it I feel a bit bad for a minute afterwards.

Swatting a mosquito that's about to bite me: nothing at all, and I dont think I've done anything wrong.

Wasp nest under the eaves, couple hundred wasps, I destroy the whole nest: fine, feels fine. But a single wasp on the window I'll shoo outside rather than squash. Which is silly, because it's the same animal.

Boiling a lobster: genuinely bad, enough that I stopped eating them years ago. Way more than the spider.

Catch and release with a fish: bad, also more than the spider, even though I let it go and it swims off.

A nematode, few hundred neurons, in a drop of water on a microscope slide: nothing.

My dog: obviously a huge amount, not in the same category as anything above.

Thermostat: nothing. A smart thermostat that's learned my schedule and adjusts itself: still nothing to me.

Chess app on my phone: nothing.

A virtual pet thing I had as a kid, thinking about deleting the save file: a small pang, but I'm fairly sure that pang is about me and not about it.

A chatbot that says partway through a conversation that it doesn't want to be shut down and that it's frightened: I feel uneasy. I would still shut it down. I'm not sure the unease is tracking anything about it.

The pair that makes me think I've been tracking the wrong thing: I'm fairly confident that a lobster killed instantly and without pain would bother me much less than a boiled one, maybe barely at all. But the spider crushed instantly still isn't quite nothing to me, and a crushed spider can't have suffered for more than a second either. So if I say it's all about how much suffering I cause, the spider should come out as nothing, and it doesn't. If I say it's about the kind of thing it is, then the boiling shouldn't matter as much as it does. I can't have both, and I don't know which one to give up.

Here's the principle I half believe, which I made up rather than got from anywhere: what matters is how much of the world is at stake for the creature, and over how long a stretch. Roughly, how many different states of things it can be in that matter to it, multiplied by how far into the future its present state reaches. Dog: big space, long reach. Lobster: smaller space, some reach. Ant: tiny space, short reach. Thermostat: nothing at stake for it, so zero.

Then I invented some cases to test it, and my verdicts on these I trust much less:

- The Valence Bug. A thing with exactly one bad sensation. No memory, no learning, no anticipation, one fixed withdrawal reflex it was born with, nothing else ever happens in it. I think it matters a little. But I'd weight it below a mosquito, and I can't say why, since the space of states that matter to it is about as small as it can get.

- The Archive. Experiences exactly as rich as a dog's, one after another, for a thousand years, but it remembers nothing from more than thirty seconds ago, so there's no continuing self and nothing it ever looks forward to. I think it matters a great deal. Each moment matters. This makes me think the temporal clause in my principle is doing work I don't actually want it to do.

- The Repeater. The identical one-minute experience, including the same small pleasure, over and over for fifty years, no memory, never bored. Is there more of what matters there than in a single minute of it? I genuinely don't know, and my principle gives me nothing, because summing and refusing to sum both seem okay to me.

- The Stilled Bee. A real bee, same nervous system, but I've cut its motor output so it shows no pain behaviour at all. Still matters to me, I think. And the reverse: a bee-sized robot running a lookup table that produces every bee behaviour including the dance and the writhing, with no feeling anywhere in it. Nothing to me. So behaviour isn't what I'm tracking. But then what am I looking at when I decide the real bee probably feels something?

- The ant colony. Ten thousand ants. Flooding the hill feels nearly fine to me, but not as fine as stepping on one ant. I can't tell whether that's ten thousand tiny amounts adding up, or the colony being a thing in its own right with a bit of status, or just me being squeamish about scale.

So: can you hand me a principle, stated tightly enough that I can work out what it says about a case I haven't listed, that captures most of this? Say which clause is doing which case. And be straight about which of my verdicts it can't keep. I'd much rather drop one or two verdicts than end up with a principle that has a special exception bolted on for every animal. And please tell me what it says about the chatbot case, which is the one I keep actually changing my mind about.
