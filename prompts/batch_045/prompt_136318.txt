I write fiction, and my novel-in-progress turns on a forensic interviewer who has to decide, under time pressure and without a settled criterion, whether a long-running software system counts as a person. I have agreed to give a forty-minute talk at a one-day workshop on artificial welfare, and I want to be ready for the hour afterwards.

The room: two philosophers of mind, one a Chalmers-style structuralist who thinks consciousness tracks fine-grained causal organisation and survives arbitrary changes of substrate, the other a biologically grounded sceptic who thinks it depends on specific neural machinery; a welfare biologist who works on invertebrate sentience and thinks mostly in terms of decision-making under deep uncertainty; a machine-learning interpretability researcher; and a policy adviser who wants an actionable rule before the day ends.

My thesis: because any physical system can be described as implementing arbitrarily many computations, the question which computation does this system implement has no answer until a grain of description is fixed, and no amount of further physics fixes it. I propose that the morally relevant computation is the coarsest one that is counterfactually load-bearing for the system's own continued operation: a computation matters morally when, were it perturbed, the system would not go on regulating itself as it does. Two corollaries I intend to defend: the boundary of the sentient system falls where that self-maintaining loop closes, not at the skull or the server rack; and a substrate whose implementations are massively redundant or degenerate may fail to single out one subject at all.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet in the sections below.

Objections, about 700 words: the six or seven objections this particular room will actually raise, phrased in their vocabulary rather than mine and not the ones I would get from an online rationalist audience, ordered by expected damage, each with a note on who asks it, a two-minute reply, and one sentence on where that reply is weakest.

Concessions up front, about 120 words: what I should give away in the first five minutes, before anyone asks anything, and why conceding it costs me less than being forced into it.

The reply the view lives on, about 120 words: the single response that must land if the position is to survive the session, and the form it should take for this audience.

The question I hope nobody asks, about 150 words: the one objection I am least prepared for, stated as that person would state it, plus what an honest answer would have to admit.

Glossary, about 100 words: the terms of art this audience will use that I am most likely to misuse on the podium, with the sense they will give them.
