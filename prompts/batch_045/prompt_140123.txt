I've been going down a rabbit hole reading Aristotle on money, and it keeps making me stare at how we actually run money now. I want to know what our setup commits us to.

The Aristotle side, as I've got it (do correct me if I'm mangaling it): in the discussion of justice in the Ethics he says money is what makes goods commensurable and so makes exchange possible, but that the real measure underneath is demand or need, and money is only a stand-in for it — a standard "accepted by agreement," which is why it's called nomisma. He also admits the stand-in drifts: "Money, it is true, is liable to the same fluctuation of demand as other commodities, for its purchasing power varies at different times; but it tends to be comparatively constant." And in the Politics, as I remember it, he says lending at interest is the most unnatural kind of wealth-getting, since money was made for exchange and not for breeding more money (something about tokos meaning offspring). I may well have that last bit wrong.

Now what we actually do:

- Prices, wages, contracts and debts are in nominal dollars, and the law is happy: hand over $100 and your $100 debt is discharged, whatever prices did in between.
- But we don't trust it either. Tax brackets get adjusted, benefit payments get cost-of-living adjustments, and governments sell bonds whose principal tracks the consumer price index — and then tax the inflation bit of those bonds as income in the year it accrues, even though the holder recieves no cash until maturity.
- Meanwhile the basis for capital gains isn't indexed at all, so you can owe tax on a "gain" that's purely inflation, and company accounts run on historic cost.
- Most central banks deliberately aim for a couple of percent inflation a year rather than a stable price level. So we let the measure drift on purpose, and then patch the drift with indexation in some places and not others.
- Some places, I gather, run a second unit alongside the currency — an indexed one that mortgages and long-term contracts are written in, while shops still price in the ordinary money.
- And most money these days comes into existence through commercial banks making loans, i.e. exactly the activity he called the most unnatural, with the state standing behind the whole thing.

So: what view of money does this arrangement presuppose? That money is a measure of something real, that it's a pure convention, that it's a creature of law, or nothing much at all beyond inertia? Be careful about what it *strictly* presupposes versus what merely sits comfortably with it — does discharging debts in nominal dollars actually require a conventionalist view of money, or would a bunch of rival theories license it just as well?

Are its parts consistent? We treat nominal amounts as the real thing for debts and accounts, and real (inflation-adjusted) amounts as the real thing for brackets, benefits and indexed bonds. Does that selective indexation betray a commitment that there's a real magnitude — real wages, real returns — measurable independently of money, which sounds close to Aristotle's need being the true measure, except that we measure ours with a price index rather than with anything about need? Is a consumer price index a degenerate descendant of his "demand," or something his picture can't accommodate?

And here's the bit I can't stop chewing on: the index itself gets redefined constantly — reweighted, chain-linked, quality-adjusted, new goods spliced in — so the "real" standard is itself a moving convention. Does that mean the regress bottoms out in convention all the way down, and would that bother Aristotle, or has he already conceded it, since he says things aren't commensurable in the strict sense and demand is a measure good enough for practical purposes?

If we took the Aristotelian picture seriously, what would have to change — index everything, write contracts in a basket unit? And if everything were indexed, wouldn't the unit of account just become the basket, with money shrinking back to a medium of exchange? What happens to interest-bearing, bank-created money on that view, and to the 2 percent target?

One more ask: keep clear what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who know this material — both the Aristotle scholarship (what he means by need as a measure, whether he's condemning all interest or only predatory lending, whether the Ethics and Politics accounts even fit together) and the monetary side. I don't want a contested reading dressed up as the consensus, and I'd rather you told me plainly which bits of the reconstruction are your own take. Also flag it if I've described any part of the modern practice wrong.
