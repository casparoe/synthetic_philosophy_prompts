I've belonged to a small giving circle for about six years — we meet quarterly, compare notes on where our money seems to do the most good, and try not to be insufferable about it — and over that time I've noticed a pattern in the way our discussions go that I can't put a name to. An argument last night with an old friend from university (she reads moral philosophy for fun; I don't) left me more convinced that there is something real here but no closer to naming it. Two cases. First: one of our members gives roughly a third of his income and lives well inside his means, and the group's attention has shifted to smaller things — how his pension is invested, whether he should move from salaried work to earning-to-give, whether his two weeks of leave would do more good spent volunteering. Each of these asks is small, collectively far smaller than what he already does, and yet the thing that seems to have changed the atmosphere in the room is not the money at all: there is now no point at which he is permitted to say enough. Second, and this is what made my friend dig in: compare an aid worker who spends a decade in hard postings at seventy hours a week with someone comfortably off who is required only to keep a running lookout, all day and every day, for the cheapest available improvement — switch a brand, sign a petition, flag a charity — at a total cost of perhaps an hour a week. The second life costs vastly less and is, to me, the more objectionable of the two; it is also the one where morality has more to say about more of your minutes. My friend thinks I have the ordering backwards, which is fair, but I don't think that's where the disagreement actually lives.

The labels she tried, and why I think each misses. She started with the standard demandingness objection: consequentialism asks too much. But my second case is designed so that the less objectionable option is the one that asks more, so if what bothers me were magnitude the cases should come out the other way round. She then said it is just the integrity complaint — that I want room for my own projects and relationships, and consequentialism alienates me from them. That doesn't fit either: plenty of people in my circle have made effective giving and impact-mindedness into their projects and their friendships, and the complaint I have in mind would apply to them just as much; and it isn't about having a life of one's own, since the aid worker's life is entirely absorbed by her work and doesn't bother me in the same way. Her last try was that it is really the disappearance of the supererogatory, everything collapsing into duty. That is closer, but it seems to me to locate the problem in the status of the actions, whereas what I am circling is something about the structure of the requirement over a life: whether a theory ever declares some region of your choices to be none of morality's business, so that within it you may act for any reason at all and leave no moral remainder. A costly duty that you discharge and then clock off from seems to me to leave that remainder-free region intact; a cheap duty that is always live does not.

So my own attempt at the carving is this: costliness (how much a theory may take from you in total) is one dimension, and pervasiveness (how much of your deliberative space it occupies, how many of your choices it makes morally loaded, whether it ever puts you off duty) is another, and they can come apart in both directions. What I want to know is whether this second dimension has been carved out under some description in the literature, and how exactly it relates to the neighbouring things — the demandingness objection proper, the integrity complaint, the loss of the supererogatory, satisficing or scalar versions of consequentialism, and the agent-centred prerogative — with an account, for each, of the precise place where it stops matching my two cases and which fits best. Please don't just hand me a name: I want the shape of the thing, what it is not, and where its edges are. In particular: (i) if there is an established concept here, does it actually discriminate between theories, or do ordinary non-consequentialist views with open-ended duties of beneficence turn out to be pervasive too, which would mean either that I have misdescribed it or that the demandingness debate has been aimed at the wrong target; (ii) is a cheap but pervasive requirement perhaps just costly in a currency I am not counting — attention, deliberation, decision fatigue — in which case my two dimensions collapse back into one, and how would we tell; and (iii) relatedly, a rule with an explicit budget (give this share, then you are off duty, no further questions asked) seems to me both less pervasive and, at least among the people I know, more productive of actual giving, which makes me suspect the complaint may not be about the agent's well-being at all but about the kind of authority the requirement claims. If nothing established fits, coin a term, define it tightly, and give me two or three boundary cases that decide what falls in and what falls out.
