I teach a twelve-person upper-level seminar on Wittgenstein's On Certainty and contemporary hinge epistemology, and I'm writing the take-home final. The last two sessions stalled on a disagreement I couldn't referee: one student insists that the commitments Wittgenstein describes — the ones that stand fast for us, that we don't arrive at by evidence, and that give the game of giving and asking for reasons its footing — are simply empirical beliefs with an unusual degree of centrality; another insists that they are not the sort of thing that can be believed at all, and that asking whether they are true or warranted is already a category mistake. Both students can produce the right vocabulary, so I need a question that cashes the disagreement out rather than restating it. To that end I've written a short unpublished manuscript under the pseudonym M. Sandoval, titled "Hinges Without Privilege," which argues that the distinction collapses: (i) we acquire these commitments from testimony in childhood and abandon them under pressure over generations, which is exactly how we treat revisable empirical beliefs; (ii) the point that doubt requires something that stands fast is just confirmation holism in different words, since on any holistic picture some beliefs are held fixed while others are revised; and (iii) if a commitment can be criticized — as we clearly want to say of the once-widespread certainty that the testimony of women or of enslaved people could not count as evidence in a courtroom — then it was in the space of reasons all along. I also want students to work through a short case: two anthropologists spend twenty years with a community in which it stands fast that certain dreams reveal who will soon die; both eventually come to share that commitment, but one describes what happened to her as revising a belief in light of long experience and the other describes it as being converted. I'd like the exam question to ask what, if anything, distinguishes those two descriptions, and what hinge epistemology is committed to saying about the difference.

Write the question as it will appear on the exam, intended for about 1,200 to 1,500 words and 25 marks, and pitched so that it cannot be answered by reciting the claim that hinges are arational certainties that make doubt possible while being themselves immune to it. The question should set students a trilemma they have to adjudicate rather than describe: that hinge commitments are (a) genuinely distinct from ordinary empirical beliefs, (b) nonetheless criticizable in a way that lets us call some historical hinge change a rational improvement rather than mere replacement, and (c) not merely confirmation holism or foundationalist externalism relabeled. Ask them to argue that the three cannot all be had, pick the horn they would give up, defend that choice against the strongest version of the alternatives, and say plainly what the view then costs — in particular what it can still say to the skeptic, and whether that remaining thing is an answer to skepticism or a refusal to play. Require them to address the acquisition problem, the problem of generational drift, and the problem of bad or ideological hinges, and require them to use the two-anthropologists case as a test of whatever account they land on. I'd rather the question not depend on recalling particular numbered remarks or on citing secondary literature by name, since I'm grading the moves, not the bibliography.

Alongside the question, give me a model answer of roughly 700 words that takes a definite stand rather than surveying the options, and a marking scheme organized around five must-hit points with the marks each is worth (total 25). For each point include a one-line note on the characteristic way students get it wrong — for instance collapsing the distinctness claim into the claim that hinge commitments are merely central, or treating conversion and revision as obviously different without saying what the difference consists in. Finally, add a short paragraph stating what separates an A answer from a B one, naming the specific moves only the A answer makes: I want the top answer to be identifiable by its argumentative structure, not by how much terminology it deploys.
