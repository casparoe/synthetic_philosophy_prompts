I'm writing a paper on the normative status of simplicity in statistical learning theory, and before I commit to a line of argument in print I want an audit of the position I've assembled. Two readers have told me the package is inconsistent; two have told me it is entirely standard. I'd like to know who is right, and about which pairs.

Here is where I stand.

1. The best available justification for Occam's razor is Solomonoff's. The universal mixture prior dominates every computable data-generating measure up to a constant multiplicative factor, and its predictions converge to the true measure's; and since short descriptions carry the most prior weight, preferring the simpler hypothesis is not an aesthetic tic but something that pays.

2. The dependence on the reference machine is not a serious cost. Exchanging one universal machine for another alters Kolmogorov complexities by at most an additive constant — fixed for the pair of machines, independent of the string — and alters the prior by at most a constant multiplicative factor. I also take this to dispose of the Goodman-style worry that any syntactic simplicity ordering is merely relative to a chosen vocabulary: a vocabulary, I want to say, is just a machine, and machines wash out.

3. At the same time, I do not want the razor to rest on a contingent claim about the world. I hold that simplicity's normative force is a priori, or at any rate not hostage to how the world happens to be or to the track record of simple hypotheses so far.

4. What actually does the work for creatures like us is a computable proxy: a truncated or resource-bounded approximation to the universal prior, or an MDL-style procedure over a restricted model class. I want to say these inherit whatever justifies the ideal, on the grounds that they are approximations to it. I do have a nagging worry that cutting the enumeration off at a resource bound should make the choice of encoding matter more, not less, since the constant in (2) is only amortized in the limit — but I cannot make that precise.

5. I do not regard the uncomputability of the ideal as discrediting it. The universal prior is approximable from below, and a normative ideal is allowed to be uncomputable.

6. I also hold that Kolmogorov complexity is the right formalization of the simplicity working scientists invoke when they prefer a curve with fewer free parameters.

7. And here is a worry I cannot shake, which I presently count as evidence against (1): the framework rewards compression, so it looks as if it has nothing to say about noisy or random phenomena — which is most of what science deals with.

Finally, my own proposed repair for the machine-dependence, which I am not confident about and would like stress-tested. Call it the coarse razor: treat simplicity as a partial rather than a total ordering, and count hypothesis A as simpler than hypothesis B only when A's shortest description is shorter than B's by more than the slack the invariance theorem permits — the additive constant measuring how far apart two admissible description languages can be. The hope is that this yields a machine-independent razor, since any ranking it returns survives a change of language.

What I want is an audit, not a survey. Which of (1) through (7) are genuinely in tension with which, which pairs only look as though they are, and for each real tension what would have to be given up and what that revision costs? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equals, and tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Stress-test the coarse razor in particular: is it even well-defined, and does it deliver what I want from it? If one of the items is simply confused, or rests on a misreading of what the theorems establish, say so plainly rather than folding it into the ranking. Where you go beyond what the results establish, mark it as extrapolation, and attach a confidence to each verdict together with your reason for it. I do not need references or a reading list — I have read the standard material; I want the diagnosis. Please answer in American English.
