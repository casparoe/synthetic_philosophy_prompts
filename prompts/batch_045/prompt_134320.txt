I work alongside an investigator in the integrity office of a multilateral development bank, and I am trying to reconstruct the rule she is actually running rather than the one she cites in her reports. Her decisions on six past referrals:

1. A roads contract awarded after the winner paid a retainer to the minister's nephew for a feasibility study that was never produced — corrupt; firm debarred.
2. A water ministry official who accepted cash condolence gifts at his son's funeral from contractors with live bids before his department, and who later approved one donor's variation order — corrupt.
3. A customary chief chairing a village development committee who steered most of a grant's microprojects to his own clan's hamlets, having received customary tribute from clan members — corrupt, even though anthropological evidence was that refusing the tribute would have destroyed his standing; she allowed that local obligation could explain the practice but not excuse it.
4. An unrestricted educational grant from a pharmaceutical company to a national clinicians' association that then issued guidelines favouring the company's drug, with no payment to any official — cleared, on the ground that no officeholder and no office was enriched.
5. A planning commissioner who had been paid counsel to the developer whose project she approved three years earlier; the project ranked first on the published criteria, she had disclosed the history, and the recusal rules did not reach former clients — cleared, with a note that appearances were regrettable.
6. A disclosed, lawful donation to a political party by a firm that later won a competitive tender, with no evidence the tender was distorted — cleared.

The new referral: a national malaria programme's procurement unit holds an annual supplier day funded by the firms holding its contracts; the money pays for the unit's staff training and for a scholarship fund for staff members' children, administered by an outside committee. No individual official receives anything of value, the practice is disclosed in the annual report, it has a long local history under a name that translates roughly as the granary, and there is no evidence that contributing, or failing to contribute, ever affected an award. A losing bidder has referred it.

Will she find corruption? Set out the two or three rules that fit all six precedents, what each predicts here, which you think she is running and why, and the single further case that would best separate the survivors. I would also like your view on what her rule treats as the object of corruption — a transaction, a personal relationship, an office, or a system — since I suspect her two clearances are doing more work than her three findings.
