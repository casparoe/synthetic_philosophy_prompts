I spend most of my working hours on a content-addressed storage system, so the vocabulary I actually think in runs along these lines: objects named by a hash of their contents, deduplication and hash-consing, structural equality set against referential or pointer equality, interning, a canonical serialization that decides when two representations count as one object, and above that a layer of replicated state machines in which several nodes run the same deterministic transition function and we need leaders, quorums, and leases precisely because the nodes are behaviourally identical. I am fluent in that vocabulary and in its characteristic reflex, that an address is either derived from content or handed out from outside the content, and that when two things are structurally identical the live engineering question is whether you are permitted to collapse them. I have no philosophy training beyond a fortnightly reading group, and last month we spent a dispiriting hour on Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles and Max Black's example of a universe containing nothing but two iron spheres of the same diameter, colour, and temperature, at some distance from one another. I came away with two incompatible convictions: that the philosophers were circling something for which my vocabulary has sharp instruments, and that I was probably flattening their dispute into an argument about deduplication. I want to find out which conviction is the foolish one.

So: a systematic translation, and I would rather it be honest than flattering. Restate in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, the following: (a) the principle itself, in the weak form in which it says that necessarily no two objects share all their properties, and in the stronger forms meant to be non-trivial, where the differences that are allowed to count must be differences in how things are rather than in which things they are, the Scholastic tag for the latter being difference solo numero; (b) the two-sphere universe and why it is supposed to refute the principle; (c) the reply that each sphere still has the property of being distinct from the other, or of occupying its own place, together with the retort that such properties are either not genuinely qualitative or else already presuppose the numerical difference at issue; (d) the haecceitist move and the alternative move of taking identity as a primitive that is not grounded in anything qualitative; and (e) the further claims people hang on the dispute, for instance that it bears on whether a thing is a bundle of properties or properties plus something bare that bears them, and that the principle is meant to hold of necessity and not merely as a fact about the world we happen to inhabit. Where a step in the translation requires assuming something my framework would not grant on its own, such as that everything has a canonical description or that every relation is finitely representable, flag it at the point where you need it rather than quietly helping yourself to it.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually want: which claims carried over cleanly, which carried over only with distortion and with what exactly distorted, and which cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all, with your view on each piece of residue as to whether it indicts my vocabulary or shows that the original claim was less determinate than it looked. I also want your verdict on a suspicion of mine, that the debate is in part a disguised argument about whether a world is more like a content-addressed store or more like a store with opaque externally assigned addresses, and that the metaphysical question cannot even be posed until someone settles which, so that the appearance of a substantive disagreement is partly an artifact of the two sides having made different representational choices before the argument began. And I want your verdicts on three variants I have not seen discussed. First, the island-universe version: the two spheres stand in no relations to each other whatsoever, no distance, no common container, two causally disconnected components each holding one sphere; does the case against the principle get stronger, or does it cease to be a case of two things at all? Second, the variant with three or more spheres in ordinary Euclidean space, where some arrangements preserve the symmetry and others, such as an irregular scalene arrangement, restore the relational differences that two spheres cannot have; does the force of the example depend on there being exactly two, and does my vocabulary have anything special to say about why two is the hard number? Third, a description-first world, one whose generating process is such that any two objects with the same complete description are by the rules of world-making the same object, so that the spheres are impossible there by construction and the principle comes out trivially true; does that tell us anything about the principle, or does it merely restate the contested assumption in the setup, and can anything in the original dispute distinguish the spheres are impossible from the spheres are not representable here? I care much less about who is right than about what the translation loses. Please reply as exactly eight numbered items, each no more than three sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion: the principle in my vocabulary; the case in my vocabulary; the replies and the haecceity and primitive-identity moves; what translated cleanly; what translated only with distortion, naming the distortion; what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all; your verdict on the residue, defect of the framework or indeterminacy in the claim; and your verdicts on my suspicion and on the three variants.
