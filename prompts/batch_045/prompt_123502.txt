I'm a functional safety engineer, about fifteen years of it — mostly SIL/PL work on industrial machinery, plus a fair bit of process-side stuff: HAZOPs, FMEAs, fault trees, LOPA, common-cause failure analysis, defence in depth, PRA for the plants where somebody actually wanted numbers. No philosophy background, and I'd never read a word of the AI safety literature until last month, when I sat in on a talk about something called "AI control." Half of it sounded like the guy was describing my job in a language I couldn't recogise. The other half sounded like he was claiming something impossible.

As far as I understood the position: stop trying to make the model *want* the right thing (that's "alignment," which he said might not be solvable, or not in time), and instead treat the model as a component that may be actively trying to defeat your safety measures — untrusted, possibly adversarial, possibly better at this than you are — and then wrap it in protocols that still get you acceptable outcomes. Watch what it does with a weaker but trusted monitor. Route the hard or suspicious cases to the weak trusted thing instead. Red-team the whole protocol before it goes live. Tripwires, honeypots, audits, logging, kill switches. The pitch is that you can get useful work out of a system you don't trust.

So here's what I want. Restate that position, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I actually use: fault vs. failure, common-cause and dependent failure, diverse redundancy, independence claims, defense in depth, safe state / fail-safe, SIL-style reliability targets, proof testing and inspection intervals, safety cases, operator error and human factors. Where would I file each of those measures in a hazard analysis? Which ones are genuine protective layers and which ones are only detection?

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about:

- What translates straight over, no distortion?
- What translates only if I squint? I have a specific suspicion here: in my world "independence" is doing an enormous amount of work — diverse redundancy, beta factors, the whole way we justify that two layers won't fail together — and I suspect it simply does not hold in this setting, because every component is made of the same pretrained soup and the adversary gets to read the layers. Tell me if that's right and if there's more in that category.
- What has no counterpart at all in my vocabulary? My guess is the idea of a component that has goals, has a model of the safety system, and reshapes its failure mode in response to the defences, instead of failing at random or wearing out. But tell me if I'm wrong about that, and what else doesn't fit.
- And the other direction: what do I have that they seem to lack? I'm thinking quantified reliability targets, certification, and the fact that in my world you're allowed to *demand proof* for the parts you call trusted.

Two things I got stuck on and would like your read:

1. My manager wants a number out of this: probability of catastrophic sabotage per deployment hour, with some target like 10^-6. I don't think that quantity is even well defined here — no base rate, near-single-shot deployment, an adversary that changes strategy in response to the measures, no ergodicity to lean on. Is that right, or is it merely very hard rather than incoherent? If it's incoherent, what do they put in its place — bounding arguments, worst cases, conservative assumptions?

2. A colleague said afterwards that this is just Byzantine fault tolerance with n equals 1 and no specification. I half agree and half think he's being glib. If you use that as your translation, say exactly where it breaks.

Last thing: don't flatter my framework and don't flatter theirs. If some of the residue is there because my vocabulary is missing something real, say so. And if some of it is there because the AI control position is vaguer than it sounds, or is quietly assuming something it hasn't earned — like that we'll know sabotage when we see it, or that the trusted parts really are trusted — say that too.
