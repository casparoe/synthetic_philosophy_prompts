I am applying for a twelve-month dissertation fellowship in philosophy of mind, and I need a proposal of roughly two pages (900 to 1,100 words) for a project on whether an ant colony could have a mind of its own. Please write it in American English, in the register of a serious funding application: precise, sober, and free of rhetorical padding.

Here is the situation the project starts from. The debate in this area keeps arriving at the same impasse. Writers friendly to the superorganism picture point out that a colony carries information about its environment, corrects its own errors, weighs options, and settles on courses of action that no individual ant represents or chooses; the skeptical reply is that none of this shows that there is anything it is like to be the colony, or that the colony is a subject of experience rather than an arena in which thousands of subjects interact. The two sides then talk past one another, because the disputed property — unified subjectivity — is precisely the property for which we have no non-question-begging test. Aunt Hillary is either a person or a metaphor, and nothing in the standard exchange tells us which.

My half-formed idea is that progress requires giving up the direct question and looking instead for structural properties that could stand in for unified subjectivity, then asking whether a colony has them. Three candidates, which I want the proposal to sharpen, or discard in favor of something better:

One, temporal grain. The colony-level states that would have to be its thoughts or decisions turn over on a scale of minutes or hours, not milliseconds. Our own conscious episodes seem to require integration inside a narrow temporal window, and successive experiences must overlap if they are to form a stream at all. Does that impose an upper bound on how coarse-grained a subject can be, and is a colony on the wrong side of it? If it is, do we conclude that no colony has a mind, or that a mind could in principle have one experience per hour — and in the latter case is there anything substantive left to say about what such a mind would be like, or does the question dissolve once the grain is stretched that far?

Two, the bottleneck condition. Whatever else a conscious subject is, its processing appears to have a point of convergence: information from any part of the system can make a difference to what the system does next, and access to that channel is scarce and competitive. Much of colony behavior looks like the denial of this. Recruitment to food by pheromone trails is stigmergic — no center, no competition for a channel, the trail itself doing the work of memory. But not all of it. In the rock ant Temnothorax albipennis, an experienced forager leads a naive nestmate to a newly discovered resource in a tandem run, the leader slowing when the follower lags and speeding when it draws too close: information passes through a narrow, sequential, adjustable channel, and the colony's shift to a new course of action appears to depend on a small subset of informed individuals. If the bottleneck condition is the right proxy, the likely upshot is that a colony has a mind with respect to some task domains and not others. I want the proposal to take that upshot seriously rather than recoil from it: explain what it could mean for a system to be a subject relative to a domain, and say whether that notion can be reconciled with the unity we take ourselves to enjoy.

Three, fission and fusion as a stress test rather than as evidence. Introduced Argentine ants form supercolonies in which workers from nests hundreds of miles apart do not attack one another, and colonies also split and bud off new ones. If minds belonged to colonies, these would be cases of two minds becoming one and one becoming two. I do not think we have independent intuitions about such cases worth trusting, so I want them used to test whether a proposed criterion delivers verdicts that can be stated without absurdity, and to locate the points at which it falls silent.

What the proposal must contain: a precise statement of the question, including an up-front specification of which notion of mind is at issue — intentionality and agency, phenomenal consciousness, or selfhood — together with an argument that the project is about one of these rather than sliding among them; a short account of why the standard positions, and the usual functionalist-versus-skeptic exchange, leave that question open; the approach, laid out month by month across twelve months, with a justification of why that approach rather than the obvious alternatives, such as reading more myrmecology and waiting for the biology to settle it, or subsuming the case under a general theory of consciousness and stopping there; the range of possible conclusions and the considerations that would decide among them; an explicit statement of what would count as the project having failed, including the real possibility that all three proxies turn out either to be independent of the question or to be unusable until the general problem of consciousness is solved first; a brief section listing the empirical claims the argument depends on and how the verdict would shift if any of them turned out false or overstated; and a paragraph addressed to a referee who holds that the project is either empirical entomology wearing a philosophical hat or a merely verbal dispute, and is therefore not philosophy at all.

Do not include a bibliography or a literature review by citation. Name the positions and arguments, and I will supply the references myself. Where the proposal relies on a biological claim, state the claim in the text so that it can be assessed on its own terms.
