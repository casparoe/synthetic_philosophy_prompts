I moderate a large general-purpose discussion forum, and one of our recurring problems is telling the difference between a poster who genuinely wants to understand why we accept something well established — vaccine safety, the age of the earth, the result of an election — and a poster who is running a denialist script, where no amount of evidence will be engaged with and the honest response may be no response at all. A philosophy podcast I listened to recently sold Wittgenstein's On Certainty as exactly the tool for this, summarizing the book as follows:

Wittgenstein's target was Cartesian skepticism, and his argument is that doubt is only possible against a background of things you do not doubt. You cannot doubt everything at once, because raising a doubt requires relying on other beliefs as reasons. So inquiry must rest on a foundation of indubitable certainties — Wittgenstein's hinges — which we know for certain but not on the basis of evidence, since there is nothing more basic to support them by. Moore's claim to know he has hands is one; so is the claim that the earth existed long before any of us did. These hinges are not really propositions at all: they function as rules of inquiry, the scaffolding against which any particular claim gets checked, and for that reason they cannot be tested and cannot be given up. The result is a new kind of foundationalism, and one that finally refutes the skeptic: radical doubt is not merely unreasonable but incoherent, because any doubt the skeptic raises already presupposes the very framework it attacks. And since hinges are the shared framework of any inquiry whatsoever, someone who denies one is not making a claim that could be true or false; they have stepped outside the space of reasons, which is why arguing with deniers is pointless.

The "finally refutes the skeptic" line struck me as too strong, but the conclusion about deniers is the part I would most like to be able to use. So I would like an accuracy audit: which sentences would someone working on On Certainty accept, which are standard simplifications a specialist would wince at but concede, and which are substantive misreadings — and please distinguish that last category from points where the secondary literature is genuinely divided, so I know which parts are contested rather than simply wrong. Then tell me what survives correction: does the hinge notion actually do any work for the moderation question, or does that practical conclusion rest on precisely the claims that don't survive? If hinges can be local to a form of life and can shift over time, is there any usable test for whether a given claim has hinge status for a given speaker — or is that exactly the question the theory says cannot be settled from the inside?
