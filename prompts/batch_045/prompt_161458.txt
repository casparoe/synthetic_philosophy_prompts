I want to work out what I actually think about population axiology, proceeding from my own verdicts on cases rather than from labels, and I would like you to test, repair, or replace the rough principle I have been carrying around. Please reason from the cases themselves; I am not asking for a survey of positions or for citations.

Set-up. Welfare is measured on a fixed interpersonal scale: 0 is the level at which a life is neither worth living nor worth not living, +100 is about as good as a human life could be, and negative values are lives that are bad for the person living them. Assume the scale is cardinal and interpersonally comparable, that welfare can be distributed across any number of people, that we can create or fail to create people at will, and that there is no uncertainty except where I introduce it. Assume also that identities do not matter: ten people at +100 is exactly as good as ten different people at +100. Please keep two relations separate throughout: the ranking of outcomes as better or worse, and the question of what there is reason to bring about. I note below a case where they seem to come apart for me.

My verdicts.

1. Adding a flourishing life. A population of ten, each at +100, versus the same ten plus an eleventh person, also at +100, no one else affected. Verdict: the eleven-person outcome is better. But I feel no requirement to bring the eleventh person into existence; either outcome seems permissible, and this gap does not trouble me.

2. Adding a barely worth living life. The same ten at +100, versus the same ten plus an eleventh at +2. Verdict: not better. I lean toward its being very slightly worse, though I am more confident that it is not better than that it is worse.

3. A superb life containing a bad stretch. Compare adding an eleventh person whose net lifetime welfare is +120, higher than anyone else's, but of which a three-year stretch contributes −40, with the rest of the life more than compensating for it, against adding an eleventh person with a flat, uneventful life at +8. Verdict: I would rather add the flat +8 life, and I am not certain I would add the superb one at all. Nothing here turns on the superb person's own retrospective attitude; stipulate that she is glad to have lived.

4. Imperceptible losses spread over many; the case I keep returning to. There are eight billion people, each at +50. Option (a): leave things as they are. Option (b): add one person at +90, which lowers each of the existing eight billion by 0.0001, a difference no one could notice, mind, regret, or report. Aggregate welfare is lower in (b) by roughly eight hundred thousand. Verdict: I go back and forth. One part of me says (b) is obviously acceptable, since the gain is a whole life and the losses are not losses anyone could be said to undergo. Another part says they are losses nonetheless and they add up.

5. The repugnant comparison. Ten people at +100 versus two hundred thousand people at +3. Verdict: the second is not better; I think the first is better. Total welfare is 1,000 against 600,000.

6. Equal totals, more people. Ten at +100 versus twenty at +50, total welfare 1,000 either way. Verdict: the first is better. Multiplying persons at a fixed total accomplishes nothing good; it reads to me as dilution.

7. The small step. Ten at +100 versus eleven at +95: total 1,000 against 1,045, average 100 against 95. Verdict: the second is better, or at worst not worse. I have noticed that iterating this judgement, adding lives slightly below the current average so that the total rises and the average falls a little each time, eventually delivers something like Case 5, which I deny. I do not know which step to refuse.

8. Fission. Suppose a technique lets us replace any one person's life with two numerically distinct lives, each exactly half as good, using the same total resources and containing between them the same total welfare: one life at +100 becomes two lives at +50. Verdict: worse. I am also uneasy about the reverse, that merging two people at +50 into one at +100 would be an improvement, and I cannot say what my resistance consists in.

9. Concentration by not creating. Nine people exist either way. Option (a): those nine at +100 plus a tenth person at +100. Option (b): just those nine, at +110 each, because the resources the tenth would have used are redistributed. Verdict: (b) is not better; I am inclined to say (a) is better, or at least that there is no reason to prefer (b). I am aware this sits badly with Cases 6 and 8.

10. Risk over existence. Option (a): a fifty-fifty chance between ten people at +100 and a world in which no one exists at all. Option (b): certainty of ten people at +60, the same ten. Verdict: I prefer (b), although I cannot point to anyone who is harmed in the losing branch of (a), and I am unsure what number a world with no one in it should contribute to any expected-value calculation.

11. Risk about quality. Adding an eleventh person whose life will be either +80 with probability 0.6 or −20 with probability 0.4, expected value +40. Verdict: I lean against adding. This suggests I do not treat expected welfare as the quantity to be summed when what is at stake is whether a life goes badly.

The principle I have been working with. Score each possible person by taking their lifetime welfare and subtracting a positive constant, a threshold fixed well above the neutral point, so that lives near the bottom of the positive range count for less than nothing; weight any stretch of a life that falls below the neutral point more heavily than an equally large stretch above it; sum these scores across everyone who would exist; and rank outcomes by that sum, treating the ranking as answering both what is better and what to do.

I can already see where this strains. It appears to get Cases 1, 2, 5, 6, 7 and 8 roughly where I want them, but it tells me that option (b) of Case 9 is better, which I deny; it does not determine whether the threshold and the negative weighting apply to whole lives or to stretches of lives, and Case 3 turns on exactly that; and it is silent about Case 10 and about the gap I recorded in Case 1. I also suspect that saving Case 3 would require something stronger than a weighting factor, perhaps a lexical condition, and I want to know whether that is a cost I should accept.

What I would like from you.

1. One or two candidate principles, stated precisely enough that I could apply them to a case you have not seen. After each clause, name the case that motivates it and the case it strains against.
2. A table with a row for each of my eleven cases, giving my verdict, your principle's verdict, and whether the two match.
3. An honest statement of which of my verdicts no principle you can construct accommodates, which single verdict you would overturn, and why that one.
4. On the tension between Cases 7 and 5, lay out the available options: refuse some particular step and say where the boundary falls and what justifies putting it there; give up transitivity of better than; stop tracking betterness as such and work with a choice function over options instead; or accept Case 5. Recommend one and state its cost.
5. On Case 9 against Cases 6 and 8, say whether my asymmetry between creating and not creating is defensible, or whether I must either accept that nine at +110 is better or abandon my verdicts about dilution.
6. Say whether the principle should operate on whole lives or on stretches of lives, and on expected welfare or on something risk-sensitive, and what your answer commits me to in Cases 3 and 11.
7. Verdicts on four cases I have not settled, with a note on whether each verdict is stable under small perturbations of the numbers: (a) ten at +100 versus ten trillion at +98; (b) a hundred people each living four hundred years at +100 versus five hundred people each living eighty years at +100, equal total life-years at the same level but very different numbers of persons; (c) eight billion people at +50 sustained generation after generation for ten thousand generations, versus a painless end to the human population after three generations in which everyone who ever lives is at +50; (d) eight billion at +50 versus eight billion at +49.5 plus a billion more at +40, so that total welfare is higher, average welfare lower, and everyone who exists in both outcomes is slightly worse off.
8. Tell me whether any part of my discomfort in Cases 4, 9 and 10 is verbal rather than substantive, and if so which part and why.
