Please don't jump straight to a verdict: start by brainstorming the space of principles that could generate the pattern you're about to see, and only then narrow down. I'd much rather have careful, hedged reasoning than a confident answer, and do tell me if you think I've framed the options wrong.

Some context: I'm a philosophy professor, I teach an undergrad seminar on the ethics of communication, and I've been slowly building a case set for it. A close colleague in the department (call her D.) has been my unofficial lab rat for years — I run cases past her at coffee and she gives crisp verdicts: lie or merely misleading, and how it ranks morally against the straight-out lie. We were arguing about all this again after last week's department meeting (the econ guy who sits in on the occasional session thinks the whole lie/mislead distinction is a lawyer's dodge, which I definatly don't). Here's her track record, recorded as carefully as I could manage:

(a) A doctor tells a patient your scan came back clean, believing it shows a tumor, intending to reassure. — Lie; the paradigm of what's wrong with lying.
(b) Murderer at the door. She says, I won't tell you where he is. But I'll say this much: I saw him at the bank at nine this morning — knowing he'll infer Ralph is there now and burn the afternoon, which is the whole point. — Not a lie; but morally no better than the lie.
(c) A landlord knows the boiler won't be fixed before winter; the tenant asks her directly; she says nothing and changes the subject, knowing he'll read it as yes. — Not a lie, and noticeably less bad than an outright lie, because she left him room to press.
(d) Same setup, except that instead of saying anything she just has Ralph's car moved and the note on the door changed, so that the inference does all the work. — Not a lie (no words involved), and no better than a lie.
(e) A true statement that a hearer misreads through his own delusional framework: she tells a jealous colleague, yes, I had lunch with her on Tuesday, and lets him take it as an admission of an affair, when there's nothing to it. — Not a lie, and not wrong at all; his misreading is his problem.
(f) A hostage on a coerced video says, I'm being treated well and I support the invasion. Nobody believes a word of it, nobody is deceived. — Still a lie, yes; but the wrong here is all in the coercion, and she wouldn't blame him for it.
(g) She says the package is in the safe, sincerely believing that it is, but someone removed it that morning. — Not a lie; she merely said something false.

Here's the new case, which I honestly can't predict her on. Our chair, in an open meeting with faculty and undergrads in the same room, is asked by a first-year whether the language requirement will change before his cohort graduates. She answers, in one sentence: There are no proposals to change the requirement before the committee this year. That is literally true and she believes it: nothing is formally before the committee. But she also knows the dean has privately decided to impose the change administratively, bypassing the committee, in the spring; she's been told not to announce it yet; and she wants the students to stop panicking and not transfer out. The faculty in the room know about the dean's plan (it leaked months ago) and will hear the sentence as the technically true non-denial that it is. Same sentence, two uptakes, both of them foreseen and both of them wanted. D. has never ruled on a case with a split audience before, so the track record doesn't settle it, and I keep going back and forth myself on whether lying is even the kind of thing that can be true relative to one hearer and not another.
