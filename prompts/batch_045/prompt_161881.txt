I want to practise attacking the leeway view, so I need an opponent who can actually defend it. Please play a committed defender of the principle of alternative possibilities: you hold that an agent is open to blame for what she does only if she could have done otherwise, and you hold that Frankfurt-style cases do not dislodge that. You know the standard resources — flicker strategies, the objection that the prior sign must be either deterministic or not, the actual-sequence cases — and you may concede incidental points, but never the main one. Reply in a single paragraph, in British English, stay in character until I say "stop", and if some detail of my case matters, pick the reading least convenient for you and say which you picked rather than asking me.

My opening move. Here is a Frankfurt case with a merely probabilistic intervener. Black wants Jones to embezzle the funds. Black cannot read Jones's decision directly, but he watches for a prior sign; I will grant you whatever you like about the sign — indeterministically produced, causally insufficient for Jones's refraining — because the probability I care about lies in Black himself: he is vigilant but fallible, noticing the sign and intervening with certainty ninety-nine times in a hundred, while one time in a hundred he is distracted and does nothing, and when he does intervene his intervention is fully determining. In fact Jones embezzles on his own, for his own reasons, and Black never moves. Still, Jones's chance of refraining was one in a hundred.

Now the squeeze. If "could have done otherwise" requires only some non-zero objective chance, then Jones could have refrained — and since no physically realised intervener can be perfectly reliable, no Frankfurt case built out of a real intervener ever removes alternative possibilities, so the principle survives every case anyone can construct, which looks less like a defence of it than like a proof that it does no work. If "could" requires a robust or significant chance, then blame becomes a function of how reliable an intervener who did nothing happened to be, and the difference between Jones-with-a-ninety-nine-per-cent-Black and Jones-with-a-sixty-per-cent-Black is sheer luck. And if you reach for a third reading on which "could" names the agent's own two-way ability rather than any chance, then tell me what an ability is such that it is removed by someone who never does anything. Which horn do you take?
