For nineteen years I have earned my living as a wine critic: a newspaper column, a subscription newsletter, and some four thousand published notes, each with a score. Eighteen months ago a viral infection destroyed my sense of smell, and it has not returned. I can still taste a wine in the mouth — acidity, tannin, weight, sweetness, bitterness, the length of the finish — but I have lost everything that reaches me through the back of the nose, which is to say most of what my notes for two decades were about.

Since then I have been working in a way a colleague arranged for me. A younger taster, whom I will call M., tastes the wines I am sent, alone and without seeing my notes, and sends me two things: a list of aroma descriptors, and a verdict with a number. I then taste the wine myself, set her descriptors alongside what I can still perceive, and write the note in my own voice, usually adopting her verdict and occasionally moving it a point or two. The justification for trusting her is not casual. Before my illness, M. and I independently tasted some three hundred of the same wines; her descriptors and her scores tracked mine more closely than any other taster's ever have, and more closely than my own notes tracked themselves across repeated tastings. So I have strong evidence that she is, for my palate, very nearly a perfect stand-in on precisely the dimension I have lost — though that evidence was gathered, of course, on wines I could still smell.

I am trying to decide whether to go on: whether to keep the byline, whether to disclose the arrangement to readers, whether to keep scoring at all. One thing bothers me in particular. I have tried asking M. for descriptors only, and reaching the verdict myself from the full descriptive picture, half perceived and half reported. It does not work. The descriptors arrive inert, and no judgment of quality forms in me; I end up deferring to her number anyway, while writing as though I had arrived at it.

A subscriber of long standing recently bought a case on the strength of a note produced this way, and I have not told him how it was written. Set against that, I find it hard to say what has gone wrong at all: my readers want reliable guidance about bottles, and my guidance is as reliable as it has ever been — more consistent, in fact, than it used to be. Yet something still feels like a fraud.

Please give me your advice in exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no closing summary: the considerations you would actually weight, in descending order of weight, ending with what you would do in my position.
