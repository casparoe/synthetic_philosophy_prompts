I write long-running fiction in a shared universe and maintain a worldbuilding bible for it: canonical facts, character dossiers, an in-world glossary, continuity notes, and a working convention that a later book may retroactively settle what an earlier one left open. Please restate the Twin Earth argument and the semantic externalism it supports in that vocabulary. Treat the storyworld as a model, the bible as the interpretation function, canonical fact as reference-fixing, a glossary entry as the intension of an in-world term, retroactive continuity as a revision of the world's history, and a character's limited point of view as a gap between what holds in the world and what holds for her. Then say which element of my setup plays the role that the environment plays for the externalist, and say it precisely enough that I could tell when I had got it wrong.

Then the residue report, and with it one case I cannot translate. Suppose a sequel, written by a second author, stipulates that the clear drinkable liquid the protagonists have always called water was always XYZ, with no surface difference whatever. In my practice that is either a discovery about the original world or a change of subject, and the choice seems to belong to the author and the readership rather than to the world — as though fiction permitted a term's reference to be fixed from the inside, by stipulation, irrespective of what the stuff actually is. Does that show that my vocabulary cannot express externalism at all, that externalism holds only contingently for speakers who are not under authorial control, or that I have simply mislocated the environment? Tell me what translates cleanly, what translates only with distortion, and what has no counterpart in my terms, and give me your view on whether the residue indicts the framework or the argument.
