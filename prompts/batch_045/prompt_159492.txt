I'm a statistician — most of my working life is hierarchical and multilevel models, model comparison by marginal likelihood and Bayes factors, posterior predictive checks, and causal inference with selection bias and missing-not-at-random data — and a friend in a philosophy department has been trying to explain to me why the fine-tuning argument for a designer is supposed to move anyone. I have no training in philosophy of religion, and every time she puts it in terms of 'explanation', 'surprise', and 'the probability that the constants of physics fall in the life-permitting range', I can't tell how much of that my own vocabulary can actually carry. So I'd like you to do the translation for me, and, more than that, to tell me what it fails to carry. Please write in American English.

Concretely: restate the whole dialectic as an exercise in Bayesian model comparison between fully specified generative models. Write out, in my terms, (i) a single-universe 'chance' model in which the constants are one draw from some distribution over a parameter space, so that the apparent tuning is a claim about the measure placed on that space and hence a prior-sensitivity problem — the analogue of a Bertrand paradox, where uniform in the constant, uniform in its logarithm, and a Jeffreys prior all give different answers, and where an unbounded space makes the prior improper and the 'probability of life-permittingness' not merely small but undefined; (ii) a multiverse model, which I take to be a mixture or random-effects structure with an unknown number of components and unknown population-level hyperparameters, estimated from what is in effect a single observation — say plainly how much one can learn about a heterogeneity parameter from one draw, and whether this model is penalized as an unidentifiable, everything-compatible hypothesis or rewarded as the natural way to absorb a selection effect; and (iii) a design model, in which the constants are the output of an agent's optimization rather than a draw, and say what the likelihood of such a model even is — whether we are looking at an implicit or intractable likelihood, a model whose only constraint is that it puts mass on what we in fact observe, and what the Occam factor does to it. Then translate two moves my friend makes that I can't get a grip on. Call the first the Conditionalist Reply: the claim that the honest comparison is not between the probability of life-permitting constants under theism and under chance, but between two models that each include the observation process — that is, the fact that constants are only ever observed by observers who exist inside the universe those constants produce — so that the selection step must be written into both likelihoods, and that once it is, the design model needs a selection component of its own (a story about which universe the designer actualizes) that costs it at least as much as it costs the chance model. Call the second the No-New-Evidence Corollary: the claim that we already knew we exist, so conditioning on our existence gives likelihood one under every hypothesis compatible with it, and the argument can only get any traction by retreating from the bare fact of existence to the precise measured values of the constants — a smaller evidential base than the rhetoric suggests. Tell me whether my toolkit has real names for these: whether the Conditionalist Reply is just the standard discipline of including the sampling and censoring mechanism in the model rather than conditioning on a post-treatment variable, and whether the Corollary is the same pathology as reusing a dataset that is already in the background information, or as doing selective inference with a test statistic chosen after looking at the data.

Finally, and this is the part I most want: give me the residue report. Which components of the argument map cleanly onto priors, likelihoods, Bayes factors, identifiability, and selection mechanisms? Which survive only under distortion — where you had to quietly add an assumption that isn't in the original, such as a measure over possible constants, a reference class of observers, or a utility function for the designer? And which have no counterpart at all in my vocabulary: the thought that the constants might have been otherwise in some sense other than being drawn from a distribution, the demand for an explanation of why there is a lawful structure at all rather than of why its parameters take the values they do, the move from 'an agent chose these values' to anything else about the agent, and the indexical awkwardness that I am not a random sample of anything and yet am being asked to treat myself as one. For each item of residue, say whether it indicts my framework — something statistics genuinely cannot express and so cannot adjudicate — or whether it reveals the original claim to be less determinate than it sounded. Close with a committed verdict in one paragraph: as a piece of model comparison, does the fine-tuning argument have a defined and non-negligible Bayes factor, does its force depend entirely on stipulations the argument itself does not supply, or is it in some third category — and which, if any, of the three models would you yourself put the most posterior weight on, and why.
