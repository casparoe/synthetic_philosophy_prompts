I build and maintain an open-source platform for running online deliberation — moderated small-group discussion, argument mapping, structured summaries — and over the last three years it has been used by a couple of city councils, a transit authority, and one state agency. I am an engineer by training with no formal background in political theory or philosophy; I got pulled into all of this after losing a fight over a zoning ordinance in my own neighborhood, where I came away convinced that the problem was not that the council members were bad people but that they were being selected by a mechanism that rewarded them for performing certainty in public. Next month I have forty minutes in front of this city's charter review commission to make the case for adding a randomly selected chamber — thirty-five residents, drawn by lot, serving staggered one-year terms — to the city's legislative process. The commission is eleven people: a retired appellate judge who will care about due process and about whether any of this is even legal under state law, a political theorist from the local university who is sympathetic to sortition in the abstract but is, I am told, deeply worried about who controls the questions a body like this gets asked, the city clerk who actually runs elections and will immediately start computing cost and response rates, two neighborhood association presidents who are convinced this is a scheme to take power away from the people who show up to meetings, a union staffer who thinks procurement and labor questions should not be decided by amateurs, a statistics professor who will ask what a sample of thirty-five can possibly be representative of, and three at-large members whose views I have not been able to determine.

The version I am defending is deliberately narrow, and I would like the prep sheet written for this version rather than for sortition in general. Three claims, and one design that follows from them. First: the real argument for sortition is not mirror-representation but what I have started calling incentive-proofness — because selection is random, no one can improve their odds of being selected by pandering, fundraising, or performing conviction, so the body has no constituency to play to, and that is precisely the property that elections systematically destroy. Second: that property is worth the most in retrospective judgment, so my chamber would have no drafting power and no agenda power at all — only a suspensive veto over ordinances in two categories, land use and police accountability, fixed in the charter years in advance, plus a power to force reconsideration of anything the council passed in the previous twelve months. Third, and this is the part I am least sure of: the agenda-capture problem is best answered not by experts or by professional staff but by a second, larger lottery — a separate panel of five hundred drawn at the same time from the same frame, any forty of whom can by majority vote put an item on the chamber's docket. There is also a technical piece, because I want the draw to be publicly verifiable: publish a hash of the sampling frame, the stratification variables, and the weighting algorithm before the draw, then reveal all of it once the panel is seated. My worry is that the moment you publish the weights in advance you make the frame gameable, since people can move, re-register, or time a filing, and that commit-and-reveal only converts live verification into after-the-fact trust in whoever did the committing.

What I want is a Q&A prep sheet for the twenty minutes after the talk: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this particular room, ordered by how much damage each would do, each phrased the way that person would phrase it rather than the way a political theorist would — the judge's version of the consent objection is not the union staffer's version, and I need to hear both. For each one: what I should concede on the spot, what I should hold, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Please flag separately the objections that land on my design specifics — the veto-only powers, the sortition-gated agenda, commit-and-reveal, the two-category limit — as opposed to the ones that would sink any sortition proposal, because I am willing to trade away design details live in the room and I need to know which ones are load-bearing. Tell me which single reply has to land for the proposal to survive at all, and which question I should be hoping nobody asks. I know the generic objections to lottocracy — competence, the loss of electoral accountability, the indignity of being governed by amateurs — so please skip those unless this room would put them in a distinctive form, and please translate the theoretical worries into claims I can defend from my own experience running these processes, since I am not going to be able to name-check the literature and would not want to.
