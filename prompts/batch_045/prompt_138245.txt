I'm putting together a first-year seminar on philosophy of biology, and the week on genetic information is always the one where the students either find the whole dispute verbal or can't see what was at stake in the first place. So I want to screen Andrew Niccol's Gattaca and use it as a case rather than as a decoration. I'd like a reading of the film through the question of whether DNA is literally a code: whether talk of genetic information is a genuine semantic notion, or merely causal-cum-teleological information of a sort that supports no reading-off of a message, or a metaphor we project onto molecules mostly because we ourselves drew up the decoding table and the cell happens to contain machinery that implements it. Read the film in both directions: use the debate to make sense of the world the film builds and the scenes it keeps returning to, and then say what the film shows about the debate that the arguments alone don't.

The specific distinction I want put under pressure is one I've been using in my notes, and I'd like to know whether the film bears it out or makes it look strained. Call it the difference between a record and a script: a record is read off a system and can be more or less accurate, while a script is addressed to someone and carries the standing to say what that person is to do. In the film, genotyping looks officially actuarial — probabilities of this or that disorder, an estimated lifespan, a risk of heart failure that quietly disqualifies a woman who has otherwise passed every test — yet institutionally it functions as a script, and much of the plot is the effort of a bureaucracy to keep the two collapsed. Two connected questions follow. First, code talk seems to want both an author and a reader: in the cell there is no author, and the decoding machinery is itself a product of the same evolutionary process that produced the sequences it reads, so on the deflationary picture nothing is written and nothing is being told. Gattaca's society seems to supply exactly the missing author and the authoritative reader: parents who place an order, clinics that certify, employers who scan at the door, a registry that decides whose letters count. If that is right, the film's real target is not the metaphysics of the code metaphor but the authority of whoever does the reading, and I'd like that claim tested rather than assumed. Second, consider the borrowed identity itself: Vincent passes because sequence, unlike a heart or a pair of lungs, is detachable — it travels in vials, it can be carried on the body of someone who does not have it, and identity becomes a matter of whose letters are in the archive. Is the plot therefore depending on precisely the feature that makes the code conception apt in the first place, the substrate-independence and multiple realizability of information, and if so does that cut against genetic essentialism rather than for it? It would be a nice irony if the code metaphor has to be literally true for the fraud to work, and if the fraud is what the film celebrates.

I'd also like the reading to be honest about where the film resists it. Its ending seems to disavow the determinism its premise requires — a man with the superior profile kills himself in his own incinerator wearing the medal he never felt he earned, the invalid brother wins the swimming contests and explains that he never kept anything in reserve for the way back, and the doctor who lets him through does so because his own selected son is not what was promised — yet Vincent only reaches the launch because someone falsifies a test, and the heart defect that opened the film is never overcome, only hidden. Does the film take a position there, or is it simply having determinism both ways for the sake of the drama? Which scenes would you show a room of first-years to make each of these points, and where would you expect a philosopher of biology to say the film misleads them about what genomes can actually predict? Finally, a colleague of mine insists that the film is not about genetic information at all: replace the genotype with a credit score or an entrance examination and you get the same moral about statistical discrimination and about people internalizing a verdict. I can't shake the objection, and I'd like to know what you think is irreducibly genetic in what the film is doing — that the readout exists at conception, before there is any agency of the subject's at all, that it is shared with relatives, that it is taken to reveal who someone really is underneath — or whether my colleague is right that the philosophy of biology is only set dressing for a familiar story about bureaucracy.
