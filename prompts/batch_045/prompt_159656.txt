Room is about twelve people: half of them write Rust or Python for a living, three have seen first-order logic properly (one took model theory in grad school and will be visibly bored if I do the standard version), and the rest are general LessWrong-adjacent readers who know Cantor's diagonal argument and roughly nothing else. No second-order logic in the room, no forcing, no Gödel numbering. I'm not going to prove Löwenheim–Skolem, but I do want to sketch the Skolem-hull move — start from a countable seed and close it under witnesses for existential formulas, countably many formulas so countably many witnesses — becuase that's the one piece of machinery that makes the result feel inevitable instead of like a magic trick.

I already know the resolution, so please don't explain the paradox to me; I need the plan for explaining it. My candidate for the carrying example is something I've been using in conversation and can't tell whether it's load-bearing or just cute: a simulation running on a laptop in the basement. From outside, every real in there is finitely many bits, so the whole thing is countable, but the inhabitants run Cantor's argument internally and correctly prove their reals are uncountable, since the enumeration lives outside and no function-object inside the simulation does the job. I call it 'the missing audit': the bijection is an audit of the structure, and an audit can't be filed inside the thing it audits. I'm worried it smuggles the wrong moral — people will conclude 'so our reals might be countable too' — which is more or less where I want to end up anyway via the Putnam-style move about our own quantifiers maybe ranging over a countable domain, but I want that to land as a question I've earned rather than a cheap gotcha, and I really don't know whether to raise it before or after the resolution.

Give me the session as exactly five numbered beats, one or two sentences each, no intro and no outro: each beat should say what has to land in it and which misconception gets killed at exactly that point. The ones I'm expecting, roughly in the order they'll form: 'so ZFC is inconsistent', 'the model is missing subsets so it's defective', 'the model is deluded about its own size', treating 'countable' as absolute, and the audit analogy mutating into 'we're probably in a countable model'. In whichever beat the example enters, tell me whether my laptop thing should carry the whole session or get replaced, and what an example has to have and lack to do this job.
