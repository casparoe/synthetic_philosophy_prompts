I am a third-year undergraduate taking a sets, measure and philosophy of mathematics option, and last week I gave a short seminar presentation on how much of standard mathematics depends on the axiom of choice. A member of the audience produced what looked like a five-line refutation of choice itself, and for four days I have been unable to locate where it goes wrong. I would be grateful for a careful diagnosis. Please answer in British English.

The set-up. Let a hat sequence be an infinite binary sequence, and let eventual equality be the relation that holds between two such sequences exactly when they differ in at most finitely many coordinates. This partitions the space of all sequences into classes, each of them countable. Call a selector for this relation a function that assigns to each sequence one representative of its own class, assigning the same representative to sequences that are eventually equal. The axiom of choice delivers such a selector, since it is just a choice function for the family of classes.

The argument, as I reconstructed it afterwards:

(1) By choice, fix a selector r for eventual equality.
(2) Consider countably many prisoners, indexed by the natural numbers, each wearing a black or a white hat, with hat sequence x. Prisoner n sees every hat m > n and not their own, there is no communication once the hats are on, but the prisoners may agree a common strategy in advance. Let each prisoner n reason as follows: the tail they see determines the class of x uniquely, because two sequences in different classes disagree at coordinates arbitrarily far out; so take the representative r(x) of that class and guess its nth coordinate. Since x and r(x) are eventually equal, the guess is wrong at only finitely many n. Hence this strategy guarantees, for every possible hat sequence whatsoever, that only finitely many prisoners are wrong and that the proportion of correct guesses tends to one.
(3) Now suppose instead that the hats are produced by infinitely many independent fair coin tosses, modelled by the usual fair-coin measure on the space of sequences.
(4) Whatever strategy the prisoners fix in advance, the nth guess depends only on coordinates beyond n, and the coordinate x_n is independent of those and still fair. So the probability that the nth guess is correct is one half.
(5) Averaging over n, for any strategy at all the limiting proportion of correct guesses is one half with probability one. I can make this precise: the centred indicators of the events guessing correctly are pairwise orthogonal, because the mth guess is settled by coordinates beyond m and hence by the tail beyond n whenever m > n, so a conditional expectation vanishes and Chebyshev on a sparse subsequence plus interpolation gives the limit. Nothing in that sketch seems to assume anything about the strategy beyond its existence.
(6) But (2) exhibits a strategy whose proportion correct is one on every sequence, that is, on the whole space, whereas (5) says that the set on which the proportion is one half has measure one. The two sets are disjoint and cannot both be of full measure. Contradiction.
(7) Step (3) is only the standard model of a fair coin, and (5) is a theorem with a proof I can write out. So the culprit must be (1) or (2): no such selector exists, and the axiom of choice is false.
(8) This is far more than I bargained for. If there is no selector, then since a well-ordering of the reals would define one by taking the least member of each class, there is no well-ordering of the reals either; and by the usual equivalences I lose the Hahn–Banach theorem, bases for arbitrary vector spaces, and a good deal of the analysis I was taught. Worse, the form of the argument seems to generalise: any principle strong enough to manufacture a strategy that beats chance this badly is refuted by the same reasoning, and I appear to be pushed towards a set theory in which probabilistic intuitions about infinite sequences of trials are left standing only by crippling everything else.

So which step do you reject? I would like to know precisely which step fails, why the step looked unexceptionable, and what rejecting it costs me. My own suspicion is somewhere between (4) and (5), because I was brought up on the classical slogan that no gambling system can beat a fair coin, and yet (2) is apparently a gambling system that beats one every single time; but I cannot turn that suspicion into a statement about a specific step, and if I simply give up (5) I seem to lose the very result that makes probability theory informative about limiting frequencies. If the trouble is that the strategy in (2) is not the kind of object to which (4) and (5) apply, I want to know exactly what restriction is doing the work and where it would have to be inserted for the proof in (5) to go through.

Two further things I would find useful. First, what does the argument actually establish once the suspect step is repaired: does it survive as a genuine impossibility result of some kind, and if so is it really a new result or only a familiar phenomenon about non-measurable sets in a new costume? Second, how much choice does the strategy in (2) really need: would a weaker principle suffice to build it, or is a selector for a relation with continuum-many classes essential? If it could be built from something weaker, my refutation would refute less than I feared, which would be some comfort.

I would prefer to keep (3) and the mathematics of (5) if at all possible, and I would very much rather not abandon the law of large numbers. Where is the cheapest place to give, and what am I paying for it?
