I'm finishing a dissertation prospectus on naturalizing mental content, and before I defend it I want an audit of the package I've assembled. I don't need a bibliography, references, or suggestions about what to read — I want to know whether the commitments below are jointly tenable, and I'd rather find out now than from my committee. Here is roughly where I've landed:

(i) Content is fixed by etiological proper function. A representational state has as its content the condition that the mechanism producing it was selected, over the organism's ancestry, for producing states that covary with. The attraction of the view is that history is supposed to settle the disjunction problem: the familiar bug-detector in a frog's or toad's visual system fires at flies but also at any small dark moving thing dragged through its field of view, and on this account the fact that it was selected to covary with flies — rather than with flies-or-dragged-pellets — is what makes the state about flies. The account is also supposed to be non-circular: truth conditions are meant to be grounded in selection, causation and function, with no intentional vocabulary doing any of the work.

(ii) The account should vindicate the standard Twin-Earth verdicts. Oscar, whose clear drinkable liquid is H2O, and his molecule-for-molecule twin on a planet where the clear drinkable liquid is some chemically different stuff, think different thoughts. Content is not intrinsic to the thinker.

(iii) In a swampman-style case — a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a person assembled by a freak chance event, with no evolutionary history and no learning history — the duplicate's states have no representational content at all. It behaves exactly as the original would, says what he would have said, and passes every behavioural test you like, but nothing it is in means anything.

(iv) Content is genuinely causally explanatory. Citing what an agent believes and wants explains what it does, and folk psychology is not a merely predictive calculus that we should expect mature neuroscience to discard. The fact that the frog's state is about a fly is part of why the frog's tongue comes out.

(v) Subpersonal cognitive neuroscience, by contrast, is entirely non-semantic. The story about circuits, tuning curves and synaptic weights cites no contents at all, and content-level psychology is autonomous from it.

(vi) Thought is productive. We form concepts — supernova, electric charge, the square root of two — and think thoughts with content that no selection history could have singled out. The first person to think about supernovae had a thought with content the moment they had it. A theory of content has to accommodate that without cheating.

(vii) A case verdict. A desert rodent has a looming-motion detector N that fires on the visual signature of an approaching snake and triggers freezing. The snakes disappear from the rodent's range and N now fires almost entirely at wind-blown leaves. Freezing during leaf-fall happens to protect the animals from a newly arrived predator that hunts by motion, and over very many generations selection maintains and sharpens N — its threshold, its wiring into the freezing circuit — precisely because it fires during leaf-fall. My verdict is that N's states still mean snake: the animals are systematically and adaptively wrong, and a mechanism's being co-opted for a new job does not rewrite what its states mean.

What I want from you: go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look like they are; rank the real tensions by severity rather than flagging all of them as equally bad; for each one, say what exactly I would have to give up and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the set; and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Please argue from the structure of the views themselves rather than from what is orthodox in the literature, and be specific about which commitment has to go in each case.
