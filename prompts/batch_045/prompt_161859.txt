I'm revising a section of my dissertation chapter on the separateness of persons objection to aggregative consequentialism, and I keep finding that the standard statement of the objection — that aggregative views treat distinct persons as if they were merely locations within a single life — slides between three claims my draft needs to keep apart: (a) that a loss to one person cannot be offset by a gain to another; (b) that in such a trade-off nobody is compensated, so the interpersonal case leaves a normative residue the intrapersonal case does not; and (c) that the real complaint is about the impersonality of the ranking itself, about outcomes being ordered from no one's point of view, rather than about the arithmetic of summing.

Could you explain how these hang together? Specifically: is (c) the core content, with (a) and (b) downstream of it, or is (c) a separate complaint that an aggregator can concede without giving up anything she needs? And can (b) be formulated without leaning on a metaphysically loaded account of who has an experience?

The case that makes the distinction bite for me: suppose a single human organism whose consciousness is permanently divided into two concurrent streams, each as phenomenally unified as an ordinary person's, each with its own projects and attachments, neither with any access to the other's experiences. Trading a decade of severe pain in one stream for a large gain in the other counts as intrapersonal by the criterion of a single organism or a single life, and interpersonal by the criterion of distinct standpoints and of who is compensated. On each of (a) through (c), does the objection license that trade? And if (b) is what carries the weight, shouldn't it also tell against some ordinary prudential trade-offs within one undivided life — say, between the projects of a person's twenties and those of her seventies?
