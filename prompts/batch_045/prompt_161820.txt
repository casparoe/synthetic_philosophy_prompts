I work at a foundation that funds both astrobiology and work on existential risk, and a colleague has been pressing an argument on me that I think I have now reconstructed correctly but that ends somewhere I refuse to go. I would be grateful for a diagnosis rather than a verdict.

(1) The silence. We have no unambiguous evidence of extraterrestrial civilizations: no signals, no artifacts, no probes, no visits.

(2) Any civilization that acquired the capacity to send self-replicating interstellar probes, anywhere in the Milky Way, would leave traces we could hardly miss, in a time short compared with the age of the galaxy. Grant my colleague his numbers for the sake of argument: at one hundredth of the speed of light, with long pauses between hops, a probe network would saturate a galaxy some hundred thousand light-years across in tens of millions of years, against a galactic age in the billions and a few hundred billion stars. So the silence is strong evidence that no such capacity has ever been exercised here, and hence that the expected number of interstellar-capable civilizations per galaxy is very small.

(3) Mediocrity. There is nothing privileged about the Earth, the Sun, or our moment in galactic history. Conditions like ours should be reproduced many times over in a galaxy that size.

(4) From (2) and (3), the bottleneck cannot be a shortage of starts. It must lie in the conditional probabilities: some step, or sum of steps, on the path from a habitable planet to a galaxy-filling civilization is extraordinarily improbable. Call whatever does the work the bottleneck.

(5) The bottleneck is either behind us, in the few billion years of evolution that produced us, or ahead of us, in the interval between now and any galaxy-filling future.

(6) Conservation. The silence fixes the approximate size of the improbability we have to account for. That total does not shrink merely because we have explained one candidate step; it is redistributed across the untested remainder of the path.

(7) Therefore any result that makes an early step look easy is, conditional on the silence, evidence that the bottleneck lies ahead of us rather than behind us. This looks to me like plain Bayesian conditionalization, not rhetoric.

(8) If the bottleneck lies ahead of us, then the long future our species imagines for itself is unlikely. Whatever stops civilizations stops them before they become visible to others, and we have not yet become visible to anyone.

(9) So the discovery of independently-arising microbial life — in the Martian subsurface, in the plume of an icy moon, in the atmosphere of an exoplanet — would be among the worst pieces of news our species could receive. The easier the early steps look, the worse it is for us.

(10) By the same reasoning, every advance in origins-of-life research is bad news. A bench demonstration that self-replicating polymers arise readily under early-Earth conditions does the same damage as finding microbes on Mars.

(11) And if inquiry into the early steps is bad news, we should prefer that it fail. A funding body that believed (6) through (9) should defund the search for biosignatures and origins-of-life work, and should hope that whatever it still funds comes up empty.

(12) The pattern generalizes past astrobiology: we should defund any inquiry whose success would be bad news — near-Earth object surveys, climate modeling, pandemic surveillance — since each is designed to produce exactly the evidence we would rather not have.

(13) That is absurd. Asteroid surveys are obviously worth funding. So something above is wrong.

My instinct is to give up the later steps before I give up the silence-and-mediocrity core, but I cannot tell whether that is good instinct or cowardice, and I worry that (10) and (11) fail for a reason that leaves (9) fully intact — in which case I have defused a bad argument and still have to live with the conclusion.

Tell me which step you reject and why it looked sound. Then price the alternatives: if the flaw is late, what does the repaired argument actually license (does it tell us astrobiology is bad news but still worth doing, or does it tell us nothing at all)? If the flaw is early, which of (1), (2), (3), or (6) is cheapest to weaken, and what else in our picture of our place in the galaxy do we lose by weakening it? I am especially unsure whether (8) smuggles in more than (5) and (7) support, and whether (11) depends on a confusion I can name but not yet state cleanly.
