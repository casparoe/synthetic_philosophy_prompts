I'm a retired physiologist — 35 years of ion channels, so I'm used to things I can actually put a number on — and since retiring I've been going to a philosophy reading group at the library, and somehow that turned into me sitting on the hospital's research ethics committee, which in practice means I initial a lot of consent forms. This question comes out of that.

At our last meeting we spent two hours disagreeing and I think half of it was that we were using the word consent for several different jobs at once. So here's what I want. I've written down eight defects a signed consent could have, and I want you to sort them into tiers by how much normative *work* the consent is still doing — 10 would be "does everything consent is supposed to do, the researcher has nothing to answer for," 0 would be "there is no consent here at all, treat it as if the form were blank." Give me a number for each and one sentence of why, and then tell me where the disagreement is real rather than verbal — i.e. which of these you'd expect competent people to genuinely come apart on.

1. Everything about the procedure is described accurately, but the participant is told it will help future patients when in fact nobody expects it to help anyone.
2. Same study, but the form omits a side effect that's mild in itself (a one-in-fifty chance of a month of bad insomnia) and which this particular woman would definitely have refused over had she known.
3. She's 82, follows the conversation fine, mild vascular dementia, forgets it entirely by the next morning, and cheerfully re-consents every time anyone asks.
4. The alternative on offer is the standard treatment, which is being rationed for reasons the researcher didn't create and can't fix, so enrolling is the only way to get treated at all.
5. No threat, no pressure beyond the ordinary kind: the consultant who'll be operating is the one who takes the consent, in the room, in his coat.
6. She consents to the blood draw but not to the genetic analysis that is the actual point of the study; the samples are unlinked and she's at no risk from it.
7. She consents because she's convinced the drug will cure her — nobody told her that, she just assumed it — and the researcher knows she believes it and says nothing either way.
8. The consent is taken by a research nurse who doesn't understand the protocol herself and can't answer a single question about it.

Two bits of scaffolding I've invented, which you should feel free to knock down. First, I keep wanting to say some defects make the consent *void* while others leave the permission standing but make the whole transaction *tainted* — is that a real distinction or did I just make it up to make my table tidy? Second, I've been calling the requirement that the person taking consent actually grasp what's being consented to the *uptake* condition, by analogy with promises. Does that analogy hold, or does it quietly smuggle in something about consent being a speech act that most accounts would reject?
