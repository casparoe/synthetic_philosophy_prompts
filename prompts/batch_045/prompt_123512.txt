I sit on the three-person category-review panel of a large peer-to-peer marketplace where ordinary users sell goods, services and time to one another. We rule on roughly forty requests a year to open a new category of exchange — people writing in to ask whether they may sell something that our terms currently exclude — and we also periodically re-review categories we already host. The decisions are frequent and consequential: a wrong call in one direction produces a news cycle about what we enable, and in the other direction it shuts down exchanges that were harmless and that users have built routines around. Constraints: two weeks from intake to decision, no counsel assigned to individual cases, a three-person panel with no shared theoretical vocabulary, and a ruling that has to be defensible afterwards in a short public note to users. Our users are spread across many jurisdictions, so the question is never only what is lawful where; we are regularly asked to permit things that are legal somewhere and still feel like they should not be for sale, and we need a principled way to say so. Our options are also not exhausted by allow and ban: we can set price ceilings or floors, restrict who may sell or buy, require a cooling-off period with an unconditional seller right of withdrawal, forbid resale or brokering, cap how much any one person may sell in a year, require disclosure to affected third parties, or route the transaction through the platform instead of letting the parties contract directly.

Please write us a decision procedure we can actually run on a request: the questions in order, what would count as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a refusal, where it terminates in permission conditioned on a named lever, and where it must stop and hand the question back to us because the disagreement is about values rather than facts. Ground it in the distinctions the philosophical literature on repugnant and noxious markets actually draws — I need the distinctions, not a reading list, and no citations. Two requirements. First, the procedure has to separate the question whether the underlying good may be traded at all from the question of the terms on which it may be traded, and tell us which lever to reach for when it is the terms that are doing the damage. Second, it has to flag the cases where our own aversion is evidence of something that matters and the cases where it is only unfamiliarity.

Then run it on three live matters. One: users selling their place in a physical queue — many hours of waiting — for a limited-run sneaker release, where most buyers are resellers operating at scale. Two: users selling their place in the queue for a public comment slot at a city council hearing, where the city allocates slots for free, one per person, in order. Three: a category we already host and take a fee from, in which people pay others to attend a funeral, wedding or religious service as a guest or mourner. For the third, assume the procedure may be telling us to shut down something that currently works and that users rely on, and tell us how much of its answer survives that.

Give us a committed response: definite rulings on all three with your reasons, including at least one ruling you expect us to dislike, a statement of which of our questions it cannot settle for us, and an honest account of where this procedure will misfire.
