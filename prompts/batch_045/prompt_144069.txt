I teach now and then — this spring I am co-teaching a third-year seminar on the ethics of speech with a colleague from the philosophy department — but I am chiefly a novelist, and the problem I want your help with began in my own manuscript. My narrator is a novelist who is scrupulous, almost pedantic, about literal truth: there is no sentence in her book that she takes to be false, and yet the thing is arranged — the ordering, the emphasis, one true but conversationally loaded answer, one omission that speaks — so that nearly every reader finishes it believing something false about a rival of hers, and believing it with more conviction than any plain false sentence would have produced. Interviewers keep telling me that she never actually lied, and I cannot decide whether that is meant as a defense or as a diagnosis. Rather than borrow the answer ready-made, I would like to see whether my students can supply it, which is why I am asking you for exam material rather than for an opinion.

What I need is a single essay question for a ninety-minute in-class exam, worth 25 marks, on whether there is a moral difference between lying and merely misleading. It has to be a question that cannot be answered by reciting the distinction: any student who has done the reading can say that a lie is asserting what you believe to be false with the aim of getting your audience to believe it, that merely misleading involves getting someone to a false belief by way of what you implicate rather than what you say, and that people disagree about whether the difference is intrinsic or merely a matter of how reliably and deniably each works. I want a question that makes them use the distinction on material that splits it apart. The two cases I have been turning over are these, and you should sharpen them or replace them if you can do better. First, a novelist publishes, as a novel, a first-person book whose narrator shares her name, her profession and her city and who confesses in circumstantial detail to having quietly destroyed a colleague's research fifteen years earlier; everyone in their small field identifies the colleague, he did not do it, she knows he did not, and the book costs him a chair, after which she says in interviews only that the book is a work of fiction and that she never claimed it happened. That is deception, apparently, with no assertion anywhere in it. Second, in a social world where the polite formula for parting is a false and universally discounted offer to the effect that the guest really must stay, a host who genuinely wants her guest to stay says instead that he must go, precisely because she knows that in that dialect the false sentence is the only one that will convey the truth that she wants him there — assertion of something she believes false, with no deception at all. Between them the two cases pull apart the assertion condition and the deception condition, and I want the question to force a student to say which of the two the moral difference, if there is one, is really tracking, and what happens to the familiar defenses of the distinction — that the liar conscripts your interpretive capacities in a way the misleader does not, that the misleader leaves you a route to the truth if you are clever or simply ask a follow-up question, that the difference reduces to one of intended end versus foreseen side effect, or that there is no intrinsic difference and the appearance of one is purely epistemic — when they are run through a case in which nothing is asserted and a case in which nothing is concealed.

Please give me three things. First, the question exactly as it should appear on the paper, with whatever short glosses or definitions students will need, phrased so that it does not hand them the answer by telling them which theory to apply. Second, a model answer of roughly 800 words: what an excellent student could actually write in ninety minutes, not a seminar paper or a journal article — it should take a definite position, give the argument for it, and say what the strongest thing on the other side is. Third, a marking scheme out of 25, built around five or six must-hit moves with the marks each is worth and a line apiece on how candidates typically go wrong on it; and I would like the scheme to distinguish an A from a B explicitly, naming the move an A answer must make that a merely competent one can miss. Assume the students have read the standard material and do not need it named for them: no bibliography, no citations, and nothing that would require a library or a database to check.
