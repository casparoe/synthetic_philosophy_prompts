I'm about two hundred pages into a novel and I've written myself into a corner with one of my characters, so I'm hoping someone can help me keep her honest.

Marijke is 68, a retired music critic, and a total bore about Schopenhauer, in the sense that she's built her whole critical apparatus on him: music doesn't copy the Ideas the way the other arts do, it copies the will directly, so it isn't about anything, it just is the shape of wanting. She has two private coinages she uses in her column: the wanting-shape, which is what she says you listen for, and consolation forgeries, which is her term for pieces that pretend the wanting goes somewhere or gets paid off. Her verdicts so far, which I've already drafted into earlier chapters:

- Bach's solo cello suites: the summit, she'd take them to the island.
- Beethoven's late quartets: the summit again, more honest than anything written since.
- Mozart's D minor piano concerto: gorgeous, but she marks it down for the major-key ending, which she thinks is a bit of a smile at the door on the way out.
- Wagner's Ring: she'll praise the orchestra for paragraphs and then says the moment it's staged with a story in it, it starts insisting that the wanting is heading somewhere.
- A massively popular singer-songwriter album about a drowned brother: she's withering. Saddest thing on the radio and the least true, listener supplies the feeling and then congratulates themselves for having it.
- Mahler's big climaxes: he keeps promising that the feeling adds up to something.
- Cage's silent piece and a well-known ambient record someone sent her: a good joke that stopped being a joke, and furniture for people who've read one book about Buddhism.

Chapter 11 is a concert she's been sent to review, and I need to know what she writes. It's a double bill. First half: 52 minutes for amplified cello and tape, built from one sustained pitch and its overtones, no melody, no pulse, no development, all of it derived from a single string bowed so slowly it only produces beating tones. The program note says the composer wanted to make a piece in which nothing wants anything, and describes it as a portrait of the will at rest, if such a thing can be. Second half: an unending melody generated by a model trained on Bach chorales, played live, never cadencing, never repeating, just an infinitely fertile wanting that never once gets what it's after. People are apparently weeping at both, for opposite reasons.

Complication, because I'm a novelist and can't leave well enough alone: the composer of the first piece is her estranged daughter, and what I actually want to write is Marijke sitting there planning her pan and then breaking down in about bar nine hundred of a piece where nothing happens.

Here's what I need. What rule, or rules, are consistent with all seven of those earlier verdicts? What does each of them predict about these two pieces, and which pairs of verdicts on the double bill would be coherent for her and which would be self-betrayal? Best guess at what she files. And the thing I really can't work out: is that breakdown the will finally going quiet, in which case her own system says she's hearing the best music of her life, or is it just her personal grief getting hooked, which is precisely the thing she despises about that grief album? Relatedly, can a Schopenhauerian even allow that music could present the denial of the will rather than just more wanting, or is the only consistent music of quietism silence? And if you could send her to any piece at all to settle which rule she's really running, what would it be?
