A member of my reading group has an argument she says shows that Molinism quietly collapses into universalism, and I can't locate the mistake. I'm confident there is one, since Molinists have denied the conclusion while endorsing everything that gives the argument its force.

(1) Middle knowledge gives God, for every possible creature C and every complete set of circumstances S in which C might be placed, a determinate truth about what C would freely do in S.

(2) Call a creature reachable if there is at least one such S in which C would freely accept grace and persevere to the end. Every possible creature is reachable: a creature who would reject grace under every circumstance it could be placed in would have its damnation settled by the kind of thing it is rather than by what it wills, and that is not the sort of freedom Molinism was invented to protect.

(3) Add what she calls circumstantial independence: whether C freely accepts grace in S depends only on C's own counterfactual profile together with the intrinsic features of S, not on which other creatures happen to exist alongside C.

(4) By (2) and (3), for any collection of creatures there is a single set of circumstances S* in which every member of the collection would freely accept grace and persevere.

(5) God is perfectly loving, and so among worlds equal in other respects he prefers one with more saved creatures to one with fewer; by (1) he knows the outcome of every world he could actualize before he decrees anything at all.

(6) So the feasible world containing the most saved creatures is a world in which everyone is saved, God actualizes it, and no one is lost.

(7) But Molinism is supposed to explain how some are lost compatibly with God's antecedent will that all be saved. An account of providence that entails universalism has overshot its brief, so something above must go.

Which step do you reject, and why did it look safe? If I have to choose I'd rather keep (1) and (5) than (2) or (3), but I want to know what keeping them costs me — in particular, what rejecting (3) does to the idea of a feasible world and to the standard move of explaining damnation by transworld depravity.
