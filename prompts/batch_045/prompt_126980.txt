I'm in a seminar on innateness and language acquisition, and I'm trying to settle on the position I want to defend in the final paper. I assembled each of the following claims because it independently seemed well motivated, but I now suspect the package doesn't fit together. I'd be grateful for an audit before I commit to it.

(1) The poverty of the stimulus, stated carefully, is an argument about the kind of evidence available to the learner, not about how much of it there is. Children hear well-formed utterances paired with situations; they are not told which strings are deviant, and such correction as they receive is unreliable and mostly targets truth or politeness rather than form. What they end up with is a grammar sensitive to constraints — island constraints on extraction, the conditions on anaphoric dependence — that no finite sample of positive data determines, because what would settle the matter is information about what is not a sentence of the language. On this formulation the argument isn't answered by scaling up the data.

(2) The conclusion the nativist should draw is nonetheless modest. Much of what has been assigned to Universal Grammar is better explained by third-factor principles: domain-general conditions on computational efficiency and on the structural architecture of generative systems. The specifically linguistic residue of the innate endowment may then be very small.

(3) The notion of innateness I want to work with is developmental. A trait is innate to the extent that its development is buffered against variation in the environment — canalized, in the familiar sense — so innateness is a matter of degree rather than a binary property of representations.

(4) I want nativism to remain a substantive thesis about our biological nature, not merely a claim about which learning algorithms are tractable. If it amounts only to the observation that a learner needs some bias or other, I lose interest in defending it.

(5) I think the results from large language models are a genuine problem for the nativist, not a change of subject. A network with no hand-coded syntax, trained only to predict the next token in raw text, apparently comes to represent hierarchical structure and to generalize in ways that look rule-governed. If that is right, the claim that such structure cannot be extracted from the signal has been empirically falsified.

(6) What actually moves me, however, is not the poverty of the stimulus but the resilience evidence. Children who receive degraded or nonstandard input still arrive at structured systems: deaf children not exposed to a conventional sign language develop gesture systems with systematic, language-like organization, and a sign system passed from one cohort of learners to the next appears to become more regular in transmission. That robustness strikes me as the strongest argument available.

(7) Finally, I am persuaded by the complaint that innateness is a mongrel notion — bundling universality, presence at birth, genetic specification, being unlearned, and developmental buffering, which come apart in actual cases — and I think the term should be retired from the theory in favour of the separate questions. I don't think this costs me (1), since I take its conclusion to be restatable without the vocabulary of innateness.

Which of these genuinely pull against each other, and which only look as though they do? Please rank the real tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad, and for each say what would have to be given up and what that costs. If a commitment is ambiguous between two readings, say which reading is doing the work and what follows on the other. If I have to choose, I'd most want to keep (1) and (4). Tell me whether keeping both is possible, and whether there is a single revision that would clean up more than one of the tensions at once.
