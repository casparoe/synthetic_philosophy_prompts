I spent my career in experimental physical chemistry — reaction kinetics mostly, and the last decade of it preoccupied with calibration and error budgets — and since retiring I have been sitting in on a reading group in philosophy. We have been moving slowly through Keynes's *Treatise on Probability*, and I have become attached to one distinction in it: that between the probability of a conclusion on a given body of evidence and what Keynes calls the weight of the argument, which turns on a balance not between the favourable and the unfavourable evidence but between the absolute amounts of relevant knowledge and relevant ignorance. The two can come apart: new evidence may lower the probability of the very conclusion you had reached, while always, on his account, adding to the weight behind it. In my own notes I have been glossing weight as *resilience*: the degree to which a probability judgment survives admissible perturbations of the evidence base — dropping one study, or changing the instrument, the operator, the analysis script, the exclusion criteria. I do not know whether that gloss is faithful to him or merely convenient for me, and I would like to know if it is being asked to do work it cannot do.

A colleague in the group, a retired statistician, has produced an argument which, if it holds, shows that the distinction can do no work at all in rational choice. I cannot find the fault in it, and I cannot accept where it ends.

Here is the case I care about, stylized but not, I think, fanciful. Two routes to an estimate of the same rate constant. Route A: one long run on a single well-characterized instrument, one operator, one analysis script; result k = 0.72, nominal 95 per cent interval [0.68, 0.76]. Route B: eleven short runs over two years, three instruments, four operators, two independently written analysis scripts; result k = 0.72, nominal 95 per cent interval [0.68, 0.76]. Grant me that the intervals come out identical because the same error model was applied to both: in my experience that happens more often than one would like. In my laboratory we built on results of the second kind and treated results of the first as provisional, and we would have staked more on them at the same odds. The argument says that, once the numbers agree, this is sentiment.

(1) *Screening.* What an agent ought rationally to choose is fully determined by the probabilities of the relevant outcomes together with their utilities. Two agents who agree on all of those and differ in nothing else that bears on them have no rational reason to choose differently. Everything else about their epistemic situation — how the numbers were arrived at, how much evidence fed them, how stable they are under perturbation — can matter to choice only by altering some probability or some utility.

(2) *Distinctness.* Weight is not a probability and is not a function of probabilities: two evidence bases can assign the same probability to a hypothesis while differing arbitrarily in weight. Assume, for the sake of the argument, that the probabilities in play are numerically measurable and fully comparable, as they are in my two routes. Keynes allows that they frequently are not, and I would rather not win on that technicality.

(3) *Relevance.* Weight is nevertheless choice-relevant. Between two courses of action whose outcomes are equally probable, a rational agent may — and Keynes at least sometimes seems to say should — prefer the one resting on the heavier evidence. This is what is supposed to separate genuine uncertainty from mere risk.

(4) *Reduction.* If weight is choice-relevant, then by (1) it must bear on the probability of some outcome, and there is an obvious candidate: weight is, or determines, the probability that the first-order estimate is the correct one — a second-order probability over first-order probabilities. But a second-order probability is still a probability, and averaging over it returns a single first-order probability again. The agent's uncertainty is therefore captured by one number after all, and weight is a function of probabilities.

(5) So (2) and (3) cannot both be true. Either weight is probabilistic, distinctness fails, and uncertainty is risk in disguise; or weight is not probabilistic, in which case by (1) it cannot guide choice, relevance fails, and uncertainty is idle.

(6) In neither case is there a third thing: no feature of our epistemic situation that is real, choice-relevant, and not capturable as a probability.

(7) *Corollary.* My two routes to k = 0.72 are rationally indistinguishable. Replication, triangulation across instruments, robustness to the analysis pipeline, preferring a result that many different methods agree on to one that a single method produces: none of this can matter in its own right, but only by moving the numbers. Where it leaves the numbers unchanged it leaves everything that matters unchanged.

I would be grateful for the following. First: which step do you reject, and why does the step look sound when it is not? I suspect (4), but when I try to say what is wrong with it I end up saying that uncertainty about a probability need not itself be a probability, which sounds to me like a refusal to answer rather than an answer; and I notice that (4) may only be an instance of (1), in which case my suspicion is misplaced and the real culprit is the screening premise. Second: is there a difference that survives scrutiny between a probability distribution over which first-order probability is correct and something like a set or range of admissible probabilities, and does the averaging step work on both? This is the crux for me. Third: what does it actually cost to give up (1)? Does abandoning it let in everything the expected-utility tradition classes as irrational, or is the door narrower than that? Fourth: is there a way to keep (3) by making weight's relevance merely instrumental — heavier evidence is likelier to survive what comes next, or buys the option to wait and learn — and does that survive the cases where no further evidence will arrive and the decision is made once? Fifth: is my stylized case constructible at all, or would any competent analysis of Route B deliver a wider interval, so that the argument is refuting a case I have only managed to stipulate? I would sooner be told that than protected from it.

If I must choose, I would rather keep (2) and (3) and pay nearly any price elsewhere; but I want to know what the price is, and I would rather be corrected about that preference than have it flattered.

Two further requests. I have no access to a university library, so please argue the points rather than directing me to a literature I cannot consult; and where you rely on a result or a distinction that is contested, say so. Please mark, throughout, which of what you tell me is comparatively settled, which is a live dispute, and which is your own best guess. If the argument is sound and the conclusion is one I ought simply to swallow, or if the question as I have framed it rests on a muddle — in my gloss of weight as resilience, say, or in the thought that two histories of evidence can be identical in number and differ in anything that matters — then say that plainly instead of being helpful about it. That would be the most useful thing you could give me.
