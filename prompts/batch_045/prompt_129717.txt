I teach a 300-level Philosophy of Medicine seminar — 18 juniors, one 80-minute session, and I have exactly one slot to spend on how death should be defined. I've run it as straight discussion before and it always degenrates into someone saying "well, brain death" and everyone else nodding, which is the opposite of what I want. So: design me a structured activity — a game, a staged committee exercise, something with real move-making and something at stake — that makes them feel the force of the problem before anyone names the options.

What they already know: they've read two newspaper-ish pieces on organ donation, so "brain death" is in the room as a phrase, and they all have the lay intuition that death is when your heart stops and you stop breathing. Assume that and basically nothing else. What I do not want is the session collapsing into picking between whole-brain, higher-brain, and circulatory criteria as if these were items on a menu. If they end up choosing one and defending it, the activity failed. The thing I want them to walk into is that the criteria come apart in cases; that no one criterion does all the work we want the concept of death to do (permitting withdrawal of treatment, permitting organ recovery, giving a family closure, closing a chart, settling inheritance); and that the disagreement may be less about the biology than about which of those jobs matters, or even about what we should mean.

Here's the skeleton I've been toying with — build on it or replace it, your call: four committees of 4-5. Each writes, from scratch, a one-sentence rule for when a patient is dead, with no access to the standard taxonomy. Then they get a deck of case cards and have to sort each into dead / not dead / can't tell. Points for sorting every card without amending the rule, points docked for every amendment made after seeing a card — so they feel, as a cost, the choice between gerrymandering the rule to fit a case and holding the rule and swallowing a verdict they hate. Then the bit I actually think is new: each committee's one-sentence rule is handed to a different committee, which has to apply it cold, in three minutes, to two fresh cases, with no clarifications allowed — as if they were a resident at 3am with a form to sign. Then a last round where a "transplant team" scores each rule on whether it permits recovery in a case where the family wants to donate and forbids it in a case where recovery would kill someone who might get better.

What I need from you: the actual rules and a scoring scheme that doesn't need money or prizes; a timeline with minute marks; a full deck of 12-14 case cards, one or two sentences each, invented or anonymized rather than real patients' stories (I don't want them arguing about facts, and I have a student whose parent died last year) — including two cards you make up yourself, ideally ones no existing protocol handles cleanly; a note on what the facilitator may and may not say during play; and a debrief that maps particular moments in the game onto particular claims, ending with the question of whether they were having a factual disagreement, a verbal one, or a decision about what to do.

Also: tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. I'm guessing at least one group writes a rule that's really just "the doctor decides"; at least one pre-med takes over their group with clinical detail; someone goes quiet; and the scoring tempts everyone to write a maximally vague rule that "can't tell" everything, which I'd want designed against rather than patched in the moment. How do I handle each? And be honest about which feature of the philosophical problem the game structurally cannot reproduce and I'll just have to say out loud at the end. Don't bother with citations, suggested readings, or a bibliography — I have all that.
