I'm a software engineer — distributed systems, about twenty years of it — with no philosophy training past a couple of MOOCs I never finished. I'd like a piece of criticism from you, and I'd like it as argument rather than as scholarship: no citations, no bibliography, nothing that depends on looking something up, and please don't rest any claim on a source I can't check. If a distinction I'm groping toward already has a settled name in the philosophy of science, give me the name in half a sentence and move on.

The situation that prompted this: two of us on my team put forward essentially the same design for a cache invalidation problem about three weeks apart, and we have since spent an absurd number of hours arguing about who "really" had it first. The argument keeps sliding between questions that I now think are different questions. Around the same time I re-watched Christopher Nolan's 2006 adaptation of Christopher Priest's novel about two rival stage magicians, and I can't stop seeing our argument in it. So: read The Prestige as a film about simultaneous invention, priority, and credit. I don't want another retelling of the usual historical cases — the calculus dispute, the two naturalists — I want the film.

Since I don't know the proper vocabulary, here are the distinctions I've been working with; use them, correct them, or replace them, but say which you're doing:

1. Independence versus leakage. Textbook multiples are supposed to be causally independent. The film's two magicians are not: there is espionage, a stolen ciphered notebook, hired lookalikes, sabotage. So the pair may not be a case of independent rediscovery at all, but of derivation wearing its costume. Does the film suggest that in a small, competitive field, apparent simultaneity is usually leakage, and does that change whether anyone is tempted to say the trick was inevitable?

2. What counts as "the same" discovery: the public effect or the method behind it. The Transported Man is reached two ways — one by a concealed double living a shared life, one by a machine that duplicates whatever is put into it. Same effect, utterly different mechanisms, and one of them depends on a real historical outsider who had no interest in magic. If discoveries are individuated by effect, this is a multiple; if by method and theory, it isn't one.

3. Three things "it was inevitable" might mean, which I think my colleague and I keep conflating: that the effect would have appeared within a few years anyway; that it would have appeared by the same route; and that the individual who produced it was replaceable, so the credit is close to arbitrary. I'd like each of these tested against the film separately, because my sense is that the film gives different answers for the two magicians.

4. Credit as a working institution versus credit as vanity. In engineering, attributing an idea to someone has real functions — ownership, accountability, someone to ask. The film's economy of secrets and notebooks is recognisably the same institution run pathologically. Is it a distortion of something legitimate, or just self-destruction?

What I want from the reading: what the film actually dramatizes about priority — including the fact that its title names the part of a trick where the thing lost is brought back, and is therefore already about whether a person has to be there to receive the credit; which three or four scenes you'd hang the argument on (I'd guess the stolen notebook, the Colorado Springs commission, the prison visit, and the warehouse of tanks at the end, but argue with me); what the ending does to the question, given that it leaves a room full of identical drowned men and an original who says he no longer knows which one he is; and where the lens simply breaks — it is a revenge melodrama about obsession, its science is knowingly fake, and it is arguably about spectatorship rather than attribution. I'd rather you told me plainly where the reading does violence to the film than stretch every scene onto the rack.

A thousand to fifteen hundred words, and end by saying what, if anything, the film shows about credit that argument alone doesn't.
