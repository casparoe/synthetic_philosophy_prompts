QUESTION 3 (20 marks)

(a) State Russell's analysis of a sentence of the form the F is G, giving all three clauses, and explain what the analysis predicts about the truth value of Russell's own example about the present King of France being bald. [6]

(b) The sentence the present King of France is not bald is ambiguous on Russell's account. Distinguish the two readings, and explain why Russell needs the distinction. [5]

(c) The theory has been criticised from several directions. Discuss (i) Strawson's objection from referring; (ii) Donnellan's distinction between referential and attributive uses of definite descriptions; and (iii) whether Kripke-style considerations about modality and rigidity tell against Russell's analysis of descriptions, or against some other, nearby view. For each: say as precisely as you can what the objection is and what exactly it is an objection to, and sketch one reply available to Russell. [9]

And here's the answer, more or less as submitted (I fixed one typo and left the rest alone):

ANSWER

(a) Russell's central idea is that a definite description is not a name: it doesn't stand for some object and then say something about that object. It is an incomplete symbol, an expression that has no meaning in isolation and gets defined away in the context of a whole sentence. The analysis of the F is G has three conjuncts, all under an existential quantifier: there is at least one F; there is at most one F (anything that is F is identical to it); and that thing is G. So Russell's example, the present King of France is bald, really says: there is exactly one present King of France and he is bald. Since France has no king, the existence conjunct fails and the whole sentence is false — not meaningless, not neither-true-nor-false, but straightforwardly false. The same apparatus is what lets Russell handle negative existentials: the present King of France does not exist comes out true on its natural, wide-scope reading, without Russell having to admit a merely subsistent king for the sentence to be about.

(b) The negation is ambiguous because the negation operator can take wide scope over the whole quantificational claim, or narrow scope, sitting inside the description. Wide scope: it is not the case that there is a unique present King of France who is bald — true, since there is no such person. Narrow scope: there is a unique present King of France and he is not bald — false, for the same reason. Russell needs the distinction because without it he would be stuck with a single verdict: he would have to say that the negation of a false sentence is true, full stop, and he would lose the reading on which the sentence is false because the description is unsatisfied. Distinguishing primary and secondary occurrences also explains why we would hesitate to assert it even when it is true: the wide-scope reading is the one we would naturally use, and it is uninformative.

(c) (i) Strawson's objection is that Russell conflates a sentence with a use of a sentence. Whether the present King of France is bald is said truly or falsely depends on who says it and when; someone who says it today fails to refer to anything, and so has not made a statement that is either true or false at all. For Strawson the existence of a unique King of France is presupposed rather than entailed: it is a precondition on saying anything true or false, not part of what is said. Russell can reply by granting that speakers standardly presuppose existence while insisting that the proposition asserted contains the existence and uniqueness conditions, and by pointing out that his analysis gives a uniform account of negation, scope and inference for every definite description, whereas a presuppositional account needs extra machinery to say what happens when the description is embedded in a larger sentence.

(ii) Donnellan's point is that a definite description can be used attributively, to speak of whoever or whatever fits the description, or referentially, to pick out a particular thing the speaker has in mind, and that in the referential case the speaker can say something true even if nothing satisfies the description. Someone who says at the scene of a murder, the murderer of Smith is insane, meaning the person now being questioned, may say something true about that person even if he is in fact innocent. But this is at bottom Russell's own distinction again: the referential use is just the reading on which the description takes wide scope over the rest of the sentence, and the attributive use is the narrow-scope reading. So Russell's theory already predicts Donnellan's cases, provided one is careful about scope — which is why Donnellan's data, interesting as it is, is not really a counterexample to the theory.

(iii) Kripke's modal considerations are the strongest objection of the three. Kripke argued that in modal contexts definite descriptions behave as rigid designators: the number of planets picks out nine in every possible world in which it picks out anything, so a sentence like the number of planets might have been even has to be understood as being about nine itself, and not about whatever happens to satisfy the description. Russell's quantificational analysis cannot deliver that, because on his account a description is satisfied by whatever happens to fit it, so the objects we talk about with descriptions float free of the descriptions. That is why the theory of descriptions breaks down once modality is in play. Russell's only reply is to invoke scope once more, but that is question-begging, since the point in dispute is precisely whether the description is doing the referential work.
