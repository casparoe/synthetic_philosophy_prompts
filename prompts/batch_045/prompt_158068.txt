QUESTION (20 marks)

In a study published in 2003, Joshua Knobe gave people two versions of a case. A company vice-president tells the CEO about a new program that will increase profits; in one version it will also harm the environment, in the other it will also help the environment. In both versions the CEO says he doesn't care about the environment at all and just wants as much profit as possible. The program is started and the side effect occurs. Asked whether the CEO brought about the side effect intentionally, most people (82%) say yes in the harm version and only a minority (23%) say yes in the help version.

(a) Explain why this asymmetry has been taken to show something about the ordinary concept of intentional action, rather than just something about how people feel about greedy executives. (7 marks)

(b) Someone in the seminar proposed this variant: one program will drain a wetland in one region and restore an equally valuable wetland in another; the CEO is told about both effects, says he cares about neither, and both occur; the net environmental effect is neutral. Each participant is asked both whether he intentionally destroyed the first wetland and whether he intentionally restored the second. Set out what the main competing explanations of the original effect would predict here, and say whether the result would settle the disagreement between them. (7 marks)

(c) Some philosophers draw a large methodological moral from the Knobe effect: philosophers lean on their own intuitions about invented cases, and if the effect is real then that method is in trouble and should be replaced, wherever possible, by experimental data about what people actually think. Assess this argument. (6 marks)

STUDENT'S ANSWER

(a) The two versions are matched on everything except the valence of the side effect. In both, the CEO foresees the side effect, doesn't care about the environment, and produces it as a byproduct of profit-seeking — he causes it in both versions, so the asymmetry isn't about causation. The only thing that differs is whether the outcome is bad or good, and that difference flips the verdict about intentionality. So a moral judgement is feeding into the ascription of intentionality: the moral judgement has to come first in the processing, and it then produces the "yes, intentionally" answer. Knobe's own conclusion is that the folk concept of intentional action is not a purely descriptive concept — whether something counts as done intentionally depends partly on normative facts about it. That's why the result matters to philosophy of action and not just to moral psychology.

(b) In the twin-wetland case the overall outcome is morally neutral, so the normative-concept explanation predicts that the asymmetry should disappear: people should treat the two wetlands the same way. The rival explanation — that what's doing the work is the agent's attitude, i.e. that people read the CEO as willing to bring about a foreseen side effect he doesn't care about — predicts yes to both, since he is indifferent to each. So this is a clean test, and a better one than the original precisely because it is within subjects: the same participant answers both questions, so any difference can't be put down to variation between individuals. If the usual asymmetry still shows up, the normative account is vindicated and the debate is essentially over; if it doesn't, the normative account is dead and we should all be attitude theorists.

(c) I think the methodological moral is right. The effect shows that intuitive verdicts about cases shift with morally irrelvant features of how the case is set up, so a philosopher relying on her own reaction to a hypothetical she invented is not in a good epistemic position. Knobe's reading is that ordinary people's verdicts get contaminated by moral outrage at the CEO, and that is exactly the sort of error experiments exist to correct. Where we can, appeals to intuition should be replaced by experimental data about what people actually think, since data is at least not one person's hunch. Admittedly the effect probably reduces in the end to the familiar negativity bias — people are much quicker to blame than to praise — which is why it matters less for philosophy than is sometimes claimed.
