Writing up a paper on the "acting for the right reasons" condition on moral worth, and I need a case engineered to spec rather than a case that just illustrates the issue. Here's what I need.

Constraints on the scenario:
- one agent, one act, one paragraph, ordinary realistic setting (a hospital, a workplace, a family — no trolleys, no designers, no neuroscientists);
- the act is right, and the agent's action has *full* moral worth — not merely excusable, not merely less bad, and I mean worth rather than credit or praise from onlookers;
- but the act's right-making feature F has to be such that no one can both act well here and treat F as her motivating reason: acting *because* of F is self-defeating, it destroys what made the act good. So any version of the condition that requires the good-making feature to figure as the agent's motivating reason (doastically, or de re through her perception of it) is unsatisfiable by anyone who acts well;
- no cheating on that: the self-defeat has to fall out of the structure of the reason, not from the agent being irrational, self-deceived, weak-willed, scrupulous, or confused;
- and it can't be either of the two familiar patterns — say in the check why not. (i) Not the Huckleberry Finn / unprincipled-virtue pattern, where the agent acts well *against* her own considered judgment: my agent's occurrent moral beliefs have to be true and fully consistent with what she does. (ii) Not merely a "one thought too many" case, where weighing the reason is idle, tasteless, or expressive of a bad motive: the self-defeat has to be stronger than that, acting on F has to *undermine* the feature that makes the act good, not just add an unattractive thought.

What the case is for, since that fixes what counts as cheating: I want to argue that the "right reasons" condition is best read as an attributability / fittingness condition rather than a doxastic one. So the case has to *generate* its verdicts from the two readings rather than stipulate them — if it works only because you built the Intuition in by hand, I can't use it.

After the scenario:
1. State F and show the act is right in virtue of it.
2. Show the action has full moral worth, and run the self-defeat: what exactly goes wrong for the agent who acts on F.
3. Show the readings come apart in the direction I need — the doxastic reading says no moral worth (or deficient worth) here, the attributability reading says full worth.
4. Say which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens if you remove it: does it collapse into the Huck pattern, or into a case where there really is no worth?
5. Give me the strongest attack a defender of the doxastic condition has, not a strawman, plus your reply.
6. Be explicit about where you're least confident — which step is most likely to fail — and what consideration would definately make you take back the claim that the action has full worth.
