Everyone keeps grabbing the same tired example — a parson, two growth curves, one of them geometric — and then the argument immediately turns into a fight about whether 20th-century agriculture counts as a rescue or a refutation, which means we just end up arguing about real history instead of about the methodology. So I want a made-up case, invented from scratch, where the verdict isn't already loaded by what actually happened.

What I need is a scenario built to order, and the specs are these: a small closed grain-growing polity (realistic, nothing science-fictional, no perfect foresight, nobody in the story smarter than their era) and a fictional theorist inside it who makes a *dated, quantitative* prediction that numbers of people will outrun food supply, with a fixed margin and a checkable date — no 'in the long run' hedging, no adjustable horizon, because if the prediction is vague then nothing is being tested. Then: the prediction has to *first fail*, and fail for a reason the theory itself supplies (a check it already names, just working harder than it expected), and *then* the predicted outcome has to arrive later, driven by the theory's own mechanism, once a finite, one-off offset is used up — and the offset has to be something the theorist could in principle have measured and seen running out at the time, not a deus ex machina. My group's first attempt used a one-off shipment of fertilizer and it felt cheap, so the offset needs to be internal to the polity's economy. Now here's the bit I care most about: I want a *decoy*. Put a second theorist in the story who made the numerically identical prediction off a mechanism so loose that any later crisis whatsoever would 'vindicate' it. The case has to be engineered so that a mechanism-first auditor (theory is 'right but early' if the mechanism it named was actually running and would have produced the outcome absent a specifiable exhaustible offset) vindicates theorist one and refuses theorist two, while a strict falsificationist (missed margin by the stated date, no early-clause allowed) kills both, and a research-programme-style auditor (survives only if it predicted something genuinely novel in the interval) splits them some *third* way that isn't just averaging the other two.

Scenario in one paragraph, then a walkthrough: run all three auditors on both theorists, stating the verdict and the reason for each of the six. After that, I want the honest part — say which single feature of the design is doing the work, what collapses if you remove it (I suspect if the offset isn't visibly exhaustible, the mechanism auditor just becomes the falsificationist again, but tell me), and how a defender of whichever view comes out worst would attack the case as rigged. It'd also help if you'd build in something I've started calling the 'audit horizon': set the test window so that it outlasts every person who was around when the prediction was made, and then tell me what changes if you shrink it so the original theorists are still alive and professionally invested at the audit date. I'm using this for a 20-minute talk where my thesis is that 'right but early' is really a claim about who has standing to declare a theory dead rather than a claim about the world, so anything that just stipulates the verdicts instead of generating them from the three rules is the one thing I can't use.
