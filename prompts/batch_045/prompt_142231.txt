I teach a third-year seminar called 'Value, Reasons and the Attitudes': fourteen students, a single 90-minute session, a room with a projector and passable speakers, and no budget at all. The session is the one on the fitting-attitude analysis of value — roughly, that for something to be valuable just is for it to be fitting to favour it in a certain way — and on the objection to that analysis which turns on the difference between reasons that bear on whether an attitude is fitting and reasons that merely make it a good idea, for the agent, to have it. Last year I simply described the standard case in which a demon or a rich patron offers you a benefit on condition that you admire him, and it fell completely flat: the students said, correctly as far as it goes, that nobody can simply decide to admire someone to order, concluded that the case showed nothing about reasons and nothing about value, and we were back where we had started inside ten minutes. I do not want to make that mistake again. I want them to walk into the difficulty themselves, make real private choices under a real if tiny incentive, and only afterwards be given the vocabulary for what they were doing.

So please design me a runnable activity for this group and this slot. Constraints: nothing should be named up front — no mini-lecture, no definition of the fitting-attitude claim, and the label 'wrong kind of reason' must not be uttered until the debrief; the difficulty has to be encountered first and described second. The stakes cannot involve money, though I can offer small genuine ones, such as choosing the reading for the final session or deciding the order of the presentation rota. Participants must commit themselves in writing, privately, more than once, so that there is something to compare afterwards. I suspect the activity needs to separate at least three things that my failed version ran together: a rating of the object, a report of how one's own state has changed, and a judgement about the object — and I also suspect it needs a case in which the incentive attaches to one kind of response (admiring, say) and a matched case in which the same incentive attaches to a different kind (wanting, choosing, hoping, intending), so that the class can feel that the incentive is not problematic in quite the same way in both. I have two further ideas I am unsure about and would rather you judge than simply execute: a withdrawal twist, in which the offer is cancelled after people have formed their responses but before anything is collected, to test whether the incentive moved their attitudes or only their reports; and a 'movability ladder', in which students rank a handful of attitudes by how responsive to incentives they expect them to be and then compare that ranking with what actually happened in the room. Please also tell me what sort of object the activity should use — a piece of music, an image, a joke, a short text — and why the choice matters.

What I need back: the rules and materials; the payoff scheme; a minute-by-minute timeline; what students write down and when; a debrief that maps specific moments in the session onto specific parts of the argument — the fitting-attitude formula, the distinction the objection turns on, the diagnosis of why some attitudes seem immune to incentives in a way others are not, and whether the problem really stops where the intuition says it does; an honest account of what will predictably go wrong when I run this (the student who genuinely likes the object, the student who announces that of course incentives are reasons and defuses the whole thing, the group that treats it as a game about points rather than about their own responses, and how I should handle each); and a frank note on which part of the puzzle the activity cannot stage at all and which I will have to supply in so many words. Please write your answer in British English.
