Two people in my reading group — a generalist and a particularist about conspiracy theories — have been sniping at each other for a year and finally agreed to an adversarial collaboration, and I got landed with drafting the document. Can you write it?

Where each of them stands, as they'd put it themselves: the generalist holds that the class has features — it runs against some official or institutional account, it explains things by hidden coordination, and it tends to absorb apparent counterevidence by reinterpreting it as further evidence — that give you a standing, defeasible reason to downgrade a theory just for being one of them, so either the prior or the burden of proof is different here than for an ordinary empirical hypothesis. The particularist holds that this whole framing is a pejorative doing epistemic work: no defect attaches to the class, you assess each theory on its evidence like any other historical claim, and the class has no unity an epistemologist should care about.

What I want is the text they could both actually sign, not a verdict on who's right: the claims both can accept (including about what the comparison with the official account is even supposed to show); the exact residual disagreement in wording each side would accept for its own view; the cruxes; and for each crux a case or test both agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance, plus what each would count as evidence against their own position. If some of the fight is verbal, say which part and why — I suspect at least the fight over "prima facie unwarranted" is.

Three things I've been pushing at them, which I want treated as the cruxes rather than just echoed back at me:

1. The leak-selection problem. The cache of insider material an outsider gets to see isn't a random sample of what a conspiracy would produce: no conspiracy, a sloppy one, and a very competent one can all surface a large, detailed, mutually consistent corpus — the competent one partly by planting material. So corpus size and internal coherence look non-diagnostic unless somebody models the selection. Which side owes a story here?
2. The competence trap. Turn up the assumed competence of the hypothesized conspiracy and more observations stop discriminating: missing evidence, blown predictions written off as bad luck, decoy defectors. The hypothesis starts behaving as though it had a free parameter and can't lose. But isn't the same true of institutional accounts, with "bureaucratic incompetence / coincidence / nobody checked" playing the identical absorbing role?
3. Error-budget asymmetry — the "unwarned" line. The official story usually arrives with a catalogue of its own known errors, corrections and gaps; the unofficial one arrives with unknown flaws in unknown places. Is that a real asymmetry in the accounts, or an artifact of who gets audited — journalists, courts and inspectors general beat on institutional accounts, and nobody does that to the alternative?

For the tests use these two, both of which I think they'd accept as bearing on it. (A) Take the historical cases where a covert program or a price-fixing cartel was later established in court or from archives, and go back to the pre-confirmation record: did they show the generalist's markers — contrarianism, self-insulation, absorbing counterevidence — at the time? If yes, those markers don't discriminate; if no, the generalist has something. Both should say in advance which way they expect this to come out and by what margin. (B) A stylized case, fully specified here so nothing needs looking up: a cache of about 9,000 internal documents from a mid-sized regulator, no chain of custody, high internal consistency, cross-references to outside events that check out. Three former employees independently describe the same covert program, agreeing on specifics but disagreeing about the year of one key meeting; two of them have a grievance against the regulator. The regulator's published account contains two documented factual errors and has never been independantly audited. Nothing in the cache carries a verifiable signature. Question: relative to before seeing any of this, does a careful investigator's confidence that the program existed go up or down? Each side writes down a direction and, if you can, a rough number, and says which feature of the case is doing the work.

Please be honest about epistemic status: mark which of the agreed claims most people working on this would accept and which are just these two happening to agree, and don't let a contested claim get dressed up as the consensus. Where you're extrapolating, or where the cruxes as I framed them are really your own reconstruction, note that in the item itself.
