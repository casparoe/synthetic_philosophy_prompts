Next month I'm giving a paper at a joint workshop — the history-of-economics seminar at my department plus a political theory reading group on empire — and what I want is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the paper itself. The room is roughly twenty people: eight or so historians of economic thought who read Smith as a theorist of commercial society and whose default irritation is political theorists raiding old texts for slogans; maybe six political theorists who work on conquest, federation and empire and who read Smith next to Locke, Burke and the federalists; and four or five people from global intellectual history and postcolonial theory who will want to know why the paper is organized around a Scottish professor's balance sheet rather than around anybody who was actually colonized. My thesis is that Smith's attack on the colonial system in the long colonial material in the Wealth of Nations is best read as a critique of a structure rather than of empire or of colonization as such — the structure being a legally protected monopoly of trade, government from a distance by a legislature the colonists don't sit in, and a war-and-administration bill that lands on people who get nothing out of the monopoly. I've been calling the upshot anti-imperialism without anti-colonialism: he has a devastating account of how empire is governed and essentially nothing to say against the fact of Britons settling and farming land other people are already using, and I want to argue that's a consequence of the framework and not a blind spot I'm quietly excusing.

Three steps, so you know what to aim at. One: the colonial trade is a monopoly case like any other, holding the rate of profit above what it would otherwise be and pulling capital out of domestic industry into a slower, more distant circuit, so the empire is a bad bargain even on its own commercial terms — there's the one-sentence paragraph where he says "Under the present system of management, therefore, Great Britain derives nothing but loss from the dominion which she assumes over her colonies." Two: the bad bargain persists because the government is influenced by the merchants and manufacturers who profit from it, so his remedy is institutional rather than a moral appeal to anyone's rights. Three: because the standard he's applying is the general opulence of the many and not rights or title to land, a well-run empire isn't ruled out by his own principles, which is why his stated remedies are fixing the arrangement — union with American representation and taxation in proportion, or letting the provinces go — rather than handing anything back. The three places I expect to get hit hardest: the union proposal, which I read at face value even though one of the historians will definately say it's an esoteric feint and that he really wanted them gone; the fact that on my reading the critique has no resources against settlement as such, only against misrule; and the East India Company material, where I want to claim the same template applies — sovereign and merchant in one body is the worst possible combination — and I'm not remotely sure it does.

So: give me the eight-ish objections I'll actually get, ordered by how much damage they do to the thesis rather than by how likely they are to be asked, and phrase each one the way that subgroup would phrase it — I want to hear the historian of economic thought saying that's not what the text is doing, in their idiom, the empire theorist saying this isn't a theory of empire at all, in theirs, and the global intellectual history people saying something about whose voice is missing from the archive. For each one: a two-minute reply I can say out loud, plus a note on where that reply is soft and on which weaker version of the thesis I should retreat to if the objection lands. I've got maybe fifteen minutes of questions, I ramble when I'm nervous, and I'd rather recieve eight things I can answer than twenty I can't. Two last things: there's a line I half remember where he seems to suggest the seat of government would end up on the other side of the Atlantic and I can never find it when I look — if someone quotes it at me I need an answer that doesn't depend on whether I've remembered it right — and please don't give me a reading list or authors to cite, I need the moves and the phrasing, not more names to drop. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
