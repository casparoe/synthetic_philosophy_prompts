Hey — I'm a PhD student working on testimonial warrant and there's something in the norms-of-assertion literature I keep going back and fourth on, and I'd rather have a clean explanation than a survey (I know the standard dialectic: knowledge norm, the Moorean "p but I don't know that p" style argument for it, merely-true-belief cases like the lucky clairvoyant, and the selfless-assertion and "you know p but you should be saying something stronger" cases against it).

What I want explained is this: the debate keeps sliding between reading the norm as a constraint on your epistemic *status* (you may assert only if you're in a position to know) and reading it as a constraint on your *reasons* for speaking (your utterance has to be guided by, or counterfactually sensitive to, your evidence for p), and I can't get clear on whether those are two formulations of one norm or two quite different norms that keep getting confused.

Two cases push me in opposite directions. (1) A pundit who asserts p, does know p, and knows that she knows it — but who would have asserted not-p just as cheerfully if her funders had wanted that instead; her utterance carries no information about how things are, only about who's paying her. (2) A speaker who asserts p on exactly the evidence she ought to be using, whose assertion is perfectly reason-responsive, but whose environment is fake-barn-ish, so that she doesn't know p. My intuitions say (1) is defective in some epistemic way and (2) is basically fine, which is close to the opposite of what a flat knowledge norm predicts.

So: explain what the status-vs-reason (or guidance, or sensitivity) distinction actually comes to, which reading the usual arguments for the knowledge norm really support, and what happens to the knowledge norm if it's supposed to handle both kinds of case — does it get supplemented, disjoined, or does it turn out we were looking at two separate norms all along?
