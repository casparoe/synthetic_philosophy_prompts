hey — I'm a med soc PhD student doing ethnography on a couple of surgical wards, and there's a pattern I keep tripping over in my fieldnotes that I think is a philosophy question wearing a hospital badge. Would love your read on it.

The practice, as concretely as I can state it: nobody gets discharged unless there is a named person — the discharge planner literally cannot tick the box otherwise. That person gets trained, by a nurse, in the hospital, in about twenty minutes, to do things that were nursing work not that long ago: sterile dressing changes, flushing a line, subq injections, managing a drain. They do it unpaid. They have no employment status, no injury coverage, no say in the chart, and no limit on hours. If they don't show up, the patient stays an extra day, the hospital absorbs the cost, and everybody narrates that as a family drama rather than as a staffing failure.

Now the mirror image. The same hospital contracts out home health aides for bathing, toileting, transfers. Those workers are paid, badly, hourly, no benefits, paid for the visit and not for the drive, and they are barred — by regulation and by agency policy — from exactly the tasks we just handed to an untrained daughter after one teach-back. The aide typically has more formal certification than the daughter. So the family member gets the skilled work, for free, and the paid worker gets the unskilled work, for wages.

And then the third thing, which is what actually got under my skin. I've been told that some state Medicaid waiver programs will now enroll a family member as the paid caregiver for their own relative — treat it as hypothetical if I've got that wrong. When I raised it at our ethics committee, the same clinicians who had leaned on the daughter for six days of free skilled care got squeamish. Paying her, they said, would monetize the relationship, turn love into labor, crowd out the good stuff, change what she's doing when she changes the dressing.

So:

1. What does this arrangement actually presuppose about care? Is there a view it commits you to — something like: the value of care is partly constituted by the relationship it grows out of, so the same physical act is a different good when it's done by a daughter than when it's done by a stranger for a wage? Or is this whole pattern explicable as plain cost-shifting plus a story about who is legible as a worker, with the relationship-talk as post-hoc decoration?

2. Which parts strictly require the first kind of view and which are philosophically underdetermined? Could someone who thinks care is just labor, and should be paid and organized like any other labor, still endorse family-first-and-free on other grounds — reliability, consent, the state not wanting to inspect homes, whatever — without conceding anything about the metaphysics of care?

3. The bit I can't make coherent, and I want you to tell me whether it's actually incoherent or whether I'm missing a unifying view. The institution seems to say three things at once: the relationship is a source of competence and motivation (that's why we trust her with the injection and count on her showing up); money corrupts care (that's why we won't pay her); and paid care is less trustworthy, so it gets less clinical responsibility. So the relationship is treated as what makes care good when the care is free, and money is treated as what ruins it — but money is also what we pay for the version we trust less. Is there one view of care that generates all three, or is the hospital running two incompatible theories side by side and relying on nobody asking?

4. Last thing, because I have to write this up. If you took the care-ethics picture seriously — dependency and responsiveness as the central moral facts rather than autonomy and contract — what would discharge planning have to actually look like? Would you pay care partners, refuse to treat kin as a resource at all, roster and cap their hours, give them standing in the decision-making, something else? I want to know where that view bites concretely, not just that it would be more caring.

I mainly need to know whether I'm describing an implicit theory of care here or just describing a budget. Either answer is useful to me.
