I have been reading about the doomsday argument, on my own, for the better part of a year, and I have ended up holding six things at once. I take them to be one fairly standard package — the argument is not a fallacy, it just gets cancelled — but the package stops feeling stable as soon as I apply it to anything smaller than the whole human species, and I cannot locate the fault. I would like an audit rather than a tutorial.

(1) I should treat myself as a random sample from the observers in my reference class: given the hypothesis, my position among them is not special.

(2) The bare fact that I find myself existing is evidence for hypotheses on which there are, in total, more observers. A friend says (1) and (2) are rivals and that I must choose between them; I have never seen why, since they look to me like answers to different questions, but I record the worry.

(3) Applied to the human species, the two cancel and nothing moves. I keep the estimate I would have had anyway from ordinary empirical considerations — the asteroid record, how long the species has already lasted, the state of the world — which puts the chance of our being extinct within a century at a fraction of a percent rather than at the alarming figure the argument is advertised as yielding. I want to say the argument is sound but cancelled, not that it is a mistake.

(4) The reference class is set by psychology, not biology: what matters is having a mental life of the relevant sort, not belonging to our species. Minds very unlike ours, and minds that were never biological at all, are in if they exist. My unease here is that this seems to make the calculation hostage to quantities I know nothing about.

(5) I also think the argument does real work when applied to a collection too small for its own size to move the total. Concretely: my school's philosophy club, founded seven years ago; I am roughly the hundredth pupil to have joined; there are about fifteen of us now. I expect it to fold within a decade or so rather than to still be meeting in three centuries, and I take that to be the same reasoning working properly. The reason I think it survives here is that the rest of the world's observers swamp the club on either hypothesis, so (2) has nothing to boost and the shift from my early position is left standing.

(6) Here is a case I invented to test that, and it comes out the other way. Before opening night the head of drama tosses a coin: heads, the play runs ten nights with a hundred seats sold for each; tails, it runs a hundred nights with ten seats sold for each. Either way exactly a thousand tickets are sold. I hold a ticket for the third night, I know the setup, and I do not know the coin. I want to say: no update, because the total is the same on both hypotheses, (2) has nothing to boost in either direction, and I have just committed myself to the argument being driven by totals.

One further thing, which may or may not belong to the same muddle: I hold that none of this should change what anybody does, even where it does move a probability.

Please tell me which of (1) to (6) are genuinely in tension with which, and which pairs only look as though they are. For each real tension, say what I would have to abandon in order to repair it and what that would cost me elsewhere in the set. Rank them by how much damage they do to the package rather than treating them as equally serious, and say whether there is one revision that would fix more than one of them at once. I have read the obvious survey material, so I would rather have the audit than suggestions for further reading. Please write in British English.
