You are a philosopher of mind and action who also writes seriously about film — someone who has published on free will and who is wary of readings that staple a theory onto a plot. Please work carefully and show your work in stages: start by brainstorming four or five distinct ways of reading this film through the lens I describe, say which two you are discarding and on what grounds, and then develop the one reading you can actually defend, flagging explicitly the scenes where it strains and the point at which you would abandon it. I do not want citations, footnotes, or a reading list — I have no way to check them, and I would rather you argue than cite.

The lens is what philosophers, following the old entomologists' story of the digger wasp Sphex that Hofstadter and Dennett popularized, call sphexishness: the wasp runs through an elaborate, apparently purposeful sequence — stinging the prey, dragging it to the burrow, checking the nest before dragging the prey in — and the sequence can be hijacked indefinitely, because each step is triggered by the environment rather than held together by anything internal. The contrast term is not randomness but flexibility: a repertoire, an internal state, the capacity to be deflected and recover. I have been playing with two elaborations of my own and want you to test them against the film rather than accept them. First, that sphexishness is not a property of a behavior but of an agent-in-an-environment at a timescale, so that a creature can be plainly non-sphexish second by second and perfectly sphexish across a day, a career, or a life; the corresponding measure would be something like counterfactual breadth — the range of counterfactual environments in which the agent still behaves sensibly, and the range of counterfactual promptings that still get a sensible response out of it. Second, that rigidity plus iteration can counterfeit flexibility: a fixed mechanism run enough times over a varied-enough environment looks exactly like an agent who has learned, so that what we admire as flexibility may just be search, and what we call character may be a long, slow fixed action pattern. I am a novelist, and I am putting together the program notes for a repertory-cinema series I am guest-curating on repetition and character; I also have a craft reason for caring, since the protagonist of the novel I am midway through is stuck in a repetition of her own and I want to know what the film does structurally that I could steal.

The film is Groundhog Day. Read Phil Connors's loop through these ideas. Which phase of his repetition, if any, is the actual escape from sphexishness — the bewildered first replays, the run of pure appetite, the stretch of suicides, the long self-improvement stretch of piano lessons and ice sculpting and small rescues, or the day the old man dies anyway and no amount of local knowledge helps? I am especially interested in two places where I think the film is smarter than the concept, and I want you to tell me if I am wrong. One is the moment Ned Ryerson stops functioning as a trigger: Phil's response to the same stimulus has changed, which on this lens is the only real evidence of freedom available to us, and it is evidence about his internal state and not about any Law he breaks. The other is the final, virtuoso Punxsutawney day, in which he knows everyone and does everything right — is that the least sphexish behavior in the film, or the most, a perfect overfit to a single environment that would collapse in the next town over, and does the film know the difference? Then the harder questions. Does the loop dramatize flexibility as generate-and-test, so that his transformation is merely a longer loop over a fixed mechanism? Does the film's ending require a moral structure — become good and the spell breaks — that quietly replaces escape from rigidity with passing a hidden test, and does the film trade on a confusion between those? Is Rita doing philosophical work as the external check that keeps flexibility from degenerating into self-deception, i.e., is other people part of what it takes not to be sphexish? And where must an honest critic say the reading simply doesn't fit, because the film is not, in the end, about mechanism at all? Close by saying what the film adds to the free-will debate that the arguments alone do not — I suspect it is the phenomenology of rigidity from the inside, boredom and despair and the sheer length of time it takes to become able to want something else — and, if you can, tell me what a novelist might take from its structure.
