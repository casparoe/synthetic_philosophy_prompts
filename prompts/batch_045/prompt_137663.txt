I teach a final-year seminar on the ethics of autonomous systems — 16 students, one 90-minute slot, chairs that move. They've already read the standard stuff about blame and automation, so no lecture up front, and ideally they shouldn't hear the phrase "responsibility gap" until after the debrief. What I want is an activity where they walk into the gap themselves and only later find out it has a name.

Design me something runnable. The shape in my head is an internal review board staged as a role-play, but improve on it wherever you like. Some system — an ICU discharge-risk model, ambulance re-routing, you pick the case — recommends something, a human is nominally in the loop, and someone dies. Constraits that matter to me: nobody lied, nobody was negligent in a clear-cut way, everyone was arguably doing their job properly, and the system was on the whole saving lives, so "it should never have been deployed" isn't available as an answer.

Mechanics I'd like worked in somewhere:

- a secret that only surfaces when they pool their private briefs: the human in the loop was more or less cosmetic. The override button existed, but the pipeline had already committed, or the alert threshold was set so high that no override had ever actually changed anything. Apparent control without real control.
- version drift: the model was retrained several times, the snapshot that made the decision wasn't archived, so it can't be re-run. They literally cannot interrogate the thing that did it.
- overdetermination: run the counterfactual test on any single person and the outcome comes out the same.
- a forced ending where everyone privately writes down a name (or "no one") plus a sanction, and you read them out. I'm expecting the blame to scatter with no target getting a majority, and that scatter is the point — so set it up so it produces that, or, if you think it's better, let them all converge on the obvious candidate and then take that candidate away. Your call, just tell me why.

The bit I care about most: they should come away able to tell apart three things that the one phrase papers over. First, nobody had the knowledge or control that blame seems to require. Second — and this is different — even granting that, somebody can still owe an explanation without being blameworthy. Third, separate again, there's a demand for outrage or punishment that has no legitimate object even though the harm is real and somebody has to answer for it. I'd like the activity to make them feel each of those on its own rather than mushing them together, and the debrief should map specific moments in the room onto each one.

Give me: the roles and their private briefs written out, materials, a timeline with minute markers, the debrief, an honest list of what will predictably go wrong — I'm guessing at least one group just blames the chief executive and another goes full "the algorithm did it" — plus how to handle each, and one thing the activity can't reproduce that I'll have to supply myself.

Lead with a single sentence giving the core mechanic and what it makes people feel, then everything else. No preamble.
