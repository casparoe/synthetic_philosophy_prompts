Hi. I've somehow ended up as the person at my work responsible for signing off the AI we've started using to draft decisions on home insurance claims, which is not a job I applied for and I'm a claims handler, not a philosopher, so apologies in advance if I mangle the vocabulary.

The setup the vendor sold us is what they call process supervision. The model writes out its working before it gives a recommendation, and instead of us just checking whether the recommendation was right, reviewers (mostly our own staff, an hour a week each) go through the working step by step and mark each step. Fine so far. But the grading rule they gave us is what I keep getting stuck on. It says a step counts as good if a competent human handler could have written it given what was in front of them at that point in the chain, even if the step turns out to lead nowhere and the model abandons it three lines later. And a step counts as bad if it only looks fine because you already know where the chain ends up — so we're told to actively discount hindsight. But then the same guidance says to mark a wrong step down less if the model catches and corrects itself a couple of steps later. Those two instructions feel like they're pulling in opposite directions, and I can't tell whether that's me misreading it or an actual tension in what we're doing.

There's surrounding stuff that seems to sit oddly with it too. We only send traces for review when a random audit picks them or when the final recommendation looks wrong, so most of what we actually grade is failure — but we're told to ignore the final answer while grading the steps, which in practice nobody manages to do. And review time costs a fortune, so we're quietly told to spend it on the expensive claims and let the small ones go ungraded, which seems to assume the value of good reasoning scales with the size of the claim in a way nobody's ever explained.

So: what does this practice presuppose? Not the sales pitch — the view it only really makes sense under. Is there a fact of the matter about whether an individual step is correct, independent of where the chain goes, or is that just a convenient fiction for splitting up the marking? Does grading the working assume that the written-out reasoning is the reasoning the model actually did, rather than a story it produces afterwards to justify a decision it reached some other way, and does anything collapse if that's false? Does it assume a right decision reached by bad reasoning is genuinely worth less to us than a right decision reached by good reasoning, given the customer only ever sees the decision? Which parts of it are strictly required and which are just things it happens to fit well with — could we drop the hindsight rule and still justify the rest?

And lastly, if we stopped buying the whole process-grading idea and went back to just scoring outcomes, what would we actually have to change about how the system is built or audited? Would anything we currently do stop making sense, or would it just be a different way of paying for the same thing?
