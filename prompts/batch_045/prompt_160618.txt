Right, quick one. I've got a fifty-minute slot next week with about twenty first-years — mostly CS, a few psychology — and I want to spend it making them *feel* what it means to induce a concept from a handful of examples. Bongard problems are the vehicle: six figures on the left, six on the right, some rule separates them.

Don't solve any for me and don't write the explanation I should give. Give me the plan for the explanation.

Some vocabulary first so we're not talking past each other — these are my words, rename them if mine are bad. A rule *fits* a problem if it's true of everything in the left panel and false of everything in the right. The *intended* rule is the one the setter had in mind, which needn't be the only one that fits. A *list rule* fits by just naming the left-hand figures ("it's one of these six") — it works by construction and generalises to nothing. And call a problem *brittle* if deleting any single figure from either panel lets a rule strictly simpler than the intended one fit.

What I want back:

1. The order the pieces have to go in, and what depends on what. My hunch is that "there's no unique answer" lands completely differently depending on whether they've already tried and failed to find one, but tell me if I've got that backwards.
2. The one problem to build the whole session around, and what it has to have and not have. I'm thinking something geometric where the intended rule is about how the shapes relate to *each other* rather than a property each shape has on its own, and where a list rule is obviously on offer — but say if that's one example doing too much work.
3. The misconceptions, in the order they'll actually turn up, and exactly where in the sequence to head each off. Ones I'm expecting: that the task is to find the rule; that "simplest rule wins" is obvious rather than a choice of description language; that more examples would sort it out; that fitting and generalising are the same thing. Add the ones I've missed, and definately tell me if one of mine isn't worth the time.
4. One thing I should flatly refuse to go into at this level, and say out loud that I'm refusing it. My instinct is to dodge formal learning theory entirely — VC dimension and that sort of thing — but maybe the honest move is one sentence of hand-waving instead of silence.
5. The question I ask in the last five minutes that tells me whether the idea landed rather than whether they enjoyed themselves.

And last: flag the bit of the plan you're least confident about, and say what would change your mind — what argument, or what actually happening in the room, would make you reorder it or swap the example out.
