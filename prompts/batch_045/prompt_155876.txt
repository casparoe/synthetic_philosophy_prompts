I chair the conduct committee of a national professional register. We hear about forty cases a year, in monthly sittings, with roughly ninety minutes per case; we can censure, suspend, or strike a member off, our decisions are published in anonymized form, and none of the panelists has more philosophy than an undergraduate course. I want to replace our current "reasonable person" test — which in practice has collapsed into "what our peers would have done," the very partiality Smith warns against — with a standing decision procedure built out of his account of sympathy and the impartial spectator.

Give me the procedure: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it returns "no sanction" and stops, and where it hands the question back to the panel as a judgment no procedure can settle. Four things it has to get right, each of which has already cost us a case:

1. The spectator's information set. Smith holds that actual spectators are dragged about by how things turned out, while the spectator within the breast judges from the agent's circumstances and motives. May our spectator know the outcome? Answer yes or no, and say what we lose either way.
2. Propriety versus merit. Most of our complaints concern conduct that demeaned someone but broke no rule. Smith separates the fitness of a sentiment to its cause from desert of punishment — but punishment, on his account, is keyed to the resentment of the person affected, and in our cases the affected person is often grateful, or absent, or impossible to identify.
3. Whose situation we are required to enter by sympathetic imagination, how long that step takes, and how to keep it from becoming an uninformed guess dressed up as sympathy.
4. The deference we feel toward rank and reputation, for which Smith has an explanation, and which appears every time a junior complains about a senior or the reverse.

Then run the procedure on three of our actual kinds of case, one of which must come out somewhere I will not like — tell me which and why: a senior member who publicly humiliated a junior during a ward round, where the junior says it was bracing, has refused to complain, and the complaint comes from a bystander; a member who falsified forty hours of continuing-education records, discovered in a routine audit, which nobody ever relied on and which harmed no one; and two members who made the same judgment call on the same information, one of whom got lucky and one of whom did not.

Close by saying which of Smith's distinctions must survive translation into a ninety-minute procedure run by non-philosophers, which can be dropped, and where the procedure will misfire. Please write in American English.
