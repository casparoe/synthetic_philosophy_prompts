Every spring my department puts on a short satirical revue for the graduate students — last year's was on the measurement problem, and this year we drew "logic" out of the hat. I've been given a two-minute slot on dialetheism and paraconsistent logic, and what I need is one genuinely good joke to anchor it, not a string of puns. The room will be about forty philosophers plus a scattering of partners and children, so the joke has to land for people who actually know the material — the punchline should turn on the real machinery (non-explosion, truth-value gluts as opposed to gaps, designated values, and the fact that a non-explosive consequence relation buys its tolerance of contradiction by surrendering some other inference) — while still getting a laugh from everyone else present. Please skip the two gags that are already exhausted: the one where a single contradiction lets the speaker prove something absurd, and the one about the logician who cannot order from a menu.

Here is the scenario I've sketched; rework it freely, but keep something like it. A provincial railway station's lost-property office has had its paper ledger replaced by a records system that, through some procurement accident, validates inferences paraconsistently rather than classically. Certain entries are flagged as gluts — items that are both signed in and not signed in — and because the system does not explode, the archive has quietly stopped delivering the consequences everyone had assumed came free. The practical catastrophe I want to joke about is the loss of disjunctive syllogism: the clerk can establish that the missing umbrella is either in the cage or on the shelf, and that it is not in the cage, and still not be entitled to the conclusion that it is on the shelf. (I also have it in my head that modus ponens for the material conditional is similarly unsafe in a glutty setting; if that's wrong, say so in the note rather than building anything on it.) If you can get a second beat out of the contrast between a glut and a gap — the umbrella that is neither on the shelf nor not on the shelf — so much the better.

Write it as something I can actually perform: two or three sentences of setup, the punchline, and one optional tag line, 130 words at the very most in total, with no stage directions, no footnotes and no citations — nobody in that room needs a bibliography, and I can't check quotations from the stage, so don't quote anyone. Then add a short note, kept separate from the bit itself, telling me: which single feature of the logic the joke leans hardest on; where you think it takes liberties — in particular whether my inconsistent ledger really depicts a glut rather than a plain recording error or a truth-value gap, whether a dialetheist would accept a bureaucratic inconsistency as a candidate dialetheia at all, and anything else a specialist in the audience would wince at; and what would change your mind, i.e. the one specific criticism of the bit that you'd accept as a reason to pull it or rewrite the punchline rather than defend it.
