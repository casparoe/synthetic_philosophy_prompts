hi — I'm a journalist, mostly cover AI and the science-of-decision-making stuff adjacent to it. I'm finishing a short piece on two puzzles that keep coming up in alignment-flavoured decision theory, and I want to close the piece with an actual poem instead of another paragraph of hedging. But I want it to be right — the kind of right where someone who actually works on this wouldnt wince, not a poem that just says "paradox!" a lot.

Here are the two cases in my own words. Please work from these rather than from whatever canonical version you have memorised.

Counterfactual mugging: a predictor you have every reason to trust flips a coin. Heads, and it hands you a large sum — but only if it predicted that you would hand over a small sum in the tails branch. Tails, and it turns up and asks for the small sum. You only ever get asked in the branch where the money is already out of reach, so paying can't cause anything. The only way to be the sort of agent that got paid in the heads branch is to be the sort that pays. (The way I picture the heads branch: the predictor ran a copy of you before the coin, and it's that copy's answer that collected.)

XOR blackmail: someone sends you a demand for money and threatens something bad — say a bomb in your car. The trick is that the letter is sent if and only if exactly one of two things is true: there's a bomb, or you would pay up. So a payer who finds a letter in the post has no bomb; a non-payer who finds one definitely does. Paying is causally inert. What arrives is a message about you.

What I want the poem to carry is the hinge both cases turn on: what you're allowed to mean by "if I had decided otherwise" — the counterfactual on your own decision procedure, the copy of you that ran in the predictor's head, the version of you the blackmailer predicted. In one case leaning on that hinge is how you get paid, and in the other leaning on it just buys you good news. My rough map, which you should correct if it's wrong: theories that treat your decision as a physical act with causal effects refuse in both; theories that ask what would be best news to hear you'd done pay the blackmailer; theories that treat the decision as the output of a function that gets instantiated in more than one place pay in the mugging and still tell the blackmailer no.

Form: a pantoum, and I'll spell out the version I mean because it seems to have variants. Four-line stanzas; the second and fourth lines of each stanza become the first and third lines of the next; and in the final stanza the two lines you'd otherwise have to invent are the poem's first and third lines back again, in reverse order, so the poem's last line is its first line. Five stanzas, twenty lines. The reason I want that form and not a free verse ramble: a line keeps coming back in a new slot and meaning something different, which is more or less what a simulation of you does, and the swap at the end should land as the XOR — the same two lines, exchanged, where it matters which one is where.

Otherwise: plain words, second person, present tense, no oracular register. Don't name the theories inside the poem; someone who knows them should be able to tell which case is which and where one turns into the other. Don't turn the choice into a fable about courage or integrity or keeping your word — it's a question about what a counterfactual on your own output even is. The thing that should land as the poem's turn is the reader realising the two cases are the same two lines crossed.

After the poem, a short note, a paragraph or two and not an essay: (1) what's settled here — I assume the setups and which theory gives which verdict is just bookkeeping, but say so if even that is disputed; (2) what's genuinely contested among people who work on this — whether paying in the mugging is correct, whether counterfactuals on your own output are coherent, whether the simulation is you; and (3) where your poem quietly took a side or fudged something. I don't want to print a live disagreement as the consensus; my editor will check the framing but not the philosophy, so I need you to seperate the two for me.
