I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy background beyond a couple of pop-science books. I'm putting together a short talk for my team about why distributed systems deadlock, and I want to open with something lighter. Please write me five jokes about Buridan's ass and the problem of choosing between two options when the agent is exactly indifferent between them.

Format: reply as a numbered list of exactly five items and nothing else — no title, no opening line, no closing remark. Each item is one joke, one or two sentences, self-contained, and readable aloud. I would rather have five real jokes, each with a setup and a payoff, than five wry observations.

On content, I want the humor to come from the actual structure of the puzzle rather than from the surface image of a donkey between two piles of hay, and I'd like the five jokes to make five different points, one apiece:

(a) In a case of exact indifference there is no option that maximizes expected utility, so the difficulty is not weakness of will but that the theory has nothing to say.
(b) Delegating the choice to a coin flip, or to a fixed tie-break rule such as always take the left-hand option, only relocates the arbitrariness one level up, since adopting the coin or the rule is itself an unforced choice among equals.
(c) If you insist that every action needs a reason which discriminates between the options, the failure is not a rare corner case: choosing between interchangeable items generally fails, and the animal starving to death in the middle is the reductio of that requirement rather than a curiosity.
(d) The two options are equally good and the two options cannot be compared at all are different diagnoses, and they push the story in different directions.
(e) There is a real position on which an agent can simply pick, neither at random nor for a reason, and a competing position on which genuine indifference never actually occurs because some trivial asymmetry is always there to break the tie.

Constraints: no puns on donkey, ass, or hay; no generic indecisiveness humor; nothing that turns on who historically wrote what, since I gather Buridan may not even have used the donkey and my audience would not know either. At least two of the five should be set somewhere my team will recognize — a leader election that never completes, a scheduler placing a job on two nodes with identical spare capacity, a code review with two byte-identical patches, a test that passes or fails depending on nothing at all. The standard I'll use is whether someone who actually works on rational choice would say the joke gets the puzzle right: if a punchline would work just as well with the topic swapped out for something else, I don't want it.
