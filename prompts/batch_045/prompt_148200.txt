Dear ——,

I wonder whether you can help me with something I have been unable to name to my own satisfaction. It came up again in last week's graduate seminar on well-being, and I would like to know whether there is an established concept for it, or whether it is genuinely unmarked territory that I should christen myself.

Take three pleasures with nothing sensuous in common: the first swallow of ice water after a long run in the heat; the moment a proof that has resisted you for a week finally closes; hearing that a rival's paper has been rejected. I take the heterogeneity of such lists as given and do not need to be persuaded of it. My difficulty begins one step later.

Every unifier I can get the seminar to accept turns out to do one job well and the other badly. The candidates that cover all three cases — being pleased that something is so, liking what is happening to you, wanting the experience to continue — are thin, structural, and non-qualitative. That thinness is exactly what lets them cover the whole range; and it is, as far as I can tell, the same thinness that leaves them unable to rank anything. The moment we ask how much pleasure is involved, the comparison is quietly made by something else: the intensity of the desire, the duration, or how much the object matters. Worse, in my own three cases the thin unifier seems to give the wrong answer outright. Drink more ice water and the first pleasure is destroyed by satiation, so it is not plausibly a pleasure you want continued. The proof's pleasure is completed and thereby ended, so what is wanted is not its continuation but its consummation. And the malicious case may involve the strongest desire of the three and the least that we are inclined to count as pleasure at all.

So: a unifier whose power to include every case is bought at the cost of its power to discriminate among them, with the consequence that comparisons get run on a borrowed scale. Is there a name for this, and if so, how are its edges drawn?

Let me say what I think it is not, since that is where I expect to be misunderstood. It is not the familiar objection that pleasures share no common quality: that objection is a demand for some shared property, and the thin unifiers I am complaining about satisfy it. It is not Mill's distinction between higher and lower pleasures: that is a claim about kinds, and it seems to presuppose that pleasures can still be compared in amount within and across those kinds. It is not the experience-machine style worry that we care about more than how our lives feel from the inside: that concerns whether pleasure is all that matters, not what our hedonic comparisons track. And it is not plain incomparability: I am not claiming such comparisons cannot be made, only that when they are made they are made by proxy.

If nothing fits, I would like a term with a definition, a list of the neighbouring phenomena it must be distinguished from, and a verdict on whether the trade-off is really a defect — since it is open to someone to argue that hedonic comparison was always meant to be proxy-driven, and that an appeal to the preferences of those acquainted with both pleasures is already a concession of that. A bare label, in either case, will not help me.
