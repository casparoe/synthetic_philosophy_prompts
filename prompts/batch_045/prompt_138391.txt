Here's roughly how the book puts the view (I'm paraphrasing a couple of sentences but this is close to what it says):

"Consciousness is an illusion. Philosophers call the felt quality of experience qualia — the redness of red, the hurt of a headache — and the illusionist says there's nothing in the brain that actually has those feels. What exists is physical information-processing, plus a set of beliefs the brain generates about its own activity. Those beliefs represent certain brain states as having qualities they don't have, the way a stick in water looks bent, and so we end up convinced that there's something it is like to be us. Strictly speaking, then, we're all philosophical zombies: a zombie is something that behaves exactly as we do but has no inner light on inside, and the only thing separating us from one is that we're zombies who insist, wrongly, that we aren't. Since the only evidence anyone ever had for qualia was introspection, and introspection is itself just a brain process that can't check itself from outside, there's no reason to think qualia are real, and the burden of proof shifts to anyone who says otherwise. Once you see this the mystery dissolves: consciousness was never a thing needing explanation, just a bad inference from the way the brain labels its own states, and all that's left for science is the ordinary job of figuring out what our experience-talk is really tracking — namely behavior."

Can you go through that more or less sentence by sentence and seperate out what a philosopher who actually defends illusionism would accept, what they'd wince at as a simplification, and what they'd flat out reject? I'm specially interested in (a) the zombie line, (b) the stick-in-water analogy, and whether it's doing more work than it looks like it's doing, and (c) whether calling the upshot "behaviorism" is fair. Also, the book later says that if this is right, a machine that matched our behavior and made all the same discriminations would be conscious in exactly the sense we are, and there'd be no fact of the matter that could set us apart from it. Does that follow from the parts of the summary that are actually accurate, or is it sliding in extra commitments?
