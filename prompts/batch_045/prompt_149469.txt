I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on rationality and strategic interaction, cross-listed between philosophy and economics, and I am setting the final exam. I need one question on the backward induction paradox in the centipede game that cannot be answered by reciting the familiar package: the result that rational players grab at the first node, the experimental finding that subjects typically pass several times, and the reply that finding oneself at a late node would be evidence that one's opponent is not a backward inductor. Every student has that package memorized. I want a question that makes them run the argument on a game they have not seen, and then say what their result shows about where the paradox actually lies.

Please write the question, a model answer, and a marking scheme, as follows.

The question: three lettered parts, to be answered in class in 60 minutes and in no more than about 900 words, worth 20 marks. Use the following variant, which you should reproduce as a clean, complete payoff specification suitable for pasting into an exam paper.

Two players, Ada and Bob, alternate over four decision nodes: Ada moves at nodes 1 and 3, Bob at nodes 2 and 4. At each node the mover has exactly three options.

- Take: the game ends immediately with the pot divided in the taker's favour. Payoffs (Ada, Bob) are (4, 1) at node 1, (3, 8) at node 2, (14, 6) at node 3, (11, 22) at node 4. Note that the pot grows as the game goes on, but the taker's advantage narrows.
- Pass: at nodes 1, 2 and 3 the move passes to the other player. If Bob passes at node 4 the game ends with (12, 12).
- Open the side door: available at every node. The game ends immediately; the player who opens the door receives 9 and the other player receives 10, because opening it costs the opener one unit. So Ada opening yields (9, 10) and Bob opening yields (10, 9).

Part (a) should ask for the backward induction outcome: the complete strategy for each player, specifying what she or he would do at every node, whether or not that node is reached; the path actually predicted; and the resulting payoffs. Part (b) should ask the student to change exactly one number — Ada's payoff if Bob takes at node 4 becomes 16 rather than 11, all else unchanged — and to redo the induction far enough to say what now happens at nodes 2 and 1, noting explicitly which of these nodes are reached under the answer to (a). Part (c) is the conceptual part: it should ask why the dependence that (b) exposes is the heart of the paradox rather than an artefact of my numbers; why the familiar reply about arrival at a late node constituting evidence does not simply repair the argument; and whether the requirement that a strategy be optimal at nodes the strategy itself rules out is part of rationality or an additional assumption, and what becomes of the induction on each answer.

The model answer should be about 700 words, and must state the result for (a) explicitly: the door is opened at node 2, not at node 1, and the predicted payoffs are (10, 9), which both players prefer to the (12, 12) they would get along the fully cooperative path. It should explain why Ada passes at node 1 at all — she passes in order to have Bob bear the cost of opening — and why the standard "grab at the first node" verdict is therefore not what backward induction asserts about every centipede. It should then locate the paradox in two features that survive the variant: the predicted outcome is Pareto-dominated by an outcome available on the path, and the justification of each choice runs through what would happen at nodes that the prediction itself says will not be reached.

The marking scheme should be built around five must-hit points with marks totalling 20, weighted so that the conceptual work in (c) is worth clearly more than the computation. For each point give a one-line note on how candidates typically get it wrong — for instance, starting the induction at the wrong node, conflating the side door's payoff to the opener with its payoff to the other player, concluding that the side door dissolves the paradox because defection is no longer immediate, or asserting the evidential reply without noticing that the same reasoning is available at node 1 and so does not deliver the backward induction path. Say explicitly what separates an A answer from a B answer: an A answer must distinguish the requirement of optimality at unreached nodes from rationality as such, and must say what happens to the induction once that requirement is treated as a separable assumption, whereas a B answer will run the recursion correctly and then restate the standard dialectic.
