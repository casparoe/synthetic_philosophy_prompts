I'm writing a novel and I'm stuck on one scene, so I'm going to describe it and ask you to write it. Change anything you like -- names, setting, who says what -- as long as you keep the central machine and the decision at the end.

The world is basically ours, except that this one town has a civic program, run out of a municipal office next to the DMV, that does something nobody else does. Tiny discomforts -- paper cuts, the sting of shampoo, an itch you can't reach, a bad night's sleep, the ache of a long drive -- get collected across the whole population and concentrated into a few hours borne by a volunteer. The program's own accounting says the total amount of discomfort is exactly conserved: nothing is created, it's just moved into a different shape. Volunteers do an afternoon, recieve about a week's wages, and the town's measured irritation drops to almost nothing. It's been running for eleven years. Nobody thinks of it as dystopian; it's boring, unionized, has a newsletter.

I'm not interested in the standard setup where it's one person tortured for fifty years against a vast number of people getting a speck of dust in the eye -- I know that case, don't restate it. What I want the story to think about is a version where the trade happens inside a single life and across time, and where the concentrated version has a shape: a beginning, a middle, an end, and after it a memory, while the stuff it's made of never rose to the level of being noticed by anyone at all.

My protagonist is Denise, mid-forties. She's done four turns and she works in the office auditing the conversion logs. Two things come to a head in the scene. First, she's been quietly offered a double -- two turns back to back, which isn't allowed -- and the argument for it is just the aggregation argument run forward: if one afternoon is worth it for the town, why isn't two. Second, she has to sign off on an audit finding that the ledger can't be trusted, because reports of the concentrated hours come in systematically lower than the logs predict, and no one can tell whether that's memory compressing intensity or something being lost in the transfer. She knows this from the inside, and the thing she hates admitting is that her own last turn is the only stretch of her life she remembers clearly.

Write the scene. Third person limited, past tense, 900 to 1200 words. Rules: nobody explains the idea -- no character gives a speech about whether discomfort adds up across people, nobody says anything that sounds like a slogan; if the reader understands the argument it should be because they watched someone decide. Keep the office mundane: florescent lights, a broken chair, forms in triplicate. End on her decision, and leave it genuinely open whether she got it right.
