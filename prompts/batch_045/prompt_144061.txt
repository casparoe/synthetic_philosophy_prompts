I sit on the governance group of a mid-sized AI lab, and I have been asked to draft the position we will take on releasing the weights of frontier models. Before the draft circulates I want a consistency check on the commitments I have written into it, because I cannot tell which of them are doing real work and which are decoration. The draft asserts:

(a) Openness is the default, and the burden of justification falls on whoever would restrict release: a lab must be able to point to specific, articulable evidence of serious risk before it withholds weights, and it may not withhold them on the basis of unspecified or merely conceivable danger.

(b) Open weights make systems safer in expectation. Independent researchers reproduce failures, find them faster and in greater numbers than internal review does, and external scrutiny is a check on the lab's own commercial and reputational incentives.

(c) Releasing weights is irreversible. There is no recall, no patch, and no way to withdraw the capability once it is out; publication is therefore a one-shot act with permanent consequences rather than a decision that can be revisited in the light of what it turns up.

(d) Release should be gated on pre-registered dangerous-capability evaluations, covering uplift in the biological, cyber and self-proliferation domains, with the threshold fixed in advance: a model below the threshold is released, a model above it is not.

(e) The license should impose no field-of-use restrictions. Downstream users may fine-tune as they wish, including to remove refusal behaviour, because the alternative is a lab writing its own contested value judgments into terms of use and enforcing them on everyone else.

(f) The evaluations in (d) should be run by independent third parties funded by the developer: not self-certified by the developer, and not administered as a government licensing regime.

(g) Labs should be legally liable for foreseeable downstream harms caused by the models they release.

(h) The case for openness is strongest, not weakest, at the frontier, since that is where power over the technology concentrates and where the costs of a small number of firms setting the terms are largest.

Two notes on how to read this. First, I would rather find out about a tension I have not noticed than have my hand held, so please say plainly where these conflict and, where they conflict, which one has to move. Second, one colleague says (e) and (g) cannot both be true, and another says (a) and (d) just restate each other; I do not trust either reaction and I would like to know whether either is right.

What I want from you is an audit rather than a position paper. Please: rank the genuine tensions among these commitments by severity rather than presenting them as an undifferentiated list; for each, state precisely what has to give and what the cheapest available revision is, together with what that revision costs elsewhere in the set; identify which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection and explain why; and name the single revision that would clean up the most. Where a tension turns on an empirical claim rather than a conceptual one, mark it as such and say which way the evidence would have to point to settle it. Where any commitment is ambiguous in a way that matters to your verdict, say which reading you are assessing. If the set is in fact consistent as it stands and I am wrong to worry, say that instead.
