I'm in an undergraduate metaphysics seminar this term and we've just finished the unit on material constitution. My essay defended the standard constitution-without-identity picture: the statue and the lump of clay that makes it up are not identical, because the lump can survive being squashed into a ball and rolled out again while the statue cannot, and identity preserves modal properties (Leibniz's Law); so there are two distinct material objects in exactly the same place at the same time, made of exactly the same matter. My tutor's written comment was that the argument is fine as far as it goes but that it generalises somewhere I would not want to follow, and I have been trying to reconstruct what she meant. The version I came up with runs like this.

(1) If x is identical to y, then x and y share all their modal properties, and in particular they share their persistence conditions: exactly the same changes are survivable by each.

(2) The lump L can survive being squashed into a ball; the statue S cannot. (Squashing destroys S; restoring the shape later would yield at best a perfect replica, not S again.)

(3) So S is not identical to L. Two coincident objects, one constituted by the other.

(4) The only difference between S and L that could be doing any work in (1) through (3) is the difference in what it takes for each to go on existing. At the time in question they have the same matter, the same shape, the same position, the same causal powers; everything they differ in is a matter of which changes they would survive.

(5) Generalising, then: for objects that coincide and share all their matter, a difference in persistence conditions is by itself enough for non-identity, and the difference need not be grounded in anything about the matter at the time.

(6) Now the case my tutor raised. A clay statue stands in a museum for a century, and each year conservators replace the outermost micrometre of weathered clay with fresh clay of the same composition, holding the shape fixed. Call the statue at the start S and the statue at the end S-star. Ordinary practice, the art historians and the museum label all say that S-star is S: artifacts tolerate the gradual replacement of their parts.

(7) But no portion of matter tolerates a change of parts. A lump has the parts it has essentially, so every lump that coincides with S at some moment of the century has ceased to exist by the end of it.

(8) By (5), then, S is not identical with any lump or portion of matter at any point in its career: at each instant it is constituted by some composite of matter that it is not identical with, and those composites come and go while S remains.

What I cannot swallow are two things that seem to fall out. First, if bare differences in persistence conditions are enough to multiply coincident objects, and nothing in the present arrangement of the matter settles which persistence conditions are in play, then nothing in the gallery settles how many statue-like objects are on the plinth: any coherent profile of which changes would be survived — surface replacement but not core replacement, replacement down to the last gram but not of that gram, and so on — looks like it defines a further coincident object with as good a claim as any to be the statue, and the question how many statues are in the room, which I would have thought had a determinate answer, apparently has none. Second, in the museum case the statue appears to have come loose from its matter: it is supposed to be a material object, yet it is never identical with any of the matter composing it at any time, and what makes it persist is nothing about the condition of that matter. One friend tells me to deny (2) and say the statue survives squashing after all; another says I should just be a four-dimensionalist and treat S as a worm with stages; a third says the honest conclusion is eliminativism, that there is no statue but only clay arranged statue-wise. All three feel like ways of changing the subject rather than locating the mistake, but I cannot say why.

So: which step in the chain above is the one to reject, and why did that step look compulsory when I wrote it down? I would like, if I can, to keep (1) through (3) and the datum in (6) that the museum's statue really is the same statue after a century of conservation, and I would rather not be told that the number of statues on the plinth is indeterminate. Please name the step you would give up, explain what made it seem safe, and price the alternatives — including the three escapes my friends offered — so I can see what each of them commits me to elsewhere in the course.
