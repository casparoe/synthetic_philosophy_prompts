DRAFT: Your brain is not telling you what it's doing

1. Every service I've ever operated has a dashboard. The dashboard shows a picture of the system, but nobody would confuse the two. A graph of p99 latency isn't the latency; it's a rendering, sampled, aggregated, and rounded off into something a human can act on inside a few seconds. It omits almost everything. It also doesn't just omit — it editorializes, because whoever built it decided which numbers matter. Crucially, it exists *for someone*: an operator with a job to do.

2. Now look at what conscious experience leaves out. You are not currently aware of the visual processing that lets you read this sentence. You were not aware of your shoes for the last hour. There's a hole in your visual field where the optic nerve exits and you never notice it. Fill-in and dropout are everywhere once you go looking. What conscious experience seems to track is not state but *change* — specifically, change relative to what the system predicted. That's exactly the design brief for an alerting dashboard: don't show me the healthy thing, show me the deviation.

3. Here's the part that actually flipped me. When I write an incident postmortem, I produce a clean causal narrative: this alert fired, that deploy caused it, we rolled back. The narrative is genuinely useful and genuinely later than the events, and it is definitely simpler than what happened, which was four people making overlapping guesses in a Slack thread. I suspect the feeling of having *decided* something is the same kind of object: a summary the system writes after the action was already selected, and then backdates. On this view consciousness isn't the thing doing the work. It's the changelog.

4. If that's only a metaphor it's worth nothing, so here's where I try to cash it out. An interface's job is to make a system legible to something else, which means it should get *thicker* where the system has to explain or justify itself, and *thinner* where behavior is automatic and well-practiced. Two predictions follow. First, expertise should feel like less: in flow states, people perform better and report less deliberation, and I think that's not a paradox but the expected behavior of a reporting layer that only spins up when the fast path fails. Second, when the real cause of a behavior is genuinely unavailable to the reporting layer, the layer should not go quiet — it should generate a plausible reason anyway. There are experiments in the ballpark of this: people pick one of two face photos, the experimenter swaps them by sleight of hand, and a surprising number of subjects confidently explain why they preferred the photo they didn't pick. That is what a fallback path looks like in software: return *something* well-formed rather than crash.

5. The piece I haven't seen anyone make, and the reason I started writing: an *addition* test rather than a subtraction test. Everyone argues about what happens if you remove consciousness. Try adding a channel instead. Suppose we give someone a novel sense — a belt that buzzes toward magnetic north, or a direct feed of some internal telemetry like blood glucose or their own reaction-time jitter. A dashboard model predicts something specific and slightly weird: the new channel should never arrive raw. It should be absorbed into the existing summary, and because summaries only carry deviations, the subject should mostly *not feel anything*, except at moments when the value moves unexpectedly. You'd get a sense with the phenomenology of balance — invisible until it breaks. Whereas if conscious experience is some basic feature of certain kinds of physical processing, you'd naively expect a new kind of "what it's like" to show up whenever you add a new kind of processing. Call it the sparse-channel argument. I realize twenty years of sensory-substitution work may already bear on this and I don't know which way it points, which is part of why I'm asking.

6. And here's the objection I can't get past. A dashboard nobody reads isn't a dashboard. If the conscious summary isn't consumed by anything, in what sense is it an *interface* as opposed to just an output? And if it is consumed, by whom? Either there's a homunculus in there reading the screen, which just restarts the problem one level down, or the consumer is the rest of the system — in which case consciousness is a message the machine sends to itself, my metaphor quietly collapses into "it's a process like any other," and there's no longer anything here to have an opinion about. I keep going back and forth on whether that collapse is a bug in my argument or the actual point: maybe the right conclusion is deflationary, and the leftover question isn't a deep mystery about experience but an engineering question about which subsystems have read access to which logs.

7. Where this leaves me, unsatisfyingly: it doesn't settle anything about AI, which was sort of why I started. If consciousness is an interface with a job, then asking whether a large language model is conscious is a bit like asking whether a cron job has a dashboard. You can't settle it from the output, and the answer depends on whether anything on the inside consumes the summary. So either the question is empty or it's empirical in a way we currently have no instruments for, and I genuinely can't tell which.
