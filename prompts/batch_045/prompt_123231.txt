You are a philosopher of science who specialises in causation and mechanistic explanation and who has spent years embedded with engineering organisations. Think carefully, and before writing the procedure itself, spend a short paragraph listing the distinctions you take to be load-bearing here and the ones you are deliberately collapsing for usability; then hold to those choices.

I run the reliability rota for a machine-vision quality-control system: fourteen plants, one model, a camera rig per line, and an unsupervised anomaly detector that fires when incoming frames or model confidences stop looking like the training distribution. It fires about thirty times a week. The question my team actually faces is never whether something is anomalous but whether two alarms are the same thing — because that decides whether we open one ticket or ten, whether a fix is expected to generalise to other plants, and whether we can tell a plant manager that a weekend of downtime will buy us anything.

Right now we triage by clustering alarms on embedding similarity, which I have come to think is the mistake: it clusters signatures, not mechanisms. Write me a procedure my on-call engineers can run in about twenty minutes with no philosophy background and no ability to query the literature. Build it out of tests rather than definitions, and centre it on the idea that a mechanism is whatever a single well-chosen intervention would suppress, while being explicit about what the engineer should do when the intervention that would separate two hypotheses is one we are not permitted to run: we cannot stop a line without the plant manager's sign-off, and we cannot retrain or roll back the model without a change ticket. The output should be exactly one of four labels: same mechanism; different mechanisms; same mechanism, different trigger; underdetermined. I also want you to say whether sameness has to be relativised to a level — sensor, preprocessing, model, actuator — and, if so, at which step the engineer fixes the level, since I suspect our loudest disagreements are people answering at different levels without noticing.

The hard cases the procedure must handle, and none of which it may simply declare out of scope: two alarms with nearly identical signatures produced by different mechanisms; one mechanism that surfaces with two different signatures; alarms that co-occur because one mechanism's output is another mechanism's input, rather than because they are the same mechanism; a mechanism that manifests only when two individually mild triggers are both present; and a mechanism that has drifted slowly enough that whether it is still the one we fixed last quarter has no crisp answer. Mark plainly where the procedure bottoms out into judgement, and who should be the one to make that call.
