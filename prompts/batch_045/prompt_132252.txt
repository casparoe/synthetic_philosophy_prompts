I'm a first-year PhD student in philosophy, and I've been trying to turn a seminar paper into something I could actually send out, so I'd be grateful for a close reading of my argument rather than general reassurance. The short version: I think I can get from purely descriptive premises to a genuinely normative conclusion, and my supervisor keeps telling me that what I'm doing is just the naturalistic fallacy. I don't see how that can be right. As I understand it, the naturalistic fallacy is Moore's point about *defining* the good — his open-question argument is supposed to show that any proposed definition of "good" in natural terms leaves a further question standing, so naturalistic definitions fail — whereas Hume's is/ought gap is a claim about *inference*: you can't get an "ought" into the conclusion unless there was one somewhere in the premises. I'm not defining "good" as anything at all, so Moore's argument doesn't engage me; I'm deriving, and Hume is the only real obstacle. (My roommate, who's a biologist, insists that the naturalistic fallacy is just the appeal-to-nature thing — "it's natural so it's fine" — which seems to be a third thing entirely. It would help me to know whether these are three names for one mistake or three genuinely different mistakes, and if they're different, which one my supervisor is accusing me of.)

Here is the argument; she calls it a "constitutivist sleight of hand," but she hasn't told me where the sleight happens. Call it the accountability bridge. (1) In any community that has the practice of holding people to account — blame, demands for justification, sanctions, the whole package — it is constitutive of that practice that participants treat one another as having reason to comply with the standards the practice enforces. (2) Claim (1) is descriptive rather than evaluative: it's a fact about what the practice *is*, of the same kind an anthropologist could report, and it commits nobody to thinking the standards in question are good ones. (3) I, and you, and everyone I could possibly address the argument to, do in fact participate in practices of that kind. Therefore (4) there are things we have reason to do and to refrain from doing, and in particular the person blamed in the case at hand had reason not to do it. I take this to close Hume's gap rather than leap over it, because the normative content of the conclusion is already sitting in (1): nothing is conjured out of nothing, and I'm not pretending that a bare "is" entails an "ought." I also think it survives Moore's test. I put the relevant question to my seminar last week — "granted that she violated what the practice demands, did she really have reason not to?" — and nobody felt the pull of an open question, which I take to be the only thing Moore's argument could ever have been testing. On top of that, as I recall, Hume's own statement of the gap is a single throwaway paragraph in Book III, Part I of the *Treatise*, where he says only that the shift from "is" to "ought" is imperceptible but needs to be noticed and explained; he doesn't prove that any particular inference is invalid. If an explanation is all he's demanding, then (1) is my explanation.
