I have no academic background in this and have been reading about chaos on my own, and I keep running into what look like three different claims collapsed into one: that a deterministic system can still be unpredictable, that arbitrarily small causes can have arbitrarily large effects, and that we cannot forecast the future because we cannot measure the present exactly enough. I want to find out whether those really are three separate failures or one failure described from three angles. So I would like a purpose-built pair of cases rather than an illustration.

Build me one ordinary-scale scenario — a kitchen, a workshop, a farm, a clinic, a stretch of road; something a person could actually build or stand and watch — containing two setups that are exhaustive of each other except in exactly one respect, which you should state. Both setups must be fully deterministic: no dice, no quantum randomness, no undocumented human choices, every later state fixed by the laws plus the earlier state. Both are observed by the same forecaster: a careful person with good ordinary instruments, a decent laptop, and as much time before the event as she wants, but no demon, no infinitely precise measurement, and no oracle that answers uncomputable questions.

The verdicts I want are the inverted ones. In the setup that is chaotic — the butterfly-effect thing, whatever the precise name is — I want her to be able to predict, well ahead and to a standard a normal person would act on, the specific quantity a normal person actually cares about; and I want the prediction to succeed because of the chaos rather than in spite of it. In the setup that is not chaotic, where neighbouring starting states converge and stay close, I want her to fail to predict the quantity of interest. Constraints on that second failure: it must not be sensitive dependence; not randomness; not imprecision in her knowledge of the laws or of the present state (stipulate that she has the laws exactly and can measure as finely as she likes); not the prediction itself causally feeding back into the outcome; and ideally not merely a matter of needing a bigger computer or more hours. The quantity of interest in both halves must be something an ordinary person would genuinely want to know, not a conserved quantity or a long-run average chosen to make the case work, and the prediction must be of a definite event or value at a definite time, not just a probability spread.

After the scenario, show me the work: the verdict in each half and why, the one respect in which the setups differ, and a pass over each candidate source of unpredictability — sensitivity to initial conditions, randomness, imprecise initial data, insufficient computation, causal feedback from the forecast, self-reference — saying for each whether it is doing work in your case and why the failure in the second half is not any of the excluded ones. Then tell me which single feature carries each half and what happens to the verdict if that feature is removed, and give me the strongest reply available to someone who holds that determinism entails predictability in principle.

Finally, two things I care about more than the case itself. If the only mechanism you can actually find for the second half bottoms out in she cannot finish the computation in time, say so plainly, and tell me whether that is a principled limit or an engineering one and whether the difference matters for the claim that determinism and predictability are distinct. And if my specifications are jointly unsatisfiable at ordinary scale, or the distinction I think I have spotted is not a real one, tell me that instead of handing me a case that meets the letter of the request. Please mark which steps you are confident about and where you are speculating.
