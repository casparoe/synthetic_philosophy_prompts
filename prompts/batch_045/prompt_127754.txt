Six months ago a close friend, who was diagnosed with bipolar I after a severe manic episode, asked me to serve as the enforcement agent under his psychiatric advance directive. If he stops his medication and becomes manic again, I am the person who signs the papers that get him admitted and treated over his objection. I said yes before I had thought about it. Since then I have been trying to work out, in a form I could actually defend to myself, when I would be right to refuse to enforce it. The same question is coming at me in an institutional form as well: I sit on the committee drafting the founding charter of a federation of housing cooperatives, and we are arguing about whether to entrench certain clauses against ordinary amendment.

So what I need is a thought experiment built to specifications, not a survey of the literature. Build me a case of a Ulysses contract that is self-consuming: one in which the precommitment was adopted as a means to a transformation in the person himself, and in which completing that transformation requires, at the end, a decision made by the transformed person exercising the very capacities the restraint suspends. That is the notion I want tested, and you should feel free to sharpen it if you can state it better.

Specifications:

1. Realistic and contemporary. One paragraph. A hospital, a conservatorship or commitment hearing, a sober-living or residential treatment agreement, a religious vow, a founder's lock-up: a device that actually exists. No science fiction, no implanted or invented neurotechnology, no perfect predictors, no fission or teletransportation, and no appeal toParfitian metaphysics about what makes someone the same person over time. I want the normative dispute isolated, not dissolved into a dispute about identity.

2. Every condition that friends of such agreements require for legitimate enforcement should hold: made in a cool hour, by a competent and informed adult, voluntarily, specific about the triggering circumstance, with a known mechanism by which the later condition (mania, craving, panic, despair) distorts judgment, and with no third party who benefits from enforcement.

3. The epistemic question is to be settled by stipulation inside the case, not argued about. Give the agreement a verification clause: an independent assessment against stated criteria has confirmed that the bound person is competent, informed, and that the refusal is not itself a symptom of the condition the agreement guards against. The disagreement that remains must be purely normative.

4. Stakes are high and irreversible, with a closing window, so that waiting to see how things settle is not available.

5. The failure of enforcement must not come from any of the standard exits, and the case should make clear that it does not: the earlier self was not coerced, mistaken, or incompetent; circumstances have not changed in any unforeseen way; the later self is competent; the enforcing agent has no conflict of interest.

6. Three verdicts, in specified directions. A precedent-autonomy view enforces. A present-autonomy view does not. A third view — I have in mind something like intertemporal co-authorship, on which a person's life is a cooperative arrangement among temporal stages and enforcement is warranted when the later refusal is attributable to the impairment the device was built to survive — must side with precedent autonomy in a textbook Ulysses case and side with present autonomy here. It must not be present autonomy relabeled: show it enforcing somewhere.

After the scenario, walk through what each of the three views says and why, and then tell me which feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to each verdict if that feature is removed, and how a defender of precedent autonomy would attack the case. I expect the obvious attack to be that any I have been transformed exemption is indistinguishable in practice from the symptom the contract exists to discount, and I want to know whether your verification clause actually blunts that or merely restates it.

Finally, one paragraph on whether a self-consuming precommitment has an analogue in constitutional entrenchment: a clause entrenched in order to make possible a political transformation whose success would render the entrenchment unnecessary or illegitimate. If the institutional case comes apart from the individual one, say what separates them — that many persons are involved, that amendment is the analogue of refusal, or something else — and whether the feature that does the work in your scenario survives the transfer.

The point of all this is practical: I need a reason that would justify refusing to enforce in some possible future version of my friend's directive, and it cannot be a reason so loose that it licenses ignoring any directive at all. An exemption that turns out to be a general loophole is a failed design, and you should say so if that is where your case lands. If the specifications cannot all be met together, tell me which one gives way and what that shows.
