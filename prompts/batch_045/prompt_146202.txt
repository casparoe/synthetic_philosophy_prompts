The question was: "Why does Aristotle think money exists, and how does money make exchanges just? Use his example of the builder and the shoemaker (or, as he also puts it, the physician and the farmer) to illustrate your answer." One student wrote this:

Aristotle thinks money is a fix for a co-ordination problem. In a barter economy the builder and the shoemaker can only trade if each happens to want what the other has; money removes that double coincidence problem, since everyone will accept it. That's why he says money has its value by convention — nomisma, from nomos — and not by nature: it works because we all agree it works, and a city can in principle make it worthless.

Money also does philosophical work for him. Justice in exchange is proportional reciprocity: a house and a pair of shoes aren't directly comparable, so they have to be made commensurable by some one standard, and for Aristotle that standard is need (chreia). Money is the stand-in for need. Need is what he says holds the community together, and money acts as a sort of pledge, so that if the person in front of you doesn't want your shoes you can still get what you need later.

The proportion is in effect arithmetic: what the builder hands over must equal in value what he gets back. In the example, the ratio between the two products is set by how much labour time went into each — Aristotle is thus often read as a forerunner of the labour theory of value, with Marx drawing on him here. And since the standard is a single rate, chreia basically amounts to a market-clearing price: the rate at which what's on offer and what people need balance out, so a just exchange is one that clears at that rate.

Interest is separate. Aristotle condemns it because money is barren — lending it produces nothing real — so charging for it is the most unnatural form of wealth-getting, part of the unlimited chrematistike he attacks in the Politics rather than household management.
