I run a Saturday-afternoon workshop series for a university forecasting and decision-making reading group: about sixteen people, mostly graduate students in statistics, computer science and economics, with a few philosophers. They are comfortable with expected value and with compound interest, but almost none of them have heard of the Kelly criterion or thought about growth rates. I have two hours with a short break, a whiteboard, a projector, index cards, dice and several decks of cards, and everyone has a phone with a calculator. Real money is out of the question, but I can put a genuine small donation behind the outcome and I can offer one modest individual prize.

What I want designed is a single runnable session in which they do the betting rather than hear about it: a game with written stake decisions, rounds resolved by an explicit randomizer that is visibly not me, and a debrief afterwards. The thing I most want them to feel, before anyone names it, is that maximizing expected bankroll and maximizing the growth rate of the bankroll come apart on a repeated multiplicative bet: the expected-value maximizer's fortune is real on paper and nonexistent in almost every actual path, because the mean is carried by a vanishing sliver of lucky trajectories. I would also like them to feel that overbetting is punished asymmetrically — that growth falls off slowly on the over-side and then turns negative once you go past about twice the growth-optimal stake — and that the recommendation is not simply "be timid," since some bets legitimately call for staking most of what you have. Nothing with a formula on the board for the first forty minutes, and no reading list: I want the activity, not sources.

The markets I was going to use, in some order you should choose, are: a 60/40 bet in your favour at even money; a 20% chance paying 5 to 1; an even-chance bet paying only 0.9 to 1; and a 90% chance at even money. I believe the growth-maximizing fractions of current bankroll for these are respectively 20%, 4%, nothing, and 80% — tell me if I have that wrong, tell me what each market earns its place in the sequence for, whether to give anyone these numbers and when, and whether the last block should instead offer two markets at once with participants free to split their stake, in which case say whether those two should be independent or deliberately correlated and what that version teaches that the single-market version cannot.

On incentives, I am considering making the donation depend on the geometric mean of everyone's final bankroll while a separate leaderboard ranks people by final bankroll and the top scorer picks the charity. Tell me whether that tension is the best part of the design or a distraction, whether the geometric-mean rule should be announced at the start or revealed at the end, and roughly how many rounds the game needs and how fast each round must run for the growth-rate difference to be visible over luck — and if you think no feasible number of rounds makes it visible, say so plainly and tell me what the activity can accomplish instead. I am also toying with a withdrawal mechanic in which players must periodically spend a fraction of their bankroll on something consumed and scored separately; advise whether that clarifies the structure or muddies it, and where it would go.

Please give me the rules and materials, a timeline with minute marks including what I say out loud, the debrief mapped onto specific moments that will have occurred, and an honest account of what will go wrong: the player who stakes everything early and wins, the confusion between betting a fixed fraction and betting a fixed amount, the rounds where the right action is to abstain and people bet from boredom, and the participant who notices the leaderboard is separable from the donation and starts playing for the leaderboard alone. Then tell me what the game cannot reproduce and I must supply in words: the asymptotic character of the growth-rate argument, the assumption that the probabilities are known, and the fact that a fixed target or a lumpy, non-repeatable opportunity calls for something other than growth-rate maximization. Finally, if there is a version in which the stated probabilities are slightly wrong so that full-Kelly staking does worse than a deliberately shrunken fraction, describe it and tell me whether it requires deceiving the room; if the same lesson can be taught without deception, use that instead. Include one last round or one closing question that would let me tell whether the idea landed or whether they have merely learned a formula.
