I have no academic background in philosophy; I read a little on my own in the evenings. A friend and I have been going round in circles over the same argument for nearly two years, and I would be grateful for the paper we ought to have written instead of having the argument for the hundredth time.

We agree about the set-up. The question is what it means to say of someone that she has a reason to do something. Every case that divides us has this shape: she has no desire at all to do the thing, she would acquire no desire for it by thinking the matter over however carefully and for however long, and still one of us wants to say that she has a reason all the same. My friend holds that a reason can hold of a person whether or not it connects with anything she already cares about. I hold that it cannot: unless some thread runs from what she cares about — or could come to care about on reflection — to her doing the thing, there is nothing left for the claim to be about. Call these the "holds anyway" view and the "must connect" view. Please do not tell me which philosophers have held which, or what I ought to read; I cannot check either, and I want the argument rather than the authorities.

Three cases have done most of the work. First, my sister is a musician: she can play a passage in a particular way perfectly well, but only when she is not considering whether to play it that way — the moment she thinks about it she becomes self-conscious and the impulse vanishes, and no amount of thinking restores it. Does she have a reason to play it that way? I cannot see how my view gets to "yes", and he cannot see why the answer is not obviously "yes". Second, a man who comes, through a course of medication, or a religious conversion, or six months of argument with someone he trusted, to care passionately about something he had always found pointless: before the change, does he have a reason to pursue it? To me the answer seems to depend on how the change came about; to him it depends only on what there now is to be said for the thing — and he says that I can separate a good change in someone's cares from a mere knock-on effect only by helping myself to the very thing in dispute. Third, someone who has come to care about almost nothing at all: not unhappy, simply unmoved. Does she have reasons?

My guesses at the cruxes are: whether a reason has to be able to survive the agent's own reflection, and what to say about the ones that cannot; whether my phrase "on reflection" can bear the weight I put on it; and whether we are even using "reason" for one thing, since I seem to mean something that could guide a person and he something with which a person can be assessed. Correct me if I have misdescribed any of this.

What I want is: a numbered list of the claims we can both sign, none of them so thin that signing costs us nothing; a table of what remains in dispute, each row giving the point at issue and then each side's own wording, phrased so that side would accept it; for each crux, the case or consideration we would both accept as bearing on it, together with what each of us should predict in advance about how it bears, including what each expects the other will be forced to concede; and a note on any part of the quarrel that is really a quarrel about words, saying which part and why. I do not want a verdict on which of us is right. Keep the whole thing, table included, to a single page: 700 words at most.
