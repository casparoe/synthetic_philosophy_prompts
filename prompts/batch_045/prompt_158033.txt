I work in programming languages and formal methods. The vocabulary I actually think in: denotational and operational semantics, program equivalence and observational equivalence, staged computation and partial evaluation (including Futamura-style specialization, where you bake some of a program's input into the program itself and get a specialized residual), call sites and continuations, Rice-style undecidability results about properties of program behavior, and fixed-point constructions for self-referential programs. I have no formal philosophy training. What I know about updateless decision theory comes from secondhand summaries of Timeless Decision Theory and its successors: the agent selects not an action but a policy, a function from observations to actions, chosen as if before any observation arrives; it declines to condition its decision on the evidence it later receives; and it is supposed to deliberate as if its choice fixed the output of the algorithm wherever that algorithm is instantiated — in physically separate copies of itself, in other agents running similar code, and inside the predictor's simulation in Newcomb-style cases.

Please restate that position, and the contrast with causal and evidential decision theory, in the vocabulary above, as faithfully as you can manage. Two identifications I have been playing with, which I want you either to vindicate or to break:

First, that updatelessness is really a claim about staging: to condition on an observation is for the agent to specialize itself on a runtime value, converting a general policy into the residual program that already has that value hardcoded. Updateless decision theory then says the specialization must be performed by the environment, not by the agent, because which program is shared with the other instantiations depends on who does the specializing.

Second, that the much-discussed logical correlation between an agent's decision and the decisions of other systems is just identity or sharing in the code: two systems' outputs are correlated exactly when their decision procedures are the same function, or share the subterm that computes the decision; and there is no further fact of the matter.

Use this concrete setting when you restate things: a policy P is compiled into N binaries running on physically separated machines whose downstream consequences differ (different continuations, in my sense). The agent knows N, knows the payoff structure as a function of the tuple of all N outputs, and knows that all N machines run P, but does not know which binary it is. State the updateless recommendation for this case in programming-language terms, and say whether it amounts to anything more than optimize the entire input-output graph of P at compile time rather than the value returned at one call site.

Then the residue. I want three things distinguished: what translates without strain; what translates only after you quietly import an assumption that is not part of program semantics (for example, that a program can be said to deliberate about its own output table, or that similarity of programs comes in degrees); and what has no expression in my vocabulary at all. In particular: program equivalence is undecidable and all-or-nothing, so my second identification gives no graded correlation between similar-but-not-identical agents — is that a limitation of my vocabulary, or does it point to updateless decision theory lacking a principled account of partial similarity? And is the agent's uncertainty about its own output the same kind of limit as the undecidability results I know, or a different thing wearing the same face?

Close with a verdict on what the residue indicates: that my vocabulary is missing notions it ought to have (self, agency, indexicality), or that the central updateless notion is less well defined than its exposition suggests.
