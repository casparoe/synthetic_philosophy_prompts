I am writing a paper on hard choices and want to defend the following thesis, which I will call (A): some hard choices are hard not because the agent is ignorant of some relevant fact, but because the values at stake are incommensurable, so that the deliberative question which option ought to be chosen has no answer that is settled by those values. A friend has produced an argument that appears to refute (A) whichever way I turn, and I cannot locate the step I should reject. I would be grateful for a diagnosis.

Fix a pair of options. A: a life spent as a composer. B: a life spent as a country doctor. Assume for the sake of the argument that the values these lives realise are incommensurable, and consider the following chain.

(1) No-verdict reading (NV). If the values realised by A and by B are incommensurable, then no comparative verdict holds between A and B: A is not better than B, not worse than B, not equally as good as B, and there is no fact of the matter — not merely no fact known to the agent — about which is to be preferred on the strength of the values at stake.

(2) Let A+ be A together with a voucher: one small, unconditional, unrestricted payment, spendable on anything at all, that makes no difference to the shape of the life beyond what it buys. A+ differs from A only by the voucher, and A+ is better than A.

(3) Small-Addition Invariance (SAI). If no comparative verdict holds between A and B, then no comparative verdict holds between A+ and B. The support offered is this: incommensurability is supposed to be a difference in the kind of value, not a gap of some magnitude; and a difference in kind cannot be closed by appending a small quantity of some further good, since closing it would require the appended good to be measurable against the very values that were said to lack a common measure. If the voucher did settle the matter, then A and B were on a common scale all along, and the original choice was never hard in the sense (A) requires.

(4) Iteration. Apply (3) to its own output: for any finite number n of vouchers, no comparative verdict holds between A+n and B.

(5) But for large enough n there plainly is a comparative verdict: a life as a composer with an enormous unconditional fortune attached is better than a merely decent life as a country doctor. So (1), (3) and (4) cannot all be true.

(6) My friend adds that rejecting (3) is no escape. His Continuity premise: if A+ is better than B while A is not better than B, then A falls short of B by less than the worth of the voucher; but then A and B are comparable after all, and the absence of a verdict between them was only ever ignorance of a quantity. So giving up (3) concedes the falsity of (A) anyway, and by a shorter route.

(7) Therefore, he concludes, either values are commensurable and every hard choice is an epistemic one, or incommensurability is idle: it never delivers the no-verdict result that (A) needs.

Three things I want from you. First, name the step you reject and say why it looked sound; I would like to keep (1) and (5), and if forced I would rather give up (3), but I cannot see what giving up (3) commits me to, nor on what ground (6) should be resisted. Second, price the alternatives: for each of the other candidate rejections, including rejecting the iteration in (4) rather than (3) itself, say what one is committed to and what it costs thesis (A), and tell me plainly which of these are live and which are unavailable. Third, address one wrinkle I cannot place: the voucher is unrestricted and spendable on anything, so its own worth is heterogeneous rather than a quantity on a single scale. I cannot tell whether that strengthens (3) by making the addition incapable of supplying a common measure, or weakens (5) by making it unclear that large numbers of vouchers yield a verdict — and I suspect the answer differs depending on which step the wrinkle is taken to bear on.

Please argue from the structure of the argument alone: no citations, no reading suggestions, no appeal to what some named author has said. Keep the prose diagnosis to 600–800 words, structured as three short sections headed Step, Why it looked sound, and Prices.
