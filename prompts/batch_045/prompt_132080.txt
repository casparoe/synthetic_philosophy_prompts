So I keep defending this at dinner and I want to know how much trouble its actually in. I'll call it Right-But-Early Malthusianism, or RBEM:

The mechanism Malthus set out in the Essay — population presses against the resource base, any gain in output per head gets absorbed into more people rather than more consumption per person, and famine, disease and war do the adjusting if nothing else does — is a real long-run tendency of any population that isn't deliberately limiting its own numbers. It just got masked for roughly two centuries by a one-off: drawing down an enormous stock of fossil energy, plus a burst of yield-increasing technical change that arrived from outside the system rather than from population pressure. And a prediction that's suppressed by an unrepeatable shock isn't a failed prediction; its an early one.

I've bolted on one refinement that I think is where I'm most exposed, and I want you to go straight at it. Call it the Window Clause: Malthusian pressure is only meant to be visible in Malthusian windows — stretches where the resource base is roughly fixed and no exogenous technical change is landing. But notice the hazard. If a window just means a period in which output per head failed to rise alongside population, then the theory ends up saying Malthus was operating in exactly the periods that look Malthusian, and we-weren't-in-a-window in exactly the periods that don't.

So: what would count against RBEM? I want defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, including ones that have arguably already happened. And I want you to separate what a committed RBEM defender would themselves concede from what they'd wriggle out of — including how much of that wriggling is legitimate and how much is question-begging.

Things I specifically want priced:

1. The reversal. What actually got us out was fertility falling as incomes rose — the preventive check running in a direction Malthus didn't describe and arguably couldn't have. Is absorbing that a rescue or a refutation? This one moves me alot more than the yield story does, so tell me if I'm weighting it wrong.

2. The reverse-causal story. If it turned out that a large share of the yield gains of the last two centuries were caused by population pressure — more mouths meaning more experimenters and more investment in intensification — rather than arriving exogenously, the causal arrow inside the mechanism flips. How bad is that, and would any actual defender concede it?

3. The pre-industrial baseline. Northwest European populations before industrialisation seem to have already been limiting fertility — late marriage, large numbers of people never marrying at all — with no Malthusian squeeze forcing them into it. Doesn't that mean the default Malthus extrapolated from was never a natural one?

4. Below-replacement fertility in rich countries. Some defenders say shrinking populations are just the positive check arriving in a gentler form. That looks to me like the insulation move in its purest form. What would make that reading honest, and what would make it a rescue?

5. A rule of thumb I made up, which you should feel free to tear apart: a long-run claim that has been continuously wrong for longer than the span it was extrapolated from gets no credit for being early unless it also says what would have to change for it to switch on. Does that rule kill RBEM, or does it kill too much — would it also convict ordinary geological or climatological claims about rare events, or any claim about a system that can react to being predicted?

Finally: has RBEM been qualified into unfalsifiability? If it has, say so plainly, and tell me whether that's really a problem for a claim of this shape — a tendency claim about a system with feedback, where the thing predicted can respond to the prediction. And give me the single observation or result that would move you most if you saw it tomorrow.
