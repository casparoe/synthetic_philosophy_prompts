The setup: I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, mostly biology and the history of biology. I've been asked to give the after-lunch talk at a one-day workshop on "the uses of the past" thrown by a history of science department, and I'm the token non-historian in the room. I get twenty-five minutes and then what the schedule calls "a lively" thirty-five minutes of questions. I recieved the program last week and the Q&A is longer than the paper, which tells you what they're expecting.

The audience, roughly thirty people. Ten or twelve historians of science and medicine, nearly all working on the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Six or seven intellectual historians in the contextualist mode — the "what was this author doing in writing this, in the terms available to them" crowd, who treat anachronism as the cardinal sin and will hear any present-day concept as a violation. Two archivists. One philosopher of history who thinks the past is what it is and that our access to it is genuinely constrained by what the evidence will bear. And four or five people who do public history and museum work, who will not care about my taxonomy at all and will want to know what goes on the wall text.

Here's the thesis, in the form I've been turning over. I want to defend something I've started calling audited presentism, in three parts. (1) Historians can't escape the standards of their own time, and the attempt to escape them mostly just hides them — the abstinence model leaves the present standard in place but unstated. (2) Not all presentism is alike, and the distinctions that matter aren't the usual ones. I separate: judgment presentism (praising or condemning past actors by present norms); explanation presentism (using a concept the actors lacked to explain what happened — calling a subsistence crisis a market failure, calling a collecting expedition extraction); and archive presentism (the fact that what survived to be studied at all, and what gets digitised first, is a product of past power plus present funding). Its not obvious to me which of those three this room will find most objectionable. (3) The remedy isn't abstinence but an audit: for every present-day standard I apply, I owe the room three things — whether the actors had the concept, whether they had the evidence, and whether a real alternative was available to them. If I can give all three, the presentism is doing legitimate work; if I can give one, I'm moralising and should own it.

The case I'll lead with is my own beat: popular writing about early twentieth century biology — the eugenics-adjacent material, heritability research, the shifting line between public health and breeding. I've watched the treatment of those people flip twice in my working life, from cranks to sincere reformers working with the statistical tools they had, and I want to know what I'm now allowed to say about them. The second case, which I think is harder for this room: whether it's legitimate to describe a nineteenth-century naturalist's collecting as extraction, given that nobody involved had the word and that the word is doing explanatory work rather than just moral work.

What I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet. Give me the eight or so objections I'll actually get, ordered by how likely they are times how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way the person who'd raise it would actually put it — I want the contextualists to sound like contextualists and the archivists to sound like archivists, not like a philosopher's summary of their position. For each: a ninety-second answer, and then, honestly, where that answer is soft. Tell me what I should concede in the first two minutes to buy goodwill, and what I should refuse to give up. Tell me the one reply that has to land if the talk is going to survive. And include the question I'm hoping nobody asks — with an answer anyway.

Two more things. First, if you think one of my three categories is doing no real work, or that I've missed a fourth kind that this room will reach for immediately, say that instead of answering the question I asked. Second, and this matters because I'm writing a piece off the back of the talk: at the end of the sheet, separate what's actually settled in this debate from what's contested among people who know it, and flag any place where you're about to hand me one camp's view as though it were the consensus. I'd rather have three contested claims marked than one confident sentence I have to walk back in print.
