I am convening a two-day adversarial collaboration in my department this autumn on interpersonal comparisons of well-being, and I need the framing document drafted before the participants arrive. I know the literature, so please do not give me a survey, a reading list, or citations — and do not lean on any empirical results beyond the ones I stipulate below. The work I need is constructive drafting: formulations precise enough that each participant could sign their own, and thin enough that neither could reasonably refuse.

The two participants, whom I will call the Comparabilist and the Scale-Skeptic, hold the following. The Comparabilist maintains that propositions of the form "A's gain under this policy exceeds B's loss" and "A is at a lower level of well-being than B" have determinate truth values fixed by facts about their lives rather than by our conventions, and that we can often know them well enough to act on, if noisily and contestably. The Scale-Skeptic maintains that well-being is measurable only within a single life, that cross-person level comparisons are at best coarse verdicts about extreme cases expressed in the idiom of measurement, and that the most we can rigorously compare are differences, denominated through each person's own trade-off rates. Both are sophisticated enough to find the slogans on each side unsatisfying, which is why I want the disagreement stated precisely rather than rehearsed.

Please draft the document in four parts.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims. I expect these to include, among others: the invariance framing of the dispute — which classes of admissible transformation of each person's utility function leave a social evaluation invariant, and what each such class licenses and forbids in the two policy cases I describe at the end; the point that a social ordering presupposes more than intrapersonal cardinality; and whatever else you think both could sincerely sign without the claims becoming vacuous.

Second, a disagreements table. Columns: the disputed proposition; the formulation the Comparabilist would accept for their own view; the formulation the Scale-Skeptic would accept for theirs; and a note on why the disagreement, so stated, is not merely about words. Where you think some part of the dispute *is* verbal — about what "utility" denotes, or about what counts as measurement — say so in a separate line and explain why.

Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions that would actually move one side or the other, each with a stipulated case or piece of evidence both would agree bears on it and each side's prediction recorded in advance. I have three in mind but do not treat my list as exhaustive:

(a) The cross-person decoder. Suppose a decoder trained entirely on within-person neural or behavioural data, using no labels from anyone else, nonetheless predicts a new person's reported experience above chance, including for people whose lives differ substantially from the training subjects. What would each side predict, and what would each take this to show if it happened?

(b) The common-anchor protocol. Suppose participants calibrate their reports against a standardized battery of controlled experiences, and the resulting scales are then used to compare people whose lives never exposed them to anything in the battery. Is this measurement or stipulated convention, and does the answer differ for differences and for levels?

(c) The range-normalization convention: rescaling each person so that their own attainable worst outcome is 0 and their own attainable best is 1, then comparing. The Comparabilist should say whether this concedes or begs the question; the Skeptic should say whether accepting it is compatible with their position or quietly abandons it. I would also like both sides pressed on an intra-personal analogue — whether they can make sense of comparing one person across a radical break in their concerns and, if so, why the same resources do not settle the interpersonal case.

Fourth, a short closing note on what would count as the collaboration failing: the outcome, if any, that both would have to regard as a genuine result rather than a negotiated compromise.

Apply the whole thing to two policy cases for me: one in which a few people with a severely restricted attainable range — chronic unremitting pain, say — would gain enormously relative to their own baseline, and one in which a large number of people with ordinary ranges would each gain slightly; where exactly does the disagreement between my two participants determine different recommendations, and where does it turn out to make no difference?
