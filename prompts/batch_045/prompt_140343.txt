I'm writing a six-month master's research proposal (around 3,000 words, assessed as a standalone document by a committee that includes both a philosopher of biology and a theoretical computer scientist). The topic is the analogy between self-replication in typogenetics and self-reference in Gödel's incompleteness argument, and I want the proposal to make precise a distinction I think the standard presentation blurs. Please don't spend any words on references, a bibliography, or on getting the secondary literature straight — I'll fill those in myself, and I'd rather the document stand or fall on the framing and the method.

For context, the system I'm starting from is Hofstadter's typogenetics from *Gödel, Escher, Bach*: strands are finite strings over a four-letter alphabet; a strand can be treated as mere data, or translated by a fixed code into "enzymes," where an enzyme is a sequence of elementary operations on strands (copying a unit, deleting one, inserting one, changing one, moving a reading head, splitting or joining strands), and those operations can in principle be applied to the very strand that encoded them. Two things I want you to respect: first, don't rely on recall of the exact code table, the arity of the codons, or the published operation list — treat alphabet size, the many-to-one-ness of the code, and the available operations as *parameters* of a family of systems, and say explicitly which features your argument depends on, so I can check them against whatever rule set I settle on. Second, don't rely on any recollection about whether a fully self-replicating strand is known to exist in the published system; treat that as part of what the project investigates.

Here is my half-formed idea, which I want you to sharpen or, if it's confused, to replace with a better one. People say "DNA is like a Gödel numbering," but I think at least two different claims get run together: (i) a *reinterpretation* claim — the same object admits two readings, as a number and as a coded formula, or as a strand and as a program, and the analogy is with the interpretive duality, not with any fixed point; and (ii) a *diagonal* claim — that the self-reproduction of a strand is an instance of the same trick that produces a sentence asserting its own unprovability. I suspect (ii) is where the analogy quietly fails, because Gödel's fixed point is *uniform*: it works for any sufficiently strong, effectively axiomatized theory, via a construction that doesn't care about the details of the coding. If a self-replicating typogenetic strand, by contrast, exists only because the particular code has degeneracies that a strand can exploit — a "hack" rather than a theorem — then the isomorphism is doing metaphorical work at exactly the point where it is supposed to be doing formal work. What would settle it, I think, is a uniformity question: is there a *compiler* — an algorithm that takes a description of a typogenetic system satisfying some stated conditions and emits a self-replicating strand in it, the way quining or Kleene's recursion theorem gives you a fixed point uniformly? Or is self-replication in these systems always an accident of the code?

I also want the proposal to confront a problem I keep tripping over: "self-replication" is too easy to satisfy. If an empty or do-nothing enzyme counts, then every strand trivially replicates itself, and if an enzyme just blindly copies whatever it sits on, the strand's own sequence isn't really doing the work. My current attempt at a nondegeneracy condition is *hereditary* replication: the replication process must be information-carrying, in the sense that most single-unit mutations of the parent strand yield copies carrying the corresponding mutation, rather than the same canonical output — so replication must propagate variation, as Darwinian replication does. I am not confident this is the right condition, or that it is satisfiable in any interesting system, and I'd like the proposal to treat it as a proposal to be tested and revised rather than a fixed definition.

Please write the proposal with: a precise statement of the question (one that has a determinate answer, including saying what would count as a negative answer); why the two readings of the isomorphism leave it open; the method, phase by phase across six months, including what would actually be computed or proved rather than discussed, and why that approach rather than the obvious alternatives (a straight conceptual analysis of "self-representation," or a brute-force search for a self-replicating strand); what a successful deliverable would look like for someone deciding whether the analogy has real content; and an honest failure section — the two or three ways this project most plausibly turns out to be vacuous, unfinishable in six months, or dependent on a choice I've made that turns out to be arbitrary.
