I had a long argument with a friend last night about geometric rationality, and I came away unconvinced but unable to say why, which is the most irritating possible outcome. He gave me a chain of reasoning that I'd like you to take apart. Here it is, in his words as nearly as I can reconstruct them.

(1) Suppose you are offered the same favorable bet over and over: you may stake any fraction of your current wealth; with probability 0.6 you get back twice what you staked and with probability 0.4 you lose the stake. Your wealth after n bets is W_n.

(2) For any fixed fraction f you commit to staking, the strong law of large numbers gives (1/n)·log(W_n/W_0) converging almost surely to g(f) = 0.6·log(1+f) + 0.4·log(1−f), and g is maximized at f = 0.2, where it is about 0.02.

(3) So if you stake 0.2 and I stake anything else, your wealth exceeds mine by a factor that tends to infinity with probability one, since the logarithm of the ratio grows like n·(g(0.2) − g(f)). This is a theorem about the whole future path, not a heuristic about averages, so it does not depend on how risk-averse either of us is.

(4) An agent facing a long sequence of multiplicative shocks should therefore choose so as to maximize the expected logarithm of wealth — that is, should adopt expected-utility maximization over wealth with the utility function u(w) = log w. The Kelly criterion is not a gambling tip; it is what rationality about wealth consists in.

(5) A policy once adopted should be applied consistently, and there is no principled line between the casino and the rest of life: capital returns, career income, and inflation are all multiplicative shocks to wealth too. So the same attitude governs every choice under uncertainty about money, including choices that will never be repeated.

(6) But u(w) = log w is extraordinarily timid. Confronted with a coin flip that returns ten times whatever you stake half the time and one tenth of it the other half — an expected return of 5.05 times the stake — it refuses to stake more than half of what it has, and it values the all-or-nothing version of that same gamble at exactly nothing, because the geometric mean of multiplying by ten and by one tenth is one. And since the logarithm of zero is minus infinity, it treats any nonzero chance of losing everything as infinitely bad: it will pay any price short of its entire wealth to remove a one-in-ten-thousand chance of total ruin.

(7) No sane person hands over ninety-nine thousand dollars to eliminate a one-in-ten-thousand chance of losing a hundred thousand, and a person who did would be worse off by every ordinary standard, having also declined a gamble with an expected return five times the stake.

(8) So geometric rationality, understood as a general theory of rational choice about wealth, refutes itself: its own argument hands you a policy whose implications for ordinary, one-shot decisions are absurd.

I tried to sharpen it and he brushed me off. My worry was this: the comparison in step (3) is a comparison of two wealth processes that both continue forever, but a real agent has a floor — a level of wealth below which something she cares about stops, permanently, whether that is a business that cannot be recapitalized or a household she supports. Once there is an absorbing floor, "the growth rate" is only defined conditional on never being absorbed, and maximizing it is a different problem from maximizing the chance of never hitting the floor; a strategy with a slightly lower growth rate can be dramatically better at staying alive. I also wonder whether the step I most suspect is really the weak one. He added a second claim I don't trust: that when the win probability is unknown and you are learning as you go, the growth-maximizing stake is always strictly less than the Kelly fraction computed at your current point estimate, because estimation risk makes full Kelly overbet. I think that is false in general — the value of the information you get by betting can push the other way — but I'd like to know whether it matters for the main argument either way.

What I want from you is a diagnosis of the chain: which step you reject, why that step looked solid enough to fool two people for an hour, and what rejecting it costs you. In particular, if you reject (5), I want the principled line you think replaces it — which choices does growth-maximization govern and which does it not, and why? If you reject (4)'s identification of maximizing the growth rate with having logarithmic utility over wealth, tell me what the argument for betting less than the naive optimum is then supposed to be about, and whether the logarithmic function is doing instrumental work there or intrinsic work. If you think the trouble is in (3), say plainly whether the "beats every other strategy with probability one" claim is a claim about each fixed alternative separately or about all of them at once, and whether the uniform version can be recovered. And tell me how my absorbing-floor objection bears on whichever step you pick: does it supply the flaw, or is it a separate complaint that survives even after the main argument is repaired? I would like, if possible, to keep the mathematics of (2) and (3) and the thought that a creature facing many multiplicative shocks should care about the growth rate rather than the expected value; I would also like to keep the thought that one's attitude to monetary risk should not change at the door of the casino, since I cannot see what principled distinction would license the change. I do not know which of these I should be willing to pay, and that is exactly the question.
