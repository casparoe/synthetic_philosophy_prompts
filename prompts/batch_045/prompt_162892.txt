I'm taking an advanced seminar on scientific realism this term and sharing a house with a PhD student in particle physics, so I keep bumping into a gap between how physicists write and how my seminar talks about what they are doing. In her field it is entirely routine to do all of the following inside a single paper: describe the Standard Model as an effective field theory, that is, as a low-energy approximation to something else, valid only below some cutoff scale, and therefore not something anyone in the field expects to be true in the long run; announce the discovery or observation of entities and quantities, whose measured values then get entered into permanently maintained reference tables that are treated as cumulative facts about the world; and rely, for whole chunks of the apparatus, the simulation, and the data analysis, on classical or non-relativistic models that everyone regards as superseded. So the theory is not true, the things it posits are nevertheless discovered, and the discarded theories of the past still do the calculating.

To get a grip on this, our seminar has adopted a small procedure: whenever someone presents a case of theory change, historical or contemporary, they have to fill in a retention ledger, sorting the superseded theory's content into three bins — what the successor kept and now treats as literally true, what it kept but only because it gives right numbers in a limited regime, and what it dropped entirely. The ledgers do not come out tidy. Symmetries and conservation laws tend to be kept; but so is some ontology across violent structural change, electrons running from late-nineteenth-century cathode-ray work through to quantum field theory; while some structure that looked like the most secure part of the old picture, absolute simultaneity, or the conception of heat as the flow of a conserved substance, went into the bin. My housemate's own gloss is that the right unit of retention is not structure versus ontology but resolution: theories are true at a scale, in the way a map is true at a scale, and asking whether they are true simpliciter is a category mistake.

What I would like is a reconstruction of what this bundle of practices — the effective-field-theory talk, the discovery talk, the reuse of superseded mathematics, and our ledger-sorting habit — presupposes about truth, reference, and theoretical success, and which rival views it quietly rules out. Specifically: does the practice strictly require something like structural realism, or would entity realism, or a thoroughly antirealist view on which a theory is only empirically adequate within a regime, license exactly the same behaviour? Can valid below the cutoff be cashed out as nothing more than yields correct numbers within error bars in regime R, and if so does that cash-out leave the practice of discovery-talk coherent, given that a term which may not refer in the successor theory is doing the discovering? Does the demand physicists place on any successor, that it recover the predecessor's numerical predictions in the predecessor's own domain, show that the old equations were tracking something real, or is it only a constraint on adequacy that an antirealist can accept? And is scale-indexed truth a genuine alternative to approximate truth, or just a relabelling of the same problem? Please separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with, tell me where it is philosophically underdetermined by several theories at once, and say concretely what would have to change in scientific practice — in the vocabulary of discovery, in how superseded theories are taught and used, in what our ledgers would even be tracking — if the implicit realism were replaced by the antirealist reading.
