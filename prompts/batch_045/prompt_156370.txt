I recently sat through an orientation session for volunteers on my university's research ethics board, and it left me stuck on a contrast whose logic I can't reconstruct. Healthy volunteers are routinely paid to let researchers do things to their bodies: to have catheters inserted, to have spinal fluid drawn, to swallow tubes, and in human challenge trials to be deliberately infected with a pathogen. Payment is openly scaled to how unpleasant and invasive the procedure is, and everyone in the room was candid that this is why people enroll. At the same time, the board's own doctrine is that payment may compensate time and inconvenience but must never be an undue inducement, and that subjects are paid for their trouble rather than for their risk — even though, as the trainers conceded, the protocols with the largest risk premiums are the ones that fill fastest, and the people who enroll in them are disproportionately people for whom a few thousand dollars is a great deal of money.

Set that beside the organ world. In most countries a person who agrees, for money, to hand over a kidney to a stranger commits a crime, and so do the recipient and the surgeons. The same legal order lets that donor be reimbursed for travel and lost wages, lets them be honored publicly, and in several systems moves them up the transplant queue should they ever need a kidney themselves; it also permits paying for sperm, eggs, plasma, and in some jurisdictions for carrying another person's pregnancy to term. So we pay for invasions of the body that carry real risk, and we pay for transfers of some of the most personhood-laden material there is, but not for the transfer of an organ whose loss the body absorbs without much complaint.

I would like you to reconstruct the implicit theory here, and I want the reconstruction at the level of strict presupposition rather than convenient fit. What view of commodification, consent, or exploitation does this pattern actually require — is the operative distinction between selling risk and selling a part, between goods the body regenerates and goods it does not, between a price and a gift-with-reimbursement, or something about what a price tag expresses to third parties who never enter the market? Is it consistent for the research side to say payment is for inconvenience while scaling it to risk? And here is the case I keep testing theories against: suppose a country kept the ban on private sales but created a single state purchaser — anyone could hand over a kidney at a fixed price, the organ enters the ordinary allocation queue under the usual medical criteria, and the seller receives a fixed annuity plus a lifetime priority claim on that queue should they ever need an organ themselves. The exploitation story seems to have no purchase there, since the buyer is the same monopsonist that already rations organs for free and allocates them by need. If a theorist still objects to that arrangement, what exactly is left for the objection to be about? And what would have to change in the actual practice — what reimbursements, honors, queue priorities, or adjacent markets would suddenly become indefensible — if that residue, rather than exploitation, were what the ban was really resting on?
