Say you're advising a small grant program. Roughly six philosopher-years of money over three years, and the mandate is: conceptual work on collective intentionality and joint action that will change how we think about groups of AI systems acting together. No empirical ML, no benchmarks — philosophy and conceptual theory. Assume the motivating case is something like several AI agents, possibly fine-tuned differently, possibly deployed by different parties, sharing an environment and either coordinating or failing to.

What I want is a defended funding agenda, not a survey, and please no reading list or citations — I've read the standard stuff, I want your reasoning. Concretely:

1. Which three to five questions should they fund, in what order? For each: what makes it upstream (what else stays stuck until it's settled), how tractable it looks in three years with philosophical tools alone, and how neglected it is.
2. Which questions in this area are overrated *for this purpose* — philosophically juicy, but where the answer wouldn't change what anyone builds, regulates, predicts, or blames? Argue for leaving them unfunded.
3. What would move things up or down the list over the next couple of years? I'd rather have "if we start seeing X, kill this line" than a ranking with nothing that could falsify it.

Reminder of the territory so we don't talk past each other, no need to expound on any of it: Bratman-style accounts where shared intention is basically interlocking individual plans plus mutual responsiveness; Gilbert-style joint commitment that no member can unilaterally dissolve; Searle-style we-intentions that don't reduce to I-intentions plus beliefs; and team-reasoning accounts where the agent reasons from "what should we do" rather than "what should I do".

Two wrinkles I want you to actually bite on rather than nod at:

(i) Clone coalitions. Ten thousand instances of the same model, identical weights, no communication between them, dropped into different corners of an environment with different observations. They end up behaving in strikingly correlated, mutually reinforcing ways — not because they planned together, but because they share a decision procedure and a training history. Is there a joint action here? Do those four families of account give clean answers, and do they come apart from each other? And the bit I care about for the funder: if we say yes, what does that buy us — better prediction, someone to hold responsible, a reason to build these things differently — that we don't already get from just describing the correlations and the shared weights? If the answer is "nothing", then maybe "do AI collectives have shared intentions?" is the wrong question, and the real one is about what work collectivity-talk is supposed to do in the first place.

(ii) Participants that can be copied, forked, rolled back, and merged. Joint commitment, mutual responsiveness, and we-reasoning all seem to presuppose that the participants persist and are countable. Weight-averaged merges and rollbacks break that. Does any of the standard machinery survive when the membership of the collective isn't well defined — or do we need a new notion of what counts as a member, and is that a two-year question or a twenty-year one?

Also feel free to say the whole framing is wrong. If you think the six philosopher-years would do more good on something else in the neighbourhood — the metaphysics of shared agency pointed at something other than AI, or a question I haven't listed — say so and defend that instead. But give me an ordering, tell me the criteria you're ranking by, and be explicit about how you're trading off importance against tractability against neglectedness, and what evidence would make you reorder.
