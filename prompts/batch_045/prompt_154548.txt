ok so this is driving me a bit nuts. Me and maybe four other people have been arguing about whether virtual objects and virtual harms are real for something like five years now, in a group chat, and nobody has moved an inch. Every time one side produces an example, the other side says "yeah but that's not a real object / that's not a real harm," and were back at the start.

Roughly the four positions. The virtual realist: virtual objects are real but dependent — they exist in virtue of code, a running process, and uptake from players — and dependent things can be perfectly real, like a debt or a border, so destroying someone's in-game stuff can be a genuine harm to a genuine if weird object of their projects. The fictionalist, in the make-believe style: the object is a prop in a game of make-believe, it has no welfare, so the only real wrong is a second-order one — you wrecked the game, you were a jerk, you ruined the fiction — and "harm" is shorthand for that. The conventionalist: real in the way property and money are, i.e. real only because a rule system plus uptake makes it so, so the answer just tracks whatever the practice happens to recognize. And the "just pixels" guy: nothing was harmed, some preferences were frustrated, log off, next question.

I made up a few cases specifically to try to separate the layers. (1) A group in a long-running persistent world spends two in-game years growing an orchard that the mechanics allow to be permanently destroyed; another player burns it down and posts the video. (2) Same, except an admin restores it an hour later — one side says the harm was undone, the other says the harm was the act, not the end state. (3) Same, except nobody designed the orchard: it was procedurally generated and the group just found it and took it over, so there's no author's intention behind it at all. (4) The "you can always log off" move, treated as an empirical claim about how costly exit actually is for someone whose whole social life is in there.

My hunch is that this isn't one disagreement but three stacked on top of each other — one about whether dependence is compatible with reality, one about what a harm has to be (setback to welfare? violation of a right? a failure of recognition?), and one straightforwardly empirical about how much people invest and how available exit really is — and that everyone is arguing from different layers, which is why nothing bites. I also suspect the famous extreme cases everyone cites (the early-90s text-world assault stuff, the VR groping cases) are doing way too much work, because the intuitions are already loaded before the theory gets a word in.

So: is that right, or is there a single real fault line underneath? Is any of it verbal, i.e. a fight about what "real" and "harm" mean? Is there a bedrock intuition on one side that can't be argued for — something like "harm requires damage to something whose existence doesn't depend on anyone's attitudes"? Do my cases actually do any diagnostic work, or would each side just digest them without moving? And given whatever you think the structure is, what would count as progress here: a distinction, some actual empirical finding about investment and exit costs (and how would you even measure that), or just dropping the realism question and asking what the practice should recognize instead? Or is this one of those questions where no progress should be expected, and if so, why exactly.
