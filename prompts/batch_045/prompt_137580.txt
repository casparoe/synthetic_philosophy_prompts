I've just come out of a long and genuinely inconclusive argument with a friend — a retired solicitor who teaches a jurisprudence option one term a year, and, like me, a member of the Corvin Society — about a motion now pending before a committee I sit on. I need to stop circling and commit myself. Please draft the memorandum I will circulate to the other four members of the Society's Rules Committee by Friday, ahead of our meeting on Friday 11 April.

The setting. The Corvin Society is a learned society founded in 1911, about 1,400 members, two journals, an annual congress, a small endowment, and a building. It is governed by a Charter and by resolutions of its general Assembly. I have set out below every provision that matters; there is nothing else in the Charter or in our standing orders that bears on the question, and there is no applicable external law. Please do not cite cases, commentators, or the constitutional law of any jurisdiction. The Charter is the whole of the relevant law, and I need reasoning that works on these words alone.

The relevant provisions, in their current form.

Article IV, §1: "Any Article of this Charter may be amended by a resolution passed by two-thirds of the members present and voting, at each of two consecutive ordinary meetings of the Assembly."

Article IV, §2: "This Article may be amended in the manner provided by §1."

Article VI, §3: "The Rules Committee shall determine all questions of order and of the interpretation of this Charter, subject to any resolution of the Assembly passed under Article IV."

Article IX: "Articles I to III may be amended only by a resolution passed by three-quarters of the members present and voting at a single ordinary meeting of which not less than ninety days' notice has been given; and no amendment of any other Article shall have the effect of amending, repealing or disapplying Articles I to III or any part of them."

Article III, §1: "Membership is open to individual persons only. No corporation, partnership, firm, association or other body, whether incorporated or not, shall be eligible for membership, and no member shall hold a vote by proxy for any such body."

The history, which is where most of the difficulty lies. Until 1998 the Charter's only amendment provision was a simple-majority-at-two-meetings rule; the two-thirds threshold was introduced in 1974 by a resolution passed under that rule, and Article IV §2 was added in 2011 by a resolution passed under Article IV §1, expressly, as the minute puts it, so that "no one could argue that the amendment article is protected from its own operation." Article IX was itself inserted in 1998 by ordinary resolution under the then-unrestricted amendment rule — 71 per cent in May, 69 per cent in October — and no one at the time suggested that anything more was needed. In 2014 a motion to create an "affiliate" category found to touch Article III was ruled out of order by the chair under Article IX; the proposers took the three-quarters route instead and carried it with 78 per cent. Article IX has never been the subject of a contested vote. In short: there is an unbroken practice of using Article IV to alter the amendment rules, including to create Article IX itself, alongside one precedent in which Article IX was held to bite on Article III.

The motion. Motion 3/24, the "Institutional Membership Resolution", was carried at its first reading in February by 68 per cent — comfortably above two-thirds, well short of three-quarters. Its second reading is listed for the ordinary meeting in June. It has five clauses: (a) amends Article IV §1 so that it reads "Any Article of this Charter, including Articles I to III, may be amended by a resolution passed by two-thirds of the members present and voting at each of two consecutive ordinary meetings"; (b) repeals Article IX; (c) amends Article III §1 to admit institutional members with full voting rights; (d) provides that "This Resolution, and every amendment made by it or under the procedure it establishes, shall have effect notwithstanding Article IX or any other provision of this Charter"; (e) a severability clause. The proposers' case, as they put it to me, is that they are not using Article IV to amend Article III at all: they are using Article IV to amend Article IV, which is precisely what Article IV §2 permits, and then availing themselves of the procedure as so amended.

The stakes are not academic. The Society ran a deficit of £340,000 last year against a capped endowment draw; the Council projects that without new revenue we sell the building or merge the journals within three years. A member has pledged £600,000 payable only if institutional memberships carrying votes are in place by 31 December. Against that, all eleven members of the editorial board of our larger journal have written to say they will resign if paying corporate bodies can vote on editorial policy, and our own consultation showed 58 per cent of members favour institutional membership in principle but only 34 per cent favour giving such members the vote. So there is majority support for something in this neighbourhood and no realistic prospect of three-quarters for this motion.

My friend's argument, which I could not defeat and which I want the memo to take seriously on its own terms rather than as a foil. It has three limbs. First: Article IX is itself the creature of the unrestricted procedure it now purports to close, so if the 1998 Assembly could not bind its successors, Article IX binds nobody and clauses (a) and (b) are superfluous rather than improper; if it could, then one generation's resolution can restructure the amendment power, and Motion 3/24 does only what Article IX did. Second: clause (d) is no more audacious than the words "and not otherwise" in Article IX; both are attempts by a present resolution to disable future ones, and it is arbitrary to treat one as law and the other as a nullity. Third: the self-reference is a mirage either way — a rule that says "every rule is hereby repealed" either repeals itself, in which case it repeals nothing, or exempts itself, in which case it says less than it appears to; so the right response to clause (d) is to ignore it and ask what the Assembly, as a continuing self-governing body, is entitled to do, not to hunt for paradoxes. By the end of the evening I found myself conceding the first two limbs and still wanting to rule the motion out of order. I could not say why, and I suspect my reason was simply that I dislike the outcome.

What I want from the memo.

A recommendation the Committee can vote on, clause by clause: what is in order, what is not, and whether the parts can be separated given clause (e). A concrete course of action with dates — what happens to the June second reading, what route the proposers should be directed to instead, and what the Council or the Secretary should be asked to do and by when. If you think the right answer is that the entrenchment can be dismantled by the two-step but Article III cannot be reached within the same instrument, or that it can be reached only at a later meeting under the procedure as amended, say that and say what follows for June.

An honest treatment of the strongest version of my friend's position — the symmetry between Article IX's entrenchment and Motion 3/24's circumvention — including, if it is where the case turns, the question whether Article IX's anti-circumvention limb reaches a two-step route whose first step does not itself touch Articles I to III. Deal too with the further question that the motion forces on us: whether there is a difference between amending the Charter and replacing it, such that some changes — for instance admitting corporate bodies with votes into a society of individual scholars — would not be an amendment of this Charter at all but the constitution of a different association under the same name, and if so who would be entitled to say so.

I also want you to confront something the Committee would rather avoid: our own authority to make this ruling rests on Article VI §3, which the Assembly can disapply by an ordinary Article IV resolution, so any ruling we give is in principle overridable by the very majority whose action it would condemn. Say what follows from that for how the ruling should be framed — as a determination of validity, as advice, or as something else — but do not let the regress become a reason for the Committee to do nothing. I need a bottom line.

Finally, close with a short section headed "What would change this": the single argument or fact that would most likely lead you to reverse the recommendation, and the place in your reasoning where you think error is most probable. If your recommendation turns on a distinction — between changing the amendment rule and destroying what is being amended, or between a valid and an invalid exercise of a power the Charter grants — state it in one sentence that a member could vote on, and if you cannot, say so and rest the recommendation on something firmer.

One last instruction, which I mean literally: no citations, no reading lists, no comparisons to the constitutional law of any country. Reason from the four provisions above, from the history, and from what a self-governing body must be able to do to remain one. My instinct is that we rule the motion out of order and point the proposers at Article IX, with a timetable that gives them a real chance at the three-quarters route in September. If you think that instinct is wrong, recommend the opposite and tell me why.
