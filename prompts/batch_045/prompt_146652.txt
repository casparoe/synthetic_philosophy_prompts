Here's the concrete thing that got me stuck, and I don't think it's in the literature, so please don't just match it to a canned reply. Imagine a checker with no inference rules whatsoever. Every step of a proof carries its cited premises plus a licence: a first-class object in the environment, checked by the kernel, saying in effect that from formulas of shapes A and A implies B you may write B. Licences themselves can be derived — you prove a theorem saying a given licence is sound, using other licences — and the kernel's only operation is checking whether a step is licensed by some licence object. Now three variants: (i) there's at least one primitive licence the kernel accepts with no derivation; (ii) no primitives, licences only ever derived from other licences; (iii) a licence that licenses its own use. Can (iii) even be written down coherently? My hunch is that in a system expressive enough to talk about its own licences, a self-licensing licence looks like a reflection principle, and that Löb's theorem is going to bite: you either get everything licensed or the licence is only forthcoming when you already had a derivation anyway. Is that right, or am I misusing Löb? More importantly, in each of the three variants, where does the regress reappear — does the Tortoise's demand get met anywhere, or does it just move from the rule to the kernel that reads the licence?
