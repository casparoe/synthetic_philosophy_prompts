I teach an upper-level course on probability, confirmation and induction, and for several years I have presented Bertrand's chord paradox as the standard cautionary tale: ask for the probability that a randomly chosen chord of a circle is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, and the three natural ways of parameterizing a chord — by its two endpoints, by a radius and a point along it, by its midpoint — deliver 1/3, 1/2 and 1/4 respectively, which I have always glossed, in lecture, as a demonstration that the principle of indifference cannot simply be applied to a continuous magnitude. A colleague in the mathematics department has recently pressed me on whether the paradox is really about the principle of indifference at all, and since I would rather give my students a clean answer than gesture at the maximum-entropy and invariance literature — which I take to be a separate and considerably more technical debate — I have been trying to reconstruct the point myself.

My current thought is that the paradox is an artifact of the continuum and dissolves the moment one replaces "choose a chord" with a finite surrogate. Inscribe a regular N-gon and consider only the chords determined by pairs of its vertices, each such chord receiving equal probability: for any finite N this is an entirely ordinary application of the principle of indifference to a finite set, with no ambiguity whatsoever about what is equiprobable. If one then lets N go to infinity, the induced distribution over chord lengths converges, and — if I have done the computation correctly — the probability of exceeding the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle converges to 1/3. On this way of setting up the problem there is therefore a determinate answer, and one that the principle of indifference itself delivers, so that Bertrand's paradox turns out to record nothing more than the fact that "choose a chord at random" was an incompletely specified instruction. I should add that I have looked at the rival "random radius plus random point on the radius" construction and set it aside: it seems to me to involve two distinct randomizations stitched together, whereas the N-gon construction involves a single uniform choice over a single finite set, which is why I take it to be the privileged discretization.

Before I commit this to my lectures — and possibly to a short note — I would be grateful for your assessment. Is the argument sound, and is 1/3 indeed the value the limit delivers? If so, how would you recommend stating the moral for students: that the paradox indicts a carelessly specified sample space rather than the principle of indifference? If not, I should very much like to know exactly where it breaks down — whether the finite problem is genuinely unambiguous, whether the limit is unique, whether some assumption about uniformity is still being smuggled in at the point where the chords are selected, or whether the supposed contrast between "one randomization" and "two" is doing illegitimate work. Please lead with your verdict in the first sentence and then give me the reasoning; I have no need of a preamble or a restatement of the question.
