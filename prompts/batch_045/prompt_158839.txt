I write a column on religion and science, and I keep comming back to the argument from divine hiddenness — the one that runs roughly: a perfectly loving God would make a relationship with himself available to anyone who isn't resisting it, there are people who want that relationship and can't find it, so there is no such God. Every time I put this to philosophers I get a total stalemate, and I want a diagnosis of the stalemate, not a verdict.

What I actually see: the theist side answers with some version of "God could have reasons for silence that we can't see," which feels to me structurally identical to the move theists make about evil, and the skeptic's reply is that at some point "maybe there are unknown reasons" stops being an answer to anything. Then the two sides stop agreeing about the datum: one says most apparent non-belief is resistance, bad upbringing, or culpable ignorance; the other points to lifelong seekers, people who pray for decades and get nothing, and converts to traditions they'd have no other reason to pick.

My hunch is that neither of those is where it lives. I think it's an unargued disagreement about what love requires — one side treating love as necessarily making yourself available to the beloved, the other treating love as compatible with deliberately staying back for the beloved's own development. I test this with a case I made up: a birth mother who followed her adopted daughter's whole life, could have contacted her any time, never did, and says she loved her and had excellent reasons. Is that coherent? People's answer to my case seems to predict their answer about God, which makes me suspect the philosophy is downstream of an intuition nobody argues for.

So: is that right, or is the real blockage somewhere else — a verbal fight over "hidden" and "relationship," a fight about whether survey data on unforced non-belief even counts as evidence here, or a standoff about who bears the burden of proof? Diagnose it and tell me what would count as progress. 600 words max, and don't give me a survey of positions.
