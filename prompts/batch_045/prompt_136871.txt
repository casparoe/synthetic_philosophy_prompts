I've got maybe fifteen hours a week for the next year and I want to spend it writing something -- a long blog series, probably -- that could actually shift how people think about one argument in AI safety. No academic background: I came in through blog posts and podcasts, I write code, and I've never been near an alignment team. The argument I keep circling is the one about worst-case versus average-case guarantees. On one side, people who say we should be trying to show a system can't do the bad thing under any input, in any situation it might land in. On the other, people who say that's a fantasy, and that what we can actually do is aim for good behaviour on the distribution we'll deploy into, using evidence from the models we already have, and iterate. I don't want a survey of that debate and please don't tell me to go read papers -- I mostly can't get them, and I want your reasoning rather than citations anyway.

What I want is an agenda. Which open questions inside this argument are most worth a year of one person's attention, and why. Rank them, and defend the ranking with the criteria you're actually using -- how much other stuff depends on the answer, whether it's tractable for me specifically (no lab, no collaborators, decent at reading math slowly), whether it's neglected or everyone's already on it, and what happening in the next year or two would move something up or down the list. The end I'm optimising for: I want to change how effort and money get split between "make systems provably safe" and "make systems empirically okay and iterate," so weight accordingly.

Here's what I've scribbled down. Tell me which are duds, which are secretly the same question in different clothes, and what I'm missing.

1. Whether "worst case" in alignment is even the same kind of object as worst case in cryptography. I half remember that for some problems people showed average-case and worst-case hardness come to the same thing. But in alignment, the thing you need a guarantee against is also sort of the adversary, and its the system whose internals you don't understand. Does the borrowing make sense at all, or is it a metaphor doing unpaid work?

2. The one I can't stop chewing on: "average" has to be measured against a distribution of inputs and deployment contexts, but systems increasingly help write the prompts they get, shape the environment they're deployed into, and pick the data they're trained on further. The baseline you're averaging over moves with the thing you're evaluating. Does the average/worst distinction survive that, or does it collapse into something else, and is that something else a question anyone is asking?

3. Why nobody talks about what I'd call "worst case over the parts I can enumerate, average over the parts I can't" -- or tail risk at the 99.99th percentile rather than literal worst case. I'd of thought this was the obvious compromise position. Is there a real reason it isn't live, or is it just not fashionable?

4. Whether failures across different systems are likely to be correlated, so that averaging over many models buys you a pile of bets that all lose together instead of safety.

5. The one I suspect is a dud: is the whole argument really about how much you care about the tail -- risk tolerance, dressed up as a methodological disagreement?

Bonus: if you think the highest-value thing for someone in my position is none of these, say so plainly and justify it, including if that means saying the question is badly posed.
