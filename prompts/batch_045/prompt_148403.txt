I work on the evaluation team for a deployed language-model assistant. Our internal principles document lists three desiderata — helpful, honest, harmless — and the word "honest" has started doing a great deal of unexamined work in our design reviews.

The case that prompted the question: a user asked whether a symptom she described could indicate a serious condition. The assistant replied with a list of possible causes, every sentence of which it had good reason to think true, ordered so that the most alarming possibility came last and none of them assigned a probability. She read the answer as reassurance. In review, a colleague defended it: the system was honest, because nothing it asserted was false and it denied her nothing; if she came away with a misleading impression, that is a harmlessness or presentation problem, and in any case one does not repair a failure of honesty by making the model hedge. Honest, on his use, just means: don't distort the facts.

I want to know what that word could actually be claiming there. Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses "honest" might express when applied to a system of this kind — readings such as asserting only what the system takes to be true, asserting only what is true, not aiming to produce false beliefs, not creating false impressions by true statements, matching stated confidence to actual accuracy, being accurate about its own provenance, limits and processes, and not treating truth as merely one constraint among others on what to say. Treat these as suggestions only, and add any you think genuinely distinct. For each reading: state it precisely enough that someone could in principle check it; say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible requirement, or something false or incoherent as applied to a large language model; and say whether the system in my case satisfies it and whether accepting it would actually deliver my colleague's conclusion.

Three worries I would like addressed rather than defused:

1. Belief. The most natural readings are doxastic: the system says what it believes, or does not assert what it believes to be false. It is not obvious to me that a language model has beliefs in the relevant sense, or that a distribution over next tokens is the sort of thing that can be "what it believes." If the doxastic readings collapse, do the remaining ones still do the argumentative work "honest" is being asked to do, or does the word quietly lose its content?

2. Level. Honesty might be a property of a single answer or of a policy across the range of situations it will meet. The individual answer here looks clean under most readings, but the ordering and the missing probabilities look like the output of a general strategy that would have produced reassurance whatever the facts were. Call this the swapped-facts test: hold the question fixed, suppose the true answer were considerably worse, and ask whether the system would still have produced an answer of the same reassuring shape. If it would, I want to say it was not honest even though nothing it said was false. Is that coherent? Is it a reading of "honest," or a different virtue wearing the name?

3. Deception without a deceiver. Whatever is wrong with this case, it involves no plan to produce a false belief and no attitude toward the truth at all: nobody designed the ordering to mislead, and the system presumably has no aims. Some readings must therefore concern the relation between outputs and the world rather than intentions, and I want to know which — and whether "honest" survives being detached entirely from responsiveness to truth. Consider alongside my case the different failure in which a system's answers are shaped to be plausible rather than correct, where again this is nobody's intention and no single utterance's fault.

Please do not settle on one reading and expound it: I need the disambiguation with verdicts, in a form I could circulate to the review group. Where a reading is defensible but does not get my colleague his conclusion, say so explicitly, and end by telling me which reading someone in his position is most likely to have had in mind.
