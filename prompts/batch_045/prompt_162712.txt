For most of my working life I was an experimental physicist, and I came to philosophy late, carrying the reflex that an infinity turning up in a physical theory is normally a symptom that the theory has been pushed beyond its domain. In a graduate seminar on the philosophy of mathematics I have been trying to turn that reflex into an argument, and I have produced one that I cannot fault but also cannot believe, because it takes considerably more down with it than I ever wanted to bring down.

Two definitions first. Call a collection concrete when its members are located in space and time and have causal powers. Call it completed when there is a determinate fact of the matter how many members it has, as opposed to its merely increasing without bound.

(1) Any completed infinite collection is equinumerous with a proper part of itself: there is a one-to-one correspondence pairing all of its members with all but one of them. This is Dedekind's criterion, and it is what licenses Hilbert's Hotel — a hotel with infinitely many rooms, every one occupied, which can take in a further guest, or absorb the loss of one, by a shift that moves each occupant exactly once.
(2) Take any completed collection, remove one member, add nothing. What remains has one fewer member than what you began with. This is not an empirical generalization; it seems to follow from what removing one and adding nothing means, and it is what I rely on whenever I check a particle count or reconcile a ledger.
(3) Cardinality is unique. Nothing both has and lacks the same number of members as a given collection; having n members and having n minus one members name different numbers.
(4) So no completed collection is infinite. Every completed collection is finite.
(5) A beginningless series of past events, a space of unlimited extent, a continuum of points or of instants, a future with no last event in a block universe: each of these either is, or contains, a concrete completed infinite collection.
(6) So the past has a first event, space contains finitely many places, any stretch of time contains finitely many instants, and the future has an end.

Three features of this worry me more than (6) itself.

First, I cannot see what concreteness has to do with (2). If the principle holds, it seems to hold for collections of numbers as much as for collections of atoms. The argument therefore does not stop at nature: it yields that the natural numbers themselves do not form a completed totality, which withdraws the license for the real analysis my former colleagues use daily — and, awkwardly, for the universal quantifications in the argument I have just written down.

Second, the discreteness in (6) is not Zeno's puzzle but something stronger: a finite ceiling on the number of distinct instants in a second, and a shortest length. I do not see how a structure like that can carry the continuous symmetries — rotations, changes of inertial frame — that every theory I have ever tested presupposes, and I would not know how to state those theories without the continuum.

Third, I had hoped to spare the future with the familiar move that the future is merely potential while the past is actual. But because I take the relativity of simultaneity seriously, I am drawn to the block universe, on which future events are as much part of the furniture of the world as present ones. If that is right, the escape is closed, and the argument hands me a last event, which is the least credible thing it hands me.

So: which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would like the step named precisely, an account of why it looked to be in perfectly good order, and a priced comparison of the exits I can see — giving up (2); denying that a one-to-one correspondence settles sameness of number and restoring the Euclidean principle that a whole is greater than its part; insisting that the step from infinite to Dedekind-infinite in (1) smuggles in a choice principle that a concrete collection need not satisfy; and distinguishing potential from actual infinity in some way that leaves the mathematics untouched. Tell me as well whether any of these exits leaves me able to keep the conclusion I actually wanted — that no completed infinity is to be found among concrete things — or whether that conclusion was never separable from the ones I find impossible to accept.
