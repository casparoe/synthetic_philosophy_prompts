I'm auditing a graduate reading group on classical Chinese philosophy, and for two sessions now we have stalled on a single passage in James Legge's nineteenth-century translation of the *Zhuangzi* (the book he titles *Kih Lo*, or "Perfect Enjoyment," in the Sacred Books of the East series). The passage is Zhuangzi's answer to Huizi, who has found him squatting on the ground, drumming on a basin and singing, after his wife died:

"When she first died, was it possible for me to be singular and not affected by the event? But I reflected on the commencement of her being. She had not yet been born to life; not only had she no life, but she had no bodily form; not only bad [sic — a typo in the scan I'm using] she no bodily form, but she had no breath. During the intermingling of the waste and dark chaos, there ensued a change, and there was breath; another change, and there was the bodily form; another change, and there came birth and life. There is now a change again, and she is dead. The relation between these things is like the procession of the four seasons from spring to autumn, from winter to summer. There now she lies with her face up, sleeping in the Great Chamber; and if I were to fall sobbing and going on to wail for her, I should think that I did not understand what was appointed (for all). I therefore restrained myself!"

A few lines later in the same book, in the exchange between Mr. Deformed and Mr. One-foot as they look at the grave mounds at Khwän-lun, the text says: "Life is a borrowed thing. The living frame thus borrowed is but so much dust. Life and death are like day and night."

I don't need a summary of "Zhuangzi's attitude toward death"; what I need is help with the mechanics of the argument, because I can't tell what work the cosmology is supposed to be doing. I've sketched three readings and suspect they are not really three: (i) the *phase-in-a-process* reading, on which death is not bad because it is one more phase in an ongoing sequence of transformations, so that dreading it is like dreading autumn; (ii) the *no-subject* reading, on which there was never a persisting subject for death to befall — a person is a temporary configuration of breath and form — so that the passage anticipates something like the Epicurean symmetry argument but with a metaphysical rather than an atomist underpinning; and (iii) the *standpoint-dissolution* reading, on which the reflection is not meant to yield a verdict about death at all but to remove the standpoint from which the question "is this bad, and for whom?" has an answer, which would explain why the sentence ends with a change in Zhuangzi ("I therefore restrained myself") rather than a discovery about his wife. Please walk me through the fourfold sequence (no life / no form / no breath / breath, form, life) and tell me what it commits the text to about what a person is, whether it is best read as a temporal sequence or as a conceptual unpacking, and which of my three readings the passage's own details — the seasonal analogy, the Great Chamber, the phrase "what was appointed" — actually support or resist.

Two further things are tripping me up, and I would rather have them addressed than skipped. First, call it the *audience problem*: a phase-in-a-process consolation seems to work only if the continuing process still matters to the one who died, but if the configuration that was his wife no longer has a point of view, then "sleeping in the Great Chamber" is either only a metaphor or it implies that some experiential perspective survives; and "what was appointed" seems to import a kind of necessity or allotment that sits awkwardly with a blind process of transformation. Second, I can't tell whether the standpoint-dissolution reading has any practical content, or whether it collapses into mere indifference: note that Zhuangzi says he *was* affected at first and then stopped wailing, so the equanimity seems to consist either in ceasing to feel grief or in continuing to feel it while ceasing to treat it as a reason to act — and only one of those, it seems to me, is compatible with his present drumming and singing. If it helps, consider how the nearby episode of the talking skull (in which the dead describe their condition as preferable to any earthly kingdom, and the living are asked whether they would return) bears on the choice between (i), (ii), and (iii) — and please tell me plainly if my three-way taxonomy is confusing distinct levels of analysis, since I would rather discard it than go on using it in my presentation next week.
