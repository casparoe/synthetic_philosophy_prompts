I got into an argument at a reading group last week and I still can't locate where the disagreement actually lives. Here's the setup. Suppose a large pre-registered multi-site study comes out tomorrow: it runs the usual battery of epistemic vignettes — Gettier-ish cases, the inherited-name case where someone gets famous for a proof actually written by somebody else, fake-barn style cases — across thirty-odd language communities, and replicates the familiar pattern of variation. But it reports two extra things. First, the variation tracks, not culture, religion, or years of schooling, but one narrow grammatical feature: whether the language obligatorily marks evidentiality, meaning the grammar forces you to encode how you came to know a thing — saw it, was told it, inferred it — whenever you make a claim you didn't directly witness. When the same cases are re-run with that marking neutralised, the differences mostly collapse. Second, the philosopher subjects in the study — actual experts who've published on knowledge — split along the same lines: the ones who grew up speaking an evidentiality-marking language give the minority answers at basically the same rate as undergraduates from those communities. Assume the methods are clean and the effects are real; I don't want to spend the answer on whether the study would really come out that way.

Here's the number I want. Given that study, what's your credence that documented cross-cultural variation in epistemic intuitions gives analytic epistemologists a serious reason to stop treating their own armchair reactions to cases as evidence about what knowledge is? By serious reason I mean it should move a careful person from case-judgments are some evidence to case-judgments are roughly no evidence, as far as the extension of the concept goes. I'm at about 0.3, my friend — she does fieldwork semantics and thinks the armchair was already on borrowed time — is at about 0.8, and I have a feeling we're both right about different parts of this.

I want the decomposition, not a vibe. My candidate sub-questions, which you should cut, add to, or reweight as you like: how much of the raw variation is elicitation artifact anyway (translation, pragmatics, comprehension, what reading of the vignette people take to be intended) versus a genuine difference in what the cases produce; if it is genuine, whether it's a difference in concept or just in which reading strikes people as the natural one; whether the expert split kills the expertise defence or just shows the experts are answering a different question about a theoretical notion rather than the folk one; whether intuitions were ever evidence at all, as opposed to a starting point for reflective equilibrium; and whether knowledge is even the sort of thing that could have a stable cross-cultural extension. Give each term a number and one line of justification, show me how they combine into your headline figure, then tell me which single term you're least confident about, which sub-question the final number is most sensitive to, and where you think the gap between my 0.3 and her 0.8 actually sits. If your number would come out different for other usual suspects — reference, moral responsibility, free will — say by how much and why. Last thing: I have one free semester and could actually run a version of this study, so tell me what single result would move your number the most, and in which direction.
