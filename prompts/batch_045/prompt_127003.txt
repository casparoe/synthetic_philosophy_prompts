Please argue that the most defensible response to Bradley's regress is the inherited-unity (or fact-first) account of the unity of the proposition, and that the regress itself rests on what I shall call the freezing fallacy.

I am preparing graduate seminar notes on Bradley's regress and the unity of the proposition, and I want to present my students with the strongest available case for one underappreciated response. To make the position precise, suppose we characterize the problem as follows. Bradley's regress, as it bears on propositions, begins with the observation that a proposition such as that a stands in relation R to b apparently has at least three constituents: a, R, and b. If the proposition is to be more than a mere list or aggregate of these constituents, it seems that something must unify them. But if that unifying item is itself a further constituent—say, a relation of instantiation, or a nexus that ties a, R, and b into a structure—then the same question arises again: what unifies the original constituents together with this new tie? And so on, apparently without end.

The fact-first (or inherited-unity) account replies as follows. A proposition is not a free-standing structured complex that must assemble its own constituents by means of an internal tie. Rather, a proposition is an abstraction from, or a truth-bearing derivative of, a non-mereological fact or state of affairs: the fact that aRb. In the fact itself, the relation R is not an inert object sitting alongside its relata; it is an essentially active relation, whose occurrence just consists in its relating a and b. Because R is essentially active in the fact, there is no gap between R and its relata for any further relation to bridge, and the demand for a connecting tie is therefore question-begging. The regress is generated only by the freezing fallacy: we illicitly freeze the essentially active relation into an inert constituent, as if it were just another object, and then ask what connects it to a and b. Once this fallacy is diagnosed, the demand for an internal propositional tie disappears. The unity of the proposition is inherited from the unity of the fact, and the fact is a primitive, irreducibly relational state of affairs that does not itself consist of a relation plus relata plus a connector.

Please make the strongest argument you can for this fact-first position. In particular, argue that (1) the freezing fallacy correctly explains why the regress seems compelling, (2) the fact-first account explains the difference between a proposition and a mere aggregate or list without positing a regress-generating tie, and (3) the view has a better cost-benefit profile than the main alternatives: it avoids Bradley's radical monism, avoids treating instantiation as a spurious extra relation, and avoids the mystery of a primitive propositional tie that relates without itself being related. You should also address the following objection: if the unity of a proposition is inherited from a fact, what accounts for the unity of false propositions, which are grasped in thought even though no corresponding fact obtains? Your argument should be rigorous and suitable for advanced seminar discussion, but it must not rely on any literature, citations, or textual exegesis; base the case entirely on philosophical reasoning.
