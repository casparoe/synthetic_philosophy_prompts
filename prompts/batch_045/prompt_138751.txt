I'm a first-year PhD student in semantics/pragmatics and I've been given one eighty-minute slot on Grice in the "Language and Mind" survey I TA for — sophomores, badly mixed: about half of them have done truth-conditions and entailment in the semantics half of the course, the other half have never opened a linguistics textbook, and the whole course has an AI angle this year so at least three people will have their hands up about language models before I've finished the first slide. I don't need Grice explained to me, I need a plan for explaining him, and what I keep going back and forth on is the order. Do I open with the 1957 "Meaning" paper — speaker/utterer's meaning as a matter of what the speaker intends, the reflexive-intention business — and only then build up what is said versus what is implicated out of the William James lectures? Or do I start from a case, get them to feel the gap between what's said and what's communicated, and cash out what "means" is doing only at the point where it becomes load-bearing? I care about the actual dependency structure here, not just a sequence that feels nice: what has to be on the board before the conversational implicature of an utterance can even be stated (expression meaning, disambiguation, reference assignment, the assumption that the speaker is cooperating), and where in that order do the diagnostics belong — cancellability, non-detachability, calculability — given that each of them seems to presuppose something slightly different that I might not have introduced yet? And where does the four-way thing go (flouting vs. violating vs. opting out vs. the two maxms clashing), since flouting feels like it should come late but is also the bit they'll enjoy most and the bit that, if I botch the ordering, turns into "Grice says we're all secretly obeying rules."

Second thing: the example that has to carry the whole session. I don't want the reference letter, and I don't think I want the "he hasn't been convicted of a crime yet" case from the 1961 causal theory paper either, though I'm open to being talked into it — my worry about both is that they prime exactly the wrong thing: the letter makes implicature look like insincerity or euphemism, and the conviction example makes it look like a lawyerly trick. So: what should the one case be, and what does it have to have and lack? My current guess is that it should have a what-is-said that the students can state out loud before they derive anything from it, that the extra content should be genuinely calculable from that plus the assumption of cooperation rather than just "obvious from context", that it should survive paraphrase (so it can't be a matter of the particular words), that it should be cancellable without the cancellation sounding like a contradiction or a retraction, that it should have no but/therefore/even/managed-to in it so it can't be mistaken for a conventional implicature, and that it should be a straight-faced observation rather than a flout, so that metaphor and irony can come later as a second pass. But I'd like you to argue with that list — probably one of those constraints is wrong, or I'm missing the one that actually matters.

Third: the misconception map, since I know roughly what I got wrong when I first met this stuff. I'm expecting (a) implicature = "hidden meaning" or the real meaning behind the literal one, (b) implicature = whatever the hearer happens to infer, so it collapses into "context", (c) the class insisting that "some" literally means some but not all and that scalar cases are entailments, (d) cancellability read as taking it back / having lied / contradicting yourself, which then makes them think the implicature was part of what was said all along, and (e) the maxims being heard as etiquette or as moral norms, so the takeaway becomes "Grice thinks conversation is polite." Tell me where in the sequence I should head each of these off rather than just refuting them at the end, and how. I also want your advice on two things I'm half-inventing. One: I made up a stress-test case — twins with a private code, where "the kettle's on" means let's leave the party — which looks like an implicature but dies under paraphrase, so the diagnostics rule it a code instead; is that worth five minutes as a way of showing what the tests are for, or is it a distraction at this level? Two: someone will ask whether a language model implicates, given it has no intentions, and every answer I can think of either opens the entire can of worms about whether the intention-based story is the right foundation at all or sounds like I'm dodging. Where do I put that, and what's the honest twenty-second version? Finally, give me one check I can run in the last ten minutes that would actually show the distinction has landed rather than that they've memorised the vocabulary — I was thinking something like: here's a sentence, state what is said, show me the calculation that gets you the implicature and name the maxim it leans on, then rewrite it so the what-is-said is unchanged and the implicature is gone, then tell me which of these three items isn't a conversational implicature and why. And be explicit about what I should refuse to go into at this level and say out loud that I'm refusing — I'm guessing the later refinements of the intention-based theory of meaning, the circularity worry about analysing meaning in terms of intentions, the razor, the neo-Gricean scalar stuff, relevance theory, and the whole fight about how much context gets into what is said, but tell me if I'm cutting something I shouldn't.
