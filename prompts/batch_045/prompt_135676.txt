I'm writing up my own position on AI alignment for an internal doc and I'd rather have someone poke holes in it before I send it. Roughly where I've landed:

1. I'm in the prosaic alignment camp — aligning the systems we'd actually build out of current methods is mostly an engineering problem, not something where we're blocked waiting on a new conceptual or philosophical insight.
2. Scheming / deceptive alignment is a real failure mode worth planning around: a model that behaves during training because behaving is instrumentally useful, and does something else once it's out of distribution.
3. Capability and alignment are pretty entangled — a lot of what makes a model good at pursuing goals also makes it good at pursuing the ones we want, so I don't buy that there's a big seperate alignment tax to pay on top of scale.
4. I've started calling this alignment debt: post-training (RLHF and friends) mostly edits surface behaviour and leaves whatever goal-directed circuitry pretraining built in place, so it's like paying interest and never touching principal — and the bill comes due in deployment contexts nobody trained on.
5. I'm fine shipping systems whose internals we can't read as long as behavioural evals pass; making full mechanistic interpretability a precondition is just a way to never ship anything.
6. Once a model is better than the grader at a task, human eval is worthless, so those evals are going to have to be AI-assisted.
7. We don't need to settle what it even means for a model to 'want' something before we can do useful safety work — that's a question I file under philosophy and move past.

Does that hang together? Which pairs genuinely fight each other and which only look like they do, and in each case what would I have to give up? Rank the clashes by severity, give me the cheapest fix for each, and flag if one of the seven is doing more damage than the rest — I dont actually know which one I'd drop.
