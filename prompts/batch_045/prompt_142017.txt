Hi — I'm teaching the fairness-and-automated-decision unit of my applied ethics seminar in a few weeks and I want to end it on something better than another round of "ok so which criterion do we pick." Two colleagues of mine in the policy school have been sniping at each other about this for years, and I've talked them into an adversarial collaboration, so I need the document they ought to have produced. Can you draft it?

The case they're fighting about: a county housing authority has 400 slots a year in an intensive supportive-housing program and about 3,000 applicants. A vendor supplies a score in [0,1], presented as the probability that the applicant will experience housing instability within 12 months. Top 400 by score get the slots. Two things make it messy. First, the recorded base rates differ substantially across the two groups the agency is required to report on. Second, outcomes are only observed for people who were actually flagged — the agency has follow-up data on maybe 15% of applicants, and not at random — so the score's own inputs are partly a record of who the system has previously watched and who it has previously helped.

Camp one I'd call the formalists: they take the impossibility results at face value and think the honest deliverable is to pick one criterion, say out loud which one you sacrificed, and audit against the chosen one. Camp two I'd call the system people: they think the impossibility results are artifacts of a static, single-decision, group-level framing with the label taken as given, that fairness is a property of the whole arrangement over time rather than of the classifier, and that "satisfying a criterion" isn't the thing we were after anyway.

Two things I want you to work into it, since neither of them has thought about these yet. First, a criterion I've been toying with: call it differential counterfactual improvement — across the two reported groups, equalize the expected gain from being flagged (say in probability of stable housing at 24 months) conditional on score, rather than equalizing error rates or calibration. It looks like it sidesteps the standard results because it's about the effect of the system's own intervention rather than about prediction errors, but it needs counterfactual estimates you basically can't get from the data the agency has, and it seems to collide head-on with giving the slots to whoever benefits most. Second: the impossibility results are usually stated at a fixed threshold with a fixed budget. I want to know whether any of the clash survives when the threshold is whatever the budget allows and the score is recomputed every six months with people able to re-apply.

What I need: (1) a numbered section of claims both of them can actually sign — about the arithmetic, about what the recorded outcome variable is and isn't, about selective labels, about what an audit can and can't establish, about the vendor's data access, whatever else you think is genuinely common ground and not just platitude. (2) A table of the residual disagreements: for each one, the formalist's position in wording they'd accept for themselves, the system person's in wording they'd accept, whether the disagreement is real or just verbal (and if verbal, say exactly where the words went wrong), the crux underneath it, a concrete case or test both would agree bears on that crux, and each side's prediction about how it comes out written down in advance. I'd like at least four rows, and please include as one row the fate of differential counterfactual improvement: is it a way out, a relabeling of the old problem, or a new problem wearing a hat.

Keep it under about 1,200 words, prose is fine, and no citations or reading lists — half the room reads on their phones and I don't want anyone hallucinating paper titles at me.
