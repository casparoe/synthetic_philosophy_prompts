I'm a high school student serving on my school's student council, and for an independent study project I'm writing a short paper on how the way a group carves a decision into sub-questions can change what the group decides. I already understand the basic discursive dilemma: even when every member's own views are logically consistent, a council that votes on the premises of a decision and then applies the decision rule can reach a verdict different from the one it reaches by voting directly on the conclusion, with nobody changing their mind in between. What I need now is a more specific example, and I'd be grateful if you would build one to a fairly tight specification.

Set the example at a student council of five members deciding whether to hold an outdoor spring dance. Treat the dance as going ahead if and only if all three of the following hold: B, the budget has been approved; V, the venue is available; W, the weather plan is adequate.

Please construct the example so that it has all of the following features.

1. Each member holds a definite view on B, on V, and on W, and each member is fully consistent, in the sense that each one's view about whether the dance should go ahead is exactly what their own views on B, V, and W imply. No member's views shift anywhere in the example; the only thing that changes is how the motion is divided up.
2. When the council votes on B, V, and W as three separate items and then applies the rule "the dance goes ahead if and only if a majority accepts each of the three," the motion carries.
3. The chair then re-runs the vote using the very same opinions, but bundles two of the conditions into a single item: one item reading "the budget has been approved and the venue is available," and a second item reading "the weather plan is adequate," with the same rule applied to the bundled items. Under this two-item agenda the motion fails.
4. Report what happens if the council instead votes once, up or down, on "shall we hold the dance?" with no sub-questions at all, and say whether the result you get there is an accident of your particular example or something that had to happen given features 2 and 3. Give a one- or two-sentence argument for whichever answer you give.

After the example, add three or four sentences on what you take it to show. The part I'm stuck on is this: since the chair can choose how finely to chop the motion up, and since both the three-item and the two-item agendas are faithful ways of representing the same underlying decision rule, is there any non-arbitrary way of saying which decomposition is the correct one? If you think there isn't, then tell me what a council that wanted to be fair should do instead — fix the decomposition in advance in its bylaws, always vote directly on the conclusion, deliberate until consensus, or something else — and say briefly why that option is better than the alternatives. If you think there is a privileged decomposition, say which one it is and what makes it privileged.

Please make the example concrete enough that I can put the five members' opinions into a table in my paper, and don't pad the answer with citations or suggested reading; I only need the example itself and the reasoning about it.
