hiya — i need a deadpan parody of a UK regulator's guidance note, roughly 800 words, numbered clauses with a couple of worked examples, headed something like "Office for Derivation Compliance — Guidance Note 14: Unlicensed Normative Ascent". Target isn't a person, it's the habit of treating the no-ought-from-an-is rule as a clean, administrable rule — Hume's law as its run together with Moore's naturalistic fallacy, i.e. as if you could just police the border between facts and values with a form and a tariff.

Voice: a tired but genuinely conscientious civil servant who takes the rule absolutely seriously and is therefore tying himself in knots by clause 3.

I want the comedy to come out of real features of the mess rather than made-up ones:

- Inspectors have to classify "derivation events" and apply a penalty tariff, but they cannot in practice tell which premises are descriptive and which aren't. Thick concepts — cruel, cowardly, generous, degrading — get referred to a Thick Concepts Working Group, which was set up in 2019 and has never issued any guidance at all.
- The licensed exemption for conditional, means-ends reasoning: if you want X you ought to do Y is permitted as a Category 3 derivation. The Office is drowning in people laundering categorical claims through it — slip in a "as we all want…" and apparently you're fine — and there's a clause about how to spot one, which admits the test doesn't work.
- Function and health talk: a knife that doesn't cut is a bad knife, a heart that doesn't pump is defective. One anonymous official clearly believes this route rebuilds the whole of ethics out of biology, and the note hovers over whether to licence it without ever deciding.
- The institutional-fact problem: a person says the words I promise and thereby incurs an obligation. Please treat this as a long-running unresolved appeal, with a footnote conceding nobody on the panel can agree whether it's a genuine counterexample or a sleight of hand.
- The Office's own enabling statute — something like "a conclusion not supported by its premises ought not to be asserted" — obviously does the very thing it bans, and its authority rests on nothing but the brute fact that it was duly passed. I'd love this set out in exactly the same flat tone as the rest, no winking, no "of course the real joke is".
- One worked example where the descriptive premise is the disputed bit — say a consultation response claiming a policy will hurt the people it's meant to help — so the inspector can't even get started on whether an ought was smuggled in.
- Near the end, someone notices that calling the inference a fallacy is already an evaluation, so it gets rebranded something bloodless like "unlicensed normative ascent", which is of course…

Feel free to invent a bit of furniture: an amnesty bin for retrospective licensing, a submission from a professor at some made-up institute who has sent in 400 pages arguing the whole distinction rests on a pun, a definition clause that has to be revised twice inside itself.

Should be funny to someone who's never read a word of metaethics and fair enough that someone who has would grumble and agree. No puns on Hume's surname, please, and don't let it turn into a lecture — the note should never notice its own predicament. cheers
