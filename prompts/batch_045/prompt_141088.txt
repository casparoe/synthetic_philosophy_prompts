Write a dialogue between two philosophically trained friends, Mira and Devin, arguing about whether a person can come to know, or justifiably believe, that a work is beautiful, moving, or bad on the basis of what others tell them, rather than from their own first-hand experience of it.

Mira defends the acquaintance requirement: aesthetic judgment is essentially first-personal, and testimony can at best tell you what to expect from a work, never put you in a position to make the judgment yourself. Devin defends the permissive view: well-informed critics can transmit aesthetic knowledge, and in practice nearly everyone's aesthetic life already rests on testimony far more than they admit.

Make them equally competent. Each should concede what is genuinely forceful in the other's position and press on the other's actual arguments rather than restating their own. Have them work through the following two cases, and let the cases put pressure on both views:

1. A musicologist with congenital amusia, who cannot hear pitch relationships at all, but who has spent thirty years studying a composer's manuscripts, drafts, and letters, and who reports with total confidence that a particular passage is subtly and devastatingly beautiful.

2. A painting whose original varnish has darkened and whose surface is now so fragile that cleaning it would destroy the paint layer, so that no one now living, and no one ever again, can have visual acquaintance with the work as it looked when it was made. A committee of distinguished conservators must nonetheless decide what best serves the painting's aesthetic value, relying entirely on chemical analysis, historical documents, and one another's reports.

Mira should have to say what her principle implies for the committee's decision, given that refusing to act is also a decision. Devin should have to explain why, if testimony really can substitute for acquaintance here, the loss still feels like a loss. By the end they should have arrived at a sharper statement of what exactly is supposed to go missing when judgment rests on testimony, or of why it is so hard to accept that nothing essential goes missing. They need not settle the disagreement.
