so there's this fight that's been going in my effective-altruism-adjacent group chat for over a year, and the two people at the center of it — Marcus and Dana — have finally agreed, sort of grudgingly, to do an adversarial collaboration. I got volunteered to organize it. Can you draft the joint document they'd sign?

Here's where each of them is, as best I understand it. I'm not going to say which one I think is right, because I honestly don't know, and I don't want that leaked into the doc.

Marcus's side, roughly: if we don't wipe ourselves out and things go okay, the future could contain astronomically many lives — orders of magnitude more people than have ever lived — spread across Earth and eventually space. So (i) lowering the chance of an early extinction or a permanent civilizational collapse matters more in expectation than anything we could do for people alive right now, and (ii) even a tiny probability of nudging the long-run trajectory somewhere better beats a near-certain small gain, because the stakes are so much bigger. He also thinks influence is concentrated in certain moments, so deliberately waiting and holding resources until the influence is high is a real strategy and not just procrastination.

Dana's side has three complaints. (a) Our causal claims about centuries out are garbage: effects wash out, we can't tell sign from noise, and running an expected-value calculation over that is multiplying numbers we made up. (b) It isn't even settled that adding vast numbers of happy future lives counts as a benefit — a lot of ordinary moral thinking is about making people who already exist better off, not bringing new ones into existence, and preventing a collapse is exactly the case where none of the future people are better off than they'd otherwise be. (c) Once you grant astronomical stakes plus guessed probabilities, you get thousands of speculative causes each claiming a small shot at a giant payoff, and we have no reliable way to rank them — so ranking them is mostly noise, and acting on the ranking is worse than doing something with measured, robust returns now.

What I want in the document:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — the things both can sign without squinting. It should at minimum cover: how bad a permanent collapse would be, and why that isn't actually the crux between them; the point that a giant number of future people settles nothing on its own without the probabilities; the seperate questions of whether the far future is worth a lot and whether we can do anything about it; and the distinction between benefits to people who would exist either way and benefits that consist in which people end up existing. Add anything you think genuinely belongs. Don't put anything in there that only one of them would accept if you squinted at it.

2. A table of the disagreements that are left. One row per disagreement, with the question stated neutrally and then two formulations — one Marcus would sign as his own view, one Dana would sign as hers — each specific enough that you could tell if someone had changed their mind. No vacuous filler rows.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions where settling them would actually move at least one of them. For each crux, give a concrete case, hypothetical, or piece of evidence both would agree bears on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance. I want at least these four covered, in whatever framing you think is cleanest:

- How long the identifiable effects of present-day actions actually last. Is there something like a half-life of influence across centuries, or do effects mostly wash out within a few generations? What would each of them expect to find if we could really trace it?
- The different-people point: whether averting a collapse that changes who comes to exist counts as a benefit at all, and how much of Marcus's case survives if it partly doesn't.
- Whether the aim should be steering the future toward outcomes we currently think are good, or protecting later generations' ability to decide for themselves. I floated an idea in the chat that maybe the whole thing should be reframed around option value: act to widen and defend the range of choices available to people later, rather than picking the outcome you like, since they'll know more than we do and a wrong pick could get locked in. Marcus half liked it; Dana said it just smuggles the astronomy back in under a new name. Put it in as a live option and say exactly what it would and wouldn't settle.
- The lock-in case. Suppose you could either make extinction basically impossible but permanently freeze in a mediocre value system, or leave things risky — a real chance of collapse — with the future's values wide open. Make both of them say which they'd take and why, and say what their answers reveal about whether they're really arguing about the size of the stakes or about something else entirely.

4. A short section on which parts of their argument are verbal — where they're using the same word for different things ("expected value," "robust," "existential risk," "dominates") — and why.

5. A short section on what each of them should count as evidence against their own position, and whether there's anything at all both would agree would genuinely move the debate, versus what they'd each just reinterpret to fit.

Format: numbered agreed-claims first, then the disagreements table, then the cruxes with the predictions laid out readably, then the last two sections. Write it in American English. And please don't cite papers, name authors, or drop footnotes — I want the argument itself, not a reading list, and half of us are reading this on our phones anyway. Don't pad it either: if a crux doesn't have a real test either side would accept, say so instead of inventing one.
