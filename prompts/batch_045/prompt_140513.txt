For the author's note to my next novel — a historical novel set in a small multilingual town on the edge of a shrinking empire in the 1880s — my editor wants a few sentences saying what I actually think about the history I am working from, on the grounds that reviewers will read the book as taking a position on it whether or not I mean it to. Before I put anything in print I would like the position itself audited. This is where I have landed, stated as plainly as I can manage.

1. I accept the modernist orthodoxy about nations more or less as received: the nation is a formation of the last two centuries or so, made possible by print-circulated vernaculars, mass schooling, conscription, censuses and administrative standardization, and the deep antiquity it claims for itself is part of the apparatus rather than evidence for it.

2. That claim concerns how nationalism arose, not what belonging feels like from inside. My characters experience their nationality as inherited and immemorial, felt before it is argued for, and fidelity to their experience obliges me to render it exactly that way — not as a construction they could in principle see through.

3. Several of the customs the novel dwells on (a midsummer procession, a style of patterned weaving everyone treats as age-old, a local saint's legend) are demonstrably recent, datable to the decades just before the novel's present, and I do not treat that recency as a mark of fraudulence. A practice that is devised and then genuinely taken up does the same social work as one that is old; uptake, not pedigree, is what makes it a tradition.

4. I hold myself strictly to the public record — who was where, what was printed, what existed and when — while taking myself to be free to invent interiors, composite minor figures, and scenes no document attests.

5. The novel contains a compilation of local customs, supposedly gathered by a regional antiquarian in the 1870s and presented with scholarly apparatus, which I invented outright. My defence is that it depicts the phenomenon the book is about rather than performing it.

6. I believe fiction can reach truths about the past that professional history's evidentiary discipline excludes — what it was like to be gripped by a belief, say — and I mean to use that capacity here. The region is currently governed by a nationalist narrative claiming immemorial roots, and part of what I want the book to do is loosen that claim's hold on readers who live under it.

What I would like is an audit, not a tutorial. Treat these six as a set and tell me where they pull against each other: which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look it, ordered by how severe the conflict is; for each real tension, what exactly would have to give and what that would cost me elsewhere in the set; and whether there is a single revision that would clean up more than one of them at once. I would be especially grateful to be told if any commitment quietly removes the ground from another — I have an uneasy sense that somewhere in here I am leaning on a distinction my own view elsewhere disallows, but I cannot locate it, and I would rather find out now than in print.
