I do lettering and some brand work on the side, and I keep coming back to that 2010 Gap logo disaster as a case study, but I want to run it through a theory I've been chewing on instead of through the usual "the internet got mad" story.

The theory: the Letter Spirit project out of Hofstadter's group in the 90s — a program that's supposed to be handed a couple of seed letters drawn in some style and then fill out the rest of the alphabet on a grid of reusable parts. The bit I care about is the idea behind it: what makes an 'a' an 'a' isn't a checklist of features you can tick off, it's a tangle of pressures and relationships, and a letter in a font is what it is because of the role it plays in that family. Style doesn't get painted onto letters one at a time, it spreads from the seed to everything else by analogy. No letter is an island, and part of a letterform's identity is the accumulated history of all the instances of it you've ever seen.

Facts as I remember them: Gap swaps the dark blue square with the white serif wordmark for a plain sans wordmark with a small blue gradient square floating behind the p, people lose their minds, about a week later Gap puts the old one back and says theyll crowdsource a replacement.

What I want is an actual mapping, not vibes. In this frame, what are the parts and what's the grid? Is the blue box a letter, a part of one, or neither? What's the seed set, and what's the alphabet it has to extrapolate out to — the store signage, the clothing tags, the website, everything downstream? What does the theory say the design team was really attempting, and does it predict that the mismatch between the generic wordmark and that gradient square would read as cheap rather than merely new? Then be honest about where it stops: it's a model of one mind perceiving, not of a few million people with different exposure histories, and it has nothing to say about the logo as a trademark, or about a retailer with slumping sales using a rebrand as a signal to investors.

Then run it the other direction. I've started calling the thing they underestimated the re-convergence cost — the cost isn't drawing a new logo, it's getting everybody's G to settle again. Does the Letter Spirit picture give you any grip on that, or does this episode show that the essence of a brand letterform was never entirely in the letterform, that some of it lives out in the population? Is that an anomaly for the theory or just a case it never modeled? And what would have had to be true — what seed would they have had to actually redraw, rather than swap in — for the new mark to have had a chance?
