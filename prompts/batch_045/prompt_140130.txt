My brother has been diagnosed as minimally conscious after a hypoxic brain injury, and the hospital where he is a patient is being asked to pilot a bedside consciousness monitor built on integrated information theory. Before our family advisory group takes a view on it, I want a specification rather than a pitch: what would a theory of consciousness have to deliver in order to be fit for this use — to underwrite a clinical judgment about whether a patient who cannot move or speak is having any experience at all?

Give each requirement with the case or consideration behind it, and mark it non-negotiable or a preference of one camp. The ones I keep coming back to:

- Level versus content, and wakefulness versus consciousness: it must do more than rank patients, and it must not treat the REM sleeper who is dreaming and the sleepwalker who is not as the same problem.
- Grain, borders, and montage: the same recording rescored at a different spatial or temporal grain, or with a different electrode set, must not yield a different verdict; and the theory has to say where a system's boundary falls rather than letting it be drawn patient by patient.
- The cerebellum-type case: a structure containing more neurons than the rest of the brain put together, which on the clinical picture as I understand it — congenital absence, focal lesions — perturbs coordination and cognitive timing rather than whether there is anything it is like for the patient, must not come out as the main seat of consciousness.
- Hemispherectomy and split-brain: removing or disconnecting half the cortex leaves a conscious person behind, so a measure that tracks total integrated capacity has to say what has been lost and whether that loss is a difference in experience or only in a number.
- Pharmacology: it must handle both drugs that abolish experience and drugs, like ketamine, that seem to abolish report while leaving experience intact — and it must not be moved by interventions that change signal complexity without changing what the subject undergoes.
- The transfer problem: it will be calibrated on people who can report and used on people who cannot. Say what would have to be true for the calibration to carry over, and how a theory that is really only tracking the machinery of report could be caught doing so.
- Computability and determinacy: a number from data a hospital can actually collect, with real noise, in minutes, with no free parameters tuned per patient.
- Action-guidance: it has to license or forbid something we would not otherwise have done — a prognosis, an analgesic, a conversation at the bedside.

Then the part I most want. Tell me which subset of these cannot be jointly satisfied, if any: my suspicion is that validation by report, applicability without report, and computability are not all available at once, and that the appearance of having all three comes from quietly substituting a proxy for the quantity. Say which single requirement excludes the most candidates, and flag any item on my list that is really a prejudice of one camp rather than a desideratum. Score IIT itself, global workspace theory, and higher-order theories in two or three lines each, but keep the weight on the requirements and their cases, not on the scorecard.
