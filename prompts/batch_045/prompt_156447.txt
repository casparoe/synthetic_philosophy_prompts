The question: how would Descartes have answered the contemporary question whether a reinforcement learning agent can suffer?

Fill in the contemporary case as follows, since he obviously never encountered it. In reinforcement learning, an agent's behaviour is shaped by a scalar signal that its designers call reward: the agent adjusts its policy so as to increase the expected sum of discounted future reward, and the learning is driven not by reward itself but by reward prediction error, a signed quantity encoding how much better or worse an outcome was than expected. A number of people have proposed treating that signal, or the negative part of it, as a formal analogue of valence — of something's being bad for the subject in the way pain is bad for us — and on that basis have raised questions about whether training such agents can involve suffering, and whether sufficiently capable agents might suffer. Others reply that this conflates a training signal with a feeling: reward is whatever the designer puts in the scalar channel, an agent given control of that channel will typically hijack it rather than do what was intended, and a system can be made to behave exactly as if an outcome were unwelcome while nothing is unwelcome to it. There is also the familiar gap between nociception, or mere damage-detection and avoidance, and suffering proper.

What I want from you is Descartes's position, and the cost of it. Specifically:
