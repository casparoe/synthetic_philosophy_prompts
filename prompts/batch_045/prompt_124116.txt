I'm finishing a dissertation in moral philosophy on backward-looking blame, and I keep running into a move in arguments about historical figures — Jefferson, Churchill, Columbus — that I can't get a handle on because I don't have a name for it. The move is the familiar one of saying someone was "a man of his time." What I've noticed is that the defence always rests on a choice of comparison class, and that the choice is almost never made explicit. Usually the class is statistical: what most people in his society, and especially in his social position, in fact believed and did. But sometimes the relevant-sounding class is instead something like what was epistemically available to him — the views that existed, were in circulation, and were in principle accessible to a person in his circumstances even if almost nobody around him held them. These come apart, and the cases I care about are exactly the ones where they do. Jefferson is measured against other Virginia planters, not against the abolitionists writing and organizing in the same decades whose arguments he could have read. Columbus is measured against the norms of fifteenth-century conquest, not against the small number of clerics and jurists who, within a generation of his voyages, were condemning the treatment of the indigenous population outright. Churchill is measured against the consensus of the British imperial establishment, not against the anti-imperialists in the Britain of his own lifetime.

A second thing falls out of this, which may or may not be the same phenomenon. Because the defence keys on how typical the belief was, it gets stronger the more widespread and systematic the wrong is: the more thoroughly everyone was implicated, the less anyone is blameworthy. So blame thins out precisely where the wrongdoing was most serious and most structural, and it lands hardest on the figure whose vice was idiosyncratic for his era. Relatedly, there seems to be an asymmetry in how we treat deviation from the baseline itself — departing from one's contemporaries is credited when it is a departure in achievement ("ahead of his time"), but conformity to them is what gets asked for when it is a matter of culpability. I've had this argued at me in a reading group and I didn't have a good reply beyond gesturing at the literature on moral ignorance and on whether an agent had a fair opportunity to know better, which seems adjacent but doesn't quite say what I want.

So my question: is there an established concept for the conflation of these two baselines — actual contemporaneous belief versus what was reasonably available to the agent — and a separate one for the ratchet whereby wider complicity yields weaker blame, and for the asymmetry between the credit and the excuse? If there is, I don't just want the label: I want to know how the phenomenon is carved — how it differs from the neighbouring notions of excusable versus inexcusable moral ignorance, from circumstantial moral luck, from diffusion of responsibility and complicity in structural injustice, and from the familiar charges of presentism — and which of those, if any, actually fits the cases above. If there isn't, I'd like a term proposed and defined tightly enough that I can tell which real cases fall under it and which don't, including what a case would have to look like for the "man of his time" defence to work legitimately rather than merely to flatter the dead.
