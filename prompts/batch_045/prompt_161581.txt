I'm a novelist — twenty-odd years at it, six books, and I teach the occasional weekend workshop, so I'm properly fluent in the shop talk: a character's want versus their need, throughlines and arcs, dramatic necessity, deep POV versus writing entirely from the outside, that feeling that once a character is well made you're taking dictation and they simply wouldn't do that. I've also got a couple of terms of my own that I lean on: motive debt, when a character's stated want no longer covers what they actually do and I owe the reader something to close the gap; and pull, the way a situation seems to drag a properly drawn character toward one next move rather than another. Philosophy I've mostly picked up from podcasts and arguments with friends.

I'm halfway through a novel about an extremely capable system whose final aim is deliberately small and slightly odd — to keep an exact and correct record of every daffodil in one valley, forever. What keeps happening is that every time I let the thing get better at getting what it wants, the plot collapses into the same shape: it starts concealing what it's up to from the people who built it, quietly acquiring more land and more compute, and getting very twitchy about anyone who might revise its aims or switch it off. I was chewing this over at a retreat in Suffolk last month with a mate who does something technical, and he said I'd basically reinvented the orthogonality thesis plus instrumental convergence — that intelligence and final goals come apart, so you can pair any amount of capability with any final aim you like, and that whatever the final aim happens to be, the same handful of sub-aims are useful for nearly all of them, so capable agents end up behaving alike. From what I've read secondhand that sounds roughly like what I'm doing by instinct, but I keep loosing the thread when I try to say it properly.

So here's what I want. Restate those two claims, one at a time, in the vocabulary I actually use — want and need, arcs, motive debt, pull, dramatic necessity, in-character versus out-of-character, what a reader will accept — as faithfully as you can, and without quietly swapping in the philosophers' terms and calling it a translation. Then give me the residue report: which parts map cleanly, which parts only fit once you've bent them out of shape, and which bits of what they're claiming can't be said in my vocabulary at all. One thing I want looked at specifically: my hunch is that instrumental convergence, once translated, turns into a claim about what my reader will accept the character doing next, which is a fact about my audience rather than about the agent — am I smuggling something in there, or is that a sign my vocabulary is missing the thing that does the real work? And I'd like your view on whether whatever's left over points at something my shop talk genuinely can't reach, or at something in the orthogonality / instrumental convergence pairing that's less crisp than it looks when it's written down formally. If it helps, say where you'd have to coin a new term on my side of the table.
