Hey — I'm on the hook for the entertainment at our end-of-term logic reading group thing (I said yes before thinking, long story), and I want one bit on the liar paradox for the people in the room who actually know the material. So: write me a joke about the Liar.

Constraints, because otherwise I'll get something generic:

- It can't just be a sentence with the word "true" in it wearing a funny hat. The laugh — such as it is — has to come out of the actual mechanics, i.e. out of the difference between the three standard families of response: the Tarski-style move of stratifying the language into levels so the sentence can't refer to its own truth; the Kripke-style move of letting it fall into a truth-value gap, neither true nor false; and the dialetheist move of letting it be both true and false and weakening the logic so that doesn't explode.

- I want the punchline to be a revenge case rather than the plain Liar. The plain version all three can defuse in their own way, and that's boring. Give me the analogue of the strengthened sentence: the one that pops back up in a new form right after somebody thinks they've handled the first one — the sentence that says of itself that it isn't true, or that it isn't even gappy, or whatever the move is that re-creates the difficulty for whoever just declared victory. Ideally make it turn on funniness instead of truth, since it's meant to be a joke: a sentence about its own funniness that resists being classified.

- Then, in a sentence or two each, tell me how a partisan of each of the three approaches would try to kill the joke, and be honest about whether they succeed. Is your bit genuinely a revenge problem for all three, or does it only bite one or two of them? If it's really only hard on the gap theorist, say so — that's still useful, I'd just rather know in advance than find out from the silence in the room.

- Practicalities: performable out loud in under a minute, no props, no slides, and followable by someone who's had one formal logic course but hasn't read any of the literature. A couple of sentences of set-up is fine; I'm not handing out a worksheet.

- One more wrinkle, from a stupid in-house rule we've ended up with: at the end of every bit the speaker has to say, out loud, "that was funny," and the audience then has to decide whether what was just said was true. If you can fold that rule into the joke itself, great. If you think it wrecks the joke, tell me why and I'll let you off.

No citations needed obviously — just the joke, plus the short notes on who can defuse it. Its due tommorow-ish, so don't overthink the polish.
