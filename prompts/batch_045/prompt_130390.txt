I am writing a short section on updating on conditional constraints — the kind of case usually illustrated with the soldier dropped behind enemy lines who receives a radio message fixing the odds of one sub-region against another, conditional on her being in the half of the territory that contains them both — and I need one clean, purpose-built example to make a single point: that the rule which holds the probability of the disjunction fixed while setting the internal ratio to the announced odds is not a neutral or default way to handle such a constraint, because whether it is correct depends on how the report came to be about the sub-partition it is about. I want a case in which that dependence is not a logician's toy but falls out of an ordinary feature of an information-gathering institution, and in which the effect is invisible in the content of the message: a careful reader who attends only to what was said should judge that the message bears on the internal ratio and on nothing else.

Please build me the following.

**The scenario.** Realistic, no more than about two hundred words, with an institutional rather than adversarial source: a search-and-rescue desk, a hospital triage board, an agricultural extension service, a public-health surveillance unit, an engineering diagnostics rota. No game shows, no hosts, no deceivers, no perfect predictors. In it:

1. There is a partition of the agent's uncertainty into four cells — call them A, B, C, D — with A and B forming a sub-partition S and C and D forming its complement. The agent cannot simply look and see which cell she is in. The prior probabilities of all four cells must be motivated inside the story by areas, frequencies, or recorded base rates, and must not be uniform; and the prior odds of A against B must differ from the announced odds by a factor of at least two.

2. She learns exactly one thing: a number that fixes the conditional odds of A against B given S. The number must be news to her, and the story must say what it is a number of — a share of the area of S, a share of the cases falling in S — and must make explicit why, given a value for it, her credence that she is in the A part of S equals that value. The usual way is to have her equally likely to be at any point of S, conditional on being in S; say so if that is what you use.

3. In one or two sentences the story must state the report-selection protocol: how the source came to report about S rather than about its complement, and what the source would have reported in the other cells. The protocol may depend on the agent's location only through a single coarse channel whose reliability is given numerically — a sensor that classifies the coarse category correctly with a stated probability, a routing or triage rule, an alert threshold, a sampling design that oversamples one stratum. The source must have no other information about which cell she is in, and the probability that some report arrives at all must be the same in every cell. The coarse channel's own reading must not be transmitted: only the conditional odds for the sub-partition it indicated.

4. Nothing in the content of the message may hint at S versus its complement. All of the diagnostic force about S must live in the protocol alone.

**The computations.** Tabulate, to three decimals, the posterior over the four cells under each of three procedures.

(a) The textbook kinematics rule: keep P(S) at its prior value, set the ratio of A to B inside S to the announced odds, and preserve the ratio of C to D.

(b) Minimum relative entropy: among all distributions meeting the announced constraint, minimize the sum over cells of q(x)·log(q(x)/p(x)), where p is the prior. Show enough of the working that I can check it.

(c) Ordinary conditioning on the total evidence — the content of the message together with the fact that the protocol produced a report about S — with the likelihoods read off the story.

The three posteriors must give pairwise distinct values of P(S), each differing from the others by at least five percentage points, and you must state in which direction each moves relative to the prior.

**The analysis.** Tell me which of the three is correct given your story and why. Identify the single feature of the case that does the work, and show what happens when it is removed: change the protocol to a fair coin flip independent of everything else, and show whether (c) collapses onto (a), whether (b) does too, and what that asymmetry reveals. Note also what would happen if the coarse channel's own reading were transmitted alongside the odds — I take it the case then collapses into a routine conditioning exercise, and I want to know whether it loses its point entirely or merely becomes less interesting.

Then handle three defenses honestly, since the example is meant to persuade someone who already accepts (a). A defender of (a) who says that once the protocol is specified the input was never a bare conditional constraint, and so the case does not touch the rule: is that substantive, or is it a retreat, and does it concede what I want it to concede? A relative-information minimizer who says that (a)'s preservation of P(S) is an extra constraint smuggled in, and that the constraint ought instead to be represented as a constraint on a refined space: what do they gain by that, and does it change the numbers? And a colleague who says that comparing (b) with (c) is unfair because (b) was built for posterior constraints and (c) for events. Tell me which of these I should concede in print and which I should answer.

My own inclination is that (a) has no default status once protocols are on the table, and I would be most persuaded by a case whose protocol is something a real institution would actually have and whose numbers are large enough that nobody can wave the gap away as an artifact of rounding. If your case quietly presupposes what I want it to show, say so rather than letting me use it.
