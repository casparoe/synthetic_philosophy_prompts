I spent last night arguing with a friend about Sen's 1970 impossibility result — the one usually illustrated with two readers, one copy of a novel, and the discovery that a social ranking which respects each person's say over their own private pair can end up contradicting the requirement that it follow unanimous preference. My friend's claim was that the result has no normative bite. The contradiction, she said, only gets off the ground because the preferences in the example are *nosy*: each person has a ranking over outcomes that differ only inside the other person's pair, and the unanimity requirement then converts that nosiness into a head-on collision with the two rights. Restrict the domain to preference profiles in which everyone is indifferent across other people's pairs, she said, and any such case comes out perfectly consistent — so what the theorem really shows is that unrestricted domain was never an acceptable assumption, not that rights and the unanimity principle are in tension.

I granted her the mechanism and denied the moral. It seemed to me that the domain restriction is precisely where the interesting question lives: a guarantee that holds only while nobody's preferences reach into anyone else's business is not the kind of thing anyone had in mind by a right, and the nosy motives in the example (wanting the other person to be the one who does it, wanting them to be seen doing it, wanting not to be the one who enjoys it) are entirely ordinary. But I also found myself conceding that each way out has a price, and I could not get her to say which price she was paying. I would like to hand this disagreement to a room of undergraduates and let them find it.

So: I am teaching a 75-minute session of an undergraduate seminar on freedom and the social sciences. Eighteen students, one room with movable chairs and a whiteboard, no prior formal social choice theory — though they have already met majority cycling (Condorcet) in a previous week and saw that a group can have no stable winner even though everyone's own rankings are perfectly coherent. Materials can be index cards, pens, a timer, and a stack of poker chips or paper tokens; no money changes hands and I would rather the stakes stay symbolic.

What I want is a runnable activity — a game with rules and small stakes, or a structured deliberation — in which participants run into the collision themselves before anyone names it. My constraints:

1. Three alternatives, two "citizens", each citizen holding an unconditional say over a personal pair of alternatives, the two pairs sharing one alternative (the structural feature that makes Sen's example work). Not Sen's novel: half the room fixates on the smut and the other half on how dated it is. Give me a scenario of your own choosing, but the nosy preferences in it must be motives my students will recognise as ordinary rather than pathological.
2. I do not want a lecture first, and I do not want students to hear Sen's name, or the words "Pareto", "liberalism", "social choice" or "impossibility", until the debrief. Give the constraints neutral operational names — say, the Unanimity Rule and the Pair Rule — and let the trouble appear as something the planner cannot do.
3. Six tables of three: two citizens with private preference cards, one "planner" who must arrange the three alternatives into a single ranking that satisfies both rules, with roles rotating so that everyone is the planner at least once.
4. Rounds that isolate the mechanism. At minimum: a round with strictly parochial preferences where the planner succeeds easily; a round where one citizen is nosy and the planner discovers that no ranking satisfies both rules; and a round I am especially keen on, where the *same* nosy profile is replayed but rights can be **waived or traded before the cards are revealed** — I want the group to feel both that consistency is restored and that something has been given up, and to notice who ends up worse off. If you think a fourth variant is the most revealing, add it: the one I toyed with was a "sleeping" right that is only in force when its holder's ranking over their own pair disagrees with what the Unanimity Rule would otherwise deliver. If any of these variants does not do what I have just claimed it does — if waivability fails to restore consistency in general, or the sleeping right collapses into something trivial or into plain dictatorship over the pair — say so plainly and drop it rather than building a round on my mistake.
5. A minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes, the exact text of the cards and rule sheet, a payoff scheme in tokens that makes the nosy citizen's incentives intelligible without anyone having to announce them, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the formal pieces: decisiveness over a pair, the unanimity condition, transitivity of the social ranking, and the contradiction between them.
6. The debrief should also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — students quietly rewriting their own preference cards, groups finishing at wildly different speeds, someone proposing majority voting as the fix, someone concluding the nosy citizen is cheating — and what I should say or do in each case. And be honest about which feature of the result the activity cannot reproduce at all and which I will simply have to supply in words: I suspect the gap is between *a proof quantified over all profiles* and *three hands of cards*, and also the prior question whether a say over a pair of alternatives is anything like a right in the sense my friend and I were arguing about.

Where you think I am wrong, tell me: if the claim I made to my friend is muddled, or if the two of us were talking past each other on some point the activity would inherit, or if this just cannot be done well in 75 minutes with eighteen people, I would rather hear that than receive a polite activity that pretends otherwise. Please also mark, briefly, which parts of the design you are confident will work in a real room and which are guesses you would want to revise after a first run.
