I teach a combined literature-and-philosophy seminar, and next week we're doing Tom Stoppard's *Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead*. I want to spend a session on the coin scene and I want to get the philosophy right rather than gesture at it, so I'm asking for a careful reading.

The setup: the play opens with the two of them flipping a coin that comes up heads over and over. Rosencrantz keeps calling heads and keeps winning. Guildenstern, who is paying, is increasingly unsettled: he gives the reasoning that each spin is independent of the last and that the run is therefore either a spectacular vindication of the laws of probability or a sign that those laws have been suspended on their behalf, and he floats some non-natural explanation before settling on nothing at all. Please read this scene, and the play's use of the coin afterwards, through the dispute about what probability *is*: frequentism, subjectivism (Bayesianism), and the propensity or single-case chance view. Paraphrase rather than quote; I have the text in front of me, and I'm not asking for sources or citations.

Some things I want handled explicitly:

1. What each interpretation would say Guildenstern is actually entitled to conclude. In particular: a Bayesian should insist that the specific run of heads is no less probable than any other specific run of the same length, so the surprise has to be located somewhere else — in the *pattern* rather than the sequence, in the failure of exchangeability, in the absence of any specified rival hypothesis with enough prior weight to absorb the evidence. Do you think the scene dramatizes something a Bayesian would endorse there, or does Guildenstern commit a fallacy that the play then quietly relies on?

2. The reference-class and finite-sequence problems for frequentism. The relevant class of events in this play is tiny, and the series is cut short in a rather brutal way: the deaths of the two characters mean the limiting relative frequency never arrives. Say whether you think Stoppard is doing something philosophically pointed with that, or whether the ending is just the ending.

3. Propensity. Propensities are supposed to attach to single cases in virtue of the actual physical setup. But these characters have no physical setup — they are in a script. So I want you to consider a view I'll provisionally call *diegetic chance*: the thought that the probability of an outcome within a fiction is fixed by what the fiction prescribes, not by frequencies, credences, or physical dispositions. Is diegetic chance a genuine fourth position, or does it collapse into one of the three — subjectivism read from the author's or audience's standpoint, say, or propensity read as attaching to a character's situation rather than to a coin? Argue it one way.

4. Where the play resists the reading. I'd rather be told honestly that the coin is a metaphor for dramatic necessity and for the characters' lack of agency inside someone else's plot than have a reading forced on it. Is there a point at which the probability talk is really about free will or about fictionality, and the three interpretations are beside the point?

Concretely, I also want: which two or three moments elsewhere in the play you'd put on the seminar handout alongside the coin scene, and one thing the play makes vivid about probability that the standard philosophical treatments of it don't.

Write this as a short critical essay in prose, with headings. End with a single sentence stating which of the interpretations the coin scene in fact dramatizes best, and then a final line in exactly this form, choosing one label:

\answer{frequentism}, \answer{subjectivism}, \answer{propensity}, \answer{diegetic chance}, or \answer{none of these}
