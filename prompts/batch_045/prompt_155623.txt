I'm a junior on my school's cross-country team, and the technical vocabulary I'm most at home in is the one I use in my training log: threshold pace, heart-rate zones, RPE, and the weekly load numbers my coach builds out of them. I've never studied economics, so I'd like you to restate the debate over interpersonal utility comparisons in that vocabulary.

Please translate the main positions one at a time: the claim that the only real evidence is what people actually choose, so comparing how well off different people are is unscientific; the claim that such comparisons are possible anyway, whether by treating money or willingness to pay as a common yardstick or by asking someone to rank outcomes without knowing which person she will turn out to be; and the further question of what a planner should maximize once comparisons are conceded, whether the sum, the worst-off individual, or something in between. Along the way I want you to mark where the line falls between saying which of two people is worse off and saying how much worse off. Use concrete terms from training as you go: each runner's threshold pace as her own scale, pace or heart rate as a common physical unit, RPE as a self-report that has to be pinned against known sessions before it means anything across different runners.

Then give me the residue report. For each position, say what carried over cleanly, what you had to bend to fit, and what cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all. I'm especially suspicious of the easy part of the analogy, the fact that pace and heart rate are genuine numbers comparable across runners, since in the economics case the contested quantity is supposed to be the thing that matters rather than merely one measurable output among others.

Finally, what would my framework say about a teammate who gets fitter on any amount of training and reports almost no suffering? If a coach maximizing total team fitness would pile the workload onto him, does my vocabulary have resources here that welfare economics lacks?
