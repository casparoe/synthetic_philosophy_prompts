I have no formal training in philosophy or economics, and for some time now I have been working through a problem on my own. It began when I tried to explain to a friend why two people can look at the same decision about who gets what and come away with different answers, even when neither of them is more egalitarian or more cautious than the other. The distinction I have arrived at, in my own terms, is this: being able to compare how much a given change helps one person rather than another (the size of a gain) looks to me like a different piece of information from being able to compare where two people start out relative to one another (who is worse off). My friend says the first gives you the second for free. I would like a case that shows her, without any technical machinery, that the second is a separate and additional input.

The case I want is a distribution problem. There is a fixed pool of something and two or three people among whom it must be divided, so that nobody's gain is free. Two choosers evaluate the options from behind a veil: each knows the options and the people, does not know which person she will turn out to be, and treats each person as equally likely. The first chooser ranks options by the expected value of her own outcome, which with equal chances comes to comparing averages. The second chooser ranks options by how well off the worst-off person is, and is indifferent to how anyone better off does. I have seen these loosely called the average rule and the maximin rule; I do not care about the labels or who invented them, and I do not want any references, reading suggestions, or citations, since I cannot check them.

Specifications for the case:

1. Realistic and ordinary: a clinic allocating hours with a specialist, a workplace dividing shifts, a family budgeting a fixed sum. No hypothetical currencies, no science fiction, no exotic devices. Quantities should be things an administrator could defend, such as hours, sessions, or points on a shared functional scale.
2. Two or three people and two options. If you need a third option or a third person, say why two could not carry the demonstration.
3. The only measurement available in the story is change: how much each person improves relative to her own starting point, on a scale on which one person's improvement can be weighed against another's. The comparison of starting points must be left open, and it must be left open for a concrete reason inside the story rather than by my stipulating that levels are in principle unmeasurable. Give me two admissible hypotheses about how the starting points relate, both consistent with every gain measurement in the story.
4. Under every admissible hypothesis, the average chooser picks the same option. Show the calculation, and explain why this is not luck: shifting a person's starting point adds the same constant to every option's average, so no reassignment of starting points can reorder them.
5. There must be two admissible hypotheses under which the maximin chooser picks different options. Show both calculations and name which person is worst off under each.
6. The divergence must not come from attitudes to risk. Both choosers face the same known probabilities, so tell me explicitly how you have kept risk aversion out of it and why the disagreement traces to the aggregation rule rather than to nerve. It must also not come from anyone having an outsized capacity to benefit: no person's numbers may be large enough that the case works by sheer magnitude. The flip should fall out of the shape of the distribution.
7. Leave slack. No ties anywhere, and every verdict should survive perturbing any single number by half a unit, so the case does not rest on knife-edge values.

After the scenario, give me the table of gains, the two hypotheses, the calculation for each chooser under each, and then three short notes: which single feature of the case is doing the work; what breaks if that feature is removed, for instance if you retune the numbers so that both options have equal totals or so that the worst-off person is the same person under both hypotheses, choosing whichever is most instructive and telling me what collapses; and how a defender of the maximin rule would attack the case. On that last point I especially want to know whether she can reasonably reply that needing the extra information is a virtue rather than a flaw, and what she would say about what the average rule's invariance is bought with.

The case is for settling an argument with my friend, so this is what counts as cheating: stipulating the verdicts instead of deriving them from the two rules, letting the disagreement rest on risk aversion or on one person's enormous numbers, and quietly answering the baseline question by assumption. If some part of the specification cannot be met jointly with the rest, tell me which part and why rather than relaxing it silently.
