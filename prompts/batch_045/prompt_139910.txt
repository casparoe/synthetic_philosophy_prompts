I'm a PhD student and I run a small cross-listed reading group, half philosophy of mind people and half NLP people, and we're closing the term on Dennett's intentional stance: are we really attributing beliefs to LLMs, or just finding it useful to talk that way? The room is genuinely split, and I want to end the last session with a poem instead of a summary. I'll be reading it out loud, so it has to work in the ear, not just on the page.

Could you write me a pantoum — you know the form: lines 2 and 4 of each quatrain come back as lines 1 and 3 of the next one, and the last line of the poem is it's first line again — about sixteen lines, in the first person, where the lines that keep returning are plain belief claims: I think this, I want that, I assumed you'd understand. The form should carry the argument: every time one of those lines comes back it has different neighbours, so it reads differently — as somebody reporting a mental state, then as a way of guessing what they'll do next, then as just the kind of thing a thing like that would emit. Nothing should get settled: when the opening line returns at the end, the reader should feel that the question of whether it was ever really an expression of belief is still open, but also that nothing was lost by not answering it.

One more constraint, since it's the bit we actually argued about: the poem should be about rationality, but not practical rationality — not getting what you want out in the world. What I mean is the weaker, talk-shaped kind: saying what fits, continuing a thought the way a reasonable speaker would. That's the version that's really doing the work when we read these systems as believers, and I'd rather the poem make that felt than state it. No jargon in the poem itself (no "weights", no "qualia", no "stance"), and both halves of the room should be able to find their own view in it without feeling parodied.
