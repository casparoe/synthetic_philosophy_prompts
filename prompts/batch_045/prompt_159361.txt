I'm a PhD student in philosophy finishing a chapter on how durable institutions — the trustees of a large grantmaking foundation, or the board of an AI lab drafting its deployment charter — ought to decide under moral uncertainty. The chapter's complaint is that the usual menu of options is drawn for a single agent facing a single indivisible decision: aggregate choiceworthiness across theories and maximize the expectation; defer to whichever theory you assign most credence; hedge against the variance so as to avoid the moral catastrophe; or split the action so that each theory gets a share of the resources, the time, or the randomization. None of these was designed for a body whose members hold different credence functions, whose decisions bind successors who will almost certainly hold different ones, and whose acts impose moral risk on third parties rather than on itself. As a companion to the chapter I want a diagnostic instrument I can hand out at a workshop of practitioners (trustees, program officers, policy staff — bright people with no philosophy training), and I'd prefer a decision tree over prose: something a participant can walk from a starting commitment to a named leaf in about eight steps, and that I can also score as a quiz afterward.

What I want from you is the tree itself, built from the logical space rather than from a list of names: start from the branching questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the actual positions in that space, and tell me which cells are unoccupied and why (incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored). Please include as leaves at least these, glossed in one line each, with no author attributions or citations — I don't want names attached to views, and where a view has no settled label please mint a descriptive one: expected-choiceworthiness maximization; deference to one's highest-credence theory; variance-averse hedging aimed at avoiding the worst moral outcome; maximin across theories; the fractional-compliance or share-of-the-action family (randomizing, splitting the portfolio, or time-slicing between theories' prescriptions); views on which a theory's categorical prohibition retains veto force above some credence threshold, so that a 15% credence that an act is forbidden outright can block it no matter how large the expected good; and views on which the admissible act must be one that every theory you give nontrivial credence could endorse from inside its own framework rather than one that merely scores best in the aggregate. I also want three cases of my own to be forced into the tree. First, the diachronic problem: should the aggregation be taken over the institution's current credences, or over a distribution over the credences its future trustees will hold — i.e. should a charter buy insurance against its own successors' moral views, or entrench the founders' credences against revision? Second, the risk-imposition asymmetry: whether a gamble that would be permissible if the moral risk fell on the decision-maker is still permissible when it falls entirely on outsiders. Third, the multi-trigger structure in which any one of several trustees can unilaterally launch the grant or the deployment, so that the probability that somebody is wrong is larger than any individual's probability of being wrong, and errors are asymmetric because one enthusiast suffices to proceed while stopping takes something close to unanimity; the question is whether that structure should raise the bar for each trustee independently of their own credences.

The deliverable: numbered branch nodes, each phrased as a single question a trustee could answer without knowing which leaf it leads to — no node may ask about a theory label, a school, or a philosopher, and each must be a concrete claim or case on which the positions genuinely come apart (a yes here and a no there should not be a matter of temperament); edge labels; and leaves carrying the name, the one-line gloss, and one sentence on the view's most awkward commitment. Please use these cases, or better ones if a case fails to discriminate: a $100M budget with credence 0.6 on funding a near-term health intervention, 0.3 on a long-run risk-reduction project, and 0.1 on the view that the money is tainted and must be returned rather than spent; the same choice re-described as an indivisible one-shot vote on a charter amendment; a proposal that 85% of one's credence says produces an enormous good and 15% says is categorically impermissible regardless of consequences; five trustees with divergent credences, any one of whom can sign off alone; and a clause that can be written either as entrenched or as revisable by simple majority. Finally, flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate rather than inventing a question to pry them apart, and add a short scoring note saying which answer patterns collapse onto the same leaf and how I should score a participant who gives inconsistent answers.
