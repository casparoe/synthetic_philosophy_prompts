I'm in a small reading group (half philosophy of social science, half a couple of people from psych) and we've been going in circles for weeks about the thing people call the gender-equality paradox. One of my friends, Tobi, finally wrote his position down as an actual argument, and I can't find the broken step — but I also can't accept the conclusion, which worries me. Here it is, slightly cleaned up:

(1) In countries that score high on the standard gender-equality indices and high on GDP, measured average differences between women and men — personality traits, personal values, and educational and occupational choice — are larger, not smaller, than in poorer, less gender-equal countries. I'm willing to grant this even though everyone keeps fighting about the disputed STEM-degrees version of it; take the broader personality-and-preferences pattern instead, and take it as roughly right.
(2) The best explanation for a difference that gets bigger as external constraints get lighter is that the difference was never being produced by those constraints. Remove the barriers and what's left is what was underneath them: freer conditions reveal underlying dispositions rather than manufacturing them.
(3) If a difference in outcomes between groups is produced by the groups' own underlying dispositions rather than by constraints, then it isn't an injustice in the sense that matters — there's no coercion, no one to blame, and nothing for institutions to correct.
(4) If there's no injustice, then policies aimed at equalising occupational outcomes across the sexes aren't remedies, they're impositions: they override real preferences in order to produce a pattern someone likes, and the people overridden are the ones who pay.
(5) So people who treat the gendered pattern of career choice in rich liberal democracies as a datum crying out for institutional correction have the burden of proof backwards. The affluent egalitarian society is the closest thing we have to a controlled experiment, and it says the pattern isn't primarily socially imposed.

Three things make me think this proves too much, or at least that something slipped. First, run (2) inside a single rich country across time: if the numbers I remember are right, the share of computing degrees going to women in the US roughly halved between the mid-1980s and the 2010s while equality only ever increased and the gene pool obviously didn't change — so either the "natural level" moves by tens of points in one generation, which means it isn't a natural level, or every arrangement counts as the free one while it lasts, which makes (2) unfalsifiable. Second, (2) seems to hand out "natural" verdicts for opposite patterns: in poorer countries where women take engineering degrees at comparatively high rates you could equally say that scarcity reveals real priorities, so the rule can't be what's doing the work. Third, take parental leave: in the Nordic countries leave is generously available to both parents, and the take-up split is still sharply gendered — which by (2) and (3) should settle the matter as natural, except that when countries made a chunk of leave non-transferable and use-it-or-lose-it, the split moved a lot. That looks like a case where "freedom" was only varied along one axis and the residual gap was being held in place by the design of the option, not by a disposition.

My own attempt to locate the problem is this: the equality indices measure equality of rights and material conditions — income, schooling, legal standing, life expectancy — and not equality of the conditions under which aspirations get formed. So imagine a society (call it Auroria) where every occupation pays the same, childcare and eldercare are fully socialised, prestige is flat across fields, and every adolescent gets the same structured six-month exposure to the whole range of trades and professions. My guess is that in Auroria the gap shrinks rather than grows, and if so then (2) is quietly trading on an equivocation between "constraints removed" and "conditions equalised": it strips out the coercive and material causes and leaves a whole class of socialisation and identity causes untouched, then calls what remains biological. Tobi says this is a dodge, because I can always postulate a further Auroria and so my own view can never lose.

So: which step do you actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the argument is valid and the work is all in a premise, say which premise I should swallow instead. Concretely, I'd like to keep (1) as roughly true, and I really don't want to end up saying that women's own stated preferences count for nothing — some people in the group go that way and it comes out patronising — but I also don't want a position where no cross-national finding could ever count as evidence about the causes of a group difference, since that's the exact unfalsifiability I'm accusing Tobi of. Is my Auroria move the real answer or is it a way of changing the subject? Please don't give me reading suggestions or papers to look up, I can't access anything and I've read more than enough op-eds on this already; I just want the diagnosis, including what each of the alternatives would commit me to if you think I have to pick one.
