I'm retired — was a chemist, spent a career doing kinetics — and I've drifted into running a small philosophy reading group at the library. Usually 9 or 10 of us, mostly retired people plus two or three undergrads who wander in, 90 minutes, and we've been slowly working through probability, evidence, that sort of thing. Most are happy with numbers, but two of them go quiet as soon as anything symbolic shows up, so it needs to stay playable without algebra.

Could you design one session for us? Here's what I want them to feel in their bones rather than be told: the difference between being unsure about a coin that's already been flipped and is sitting under a cup, and being unsure about whether some large number is prime. In the second case nothing is random anywhere — it's already settled by the axioms, I just haven't done the computing. And I gather this is awkward for the standard Bayesian picture, where an ideally rational agent already knows everything that follows from what it knows, so it shouldn't have any uncertainty about logic at all.

So: some betting game or market with play money on claims that get settled at the end of the evening. Mix them up — a couple settled by a hidden coin or object, a couple settled by a computation you run on a laptop or that I precompute earlier (is this 200-digit number prime, is the thousandth digit of pi a 7, whatever works). No lecture first, and I shouldn't say the phrase logical uncertainty until afterwards.

The part I care most about, and where I'd like you to be inventive, is the last twenty minutes. My worry: the usual demand is that your prices be probabilities — they add up, no Dutch book. But if my uncertainty about logic comes from being finite, then demanding that my prices be probabilities *right now*, including price 1 on every tautology, is demanding something I literally cannot do. So what replaces it? My amateur guess, which I'd like the session to either push the group toward or expose as wrong: the right standard isn't coherence at every moment, it's something like — you can't be reliably milked by an adversary who's allowed to think longer and run code than you are, and in the limit you converge to the truth. Can you build a round where those two standards visibly come apart, where prices are Dutch-bookable and yet fine in the limit sense, or the reverse? An adversary role who can run code and hunt for free money might do it, but you'll know better than me.

I also thought it'd be good to slip in one claim that no amount of computing settles — something true but unprovable in ordinary arithmetic, or just permanently out of reach — and let the group notice for themselves that the market has no idea what to do with it. Good idea or will it just muddy the water?

Give me the claims and how each is settled, the rules and payoff scheme, a timeline with roughly what I say when, a debrief mapping what happened onto the ideas, what'll go wrong the first time I run it, and be straight with me about which bit of the idea the game can't produce and I'll just have to explain.
