I build distributed systems for a living and have been reading about the socialist calculation debate in my spare time, with no formal training in philosophy or economics, so I'd be grateful if you translated the jargon into terms I can reason about.

I'd like you to compare two lines of argument rather than summarize a debate.

The first is Mises's claim that a socialist commonwealth cannot perform economic calculation, because without private ownership of the means of production there are no market prices for factors of production, and without such prices there is no way to compare alternative production plans on a common scale — so a planner can check that a plan is physically feasible but cannot check that it is the better of two feasible plans.

The second is Lange's reply: a central planning board can announce shadow prices, instruct managers to minimize cost and equate marginal cost with price, watch for surpluses and shortages, adjust the prices, and iterate. The market is thereby simulated rather than abolished, and the outcome can in principle match a competitive equilibrium.

Here is where I want you to go beyond the textbook version. I find it useful to separate two jobs that the planner's simulation might be expected to do:

(A) static allocation — given a known list of techniques, resources and consumer preferences, converge on the plan that satisfies the board's rules;
(B) discovery and adaptation — finding out which techniques, products or uses of a resource are worth trying in the first place, including products nobody has thought to produce and resources nobody has yet thought to value.

My own worry, which I'll name "closed-loop blindness" so we can refer to it, is this: in job (A) the simulator has an error signal (a surplus or a shortage) that is generated inside the model, so it can only tell you that you have failed to satisfy the rules you already encoded; it can never tell you that the rules, the objective function or the list of candidate products was wrong. In a real market, by contrast, someone who thinks a resource is undervalued can act on that belief, commit their own property to it, and be proved right or wrong by a signal that the planners did not define. My question is whether that asymmetry is a genuine defect of the simulation reply, or whether Lange's iterative procedure already has resources to handle it.

So, concretely, I'd like you to:

1. Say which of the two arguments does more damage to the other once (A) and (B) are separated: does Mises's objection only bite on (B) while Lange's procedure genuinely answers (A), or does one of the two collapse in a way I'm not seeing?
2. Tell me whether "closed-loop blindness" is really Mises's argument in disguise, really Hayek's later argument about the dispersal of knowledge of particular circumstances of time and place, or an objection that belongs to neither of them.
3. Say whether Hayek's knowledge argument is best understood as an extension of Mises's calculation argument or as a distinct argument that would stand even if the calculation problem were solved.

If any of your claims depend on a specific interpretation of Mises, Lange or Hayek, say explicitly that it is contested rather than stating it as settled.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the single verdict on points 1 through 3 that the rest of the answer defends — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and without restating my question back to me.
