I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I've been arguing with a colleague for months about the epistemic status of the Church–Turing thesis. My colleague holds that the physical version of the thesis — no real device computes anything a Turing machine cannot — is a contingent claim about the world, corroborated by a century of failed attempts and in principle refutable by a machine. I hold that the real content is conceptual: the standard formalisms are provably equivalent to each other, the thesis identifies our pre-formal notion of effective calculability with one of them, and putative counterexamples either have no well-defined output or quietly change the subject. Please draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between these two camps (call them the Empirical camp and the Conceptual camp), written for a technically literate reader with no philosophy background.

Deliverable, in four parts:

1. A numbered list of claims both camps can sign. It should separate (a) the equivalence of the standard formalisms, (b) the claim about our pre-formal notion of effective calculability, and (c) the claim about what physical systems can do — with agreement about which of these is provable, which is contested, and what each side is willing to count as the output or readout of a physical process.

2. A disagreements table: for each live issue, the wording the Empirical camp would accept for its own position, the wording the Conceptual camp would accept for its own, and a verdict on whether that issue is substantive or merely verbal.

3. The cruxes, each with a case or argument both camps accept as genuinely bearing on it, and each camp's prediction recorded in advance — including what each camp would count as losing. At least three:

(i) The Halving Clock. A fabricated chip runs an exhaustive search for a halting computation of a given program and flips a bit to 1 if it finds one; its clock period halves at every step, so infinitely many steps complete within one second (stipulate that power, heat, and precision limits are engineered away), and the designers stipulate that the bit's value at t = 1 second is the limit of its earlier values. Suppose such chips were built and, on cases we can check independently, reliably read 1 for halting programs and 0 for non-halting ones. Is a non-recursive function thereby computed, or is the stipulation about the limit state doing the work? What does each camp predict, and what would each concede?

(ii) The Burden Question. Is the historical record of failed attempts more like the record behind no-perpetual-motion — corroboration of a contingent principle — or more like the record behind attempts to prove the parallel postulate, where the question dissolves once the notion is sharpened? Each camp should say which analogy is closer and what it predicts about how this dispute will look in a century.

(iii) The Asymmetry. Nobody expects a mathematical discovery to refute the thesis, but some expect a physical one to. Does that asymmetry show that (b) and (c) are independent claims, or that (c) is the one doing the real work?

4. A short closing paragraph: the answer both camps could jointly give a working programmer who asks whether some new analog, quantum, or learned system could break past the Turing limit.

Constraints: plain English and pseudocode, no notation a working programmer would not follow, and define any term of art you use. No citations, references, or reading lists — argue from the structure of the problem. Where part of the dispute is merely verbal, say which part and why; where a crux is untestable even in principle, say so rather than manufacturing a test.
