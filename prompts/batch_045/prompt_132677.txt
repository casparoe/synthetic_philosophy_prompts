someone in my ethics reading group keeps killing the separateness of persons objection with one analogy: choosing for a group is exactly like choosing for yourself over time. If I can definately take ten days of post-op misery in exchange for twenty extra good years, then trading ten units of pain here for twenty units of good there is legitimate — and if that's fine inside one life, he says, all that changes when the pain and the good land on different people is who happens to be standing where, which is supposed to be morally irrelevant anyway. So utilitarianism is just prudence with a wider you, and the old Rawls-style complaint that it treats humanity as one big person is really a complaint that the parts of a life are separate too. He's even got a formal version: count everything in person-years, treat a life as a bundle of consecutive person-years and a population as a bundle of simultaneous ones, and claim the two aggregations are literally the same operation, so anything that licenses one and not the other has to point at the difference between consecutive and simultaneous bundling. He also waves fission-style cases at anyone who says the difference is that one experiencer undergoes both ends of the intrapersonal trade.

Can you audit this? I want the mapping — what plays the person, the time-slice, the boundary of death, the single undergoer, the plan of life — and then where it breaks, and which of those breaks actually matter for the work he wants it to do. Is it psychological continuity doing the work, or the fact that the same someone gets compensated, or the direction of time, or just that his person-year currency quietly settles the question by chopping lives into pieces before he starts? Does the objection survive in any form once you grant the analogy, or does it turn into a different claim — about compensation, or about who has to bear the cost, rather than about how many consciousnesses there are? And if the analogy is bad, is there a better one?
