Next month I'm giving the after-dinner speech at the twenty-fifth reunion of the graduate reading group I belonged to at the end of the nineties — the one that met monthly, said "steelman" without irony, and devoted most of its energies to taking other people's thought experiments apart. Roughly a third of the room went on to do that for a living; the rest are spouses, siblings, and former members who now do entirely unphilosophical work, and who mostly remember the group as the place where eleven people once spent two hours on whether a violinist counts as a person.

The dinner is in memory of our convener, Alan Rees, who died in March. Rees built intuition pumps rather than dismantling them, and his most-cited one — the Locked Archive case, about an archivist who may destroy a sealed collection she has sworn never to open — is the reason several of us ended up in philosophy. Our group's late jargon distinguished pumps that *earn* their verdict, where the verdict tracks one stated variable and survives swapping out every incidental feature of the scenario, from pumps that *borrow* it, where the verdict rides on framing or on a background assumption so local to the room the case was built in that it never travels. I'm now fairly convinced Rees's was the second kind, and that it worked on us precisely because we shared an assumption none of us would ever have agreed to state.

Please write the seven-minute speech. It has to honor him without either re-running the Locked Archive as though it still struck me as probative — the philosophers would hear the strain in it, and it would be false — or explaining to a dinner room why the case misleads, which lands as a posthumous debunking and loses the other two-thirds. And it must not leave the non-philosophers with the impression that their bafflement was the flaw in the case.
