Hi — hoping you can help with something that's half practical, half philosophical. I do intake at a small outfit that brokers what people in this space call moral trades: two funders who disagree about what's actually worth doing each spend money on the other's cause, on the theory that both end up with more of what they care about than if they'd just spent on themselves. Our broker (call her Marta) decides what we'll put our name to, and I've now watched her say yes and no often enough that I can't work out what rule she's running. Her track record:

1. Straight swap between a global-health funder and an animal-welfare funder, $5M each way: yes, no hesitation.
2. Tiny foundation whose entire value was that a grove of one particular tree species be planted and tended for fifty years; they put $9M into a deworming program in exchange: yes. She has never once required that a demand make sense to anyone else.
3. Deal where one side's contribution required its staff to mislead their own donors about where the money actually went: no.
4. Deal where one side's contribution meant breaking a signed agreement with a partner org: no.
5. A proposed trade with a group inside another country's sealed-off program that we could never contact and would never hear from, justified on the grounds that they're presumably running the same reasoning and will do their part anyway: no.
6. A deal with a counterparty that had reneged on a previous trade: no — but she brokered basically the same deal a year later once they posted a bond with a third party.
7. A deal where the small side was six months from losing its charter and had no other counterparty, and the big side's terms handed it maybe 5% of the gains: no.
8. A secular group that wanted a religious charity to publish a statement saying the charity's own mission was less important than theirs: no.

Now the one on my desk. Ardent is a large poverty-focused foundation. Clause is small, and its only value is that a particular nineteenth-century legal archive be preserved — the physical paper, in the original building — and that Ardent publicly state, on it's own letterhead, that it regards preserving the archive as being as important as its core work. Clause puts $40M of its money into Ardent's programs; Ardent spends $3M on the archive and publishes one statement drafted by Clause.

The wrinkles as I see them. Ardent's board is happy to publish, but their own staff don't think the sentence is really true — they'd call it a harmless overstatement rather than a lie, and nothing is being concealed from donors about where money goes. Ardent's donors gave for poverty and were never asked; an internal poll suggests most of them wouldn't mind, but only most. Clause has about eighteen months of runway before it has to deploy or dissolve, has no other counterparty, and would get, in money terms, a small fraction of what it's handing over — though it would also get literally everything it cares about. And Clause has turned down a digitisation option that would secure the same documents for a tenth of the price, on the grounds that it's the paper it cares about, not the contents.

What I want from you: will Marta broker it? More usefully — give me the two or three rules that fit all eight of her verdicts about equally well, say what each of them predicts here, tell me which one you think she's actually running and why, and name the single variation on this deal that would tell them apart. (Same money, statement dropped? Same deal, donors actually asked and 90% say fine? Same deal, Clause has three years of runway and two other counterparties?) If you think her precedents genuinely don't reach this far, say so and tell me what principle she'd be committing herself to with each choice. I care less about the yes/no than about which of those rules I should assume next time.
