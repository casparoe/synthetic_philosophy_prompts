yo, I need a dialogue for a reading group thing I'm running, and I want it to be an actual argument — not two monologues that politely miss each other.

Setup: both characters are thoroughgoing patternists about personal identity. Neither one gets to say the self was an illusion all along, or that you need an immaterial soul, or that identity has to be all-or-nothing because identity just is all-or-nothing. They agree a person is a pattern of a certain sort realized in a physical system. What they disagree about is whether such a pattern can be partially realized in more than one brain at the same time, and whether partially realized means the same self existing to a degree, or just something that resembles that self.

Character A, call her Marion, is developing Hofstadter's line about a dead person's I living on inside the people who knew them. She lost her partner a couple years ago and thinks something she insists isn't merely metaphorical: a piece of him is still running in her head, and how alive that piece is depends on how much it steers what she actually does day to day — more alive when she hears his voice in some decision, less alive when she's just got a few stale facts about him stored away.

Character B, call him Dev, holds that what's in Marion's head is a causal descendant of her partner rather than a part of him: a recording that acts, not a subject that persists. He has to be strong, not a guy repeating the word identical. Give him the best version.

Some constraints on the fight:
- Both of them should be good enough that they reformulate under pressure, and each should concede at least one real thing — a case their own framing handles badly.
- Work in at least these three pressure points, where I don't think the answer is obvious:

1. The arithmetic problem. If the pattern is spread over five people — a lot here, a little there — does that add up to one self with a total magnitude, or to five separate partial things? If it adds, then a half-remembered celebrity who lives on faintly in ten million people ends up more present in the world than a grandmother living vividly in one grieving daughter, which feels like the wrong result. If it doesn't add, then Marion's partner is now five or fifty non-communicating fragments, and she owes some story about what that is like from the inside, or about why there's nothing it's like.
2. The drift problem. Marion's model of him is quietly being edited — confabulated details, smoothed-over memories, and she knows it. So is the distributed I fading, or mutating into a person who never existed? Then Dev gets the follow-up: if Marion writes a memoir that hands a thousand readers a better-preserved copy than the one she carries, does he come back stronger after she dies? Marion should get to answer, including where she draws the carrier line — books, recordings, institutions, habits passed on to kids — and why brains matter specifically.
3. The inertness problem. Dev's best objection is that the pattern in Marion's head takes in no new input and acts on the world only by being her, so it isn't a subject at all. Marion gets the obvious counter: someone in dreamless sleep or a reversible coma is also inert right now and we still count them as persisting. Dev then has to find a principled difference between temporarily decoupled but still hooked up and permanently decoupled, and under questioning should admit that this difference is doing a lot of work in his view and might itself be a matter of degree — which is awkward for him.

Near the end, have them hit the recombination case: two of the survivors talk to each other for years and their models merge into something matching neither original, and neither of them can say whose I that is. Don't resolve the thing. Close on a concrete disagreement about what would even count as evidence here, or on one of them admitting they no longer know what question they're asking.

Start straight in on the dialogue — no preface, no note explaining who believes what, no summary afterwards. Somewhere around 900 to 1200 words, and make them sound like people talking rather than encyclopedia entries with names stuck on. Kick off mid-argument if you like, just no preamble before the first line.
