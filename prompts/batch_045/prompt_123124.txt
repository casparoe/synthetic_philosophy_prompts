I write the periodization and label copy for the antiquity galleries at a museum of world history, and our house style has a habit I can neither justify nor get rid of. Every civilization on the timeline gets a single year of "fall": Rome gets one date in the late fifth century, although the Roman state, its law and its institutions carried on in the east for nearly another millennium; the lowland Maya get a "collapse" at the end of the Terminal Classic, although millions of Maya speakers and a great deal of Maya social structure are still there today; the Bronze Age palace systems get a "collapse," and then the next case in the same gallery, which looks fairly similar demographically, is described as "transformation" or "continuity." Easter Island is presented as a fable about a society that ruined itself, which appears to be a different genre again.

What I need is not the history but the theory this practice carries: what must be presupposed for it to make sense to assign a society a termination date at all, and to call some endings failures and other endings mere changes? Please reconstruct that theory, and answer in exactly five numbered items of one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion:

1. What has to be true of a society, and of what its persistence through time consists in, for dating its end to make sense at all — and does the practice take "collapse" to name the loss of an occupancy floor (population, settled area, number of occupied sites) or the loss of a repertoire of capacities (writing, taxation, monumental building, long-distance exchange)? Call these the occupancy reading and the repertoire reading, and tell me which one fall dates actually track.

2. Which of the two does the practice strictly presuppose, as opposed to merely fitting comfortably with — and is there any coherent way to keep assigning fall dates while holding that nothing really ended?

3. Is the inconsistency in the house style real, or can one and the same theory license collapse language here and continuity language there? If it can, name the variable that is quietly doing the work: tempo, scale, whether the successor population is one we happen to have a separate name for, whether we approve of the outcome, or something else.

4. Test the reconstructed theory on a case I will stipulate. The Kesh polity loses its script, its palaces, its standing army, and roughly four fifths of its population within three generations; five centuries later, the people in the same valley farm the same terraces, speak a language descended from Kesh, and worship recognizably the same deities under new names. On your theory, is that a collapse, a transformation, or neither — and if the theory returns no determinate verdict, say plainly that it does not.

5. If we abandoned the failure framing, what would concretely have to replace the fall date on a wall label, what would we lose by the change, and is there anything a visitor could then no longer be told?
