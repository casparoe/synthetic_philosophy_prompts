Imagine David Hume being shown a monetary order of a kind he never encountered: the total quantity of the unit of account is fixed by unalterable code that no sovereign can revise; nearly all exchange is executed by autonomous software agents that reprice continuously; and states can finance themselves only by borrowing in that unit.

I would like a reconstruction of where he would land, drawn from the machinery of his essays on money, commerce, the balance of trade, and public credit rather than from his reputation as a critic of mercantilism. Three elements of that machinery seem to pull apart under these conditions, and I want each addressed: his claim that money is not an object of commerce but merely an agreed instrument for facilitating exchange, together with the price-specie flow story, which looks vindicated in its purest form once repricing is instantaneous; his one allowance of real effects to money, the interval in which a growing stock of money animates industry before prices have caught up, which continuous repricing seems to squeeze to nothing; and his expectation that perpetual public borrowing ends badly, made sharper when a state cannot inflate away what it owes. Would he greet a hard cap as the self-correcting mechanism finally completed, or as recreating the kind of monetary scarcity he associated with stagnant ages?

Throughout, keep clearly apart what Hume demonstrably committed himself to in the essays, what a defensible extension of those commitments yields once you state which ones you are weighting, and what is your own economics attributed to him; mark which links in the chain are load-bearing and where the extrapolation becomes underdetermined. Flag where Hume scholarship is genuinely divided — how much short-run non-neutrality he really allowed, how literal his warnings about public credit were meant to be, what the price-specie argument was supposed to establish — and do not smooth those disputes into a confident reconstruction. Close with a one-sentence verdict, with a confidence level, on whether the arrangement would on balance have cheered or alarmed him.
