I teach a third-year seminar on social epistemology and one of my students — sharp, annoying in the productive way — came to office hours with an argument I can't find the seam in. I've reconstructed it below roughly as he gave it. He calls it the mirror-image gambit and says that if it works it sinks the whole "conspiracy theories are prima facie unwarranted" literature, generalist and particularist alike.

(1) Call a theory *sealed* if, for every piece of evidence E you could in principle acquire, the theory's adherents can redescribe E so that it confirms the theory rather than disconfirming it. A sealed theory is unfalsifiable, so no body of evidence could ever warrant belief in it: warrant requires being appropriately sensitive to how things actually are.

(2) Conspiracy theories are sealed, or seal themselves as they grow. No leaked memos? The leakers were silenced, or the memos are forgeries planted by the conspirators. An official inquiry clears everyone? The inquiry was captured. Nobody in the press will touch it? The press is in on it. There is no evidence you could hand a committed conspiracy theorist that could not be absorbed.

(3) So belief in a conspiracy theory is unwarranted — and not merely because most conspiracy theories happen to be false, but because of the structure of the theory itself. This is the bit I want to keep, along with the errant-data version of it (the one where the black mark is that the theory leaves so much of the evidence unexplained).

(4) Now take an ordinary, sensible inquirer — call her Auditor — who accepts the official account of some episode: no conspiracy, nothing to see here. What does she cite? The inquiry that found nothing. The absence of credible whistleblowers. The fact that in fifteen years nobody has produced a document.

(5) But a *successful* conspiracy is precisely a state of affairs in which the inquiry finds nothing, the whistleblowers don't come forward, and no document ever surfaces. So the evidence Auditor is resting on is insensitive: it looks the same whether the conspiracy happened or not. Call a position *sated* when it rests on a body of evidence that does not discriminate between a theory and its denial.

(6) Being sated is the mirror image of being sealed. In both cases the believer's confidence is unhooked from whether the conspiracy happened, and the direction of the redescription is an accident. So the defect identified in (2) is equally a defect in Auditor's acceptance of the official account.

(7) Generalize. For any conspiracy claim C, either the case for C is defective (sealed) or the case against C is defective (sated) — and in the interesting cases, both. So nobody can ever warrantedly believe that a conspiracy occurred, and nobody can ever warrantedly believe that one didn't.

(8) But plainly we can. Watergate. Tuskegee. The tobacco firms sitting on their own lung-cancer data for decades. The surveillance programs that only became undeniable the day somebody leaked them. And (7) also implies that in those very cases nobody was warranted in accepting the official story beforehand, which is absurd — some people were, and some people were right to be suspicious.

(9) So either the argument in (1)–(3) is no good, or it is good and it takes the official account down with it. Either way there is no interesting asymmetry between "conspiracy theory" and "official story," and the research programme is a sleight of hand that makes a political preference look like an epistemic one.

I keep wanting to say: sealed and sated aren't the same property and (6) is where he swaps them. Sealed looks modal — no possible evidence could count against you — whereas sated is a fact about the evidence you happen to have, and it's curable by getting more. Auditor can say "find me a memo and I'll change my mind," and the sealed theorist can't say anything parallel. But he has a reply that I haven't answered: if the conspiracy is real and competently run, there *are* no memos to be found, so Auditor's readiness to be moved by a memo is as empty as the theorist's readiness to be moved by anything. At that point I can't tell whether I've located a flaw or just restated the standoff in different vocabulary.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I have to choose I'd rather keep (3) — that the structure of a conspiracy theory can itself be an epistemic mark against it, over and above the low base rate — and give up the symmetry in (7). But I want to know what that costs me, and in particular whether keeping (3) commits me to something ugly about (8): the real, documented conspiracies arguably looked exactly as sealed as the crank ones right up until the day they didn't, and I'd like to know whether my student is entitled to that point or whether he's smuggling something in with it.
