QUESTION 3 (30 marks)

Thomas Nagel and Bernard Williams both published classic papers called Moral Luck, Nagel's written as a reply to Williams's. Answer all parts.

(a) [4] State, in your own words, the principle about moral assessment that generates the problem of moral luck, and say briefly why it looks like a paradox that can't be solved rather than a difficulty that's merely hard to deal with.

(b) [8] Nagel distinguishes four varieties of luck: constitutive, circumstantial, causal, and resultant. For each, give an example of your own (not one from either paper) and one sentence on why it causes trouble for the principle in (a).

(c) [6] Williams introduces the notion of agent-regret, in connection with a lorry driver who kills a child through no fault of his own. What is agent-regret, how is it supposed to differ from remorse or guilt, and does it solve the problem in (a)? Say why or why not.

(d) [7] In the Gauguin case, Williams suggests that the only thing that could justify Gauguin is his success, and that this justification is essentially retrospective. Explain what the retrospectiveness consists in and why Williams thinks it matters. Then say whether this is best read as a solution to Nagel's problem or as a different kind of claim altogether.

(e) [5] A classmate objects: resultant luck isn't real moral luck. When we blame the drunk driver who kills a child more than the drunk driver who gets home safely, we aren't responding to the outcome; we're treating the outcome as evidence of how badly he was driving. The outcome is epistemically relevant, not morally relevant. Assess this reply.

STUDENT ANSWER

(a) The principle — call it the control condition — is that a person can be morally assessed, blamed or praised, only for what is within their control: what they will and do, not what merely happens to them. Nagel's problem is that this condition is very hard to give up but also very hard to live with, because nearly everything we actually assess people for depends on things they didn't control: the kind of person they are, the situations they end up in, and above all how their actions turn out. If the condition is right then almost nothing is left to assess — the area of genuine agency shrinks to almost nothing — and if you follow it all the way down you end up not being able to blame anyone for anything, since any action is the product of a causal chain you didn't author.

What makes it a paradox rather than just a difficulty is that we can't resolve it by dropping one side. We can't stop assessing people: moral assessment is part of what holds a community together, and we need it to get people to behave. And we can't give up the condition either, because once you notice that someone didn't control something, it stops making sense to blame them for it. So I think the upshot for Nagel is that there is no single standpoint from which these judgments get made. From inside deliberation you have to treat what you do as up to you; from outside, your action is just one more event in the world; and there is no way to combine the two into one view. So there isn't one fact of the matter about how blameworthy someone is — there are these two standpoints and they don't add up.

(b) Constitutive luck: luck in the kind of problems and situations you're faced with — the tests life hands you. Example: someone who happens to leave Germany in 1929 and so is never faced with the choice of whether to go along. This is a problem for the control condition because whether you get tested at all isn't up to you, and yet who we take you to be depends on how you fare in the test.

Circumstantial luck: luck in the kind of person you are — your temperament, your impulses, the dispositions you were born with and brought up into. Example: two parents, one of whom is quick-tempered and impatient with her children through no doing of her own, the other not. We blame the first for being a bad parent, but the difference between them is in what they're made of, not in what they chose.

Causal luck: luck in how your action is produced by antecedent circumstances — whether the causal chain leading up to what you did is one you had any say in. This is basically the free will problem in a new costume. Example: if determinism is true, then whether you return the wallet you found is fixed by your genes and upbringing, and you had no more control over those than over the weather. (I've heard it said that causal luck isn't really a separate category and collapses back into the other kinds. I'm not sure about that, but it does seem to at least overlap heavily with them.)

Resultant luck: luck in how your actions and projects turn out. Example: two people leave work after three drinks and drive the same route at the same speed; one meets a child who runs into the road and kills her, the other gets home and nothing happens. The difference between them isn't anything either of them controlled, and yet we blame the first enormously more — the law does too, vehicular homicide against a ticket — and so does he. This is the most direct problem for the control condition, because the thing doing the work in our assessment is precisely the thing that was out of his control.

(c) Agent-regret is the regret you feel for something you did, as opposed to the regret a bystander feels about the same event. The lorry driver who kills a child through no fault of his own can't feel remorse: he did nothing wrong, there's nothing he should have done differently, nothing to wish undone. But he doesn't feel what a passerby feels either. He feels it as the person whose action it was — he feels that he did it, and he'll go on feeling it. So it's first-personal in a way spectator regret isn't, while not being guilt or remorse, since it carries neither the thought that one acted wrongly nor the wish that one hadn't. That's also why the outcome matters to him even though it wasn't his fault: what he regrets is that this came of what he did.

Does it solve Nagel's problem? No, and I don't think it's meant to. It's a description of a sentiment, and it's Williams's way of showing that our ethical experience is larger than morality in the narrow sense of obligation and blame. The driver's feeling isn't a verdict on him, so pointing to it doesn't reconcile the control condition with the fact that we respond to outcomes. It does show why we shouldn't expect the kind of solution Nagel wants: the outcome isn't just an input to a blame calculation, it's something the agent has to live with.

(d) The point about retrospectiveness is that the only thing that could justify Gauguin's leaving is that he turns out to be the painter he hoped to be, and he can't know that in advance. So the warrant for the action only exists after the fact, and it depends on how things turned out. At the time of deciding, it will be worth it if I succeed is available to him as a reason to go, and that's what makes his choice rational; we, looking back, say either that he was justified or that he wasn't.

And if he does succeed, the success vindicates him: the moral objection to his abandoning his family is, in the end, outweighed by what he makes of his life. So Williams's view here is that in cases like this the justification really is a matter of outcomes — which is Nagel's problem restated as its own solution. Moral luck is real, and it reaches all the way down into justification, not just into blame. So yes, I read this as Williams's answer to Nagel, and as a broadly consequentialist one.

(e) The objection gets something right: sometimes outcomes are only evidence. If the difference between the two drivers is that one was weaving all over the road, then the death tells us something we didn't know about his driving, and we're blaming him for the driving. But it can't be the whole story. First, Williams's lorry driver: no fault, nothing to learn — he was driving carefully, the child ran out — and yet we don't think his relation to what happened is just that of a bystander. If outcome were merely evidence, there'd be nothing left over in that case, and there clearly is. Second, two assassins, equally determined and equally skilled, one's gun jams: we treat the successful one as having done something worse, and not because we infer a worse will, since we know he didn't have one. Third, the objection assumes the only thing an outcome can do is carry information about the agent's prior state. Another possibility is that what we assess is the action as it fares in the world, so that outcome enters assessment directly rather than evidentially. Nagel's point, I take it, is that you can't expel the outcome from assessment without ceasing to assess action at all, since action is essentially something that happens in the world.

One weak spot: if the two drivers really are identical in every respect that's up to them, the objector can say we're simply being inconsistent — and that's Nagel's own view too. He doesn't think our outcome-sensitivity can be justified; he thinks we're stuck with it. So maybe the thing to say is that the objector is right that we can't justify it, and wrong that this lets us explain it away as evidence.
