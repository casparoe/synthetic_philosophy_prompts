For roughly fifteen years I have read LessWrong, Overcoming Bias, and Slate Star Codex / Astral Codex Ten, and my working epistemology is Bayesian by default: beliefs as distributions, updating on likelihood ratios, calibration and proper scoring rules, forecasting tournaments, prediction markets, base rates, the optimizer's curse, and the standing injunction to steelman before engaging. I think in this vocabulary more fluently than in any other, and I have noticed an unfortunate consequence: when I read the methodology literature around the replication crisis, I come away thinking the frequentists must simply be confused, because nothing they seem to be protecting looks like a thing my framework has room for. I would like to fix that, and I am writing the result up for a small reading group of people with the same background.

So: please restate, in my vocabulary and as faithfully as you can, the strongest version of the frequentist complaint that motivates the replication-crisis literature. The target material is the cluster of worries about researcher degrees of freedom, undisclosed analytic choices, optional stopping and peeking, multiple comparisons, and the associated demands for pre-registration and for procedures with known long-run error rates. Give me the steelman, not the strawman: I already know how to say that frequentists irrationally care about data they did not get, and that version is not interesting to me. I want the best move available from their side, expressed as priors, likelihoods, posteriors, calibration, scoring rules, and, where it matters, multi-agent or adversarial settings.

I have been playing with a way of cashing part of this out myself, and I want to know whether it is a real translation or a case of me smuggling my own assumptions in and calling them translation. The idea: treat the evidence a paper reports as not simply the likelihood ratio it claims, but as that ratio discounted by a flexibility tax, a factor below one reflecting how many defensible analyses the researcher could have run and how much the field's norms allowed them to choose after seeing the data. On this reading, a pre-analysis plan is a commitment device whose value is that it shrinks the tax, and a p-value is a crude public proxy for how large the tax should be.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. First, is the flexibility tax fully expressible in Bayesian terms, or does estimating it require a base rate over a reference class of studies, so that long-run frequency sneaks back in through the back door as a way of estimating a hyperparameter? Second, where exactly does the translation distort things: is the thing being protected a property of a data-generating process, or of a scientific community's incentive structure and publication filter, and can my single-agent vocabulary represent the difference? Third, and most important: is there anything left over that has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? In particular, I want to know whether the frequentist's insistence on error-rate guarantees is best understood as a claim about the calibration of a reporting institution under adversarial selection rather than as a claim about any individual's belief state, and whether, if so, my framework can register that claim as a genuine desideratum or can only model it by ceasing to be about inference.

Do not lean on remembered empirical results or specific papers; argue from the structure of the two positions. If you want a concrete illustration, use the much-discussed social-psychology replication failures of the 2010s at the level of what is common knowledge, and avoid anything that would require looking a number up. I cannot search, and I am not asking for citations or reading lists.
