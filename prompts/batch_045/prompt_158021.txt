I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy training, and I got drafted into a reading group at work — my team builds scheduling and intake software for a chain of community clinics, and a few of the clinicians sit in. Last session we got stuck on whether any model of ours should ever take a patient's race as a feature, and the most philosophically aggressive clinician in the group produced an argument that I now think proves too much. I've reconstructed it as cleanly as I can and I still can't locate the loose step.

(1) A kind earns a place in science and in policy only if where its boundaries fall is settled by the world rather than by what anyone believes or does.
(2) A kind fails that condition if its extension would change were we to change our collective beliefs and practices while holding every non-mental fact fixed.
(3) Race fails it: human genetic variation is continuous and clinal rather than cleanly clustered, the official categories have been redrawn repeatedly and differ between countries, and people get reclassified when they move.
(4) So race earns no place in science or policy.
(5) But (1) and (2) are not about race in particular. Money, property, contracts, citizenship, unemployment, recession, invasive species and endangered species all seem to fail (2) as well; so do the classifications I use every day — whether a change is breaking, whether an incident is severity-one, whether a service is legacy. Nobody on my team thinks severity-one is a fiction, and nobody sensible thinks unemployment figures are nonsense.
(6) So (1) and (2) are too strong: a kind can be perfectly real, rigorously tracked and fit for policy while depending on our practices for where its boundaries fall.
(7) And then I am stuck, because race is about as causally load-bearing as a kind gets — clinicians treat patients differently by race, so the classification really does predict outcomes — so by (6) race is real and usable, which is exactly what (4) denies.

I want to keep something in the neighbourhood of (4), and I definitely want to keep the severity-one and unemployment verdicts in (5). If a premise has to go I would rather drop (2) than either of those, but I cannot see what dropping it costs me.

My own guess at a repair, which I do not trust: three different ways a practice can matter to a kind — constitutive (no widespread acceptance, no money), carving (we choose a threshold on a continuum that is there anyway: hypertension lines, poverty lines, severity levels), and looping (classifying people changes the people classified, so the kind's own predictions come true). I cannot tell whether that sorts the cases or just relocates the problem, since money and unemployment loop too, and race looks like carving as well as looping.

Where exactly does the chain break — or if it does not break, which premise is cheapest to give up and what does giving it up cost me? Please name the step, explain why it looked fine, and then price the alternatives you did not pick, ranked by how much each costs. If my three-way distinction is doing any real work, say which of the three is carrying it. No citations, papers or further reading, please — I have no way to follow them up. Keep the whole thing under 500 words.
