I'm closing the last session of a graduate seminar on early modern philosophy of mind, and the final text is the stretch of Part Five of Descartes's *Discourse on Method* where he argues that animals are machines. Rather than summarizing, I'd like to end with a villanelle, and I'd be grateful if you'd write it.

Set it in the voice of the creature Descartes asks us to imagine: a machine with the organs and outward shape of a human body, imitating our actions as far as it is possible to do so, addressed to a Cartesian observer who is trying to decide whether it is a real human being. On Descartes's own account that observer has exactly two certain signs to go on — that the creature cannot use words or other signs to declare its thoughts to others (a parrot emits words; it does not answer to the sense of what is said, and humans born deaf and without speech still contrive signs), and that it acts only through the disposition of its organs rather than by reason, which is a universal instrument fitted to every circumstance. I want the poem staged at the point where the observer applies both signs and finds that neither settles the question.

Two constraints, both of which should make the form carry the argument rather than decorate it:

First, of the two refrains, one should be a line in which the speaker claims something about its own inner life — a claim only a thinking thing could make, and one no stretch of behaviour could confirm — and the other a line in which the speaker accurately, without self-pity, describes the mechanism that produces that very line. Neither refrain should cancel the other. Their alternation should feel like the two signs taking turns: whatever presents itself as evidence of a mind gets redescribed as the arrangement of parts, and whatever presents itself as machinery is uttered by something that is speaking.

Second, the closing stanza should make clear that the observer's refusal of assent is not, in the end, a verdict about the signs. Have the turn land on the thought that the denial of thought to animals does not rest on behaviour at all, but on what a thinking thing is supposed to be — indivisible, and so not the sort of thing that the configuration of extended parts could produce. That is why a creature can satisfy every behavioural test and still be judged a machine.

One further thing I'd like registered, and not resolved: the observer has to live with the fact that a human being who cannot speak — an infant, or someone who never acquired language — fails the first sign outright and is nevertheless credited with a mind. Let that sit as a cost rather than as a conclusion, and keep it clear of both sentimentality about the machine and easy paradox about other minds.

Accuracy matters more than beauty, but I'd like both: anyone who knows the text should recognize the argument being staged and find nothing in it Descartes would have had to reject, while anyone who doesn't should still hear a poem that stands on its own. Keep to the villanelle's nineteen lines. After the poem, add a short note — a paragraph at most — saying which refrain is which, and where you meant the turn to fall; I'll be reading it aloud and want to know where to pause.
