I'm in a final-year seminar on blame and accountability, and this week's session was on hypocrisy. Our section leader leaned hard on an analogy I have been turning over since, and I would like it audited properly before I build a paper on it.

The analogy is that moral blame is like a civil action. On her telling, to blame someone is to bring a claim against them: the blamer needs standing in roughly the legal sense, meaning a particularized injury traceable to the person she is addressing, and even a blamer with standing can be barred, because a court of equity refuses relief to a plaintiff who comes to it with dirty hands — you cannot invoke a norm you are yourself flouting. She uses the analogy to carry a strong conclusion, namely that a hypocrite's blame is not merely impolite or unpersuasive but infelicitous: it makes no claim on the person addressed, that person may set it aside entirely, and nothing is left over — no reason to reconsider, no reason to feel anything, no residue of accountability.

A classmate objected that the analogy imports the very thing in dispute. Legal standing, he said, is a jurisdictional gate on a scarce public resource: it decides who may set the coercive apparatus of the state in motion, and, at least in its American form, it exists to keep courts from being used to air undifferentiated grievances. Blame is address between persons who are, on the account she is giving, one another's equals: no adjudicator, no docket, no remedy to allocate, no sovereign. So whatever work standing does in the moral case, it cannot be that. She replied that the gatekeeping rationale is inessential; what carries over is the structural point that whether a claim against you can be pressed at all can depend on facts about the claimant and not only on the content of the claim.

I want more than a verdict on that exchange. Please lay out the full correspondence table first: what plays the role of the plaintiff, the injury in fact, the cause of action, the court, the remedy, the affirmative defense, and the requirement, in the doctrine she is borrowing, that the plaintiff's misconduct concern the same subject matter as the suit. Note in passing that the doctrine is discretionary and is weighed against other considerations, and that it is a defense to relief rather than a finding that the underlying claim is false.

Then sort the disanalogies into the ones that actually bite on her conclusion and the ones that are decorative. Three I cannot settle myself:

First, two people both guilty of the same kind of thing. In law the defense is available to whichever of them is the defendant, so each is barred against the other, and there is still a prosecutor or a different, clean plaintiff to vindicate the norm. Morality has no party who stands outside the practice. So if blame forfeits standing whenever the blamer is compromised, the norm loses every enforcer it has and the worst offenders end up in a zone of mutual impunity. Is that a genuine consequence of the analogy, or am I misreading it?

Second, the confessing hypocrite: someone who did the thing, is still doing it, but has owned it publicly and is trying to stop. Equity looks backward at conduct; blame seems to respond to where the blamer now stands with respect to the norm. If acknowledgment cures in one case and not the other, then the two are tracking different kinds of condition, a historical bar versus a present relational one.

Third, whether the analogy quietly settles a question it was brought in to help answer: the same-subject-matter restriction would make forfeiture norm-specific, but the intuitive case for taking hypocrisy seriously is often norm-general.

Finally: does the strong conclusion survive, and if the litigation model is the wrong one, what would be a better analogy — appealing to the rules of a practice one is currently playing, a demand among equals, a request within an ongoing relationship? Please argue from the structure of the cases rather than from what is supposed to have been said in the literature.
