Write an internal memorandum, roughly 800 to 900 words, from the Office of Epistemic Assurance at a large risk-consulting firm, distributed to department heads under a subject line like "Rollout of the Knowledge Attestation Standard (Revision 3)." The register is deadpan corporate: header fields, a distribution list, a brief "background" paragraph, numbered action items, and an appendix. Do not let anyone in the document step outside the voice to explain the joke, and do not include footnotes, citations, or the names of any philosophers.

The backstory, which the memo should presuppose rather than narrate at length: after a costly incident in which an analyst acted on a belief that turned out to be false, the firm has adopted an attestation policy under which no one may act on a belief unless they can certify that they know it, and can certify that they know that they know it, and so on up the ladder. The appendix is the certification ladder itself: signature lines for level 0, level 1, level 2, and so forth, with a note that forms are available "for as many levels as the matter warrants."

The humor has to come from getting the epistemology right, so build it out of the actual structure of the view being satirized. The memo should discover, in the firm's own vocabulary of tolerances, safety margins, calibration, and sign-off authority, that demanding the second level is not the same as demanding the first, and that nothing about being in a good position with respect to a fact automatically puts you in a good position with respect to your being in that position. Then give the margin-for-error problem its full weight: for an attestation to be worth anything it has to be one the signer would not have made had things been slightly otherwise, so every level requires a margin wider than the level below it, the margins stack, and the required assurance runs off the top of the scale no matter how confident anyone gets — confident people are not the ones the policy is safe for, because confidence is not what supplies the margin. Include at least one concrete, slightly pathetic case: an analyst on the night shift, a few minutes apart or a few tenths of an hour of sleep apart from her threshold, alert by any ordinary standard and unable to say whether she is alert enough to sign that she is alert enough to sign.

Have one dissenting voice — someone from Quantitative Methods, say — state the problem cleanly in the language of tolerance stacking and be answered with jargon, reassigned, or thanked for her input. The memo's own recommendation should be a straight-faced reductio rather than a retreat: the firm either redefines "knowledge" as whatever survives the ladder (and quietly notices that nothing does), or restricts attestation to beliefs about which no one could possibly be wrong, or replaces judgment with a randomized decision procedure until the Assurance framework is "fully specified." A reader who knows the debate about higher-order knowledge and the luminosity of the mental should recognize the satire as fair and aimed at the principle embedded in the policy, not at a cartoon of it; a reader who has never heard of either should still be able to tell, without being told, exactly which assumption is being ground down.
