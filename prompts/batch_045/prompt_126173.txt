So I've got a works-in-progress talk next Thursday at a department I'm visiting, on a reading of Wittgenstein's On Certainty that I've been kicking around, and what I want from you is Q&A prep, not comments on the paper.

The thesis: hinges aren't propositions or beliefs with some wierd special epistemic status. They're questions that a practice has let go dead. My slogan is that a hinge is a question nobody's asking any more. Moore holding up his hand, that the earth was around for ages before you were born, that the person talking to you has a mind — none of these is certain because we bear some arational but rationally mandatory commitment to it. It's certain because in that practice there's no live question it would be an answer to: no procedure for settling it, no uptake for an answer, nothing that would go differently either way. Groundlessness isn't a status on a proposition, it's just that grounding is answering a question and there isn't one. I think this buys more than the standard stories do: it explains how a hinge can change on you — conversion, growing up somewhere else, a research field hitting an anomaly — without my owing you an account of rationally revising a belief, and it explains why claiming to know one of these sounds off: you're offering an answer to a question that isn't in play.

The room, about fifteen people. Two Wittgenstein people: one therapeutic or resolute type who thinks the whole hinge-epistemology industry is a betrayal and will want to know why my view isn't just another theory of the exact kind the later work is trying to get us to stop producing; one committed hinge epistemologist who'll say this is what everyone already says in different words. Three mainstream epistemologists working on knowledge, skepticism, disagreement — they'll ask whether this is foundationalism, coherentism, contextualism or externalism rebadged, and they'll press the regress argument. Two social and political epistemologists who work on deep disagreement, testimony, epistemic injustice — they'll go after conspiracy theorists and after who has the power to keep a question dead. One philosopher of science working on Kuhn and incommensurability. A formal epistemology grad student who'll ask what any of this looks like in a model, or whether I'm assigning anything probability one. And the department's ancient philosophy person, who comes to everything, and will ask how this differs from Aristotle on first principles.

The example I'm building the talk around: a lab where nobody checks whether the mass spectrometer was calibrated that morning; a new postdoc starts asking; it turns out it hadn't been recalibrated in three weeks and most of a year of results is worthless. My line is that it was a dead question for that group, then it went live, and that's all hinge change ever is.

Give me the objections in the order they'll actually come, phrased the way each of those people would phrase them, with what I should concede, what I have to hold, a two-minute reply each, and a note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should give away in the first two minutes before anyone asks anything, which single reply has to land for the paper to survive at all, and the one question I'm hoping nobody asks.
