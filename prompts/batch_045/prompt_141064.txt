hey — for this one please act as a professional philosopher who works on anthropics, simulation arguments and longtermism, and who is also annoyingly good at making one-page teaching handouts. think carefully, and start with some brainstorming about the logical space before you draw anything: I want the tree built out of the questions and the combinations of answers they allow, not out of a list of positions you already had in mind.

My reading group (eight people, half of them ML folks who are pretty skeptical of longtermism) is doing one session on whether the simulation argument should make you *less* of a fanatic about the far future. By fanaticism I mean being guided, in practice, by very small probabilities of astronomically large payoffs: x-risk reduction, trying to steer the value of a future containing huge numbers of people, the "it's worth it if there's a one-in-a-million chance we grab the whole future" style of reasoning. The session question is whether taking sim-hypothesis seriously (say, you put 5-10% on "we are a computation somebody else is running") should turn that dial down, up, or leave it alone.

What I want: a decision tree / flowchart we can hand out, with a short reading key.

- Start node: the reader grants some non-trivial credence that we're in a sim. Anyone who doesn't ends up at a "business as usual longtermist" leaf immediately, and that's fine — note it and move on.
- Every branch node must be phrased as a concrete question a smart person could answer without knowing which position it leads to: "you're told the sim is scheduled to shut down in 2060 and nothing you do changes that — do your choices still have astronomically large expected consequences, and if so through what channel?" No node may just ask "are you a truncationist?" or "do you think simulated minds matter?"
- The distinctions I definately want doing the branching work: (1) whether sim-ness *truncates* the reachable future or *amplifies* it (cheap stackable sims, sims inside sims); (2) whether minds running in a sim count as moral patients in the same way, and whether their welfare adds to or substitutes for base-reality welfare; (3) whether anything "leaks out" of the sim into base reality, and by which channel — at minimum include the "blueprint" channel (the operators are treating our run as a policy experiment and later implement whatever institutions produced good outcomes, so doing well in here has giant upstream effects) and the "resurrection lottery" channel (operators re-instantiate or promote some of the sim's people afterwards), and add a third of your own if you think one is missing; (4) whether your decision is correlated with those of near-duplicate copies of you in other runs, so that choosing well counts once or counts many times; (5) whether sim-uncertainty *fattens* the tails on the value of the future and so makes fanaticism worse rather than better.
- One weird hypothesis I want to be a reachable leaf, because someone in the group is going to raise it: the operators run more copies of our era the more *interesting* it is (tourism, entertainment, whatever), so expected value depends partly on how dramatic or novel our branch is, which makes the fanatical calculus come out somewhere bizarre. Give that leaf a name and one sentence on its most awkward commitment.
- Each leaf gets a name (use the existing name if the position has one, "unnamed" if you think nobody holds it) plus one sentence on the most awkward thing it's committed to.
- Flag any combination of answers that seems logically available but that nobody occupies, and say briefly why — incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Also flag any two leaves your tree can't really separate rather than pretending it can.
- Aim for something that fits on one page: 12 branch questions max, maybe 8-12 leaves. We don't want any bibliography or citations, and please don't invent papers — if you attach a name to a position and you're not sure it's a real thing, call it unnamed instead.
