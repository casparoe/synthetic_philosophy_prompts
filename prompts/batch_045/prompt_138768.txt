My aunt Ruth is the reason the planetarium's new wing exists. She gave the money — a serious amount of it — and she has been a working astrologer for something like forty years: charts, regular clients, a column in the local paper years back, classes she taught. She is not a crank about it and she is not a joke about it, and she is also the person who drove me to astronomy club every Saturday for three years and bought me my first telescope.

The dedication is in three weeks and I'm giving the remarks, about eight minutes. The room is the problem. Half of it is the university astronomy side: the department chair, who spent a year quietly trying to get the gift turned down, and two grad students who think the word astrology should not be spoken inside a building with a telescope in it. Then there's the town, because the town paid for the rest of it and they want to hear about school groups. And then there's Ruth and maybe a dozen of her friends, who know perfectly well what professional astronomers think of them.

I dont want a speech that sneers at her with a polite wrapper on it, and I also can't stand up in a planetarium and say the planets shape our characters. Write me one that doesn't cheat in either direction.

What I want it built on is Kepler, because he's the one figure both sides have already half-claimed. As I understand it — and this is about my level — he cast horoscopes and took money for them through most of his working life while also being an exact and serious astronomer, and he tried to strip astrology down and rebuild the parts he thought could be argued for in terms of geometry and harmony, throwing out a lot of the traditional apparatus. There's a line that circulates online about astrology being the foolish daughter of astronomy who still has to feed her mother; I've never seen an actual source for it and I don't want it quoted. Paraphrase it or leave it out — Ruth's friends have had that line used at them as a put-down and will hear it as one.

The structural things I need handled rather than dodged:

- I want the speech to rest on the real fact that the best observational astronomer of that generation did horoscope work and argued for it, and not on either cartoon version: not "he kept a superstition because he needed the cash," not "he was a mystic who happened to be good at ellipses." I gather there's a genuine scholarly argument about how continuous his astrology was with the rest of his physics; if that's right, the speech shouldn't pick a side it can't defend. Put it in a note and keep it out of the text.
- The astronomers have to be able to sit there. So it can't imply that astrology as Ruth practices it is defensible now, and it can't pretend the demarcation question isn't real.
- Ruth has to be able to sit there. So it can't be a "we used to be credulous, now we know better" story with her cast as the thing we grew out of.
- What I'd actually like to be able to say is something about taking the sky seriously, about being wrong in a way that isn't the same as being foolish, and about the two practices having been one practice once — and I want it to be true rather than merely moving. My mom is in the front row; she'll cry if it's lovely and she'll be furious with me if it's condescending.

There's a plaque going up at the end of it and my last line has to hand off to the unveiling without sounding like a ribbon-cutting. Give me the remarks, then a short note after with anything historical you weren't confident enough to put in the text so I can cut it. No sources or citations in the speech itself — nobody wants to hear a footnote at a plaque.
