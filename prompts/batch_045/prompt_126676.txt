I'm helping write a policy memo on AI race dynamics and I keep saying "the alignment tax" as if it picks out exactly one thing, and the more I use it the less I think it does. So before I commit to anything on paper, help me write the spec: what would a theory of the alignment tax have to get right before it could tell me whether some concrete intervention — a compute threshold, a licensing regime, an export control, a voluntary pause among a handful of labs — makes things better or worse, and by roughly how much? That's the only job it has to do; I don't need it to do anything else.

Give me a desiderata list, each with the case or consideration behind it, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are just what one camp happens to want. I'll start with my own half-baked decomposition so you have something to fix or throw out: as far as I can tell there's a rate question (how much does being careful actually cost you), a base question (cost measured in dollars, in calender time, or in how capable the thing you end up shipping is — and those can come apart in either direction), and an incidence question (who pays, and whether the cost lands mostly on actors who were going to be careful anyway, which is an adverse-selection effect I feel like most versions of the argument skip).

Cases I want handled at minimum:

- the one place there's real experience: safety work that also made the products more useful, so the "tax" came out negative. Is that a degenerate case the theory should just record as tax = 0, or is it evidence that the whole tax metaphor is wrong?
- safety theater: a lab spending a lot on something it calls safety that costs it nothing in capability or time, and that a regulator can't distinguish from the real thing. If the theory can't tell paying the tax from claiming to pay it, it's useless for my purpose.
- the unilateral case: the constraint binds on several actors but defection by any one of them is enough. My sense is the relevant number isn't the average tax but the tax as seen by the marginal actor who'd defect. Is that right, and what follows from it?
- the scaling case: if alignment gets harder as systems get more capable, the tax isn't a fixed fraction of R&D but something increasing, and at some point it's the entire budget. Does the theory need a view on the shape of that function, and can it say anything useful without one?
- what the baseline is. I've started to think "tax" smuggles in that the unconstrained race is the neutral case and safety is the deviation from it. Real problem with the concept, or am I overreading a metaphor?

Where I most want your brain: which pairs of desiderata can't be jointly satisfied. My guess is there's a clash between wanting numbers strong enough to rank two policies and the fact that the counterfactual — what the frontier would have done — is unobservable and maybe not even well defined; and maybe a second clash between treating the tax as a fact about the technology and treating it as a fact about institutions, given that interventions only move the second. If the list is jointly unsatisfiable so that the honest answer is "no theory of this does what you want," say that, and say which requirement to drop.

Then, briefly — one line each, I don't need a scorecard — which requirement does the most work in ruling out the accounts people actually give (the "safety is x% of compute" one, the "races are prisoner's dilemmas" one, the "then just don't build it" one). No citations or reading list please, I can't chase them and I don't care who said what first.

One epistemic thing: separate what's settled from what people who work on this genuinely disagree about, don't make a contested claim sound like the consensus, and where you're extrapolating past anything anyone actually knows, say so in as many words — that's the part I'll get yelled at for in the comments.
