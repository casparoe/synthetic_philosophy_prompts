So me and my friend Jono had this really long, kind of stupid argument last night (we were both on our second bottle and it went on way past the point where either of us was making sense) about Dennett's phrase competence without comprehension and what he calls Darwin's and Turing's strange inversions of reasoning — I'm pretty sure the phrase shows up in Darwin's Dangerous Idea but Jono insists it's the later book, whatever, the point is the pattern where a process produces something that works beautifully without anyone or anything involved having the faintest idea why, and the reasons only get bolted on afterward. Jono's position — which he named, annoyingly, and now I can't get it out of my head — was the retrofit fallacy: whenever people finally come up with an explanation for a practice that's been working for centuries, the explanation is a decoration, a story told backwards, and the moment you write it down and start following the story instead of the practice, things get worse, because the practice was carrying information that the story throws away. He also had this thing he called ghost reasons — explanations that sound like mechanisms but are really just the practice restated in fancier vocabulary — and he says most of what gets taught to apprentices everywhere is ghost reasons, and that the apprentices learn fine anyway, which proves the reasons were doing no work. I pushed back: my claim was that comprehension is precisely what lets competence travel, that a practice you can't state is stuck in exactly the conditions it evolved in, and that the second you need to fix it, adapt it to a new climate, or teach it to someone who isn't standing in the room with you, ghost reasons and real reasons stop being interchangeable — and yeah, spelling things out is lossy and sometimes makes people choke, but that's a cost, not a refutation. We did not resolve it.

So: write me a short story that dramatizes this. I want the setting to be really concrete and unglamorous — a small monastery on a made-up Greek island (name it whatever, throw in a couple of invented Greek words for the gear and the steps) where the brothers have been making one specific cheese, or curing one specific fish, or brewing one specific thing, for four hundred years, following a rite with maybe eleven steps, several of which are obviously ritualized: a chant that nobody realizes is timing the stirring, a prohibition, an order of operations that seems arbitrary, a tool that has to be washed in a particular cistern and nowhere else. Nobody there can tell you why any of it works; they'll tell you why, but what they tell you is mostly about a saint. Then a food scientist (or a microbiologist, or just a very stubborn outsider) shows up in, say, the early 1960s, figures out a plausible mechanism for two or three of the steps — some of it right, some of it wrong — and writes the whole thing up as an explicit protocol with the reasons attached. Then I want the test, which is the part Jono and I kept circling: hand the written protocol with its reasons to one newcomer and hand nothing but imitation and scolding to another, and let us watch what actually happens over a season or two. Give me at least one ghost reason that a character fully believes and that the story later reveals as either accidentally true or catastrophically false. And give me one scene where somebody asks whether the monastery knows something that no individual monk knows.

Some craft notes, because I'm picky: past tense, third person, somewhere around 1500 to 2000 words, and give it a title. Please don't end with a character sitting down and explaining the moral in a tidy paragraph of dialogue — I want the last scene to do the work, so that the reader can tell which of us was closer to right by what happens rather than by what's said, and I do want the story to come down on a side rather than shrugging and saying it's complicated. It's fine if there's a real cost shown on the winning side too: Jono bet me twenty euros that any story about this ends up secretly agreeing with him, so if you go my way, don't make it a walkover, and if you go his way, at least let my transfer point land at least once before it gets answered.
