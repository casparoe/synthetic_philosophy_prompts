Possibly I've spent too long thinking about this, but here goes. I'm in a small reading group on decision theory (mostly econ grad students, plus one philosopher who says "that's just consequentialism" whenever anyone says anything) and I've ended up owning a position I'm not sure I can cash out: the Allais pattern isn't a mistake. Not "a forgivable mistake because EU asks too much of us" — an actual, rationally permissible preference. I've started calling it a taste for settledness: a preference, over and above the final probabilities over final outcomes, for outcomes whose source of variation isn't left hanging. On that reading, the person who take the sure $1M over the 89/10/1 gamble and then takes the 10% shot at $5M over the 11% shot at $1M isn't contradicting herself; she's paying for something EU's formalism is blind to, and independence is a bad axiom rather than a principle whose violation makes you exploitable.

What I want is a map of my exposure, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. I've got three probes I keep testing the view against and I'd like you to say what each would show if it came back the wrong way, and how much it should move me. (1) De-certainize it: rerun the first pair with the sure million replaced by a 99% chance at the million, everything else the same. My view says the pattern should broadly survive, because what's doing the work is the structure of the risk and not the word "certain"; if it collapses at 99% I think I'm in real trouble — but how much, and could a defender absorb it by saying the certainty effect is just one expression of something more general? (2) Seal it: the "certain" option's outcome has already been fixed by a draw an hour ago, you'll simply never be told, so it's objectively settled but epistemically open. My view predicts the pull of that option is undiminished; a pure framing-artifact story predicts it tracks the description. (3) Price instead of choose: if the Allais gap shows up in choice but not in what people say the options are worth, is that evidence the preference isn't really there, or just that the two tasks measure different things? I'd also like you to name what my view predicts that the "it's just an error" story doesn't — where the two genuinely come apart.

Then two things I'd like you to not breeze past. First, the big one: someone in the group argued that once you drop independence you're money-pumpable in sequential problems unless you hold onto some separability condition or bite the bullet and become a "resolute chooser" who sticks to a plan you'd now rather abandon. Which of those should a defender of my view actually take, and what does the bullet taste like — is resolute choice a real option or a name for a problem? Second: someone else muttered something about a "fanning-out" prediction that every theory which gives up independence but keeps consequentialism is supposedly stuck with, and an example (Machina? probabilities like 0.50 and 0.51?) that breaks even those. I only half remember it, so reconstruct it if it's real and tell me whether it hits my position or only the specific functional forms people use to model it. Related worry: has my view been qualified into unfalsifiability? If any old preference pattern can be accommodated by some weighting function or other, "independence isn't a requirement of rationality" starts to sound like it has no content, and I'd rather be told that plainly. Please also seperate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this, tell me which defeaters someone who already agrees with me would concede versus dismiss as question-begging, and don't hand me a consensus that isn't there — I can't tell the difference, and I'd most want to be moved by the sequential-choice stuff and the de-certainization probe, but rank by what should move me rather than what would.
