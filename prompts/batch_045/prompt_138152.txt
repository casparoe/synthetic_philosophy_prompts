I work in regulatory cost–benefit analysis, where every option is scored as a difference against a stated counterfactual baseline, and where it is taken for granted that the ranking of two options cannot change if you restate the baseline, so long as the two options themselves are unchanged. Reading about the repugnant conclusion in my spare time, I keep running into what looks like a violation of exactly that assumption, and I don't know whether there is an established concept for it, or whether I have noticed something that has no name.

Two instances of the pattern.

First: take a population of ten people, all at a very high level of welfare, and consider whether to bring an eleventh person into existence whose life is clearly worth living but well below the level of the ten. Much of what I have read seems to conclude that this would not be an improvement. Now take the eleven-person population and ask the question from the other end: would it have been better if the eleventh person had never existed? The same literature seems very reluctant to answer yes. Each answer may be defensible on its own, but together they leave the pair ranked in one direction and unranked in the other — and which one you get depends on which population the question told you to stand in.

Second: the pattern does not seem to need an existing population at all. Ask which of two merely possible populations a creator should bring into existence, and the theories that avoid the repugnant conclusion tend to give you something that behaves less like an ordering of the two and more like a permission to move, or not to move, from wherever the question started. The starting point is supplied by the way the case is presented rather than by anything in the case. On my home turf this is the same shape as the complaint that a welfare ranking should not depend on which option you label the status quo, or on which good you use as the numeraire; I want to know whether the population-ethics version of that complaint has been isolated and named, and how far the analogy actually goes.

I should say what I think this is not. The people in my first instance are the same ten people in both options, differing only in whether an extra person joins them, so this is not the puzzle about choices that change who comes to exist. It is also not, I think, simply the view that there is no duty to create happy people but a duty not to create miserable ones — that is a substantive moral claim, whereas what I am describing looks like a structural feature of how certain theories deliver verdicts, and I want to know whether the structure can be had without the substantive claim, and whether it must be had by anything that blocks the repugnant conclusion at all.

Please don't rest anything on who said what or on tracking down references; I have no access to the literature from where I am writing, and I would rather have the argument than the citations. I will not be satisfied with a bare label, and I will not be satisfied with being told that this is just the creation asymmetry under another description — if that is your verdict, I want to see the exact respect in which the two collapse into each other and what would be lost by collapsing them.
