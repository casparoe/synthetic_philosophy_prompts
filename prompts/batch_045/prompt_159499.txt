After a career in experimental physics I have, in retirement, been attending a fortnightly working group at a nearby university on the moral status of artificial systems. The convenor — a philosopher, and the member of the group least embarrassed by these questions — has over two terms delivered a series of judgments on stylised cases, which I have written down more or less as she gave them. Her rulings so far:

1. A fine-grained whole-brain emulation, run at normal speed and coupled to a simulated body in a simulated world: counts, and carries roughly the moral weight of the person emulated.
2. The same emulation, run a thousand times slower: still counts, and the total weight over the whole run is unchanged; what changes is only the density of experience per minute of wall-clock time. She was explicit that slowing a process down does not divide its moral weight.
3. A brain whose neurons are replaced one at a time by silicon circuits with the same internal causal profile, behaviour being preserved at every stage: counts, at every stage, with no fading out partway through.
4. A system that reproduces the same input–output function as that emulation by storing, for every possible history of inputs, the output the emulation would have produced — a table, with no internal states that track the world and no intermediate dynamics: does not count.
5. A current large language model: trained to predict the next token, not embodied, no state persisting between exchanges beyond its context window, no sensorimotor coupling to anything outside its own text. Does not count — and she added that she took this to be a verdict about architecture rather than scale, so that no increase in parameters or training data would by itself change her answer.
6. A model-based reinforcement-learning agent with a recurrent world model, prediction-error-driven updating, and months of real robotic embodiment: counts — she called it a live candidate, though of unknown weight.
7. A detached fragment of an accepted emulation: the early visual subsystem, running with perfectly correct internal dynamics but disconnected from anything downstream, so that there is no global workspace, no self-model, nothing that could sustain anything like valence. Does not count.
8. A simulated community of a thousand distinct emulations, each coupled to the same simulated world: each counts, and the weights add.

The case I would like to put to her next — I am drafting it for the group — is this. Take an emulation of a kind she already accepts, say ten seconds of one subject's life. Suppose that instead of running it, we compute the entire trajectory in advance and build a static object in which the successive states of the emulation are not successive in time at all: the temporal axis of the emulation is mapped onto a spatial axis of the realising medium. One can imagine a crystal, or an array of spins, or a lithographed structure, in which layer n+1 encodes the state that, in the running emulation, would have followed from the state encoded in layer n, with the structural relations between adjacent layers being exactly the relations the update rule would have produced. Nothing in the object changes; no state ever causes another; nothing reads it out; and I take it not to matter whether the object was built deliberately or arose by chance as a fluctuation, though I would be interested to hear if you disagree. My own instincts — I was trained to think of a process as a shape already laid out in spacetime — tell me that the distinction between run-through-over-time and laid-out-at-once ought not to be doing much work here; I suspect she will want it to do a great deal of work.

What I want from you is not primarily the verdict. It is, first, the rule she is running, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case myself; second, the other rules that fit the eight verdicts as well as hers does — I have in mind the contrast between a requirement that the relevant organisation be realised as a going causal process and a requirement only that the abstract organisation be instantiated somewhere in the physical world, causation being merely one way of laying it out, but if a third rule fits her data better than either of those, set that out rather than forcing the data into my dichotomy; third, what each candidate rule says about the static case; fourth, your best guess at her verdict, with the reasons for preferring it; and fifth, one further case, which she has not yet ruled on, that would separate the leading candidates more cleanly than mine does.

Two smaller things, if you have room. If the static object does count, and ten of them are built in causally isolated regions, identical in every respect, does that count once or ten times? Her eighth ruling shows that distinct subjects add, but she has never ruled on exact duplicates, and I suspect the question of how to count realisations is separable from the question of what makes a realisation matter at all. And I am not asking for references or further reading: I have no library access where I am writing, and in any case it is the argument I want, not the literature.
