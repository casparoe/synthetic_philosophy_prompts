Our interpretability reading group has been going in circles for about a year on whether neural networks really have representations, and I want a diagnosis of the stalemate rather than a verdict on who's right.

Here's where each side actually is. The feature realists point to: linear probes in early layers recovering things like whether a chunk of text is in French or whether an open bracket is still open; sparse autoencoders (SAEs) trained on a residual stream giving directions that look monosemantic, that fire on categories humans can label, and that shift behaviour in the intended way when you clamp them; and the fact that the same directions show up across models trained from different seeds once you align the two activation spaces in an unsupervised way. They also lean on a structural point — that the MLP acts elementwise, so the residual-stream basis isn't as arbitrary as picking random coordinates in a vector space.

The skeptics reply that every one of those results is a fact about a decomposition we chose, not about the network. The SAE optimizer hands you one of many dictionaries that sit equally well on the sparsity/reconstruction tradeoff; a probe is just a linear functional that happens to exist; and clamping shows that your intervention is causally efficacious, not that the network was using that variable. Call them features if it helps, but don't confuse a coordinate system that's convenient for us with the network's own ontology.

Three results keep getting waved at each other and none of them has moved anybody:

1. Two SAEs trained on the same layer, different seeds and dictionary sizes, produce two dictionaries that aren't permutations of each other, that are equally good on the curve, that are both steerable, and each of which predicts the other's activations well. Realists say that's just features splitting into sub-features — hierarchy. Skeptics say that's underdetermination, full stop.

2. A preprint someone brought a couple of months ago that I can't stop thinking about. Two models, same architecture, same data, different seeds, loss within noise of each other. For most features in model A there's a matching direction in B under the unsupervised alignment, and clamping it does the same thing. But for one cluster — stuff that looks like tracking whether a syntactic construction is still open — B has no single direction. A probe recovers the information perfectly, but it's spread across about six directions; clamping any one of them does nothing, and you have to clamp all six to get the effect you get from A's one feature.

3. The mirror image: a probe for some boring syntactic property hits 99%, no SAE at any dictionary size finds a feature correlating with it above chance, and there's no direction whose clamping changes anything.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? My half-baked theory is that the two sides aren't arguing about the network at all — they're arguing about whether a decomposition has to be *unique* to be real, which is the same shape as fights over whether a system's natural degrees of freedom are discovered or imposed. If that's right it's basically verbal. But both sides keep running new experiments, which suggests they think there's an empirical or formal question buried in there — something about whether the network satisfies the conditions under which a dictionary is identifiable, or about whether there's a privileged basis at all.

What I want: a diagnosis with reasons, using what each camp actually says above, and an honest account of what would count as progress. A theorem about identifiability? A benchmark? A distinction — say, between information being present in the activations, being implemented as a causally separable variable, and being axis-aligned? A change of question, from "does it have representations" to something like "does this description let me predict or control it"? Or is this one where we should definately expect no resolution, and if so, why.
