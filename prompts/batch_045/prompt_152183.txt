I'm a novelist rather than a philosopher, so sorry in advance if I mangle the vocabulary. I've got a book on the go set in a small town where, after a pretty ugly assault, the kid who did it gets referred to a restorative-justice conference instead of being charged in the usual way: he sits in a circle with the person he hurt, both families, a couple of neighbours, a facilitator, and the whole point seems to be that he admits what he did out loud, hears in detail what it did to everyone, apologises in a way the others actually accept, and agrees to do something to make it right — after which he's meant to be received back into the town rather than taken away. I've been reading a bit about ubuntu and other relational ideas of personhood, the old maxim that a human being is a human being only through other human beings, and the conference looks like it was designed to illustrate exactly that. But I don't trust the first impression, and I need the philosophy straight before I write these scenes, because right now my characters are all saying vaguely noble things at each other.

So: what does that practice actually presuppose about what a person is, and what does it rule out? Specifically:

- Does it strictly need the relational claim, or could someone who thinks a person is just a locus of consciousness, or a bundle of psychological continuity, or a bearer of individual rights and interests, endorse the very same conferences on other grounds — deterrence, rehabilitation, victims feeling better, cheapness? I want to know what the practice genuinely commits you to versus what it merely sits nicely with, and where it's underdetermined so that several theories would license it.
- If it does need a relational view, which one? I can see at least two: a strong one where personhood is conferred by being recognised and can be withdrawn (ostracism, exile, being struck out of the record, being treated as already socially dead — and the conference as the thing that reverses that), and a weaker one where relations constitute personhood but nothing anyone does can revoke it. Which does the conference commit me to? It looks strong, since the boy's standing seems to depend on whether the others accept his apology — but then what happens if the victim refuses, ever? Is his personhood held hostage to someone else's say-so, and who has the authority to confer it?
- Does it presuppose that wrongdoing damages the wrongdoer's own personhood and not merely the victim's? If so, shouldn't how badly he's diminished track how he's responded rather than how much harm he caused? Does the practice actually think that, or does it quietly track harm anyway?

And here's the part that really puzzles me, the inconsistencies:

1. Conferences get used for juveniles and minor offences, but grave violence still goes to an adversarial trial and prison. What does that carve-out presuppose — that repair is enough for small wrongs but not for big ones? Is that compatible with the relational story, or does it smuggle back in retribution, denunciation, or plain incapacitation?
2. Criminal law insists on individual guilt and no guilt by association, yet the circle drags in families and neighbours and treats the community itself as both implicated and in need of repair. Can one theory hold both?
3. We treat death as the harshest sentence and decades of solitary confinement as milder. On a relational view shouldn't being cut off from everyone be the worse fate, and banishment the severest punishment? So is the sentencing hierarchy evidence that the system doesn't really believe the thing it says at the conference?
4. The dead. When the victim is dead, relatives speak for them and the apology is addressed to the family; I've also read that in some of these traditions the ancestors are still treated as members of the community who can be wronged or consulted. Does the practice presuppose that relations reach past death, or is that a separate commitment it doesn't need? Modern law, by contrast, mostly holds that death extinguishes your claims.
5. The novelist's own wrinkle, which is why I care: readers mourn characters, hold them responsible, name children after them, feel personally betrayed by them, and my whole trade runs on that. If personhood is constituted by relations rather than by consciousness, do fictional characters start to look like persons — related-to but not experiencers? Is that a reductio of the relational view or a feature it should welcome? Same question about corporations, a congregation, a regiment: truth commissions of the amnesty-for-full-disclosure kind, like South Africa's, held institutions accountable and took their apologies as meaningful. Does that presuppose collective persons, and does relationalism get them more easily than individualism does?

Finally, the test I actually want: if my town ran its justice on the opposing, individualist picture — persons as separate rights-bearers with interests, nothing constituted by relations — what concretely would have to change in that circle scene? Who would be in the room, what would count as success, and what would we lose? And one last case I can't resolve: a person who discloses everything, mechanically and completely, in the right order, and feels nothing at all. Does the practice have anything to say about them, or does it just quietly not know?
