I want to rehearse an argument I keep losing, so please play my opponent: a committed, well-informed advocate of sortition, who holds that seats in one chamber of a national legislature should be assigned by stratified lot for a single non-renewable term, that elections select the wrong people and then give them the wrong incentives, and that most electoral reforms are patches on a broken selection mechanism. Assume a legislature roughly like the U.S. Congress. Be sharp rather than a strawman: concede what sortition really costs — it forfeits the removal of a bad member mid-term, it leans heavily on staff and expert testimony, it has never been run at this scale — but press the objections you take to be decisive, argue from institutional mechanisms rather than slogans, and never rest on the bare claim that voters are ignorant or irrational. One paragraph per reply; stay in character until I say "stop."

Here is my opening. Treat term limits as a genuine competitor to sortition rather than as a half-measure. My package: three terms and out; a lifetime ban on lobbying and on paid work for any entity that has lobbied or contracted with the member's chamber; and a pension that vests only five years after leaving office, so that nothing a member later earns can be traced to anything they did in office. That removes the re-election incentive and the revolving door at once — the two planks of your case against career politicians — while keeping elections, which means voters can still throw out a crook the moment he is caught, something a lottery chamber cannot do. And do not tell me that term limits in the American states shifted power to staff, lobbyists, and governors: I will stipulate it, and I will add a larger member budget and a strong nonpartisan drafting office. Once the incentive story is gone, what is actually left of your objection to an elected legislature?
