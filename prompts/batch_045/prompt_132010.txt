Set-up, so we're on the same page: Searle's Chinese Room. A man who speaks no Chinese sits in a room, Chinese writing comes in through a slot, he matches it against an English rulebook and passes Chinese writing back out, and to people outside the room his replies are indistinguishible from a native speaker's. Searle concludes that neither the man nor the room understands Chinese, and hence that running the right program can't be sufficient for understanding.

The angle I want you to use is a distinction I'm making up on the spot. Call a system's competence *interpretively implemented* when the causal chain that produces its behaviour runs through some component that reads and grasps a specification of what the whole system is meant to do — somebody, or something, has to understand "when this comes in, send that out" and then comply. Call it *non-interpretively implemented* when nothing in the system does that: the parts just get on with their local jobs and no sub-part is consulting a description of the whole. The room is obviously the first kind of case. A human brain, on any ordinary telling, is the second: there's no little reader in there working from a manual of you.

Argue that this distinction, and not the familiar "syntax isn't semantics" line or the appeal to the special causal powers of grey matter, is what's really load-bearing in the Chinese Room — and that read this way the argument survives both the System Reply (the room-plus-man-plus-rulebook as a whole understands, even though no part of it does) and the Robot Reply (stick the system in a body with cameras and limbs so that it's properly hooked up to the world).
