Hey, I need someone to poke holes in a package of things I've come to believe, because I've been turning this over for a couple years now and I genuinely can't tell whether I have a coherent view or just a pile of gut feelings with a flag stuck in them.

Background, since it probably explains my priors: I tend bar four nights a week at a place that's mostly regulars, and I moderate a decent-sized Discord for a game I used to be really into, so I spend an unhealthy amount of time watching what people do when somebody wrongs somebody else and there's no obvious authority to appeal to. A while back I stumbled across that three-way scheme people talk about — honor cultures, dignity cultures, victimhood cultures — and I want to say up front I have no academic background in any of this, I just read about it somewhere online and it clicked hard enough that I've been applying it to everything since. As I understand it: in an honor culture you're supposed to answer slights yourself, and your standing depends on whether people think you can be messed with; in a dignity culture everyone has equal standing, the correct response to a small slight is to not treat it as a big deal, and the serious stuff gets handed to institutions; in a victimhood culture you also don't retaliate, but instead of shrugging it off you take the grievance public, and your standing goes up with how aggrieved you can credibly claim to be.

Here's roughly where I've landed. (1) I think the scheme is basically right as a map — I can point at three regulars who each are one. (2) I think honor culture gets a worse rap than it deserves: being willing to answer for yourself instead of running to somebody is a real form of self-respect, and in practice the guys at my bar who will physically step in are the reason the rest of us are safe, and they would never in a million years call the cops. (3) I still think dignity culture is the best of the three, mostly because it's the only one where your standing doesn't depend on either your capacity for violence or your capacity for public suffering. (4) I think victimhood culture is a genuine regression — competing over who is more damaged makes every dispute worse and it pays people for performing injury. (5) But I also think most of what gets mocked as victimhood culture isn't actually that: a lot of it is people who were never permitted to make a dignity claim in the first place finally demanding that the people in charge treat them like everyone else. I keep coming back to MeToo here — if your boss harasses you and HR does nothing and you go public, that isn't status-seeking, that's asking for the equal standing dignity culture already promised you. (6) And I've got one of my own that I don't think fits the scheme at all: what I call audit culture, where a conflict gets neither answered personally nor taken public, it just gets filed — HR tickets, incident reports, mediation, insurance claims, performance plans. Nobody's honor is at stake and nobody gains status from being injured, but whatever punishment happens is administered by nobody in particular and can't be appealed to anything. I think this is where most of middle-class life actually happens now, and in one specific way I think it's worse than all three of the others: at least in the others you can see that something happened and see who did it. (7) Finally, I don't think any of these is morally superior in the abstract — they look to me like solutions to different problems about who enforces the rules, honor showing up where the state won't protect you, dignity where it will, victimhood where the institutions exist but don't care about you — so the whole which one is morally superior question feels a bit like asking whether a coat is better than a fan.

One more thing, a case I can't get out of my head, because I think it shows I'm contradicting myself somewhere and I can't find where. A couple years back on the Discord, one member posted a long and pretty dramatic public accusation about another member, and I banned the poster for stirring up drama — it felt obviously right at the time. Then over the next year three other people independently told pretty similar stories about the same guy, and it turned out the first poster had been telling the truth the whole time. I still think I was right to ban them given what I knew, but I can't make that sit with (5), because (5) says going public when the mods won't act is exactly the dignified move, and that is precisely the thing that got them banned.

So please audit the set: which pairs of these actually conflict, which only look like they conflict, and for each real one, what would I have to give up and what does giving it up cost me? Rank them by how severe they are instead of listing them as equally bad, and tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I don't know which commitment I'd rather drop — if I did I'd have dropped it already — so don't ask me to pick first. And please don't just reassure me that it's all basically consistent and I'm overthinking it; my friend Priya has been telling me that for a year and it hasn't unstuck anything.
