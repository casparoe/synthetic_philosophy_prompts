I help organize the end-of-term comedy night for my philosophy department, and this year one of the performers — a visiting PhD student who does standup on the side — has asked for a bit that is about something more specific than the usual material about academics being useless at everyday life. I've put G. E. Moore forward, and I'd like you to write the bit: a short comic dialogue, two or three characters and maybe a dozen exchanges, ending in one punchline.

The laugh has to turn on the actual machinery of Moore's account of intrinsic value, not on the fact that philosophers have strange hobbies. The ingredients I'd like used: the method of isolation Moore uses to test what is good in itself (would this thing still be worth having if it existed quite alone in the universe?), his ranking of the goods that survive that test — the personal affections and the enjoyment of beautiful objects, placed far above everything else we know of — his insistence that what matters is the consciousness or appreciation of beauty rather than the mere existence of beautiful things, which on his view would be worth very little in a universe with nobody in it to see them, and his doctrine of organic unities, on which the value of a whole is not the sum of the values of its parts, so that a whole containing something intrinsically bad can be worth more than a whole made up only of good parts. Someone who has read Moore should come away able to say: yes, that is a thing his view really is committed to, or that is a genuine awkwardness in it.

The wrinkle I'd like you to build the bit around is one I made up, so you won't have seen it anywhere: suppose the characters are playing a party game I invented called Isolation, in which each person has to justify their plans for the weekend by asking whether that activity would still be worth having if it were the only thing in the universe. One of them, who has read rather too much Moore, notices mid-game that the test applied honestly to friendship seems to dissolve friendship into a species of aesthetic appreciation: what you value about your friend, considered in isolation from everything else, is the pleasant consciousness you have of them, which makes the two great goods on Moore's list collapse into one, and turns the ranking of them into a ranking of kinds of looking. I'd like the punchline to land on or near that collapse — the discovery that a consistent Moorean cannot distinguish loving your friend from enjoying the sight of them, and that this either makes the character behave absurdly toward the friend standing right there, or makes something in the friendship visibly go wrong at the moment of the discovery.

Two constraints. First, no generic nerd humour and no winking at the audience about how obscure any of this is: the setup should be followable by someone who has never heard of Moore, while the punchline should be considerably better for those who have. Second, keep it performable in front of colleagues and their partners, and don't resolve the philosophical problem in the joke — the bit should leave the tension standing, since the point of the laugh is that the character is trapped by an argument rather than by stupidity.
