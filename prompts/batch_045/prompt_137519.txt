I teach a graduate seminar on personal identity and practical reason, and we have just finished two weeks on the authority of advance directives. My students now have three well-worn ways of setting a directive aside, and they reach for them automatically: (i) the patient is no longer the person who wrote it, so the earlier self's self-determination does not extend to her; (ii) the situation that has actually arrived is not the one the directive contemplated, so the document does not cover it; (iii) the earlier self would have written something different had she known what we now know, or her values have since changed. I want a case that closes all three and still comes out against honoring the directive.

So: construct a case in which a procedurally impeccable directive — competently written, well informed, specific, never formally revoked, and containing a self-binding clause of the Ulysses kind, instructing that any later refusal by the patient is not to be counted as hers — should nevertheless be set aside at the later self's request, and in which none of (i), (ii) or (iii) is what does the work. Concretely: the later self must be psychologically continuous with the woman who wrote it and must remember writing it; the clinical situation must be the one she plainly had in mind; and her values must not have changed — I want her to insist, plausibly, that they have not. What has changed is that she now occupies the state the directive was written about, and from inside it she claims to see something she could not have seen from outside: not a new fact about her prognosis or her treatment, since she had every proposition a well-counseled patient could have been given, but what her own standing ends actually require from in here. Her claim, as I want you to stage it, is interpretive rather than revisionary: she was always authoritative about what matters to her, but not about how that applies from inside a condition she could not then imagine, and the directive taken literally would now defeat the very thing it was written to protect. Keep it realistic — a ward, a clinic, a care home, nothing science-fictional, nothing turning on the fine print of any real statute, and the scenario itself in a single paragraph. Arrange matters so that the later self is not, on balance, experientially better off if the directive is set aside; I do not want the override to be a disguised appeal to present comfort. If you find that the ban on (i) to (iii) cannot be honored, or that any case of this shape secretly is (iii) in a costume, tell me so plainly instead of fudging it — an honest failure is more use to me in a seminar than a case that cheats.

With the case in place, do the check. Say what three views would each do with it and why: simple precedent autonomy, on which the point of a directive just is to let an earlier self bind a later one; the integrity or critical-interests view, on which a directive is at best evidence of what the shape of a whole life requires and can in principle be defeated by better evidence about that; and the skeptical view that stresses the present patient's experiential interests and the danger of a dead hand. I want precedent autonomy to honor the directive and the integrity view to set it aside — I am aware that this reverses the direction in which the integrity view is usually wheeled in, and I mean to — with the third view neutralized by your stipulation about experiential welfare, so that nothing in the argument quietly leans on it. Then, separately: name the single feature of the case that is doing the work; show what happens to the verdict when that feature is removed, in particular if the later self's discovery is made propositional (a piece of information she simply lacked) and if the self-binding clause is dropped; and give me the strongest reply available to a defender of precedent autonomy, not a straw man. The use I have in mind is ninety minutes of seminar: I want students to end up arguing about whether the earlier self is ever an authority over the application of her own values or only over the values themselves. Close with two or three sentences of your own assessment — how confident you are that the case meets all of these constraints simultaneously, and which one of the claims above you are least sure of. My own guess is that the weak point is whether reinterpretation here is really distinguishable from a change of mind, but tell me if you think it lies somewhere else.
