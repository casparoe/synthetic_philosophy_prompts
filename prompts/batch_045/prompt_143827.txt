I am a novelist, and what I need from you is a document rather than an essay. The book I am working on is set in a small, orderly republic in which immaterialism is not a seminar topic but the working metaphysics of the civil service: the courts, the land registry and the insurance industry all proceed on the view that for a thing to be is for it to be perceived. For a scene in Chapter Nine I want an in-universe artifact — an official guidance leaflet, issued by whatever registry or ministry you think fit, explaining to ordinary citizens how to have a chattel or a parcel of land recorded as "in being," and what to do when a record goes wrong.

It should be written in numbered clauses, with a title and a file reference, and read like a real government leaflet: brisk, mildly menacing, saturated with forms and fees. The comedy has to come from fidelity to the position, not from jeering at it. Stay deadpan inside the frame — the document must never suggest that the metaphysics is false, never wink at the reader, and never crack a joke that belongs to our world rather than to that one. Do not quote the historical Berkeley and do not mention scholars, interpreters or philosophy departments inside the piece.

Two features in particular I want it to bite on. First, the argument that one cannot even think of a thing as existing unperceived, since the attempt to do so is itself a way of conceiving it: let the leaflet's own procedure become entangled in this, whether through a form that cannot be completed correctly, a claim that is validated and annulled by the single act of filing it, or some trap of your own devising. Second, the doctrinal fallback that the unbroken perception of the divine mind keeps things in being during the hours when no finite mind is attending to them — and, following from it, the quiet awkwardness that the officials paid to attend to things are, on their own official metaphysics, performing a service that requires no performing, while in practice nobody will authorise closing their office.

I would also like the document to have to do real work on some genuine wrinkle rather than coasting on a slogan. One I had in mind: on a common reading of immaterialism, two minds never literally perceive one and the same thing, only resembling ones, so title disputes between two claimants would be settled by tribunals adjudicating "sufficiency of resemblance," with costs scaled to the degree of dissimilarity. Use that or replace it with a better one. The result should be legible to a reader who has never heard of Berkeley and funny to a reader who has.

Length: 600 words maximum for the document itself. Below it, add a separate note to me of no more than 100 words marking which commitments you treated as settled and which are genuinely contested among people who know the period — for instance how much weight the divine-perceiver move can bear, and whether the argument about conceiving is best read as a conceivability argument at all — and where you exaggerated or took a liberty for the sake of the joke. I want to know what a specialist would concede is fair and what would make one wince.
