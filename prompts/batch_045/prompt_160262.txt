I'm a PhD student in philosophy (political philosophy, if it matters) and I keep bumping into one feature of how my field actually runs, and I'd like the implicit theory behind it spelled out.

When you submit a paper, referees will reject it for not engaging with the recent literature — meaning roughly the last couple of decades of journal articles — even when the paper is in direct conversation with a classic from the 60s or 70s and the objections it has to answer are basically the same ones that classic faced. But nobody behaves as if anything has been settled: no referee ever says this position was refuted in 2005 so there's nothing to do here, and it's completely normal to publish a paper reviving a view everyone stopped defending thirty years ago. We also have a whole professionalized subfield, history of philosophy, that treats old texts as live objects of research rather than as superseded stages, and nobody finds that odd in the way it would be odd for a physicist. And dissertations have to make an original contribution, even though there is no body of established results to add to.

What view of progress does this package presuppose? My rough guess is something cartographic or dialectical rather than convergent: the field thinks it keeps getting better at mapping the space of available positions, the objections to each, and what each one costs you elsewhere, and that recent work sits further along that map, which is why recency works as a proxy for quality. That would let us demand novelty without ever claiming theses get settled. But I want the implicit theory reconstructed properly and separated from what it merely fits with: does the recency norm strictly require a progress claim, or is it consistent with the flat view that nothing accumulates? Is there a version of the practice on which a 1970 paper counts as just as dialectically advanced as a 2020 one? Where exactly is the practice philosophically underdetermined — would a no-progress-at-all view license everything except the recency norm, or does it also undermine the original-contribution requirement and the history/contemporary split? And concretely, what would have to change in how we referee and how we train grad students if the field gave up the progress assumption: would the history/contemporary split dissolve, would we stop treating old work as background, would we still be able to say a paper is outdated? Also tell me whether the two halves — novelty is mandatory, nothing is ever settled — are actually in tension or whether the notion of progress that fits the practice dissolves it.
