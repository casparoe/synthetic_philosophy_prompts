Our department is hiring one person from a shortlist of two: a young philosopher of mind with a speculative, unproven research programme and almost no teaching record, and a senior historian of science with a long record of graduate teaching and committee service but a research profile that has plateaued. When I asked the committee to work out an overall ranking — research promise, teaching, collegiality, weighted somehow — a colleague said that you can't compare apples and oranges, and concluded from this that there is nothing here for a committee to reason about, so we should simply adopt the dean's preference.

Please precisify that slogan. Lay out, as a menu rather than a single analysis, the distinct claims it could be making: about whether the goods involved share any unit of measurement, about whether either candidate can be better than the other at all, about whether they might be neither better nor worse nor equally good, about whether any overall ranking would be constructed by us rather than found, about how much rational slack is left once the evidence is in, about whether the difficulty lies in the values themselves, in our concepts of them, or in our epistemic position. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, substantive and defensible, or false; whether it is a claim about value or about our knowledge of value; and — the part that actually matters for us — whether it would entitle my colleague to "nothing to reason about, defer to the dean."

In particular, separate the thesis that no ranking is there to be discovered from the thesis that no ranking can be justified or defended, and say what each implies about what a committee facing incomparable options should do: defer, flip a coin, deliberate about which standard to construct, or something else. A useful test to apply to each reading is whether a small improvement to one candidate — a slightly better teaching dossier, say — would settle the choice, and what that would show.
