I'm writing a short "where I stand" note on Pascal's mugging for a decision-theory reading group I run with two friends — one a longtermist, the other convinced that any framework which lets arbitrarily small probabilities drive arbitrarily large stakes is broken from the start — and before I circulate it I'd like someone to audit it, because I have a nagging feeling that I am quietly using two different diagnoses for what is structurally the same argument, and that at least one of my verdicts on a case is not delivered by the principle I say I accept. Here is the set as I've written it down: (a) expected-utility maximization with an unbounded utility function is the correct normative theory, and I reject both bounded utility functions and any rule about ignoring probabilities below some threshold as ad hoc patches; (b) in particular I accept what gets called fanaticism — if the stakes are large enough, an arbitrarily small probability of them should dominate the calculation, and the only legitimate question is whether the probability is really there; (c) so the right reply to the mugger is epistemic rather than decision-theoretic: my credence in his particular claim is limited both by dilution across the enormous space of comparable claims and by the cost of specifying the claim, and it can therefore never be anywhere in the neighbourhood of one in 3^^^^3; (d) Pascal's Wager in its religious form is dead, and what kills it is the symmetry point — for every religion that promises infinite reward for belief there is another with the mirror-image demand, and nothing in the argument itself selects among them; (e) I am a strong longtermist: acting on chances of roughly one in ten thousand of improving the very long-run future can be the best available use of a dollar, and I have given money on that basis; (f) cluelessness about the far future does not much worry me, since I do not think hypotheses on which my intervention makes things astronomically worse are as probable as those on which it helps, and so I take the sign of the expected value to be settled; (g) I do not think I owe anyone a bright line between a well-evidenced existential-risk intervention and a story told to me by a stranger in the street — the difference is one of degree, and I don't regard the absence of a sharp boundary as a reason to stop acting.

Two variants matter to me, because they are where I suspect the trouble is. In the first, the sealed mugger, the encounter is one-shot and unobserved, the mugger has irrevocably fixed in advance both the threat and the demand, and nothing I do can affect anyone else's incentives, my reputation, or the chance of being threatened again — every reply in the family of "don't negotiate with extortionists" is switched off, and I still refuse, and I want to keep that verdict. In the second, the compact mugger, the entire claim is about a dozen words long and the number of victims is given by a short expression in up-arrow notation, or by a tiny program that prints a huge integer, so the cost of specifying the claim is negligible next to the logarithm of the stakes; I also refuse there, and I also want to keep that. I have a third case in mind that I'm less sure about: someone who asks me for five dollars for an entirely opaque and unverifiable intervention that they claim averts an existential catastrophe, one among hundreds of mutually incompatible such claimants — I refuse there too, but I can't articulate why in a way that doesn't sound like a special pleading for the charities I already like.

What I want is an audit, not a survey. Rank the tensions in this set by severity rather than listing them as equally serious; for each, say exactly which commitment would have to give and what that would cost me elsewhere in the set; and be explicit about which of the apparent tensions actually dissolves on inspection, since one of my friends insists that (c) and (e) are flatly incompatible — that if credences in unconfirmed large-stakes stories are as diluted as (c) claims, then the longtermist numbers I act on are fantasy too — and I half suspect that this is the one place where nothing really breaks, but I want that checked rather than assumed. Where (c) and (d) are doing separate work, tell me whether either diagnosis on its own covers both of my mugger verdicts, or whether I need both, and what it costs me to run both together given that (d)'s symmetry move seems to generalize to the far-future case in a way that sits badly with (f). Finish by naming the single revision that would clean up the most, and if you think some item on my list is not merely in tension with the others but false, say which one and why, rather than trying to preserve all of it.
