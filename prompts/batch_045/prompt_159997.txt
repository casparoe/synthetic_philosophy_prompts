Apologies if this is more of a thinking-out-loud than a question. I'm rebuilding a grad seminar on naturalizing mental content for next spring — my notes from the last time I taught it are a shambles — and a colleague in philosophy of biology (she works on functions and is very sympathetic to the teleological story) pinned me at a department lunch with a picture that I think is doing more work than she's admitting. The picture is the instrument one: natural selection is the engineer; a trait is a detector the engineer built and calibrated; the state the detector goes into is the reading on the dial; the downstream mechanism that consumes the state is the user who reads the dial; and the etiological story about why the trait is there — its proper function and its "Normal explanation" — is the manual, which fixes both what the reading is supposed to indicate and when the instrument counts as broken. She then said, and that's it, that's the whole naturalization: because a gauge can be stuck, a belief can be false, so misrepresentation comes for free and nothing irreducible is left over.

What I want from you is an audit rather than a verdict. Give me the correspondence explicitly — a table is fine: engineer / instrument / reading / user / manual / recalibration / malfunction / spare part pressed into a new use — and then tell me which rows are honest and which ones quietly import from the artifact side the exact thing the biological side was supposed to earn. My own guesses, which I don't trust: that the engineer row brings in foresight and a standard of "supposed to" that selection just doesn't supply; that the manual row conflates an effect selection historically tracked with a norm the trait can violate; that the user row is doing the real work and can't bear it, because the consumer's own proper function is just more selection history, so error either dissolves into plain causal failure or stays stubbornly normative; and something about the very first mutant — the detector before there was any selection for it — where the artifact picture seems perfectly happy to call it a representation and I can't see what the biological story is supposed to say. Tell me which of those are the same point in different clothes and which are just wrong.

Then the case I've been torturing myself with, because I want to see whether the picture has a fixed answer or whether it hands me back whatever story I happen to tell: a moth has an ultrasound-sensitive neuron B that was selected because it fired in response to a bat species' echolocation and triggered a dive that saved the moth. Those bats go extinct locally. Millenia later a parasitic fly evolves clicks in the same band; moths whose B fires dive; and the divers now outreproduce the non-divers, because the fly is what is eating them. Does B mean bat, bat-ish thing, aerial predator, or fly? Is the old content overwritten, does B now mean two things, or is the old content the only content and the fly case just lucky? Does the analogue of "the engineer recalibrated the gauge" map onto anything here, or does recalibration collapse into just "whatever selection currently maintains"? And a smaller version of the same worry: a detector built to indicate X whose consumer mechanism has since been lost — a dial in a derelict control room — does the artifact picture say it still says X, and is that what I want to say? Last thing, and it's what I actually care about for the seminar: if the instrument analogy can't carry the conclusion she wants, is there a better one, or is the honest thing to tell the students that the analogy was carrying the whole naturalization?
