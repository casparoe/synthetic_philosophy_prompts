Don't give me a survey of the literature, no bibliography, no citations, and please don't just list the positions back at me — I've read all of it.

Here's my situation. I've been running a reading group on cognitive penetration of perception and I'm trying to write a short critical overview of it, and I've become convinced the debate has stalled in a specific, annoying way. The two sides:

Pro: what a subject believes, wants, fears or expects can change what her visual experience is like, not just what she says or judges. Their stock cases: perceptual learning / expertise cases (the trained birder, the radiologist, the trained nose — after learning, things literally look different); belief-and-affect cases (you hear nasty gossip about someone, and their face now looks angry to you); need- and desire-driven cases (thirsty subjects report the water as closer, hills are reported as steeper when you're tired or carrying a heavy pack). And the theoretical payload they attach to it: if experience is penetrated, then experience can inherit the rationality of the penetrating states, which is supposed to be epistemically bad — perception loses its special role as a source of justification.

Anti: no it can't, or at least nothing here shows it can. Their moves: Fodor/Pylyshyn-style informational encapsulation of early vision; "that's attention, not perception"; "that's a judgment, a memory effect or a response bias read off a verbal report"; "what you've shown is diachronic — cognition changed the wiring over time — which everybody already grants and which isn't the interesting claim; the interesting claim is synchronic, online penetration"; plus a big review-style paper arguing that most alleged top-down effects are artifacts of how the experiments were run, and ordinary replication worries about the motivational and social ones.

Nobody moves. Both sides know all the cases and all the replies. So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually?

My own hypothesis, which I want you to test rather than agree with: it isn't a dispute about perception at all, it's a methodological dispute about what counts as a datum. The enthusiasts treat first-personal "it looks different now, and it kept looking different after I found out the gossip was false" style reports as evidence about experience; the skeptics quietly require something that dissociates experience from judgment, attention and response bias (psychophysical signatures, aftereffect transfer, neural markers) and treat introspective reports as non-evidence, on the ground that introspection can't distinguish a change in what you see from a change in what you spontaneously judge, attend to, or are prepared to say. I also keep hearing a different verdict — that it's all a "border dispute", a fight about where to draw the perception/cognition line with no fact of the matter to settle it, so it's verbal. Both of those feel partly right and partly like a way of changing the subject, and I can't tell which part is which.

Here's the thing that pushed me toward the methodological diagnosis, and I'd like you to chew on it specifically. I tried to design a test that would separate the competing explanations. Call it the reversal test: if a case is genuine online penetration, then removing the penetrating state should remove the effect within seconds (tell the subject the gossip was false, or that the person in the photo is someone else), whereas if it's diachronic rewiring from learning the effect should persist. But then I noticed the test doesn't discriminate anything, because attention also flips within seconds, and so does any post-perceptual reinterpretation. And I couldn't come up with any behavioural design that does discriminate: for any pattern of reports, an attention story, a judgment story and a genuine-penetration story can be made to fit. Maybe that's just my failure of imagination, or maybe it's the real structure of the dialectic, in which case the yes/no question is under-determined by any evidence we could in principle get and the whole thing is ill-posed.
