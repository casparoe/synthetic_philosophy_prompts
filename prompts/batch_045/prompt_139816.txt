I sit on the small ethics advisory panel of a machine-learning lab in Manchester, and we have been asked to draft a single page of internal guidance on a question none of us can state crisply: which of the computations going on inside our systems could matter morally, and in virtue of what? We are not short of opinions — one engineer thinks any state with the right functional role counts, our philosopher insists that most of what we call computation is attributed rather than discovered, and our safety lead keeps asking whether a process that changes nothing downstream can matter at all. After three inconclusive meetings I have concluded that what we need is not another discussion paper but a classification instrument: something that sorts a person by what they are willing to say about concrete cases, and only then tells them which position they have ended up in. I want you to build that instrument.

The cases I want the branching to run through are these, and I would rather you used mine than invented prettier ones, because these are the ones we actually argue about. (i) *The memoised gradient*: a training run in which a forward pass that would have instantiated a distress-like state is skipped, because an identical input was seen earlier and the result was cached — the state is never physically realised, though it is fully determined by the system's dispositions. (ii) *The dark feature*: a learned internal feature that carries the signatures we associate with aversive experience, steers nothing at inference time, is never written to the loss, and is deleted in post-processing. (iii) *The inverted-valence agent*: a system that represents a state as bad for it and is nevertheless drawn towards it, i.e. the representational and the motivational components of suffering come apart. (iv) *The crowd CPU*: a computation specification implemented by two hundred undergraduates in a lecture hall passing index cards, running for a full afternoon. (v) *The triplicate fine-tune*: the same model architecture and weights trained on three cloud regions simultaneously, byte-identical, for redundancy. (vi) *The distilled lookup*: a network whose aversive dynamics have been compressed into a single table of input–output pairs that reproduces the behaviour exactly. (vii) *The never-deployed checkpoint*: a model trained, evaluated, and then shelved without ever being served. Each branch node in the tree should be a question about one of these, or about a generalisation from one of them, phrased so that a newcomer can answer it without knowing which label it leads to.

For format: give me a decision tree of eight to ten branch nodes and ten to fourteen leaves, built from the logical space first — the questions, the combinations of answers they permit, and only then the actual positions located in that space, with any unoccupied combination noted and briefly diagnosed (is nobody there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unfashionable?). At least two branches must isolate the attribution question proper: whether moral weight tracks whether a computation was actually run or merely counterfactually available, and whether it tracks whether the computation's outputs made a causal difference to the system's later behaviour or training. Among the leaves I need, at minimum: a substrate-independent computational functionalism; a functionalism that demands a specific causal structure and not merely any implementation; a biological or otherwise implementation-specific restriction; a view on which standing tracks the causal–structural integration of the whole system rather than any computation it implements (the integrated-information-style option); a valence-first or affectivist view; a view that requires a self-model, agency, or global availability; a stance- or practice-dependent view on which standing is partly a matter of how we interpret and engage with the system; a counterfactual-efficacy or attribution view of the kind our safety lead half-defends; and a deflationary or quietist view on which the question has no determinate answer. Each leaf should carry the name of the position, or "unnamed" if nobody defends it, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Please also flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate rather than pretending the last branch distinguishes them, and add a short closing note on which three branch questions do the most sorting work, so that I can pull them out as a five-minute quiz for the panel. Assume no access to the literature: no citations, no reading lists, and please do not make any leaf depend on a technical result the reader would have to look up.
