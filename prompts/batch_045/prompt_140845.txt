I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've been hanging around a philanthropic fund that allocates by expected value per dollar — the kind of place where someone will say, with a straight face, that a 1-in-10,000 shot at preventing a civilization-ending catastrophe beats a program that definately saves ten thousand lives next year, because the expected number of bodies is bigger. What I can't get anyone there to explain is where they stop. The same fund routes real money toward long-shot catastrophic-risk work, but it will not hand five dollars to a stranger who walks in claiming he'll save 10^30 lives with his mind if they just pay him; nobody even feels bad about that, and when I press them they mutter something about their probability estimates "not being real" down at that scale. So the practice isn't plain expected-value maximization, whatever their website says, and I want the implicit theory reconstructed: what rule are they actually following, and does it secretly presuppose fanaticism — that a tiny enough probability of a big enough payoff swamps every ordinary consideration — or has it quietly rejected fanaticism while keeping the vocabulary?

I've been using a made-up distinction in my notes and I want you to test it rather than humor it. Call it the calibration floor: the smallest probability a funder is willing to act on, because below some threshold their own track record says their number is noise rather than evidence, so the mugger's one-in-10^30 isn't a very low probability but no probability at all, whereas the catastrophe estimate is a real one. Is that a principled line or just relabeling the discomfort? Relatedly, does any of this need the law of large numbers — the usual "in the long run EV wins" story — given that neither the catastrophe case nor the mugger case will ever be repeated enough times for that story to apply? And does the practice strictly presuppose that value aggregates across people, that saving 10^12 lives is 10^12 times as good as saving one, or would a view that refuses to aggregate license the same allocations for different reasons?

Give me exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences (three at the outside), no intro, no conclusion, no headings, in American English: (1) the decision rule the practice is really running on, stated so I could read it back to the fund's board; (2) the single commitment it cannot drop without becoming unintelligible, with the case that shows it; (3) one commitment everyone assumes the practice has that it strictly doesn't need — tell me which of the things people throw at expected-value reasoning is really just a prejudice of one camp; (4) whether my calibration floor separates the catastrophe case from the mugger case or collapses under pressure, and if it collapses, what actually does the work instead; (5) what would concretely change in their funding meetings if they swapped the implicit theory for a named rival — ambiguity aversion, bounded value, satisficing, whatever fits. Don't cite papers or name authors; I can't check them and my editor cuts them anyway. And please don't use item (1) to tell me that EV maximization implies fanaticism — I know that much, I want to know what this practice implies.
