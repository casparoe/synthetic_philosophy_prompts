I teach a grad seminar on philosophical theology and I've been slowly writing up a short paper — I'd like it to turn into a journal article eventually — that tries to open up a worry for Molinism that I don't think is in the literature in quite this form. My colleague down the hall, who does decision theory rather than religion, says the central move is just the old complaint that middle knowledge is "merely" a bookkeeping device for the actualization of a world, and I cant tell whether she's defintely right or whether I've got something slightly different. Before I circulate the thing to students I want someone to tear into it. Here are my notes, in the order I'm planning to run them.

(1) Setup. Middle knowledge — God's knowledge of what any possible creature would freely do in any circumstance in which it might be placed — is meant to buy providential control over history without overriding freedom: God picks the circumstances, the creature supplies the free response, and the pair is known in advance.
(2) Distinguish thin counterfactuals of freedom (if S were in circumstance C at t, S would freely do A) from thick ones (if S were in C at t, S would freely do A, and given that, T would freely do B in the resulting circumstance D, and so on down the line). Providence, I argue, needs the thick ones: you can't steer a history with the thin ones.
(3) The pinch. Thick counterfactuals can't be assembled by iterating thin ones, because the circumstance D in which T acts is itself counterfactually dependent on S's free act, so the antecedent of the later counterfactual embeds the truth of an earlier counterfactual. Obvious Molinist reply: God doesn't assemble anything step by step; He knows complete feasible world-profiles at once, all the counterfactuals together.
(4) My "collapse" charge. Once that's the resource, middle knowledge is doing no distinctive work: what you have is knowledge of which complete world to actualize, and the Molinist machinery of circumstance-plus-counterfactual-plus-actualization is idle decoration. Molinism quietly becomes the view it was invented to improve on.
(5) Second worry, which I think is separable. If the choice is over whole feasible worlds, you inherit the no-best-world problem: for any feasible world there may be a better one, or a merely incommensurable one, with no maximum to select. Then God's selection is either arbitrary or it satisfies a threshold. Call it the providential satisficing problem: Molinism needs either a maximizing structure over feasible worlds or a satisficing threshold, and I argue both cost the Molinist something she wanted — the first buys a kind of moral bookkeeping she denied, the second makes the actual world's amount of evil look unprincipled.
(6) An out I toy with and then half-reject: impose a lexical ordering keyed to one dominant good, which restores a maximum but, I suggest, reproduces the shape of the old transworld-depravity style reasoning, so it isn't really available to someone who liked Molinism for independent reasons.

What I'd like from you: comments keyed to moves (3) through (6), under 600 words total, numbered, no preamble. For each, tell me whether the move is actually arguing or just relabeling something familiar; give me the strongest reply a Molinist would make to the collapse charge in (4), phrased as she would phrase it; tell me honestly whether (5) is new or the no-best-world argument in a costume; and end with one concrete suggestion for restructuring — should (5) come first and (4) become a footnote, should I drop (6), or should the whole thing be recast as an argument about what "control" has to mean for providence to be worth wanting?
