I did just over thirty years as a government fisheries biologist, and since retiring I've been volunteering as the verification coordinator for our regional bird atlas — which means I'm the person who looks at what three hundred-odd citizen volunteers send in and decides what gets entered as a record, and at what confidence level. In the last couple of years I've also started reading philosophy, mostly out of stubborness, and I've got properly stuck on that raven example: someone pointed out that a white shoe is evidence that all ravens are black, because saying all ravens are black is apparently the same claim as saying everything that isn't black isn't a raven. My volunteers think this is a joke and I'm not sure it is, and it matters to me practically, because a fair number of the things the atlas is supposed to be able to say have exactly that shape: no pale-morph ravens occur in this region, every nest of species X in the county is on a cliff, all the individuals in the island population are the dark morph.

So here's what I want. A decision procedure my verification team could actually run on a single submitted record, to say how much evidential weight it gets for a generalization of that all-X-are-Y shape, and where to stop with a no. Not a philosophy essay — I need something a retired volunteer with a field guide and a laptop can work through in ten minutes, thirty to sixty times a week in spring, with no maths past a ratio, and with an explicit instruction about what to do when the number it wants can't be had. It has to hand the decision to a human being at the points where the question is really about what we're willing to stake rather than about evidence, and say so out loud rather than dressing it up as a rule. And it has to be honest about which steps are judgment calls.

The hard cases I keep hitting, which the procedure has to actually deal with rather than wave at:

One volunteer has genuinely submitted a spreadsheet of three thousand photographs of non-black objects — shoes, sheep, a grey heron, a cloud — and wants them entered as supporting records. Another has proposed we redeploy volunteers to go out and photograph white things instead of birds, on the grounds that it's cheaper.

The atlas currently gives "negative" records — I looked at point 47 for an hour and saw no ravens — zero weight, and archives them, while at the same time treating forty years of nobody ever reporting a pale raven as real evidence that there aren't any. That looks inconsistent to me and I want to know whether it is.

Dependence: fourteen people photograph what is almost certainly the same bird at the same landfill. The same bird is reported forty times across a season. And a bird described as all black in June is photographed pale in November, which might be molt, or age, or wear, or a different individual entirely.

Definitions: our field guide separates ravens from crows partly on size and tail shape and partly on the bird being all black. If being all black is part of how you identify a raven, then a pale bird can't be a raven by definition, and it feels like the generalization has become unfalsifiable and every weight my team assigns is meaningless. I want a step that catches this before we waste anyone's time.

Closed versus open populations: on one offshore island the raven population is colour-banded and thought to be around two hundred birds; on the mainland nothing is closed and nobody knows. Should the procedure branch there?

And lab results: a student sends genotype data showing the dark-morph allele in twelve sampled birds. Where does that sit on the same scale as a photograph, if it sits on it at all?

Then run it on three cases. First, a single pale raven photographed at a landfill in November by one observer, mediocre photo, reportedly black in June, no specimen, species identification contested by two committee members, and a committee rule that wants two independent observers or a specimen. Second, the three thousand non-black objects. Third, nine thousand black-raven checklist records accumulated over forty years — and give me the honest answer on that one even if it comes out that we should stop collecting them, because I suspect it might, and I'd rather hear it plain than have it softened.

Finally, tell me what the procedure can't do. My guess is that it can only ever rate a record against one hypothesis at a time and has nothing to say about what that same record is worth to the atlas for distribution or population trend, but you tell me. I've no library access any more, so please don't send me off to read somebody — just argue for the procedure you give me.
