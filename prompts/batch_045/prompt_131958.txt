I sit on the acquisitions and display committee of a small museum. We own a seventeenth-century panel painting in poor condition: much of the original paint layer is gone, and what a visitor actually sees is largely the work of nineteenth-century restorers. A conservator has proposed exhibiting a technically flawless replica instead, made by a specialist using period materials and techniques and indistinguishable from the panel by any visitor, and moving the panel into storage on the grounds that the experience we offer the public would be unchanged while the object itself would be preserved.

I want to object that this would be cheating the public: the original has something the replica lacks, and putting the replica in its frame exploits that fact. But I cannot say what the something is. It is not a property anyone can see, since by hypothesis the two are visually identical; and if I say that it is merely provenance, that this one was made by the named painter, then I seem to be valuing a pedigree rather than a picture, which looks like philistinism of another kind.

So: what does the original have that the replica does not, and in virtue of what could that difference matter to the experience or the value of the work, and not only to its price or its place in art history? And does the condition of our panel undermine the answer — if what matters is physical continuity with the painter's hand, have we already lost the original to the restorers, and if not, why not?
