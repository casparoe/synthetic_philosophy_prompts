I convene a graduate workshop in philosophy — 16 participants, mostly epistemologists and a few political theorists, almost none of whom have read anything about machine learning — and I have 90 minutes in which I want them to acquire a felt understanding of what debate is supposed to buy you as an oversight method, and of what that hope rests on. Design a runnable activity for this group.

The skeleton I have in mind, which you should replace if you can do better: a sealed box of roughly 40 numbered index-card witness statements concerning one incident; the facilitator alone knows the truth of a yes-or-no question the cards jointly settle; two debaters have read every card, the judge has read none, and the judge may have individual cards unsealed for them at a cost drawn from a small budget of unsealings. Play for chips or counters, no real money, and state explicitly what each debater and the judge are playing for in each round and how that differs between rounds.

What the sequence must do, without anyone's naming it in advance — the phrase scalable oversight should not be spoken until the debrief: first have participants find out for themselves that the more fluent advocate wins irrespective of who is right; then let them find out that forcing claims downward into sub-claims the judge can check shifts both who wins and how often the judge is right; then sabotage that repair in a final round by making available a class of claims the judge has no way to check, so that the group feels the mechanism fail rather than being told where it fails. Give me the rules and materials, the timeline for three rounds plus debrief, and the ground rule that changes between each round.

The debrief should map particular moments onto particular claims about the method, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — including what to do with a participant who refuses to argue the side they were assigned, and with a judge who simply defers to the confident speaker or wastes the unsealing budget on irrelevant cards — and be frank about which feature of the real oversight problem the game cannot reproduce at all and I will therefore have to state myself.

Finally: name the single design choice you are least confident will actually produce the experience I am after, and say what you would observe during the session that would convince you that choice was a mistake and should be reversed or dropped.
