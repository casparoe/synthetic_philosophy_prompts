I sit on a small university committee that has been asked to write the discounting rule for a new "temporal impact statement": every capital project above a threshold has to be costed over a two-hundred-year horizon at an explicit social discount rate, and the committee has to say what that rate is and why. We keep talking past each other, so I've agreed to run a one-hour workshop for the policy graduate students attached to the committee, and what I want from you is the handout — a decision tree, not a literature review. The tree should start inside the debate rather than at the door: assume we already accept that some far-future benefits are worth paying for, that money not spent now can be invested at some positive real return, and that future people's welfare counts in the first place. Then take a student from there to a named position on what, if anything, is being discounted. Every branch must be a concrete case with two or three answer options, phrased so that someone can answer without knowing which view it leads to — no node may simply ask "does the future matter less?" or "what rate do you favour?" Please make the tree self-contained: no citations, no reading lists, no "see so-and-so's paper," since the point is to sort people by what they say about cases. Also, if you aren't reasonably sure a leaf has a standard label in the literature, call it "unnamed" and describe it in a line; a confidently wrong name is worse than no name.

Give me eight branch questions and nine to eleven leaves. Among the cases I want used: a sea wall whose entire benefit lands in year two hundred; the same sea wall when we are told there is a substantial chance no beneficiaries will exist, which I want split into two sub-questions — whether you would rather record that as a discount or as a probability, and whether your answer changes if the project itself reduces that chance; a case in which you are stipulated to know that the beneficiaries will exist, be identifiable, and be no richer than we are; a case that asks whether the gap between years 100 and 101 is as large as the gap between years 0 and 1, to separate constant from declining rates; an intrapersonal case about a person's own future discomfort, to separate prudential discounting from discounting across persons; a backward-looking case — compensation owed for a seizure of land three centuries ago versus one five years ago — since I suspect a real time preference should apply to past harms too and a mere hazard rate shouldn't; a variable-population case where part of the benefit is that additional worthwhile lives come to exist; and, as the last branch, the worry that a zero rate together with optimistic growth apparently demands absurd saving from the present, which is where a criterion refusing both the dictatorship of the present and the dictatorship of the future should become available. The leaves I need present are: no discounting of any kind; zero pure time preference with a positive social rate derived only from growth, inequality aversion or hazard; a positive pure rate defended as an ethical judgement about temporal position; a positive rate defended as deference to actual or revealed preferences; a declining-rate view; the view that the appearance of discounting is entirely survival or institutional risk and therefore probability rather than preference; discounting within a life but not between persons; and one leaf built on a distinction I've been pushing in committee and can't get anyone to engage with — that we might discount the *stringency of a claim* rather than the *magnitude of a good* — so that, say, a promise made now to monitor a waste repository for a distant cohort binds more tightly than the size of the benefit to that cohort would explain, even though the benefit itself is not worth less. Each leaf should carry one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Then note at least three combinations of answers your tree permits but nobody occupies, with a one-line diagnosis of why (incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored), and flag at least two pairs of leaves your tree cannot separate, saying which further case would separate them or why none could.

For what it's worth, here is where I think I land, and I want the tree to place me rather than flatter me: I refuse to discount once I'm told the beneficiaries certainly exist, are identifiable, and will be no better off than us; I do want a much lower rate for the year-two-hundred sea wall than a constant rate would give, mainly because I think our confidence about the far future decays; I'm comfortable discounting my own discomfort next year against my discomfort today but uneasy doing that to a stranger's; I think promises to future generations bind more tightly than the benefit size alone explains; I don't discount harms that lie in the past; and yet I also think it would be grotesque to demand that this generation save at the rate a zero discount rate with optimistic growth seems to imply. If my combination doesn't reach a leaf, tell me which node it breaks on instead of smoothing it over. At each branch add a brief note on which answer you think is genuinely best defended, and at the end say what would move you off both the leaf you've put me in and the leaf you'd recommend to the committee: which argument — the divergent-sums worry, the appeal to actual preferences, the case for treating hazard rates as probabilities rather than preferences, the thought that a rate applied to past harms is pure temporal chauvinism — or which empirical finding about long-run growth or institutional survival would do it, and where your own confidence in the tree is lowest. Finish with a single machine-readable line containing the label of the leaf my answers land on, exactly as you named it, or "unclassifiable" together with the number of the node where my combination breaks: \answer{...}
