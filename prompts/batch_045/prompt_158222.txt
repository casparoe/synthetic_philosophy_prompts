I'm retired — I was an atmospheric physicist for most of my working life — and I've somehow ended up as the handling editor who decides what authors are allowed to claim in their abstracts. I came to philosophy late, via philosophy of science, and I've been reading around verisimilitude. As far as I can make out, Popper's original version (roughly: true consequences minus false ones) was shot down in 1974 — the upshot being, if I've got this right, that no false theory can be closer to the truth than another, which is awkward given that most of our models are strictly speaking false — and the replacements that came afterwards don't agree with each other. Meanwhile authors and referees say "closer to the truth" constantly, and my editor-in-chief's only rule is that we don't let anyone claim anything we can't defend.

So what I want is a procedure my referees can actually run. They're unpaid, they've got maybe fifteen minutes, none of them has any philosophy, and whatever I hand them has to fit in a box on the referee form and survive being read by a managing editor who wont read the literature.

My own untrained idea is a four-anchor test: no verisimilitude claim gets through unless it names the target (what is it a model of), the aspect (which quantities, or which bit of structure, is supposed to be closer), the metric (how close, in units we can actually compute), and the comparator (closer than what, exactly). If an anchor can't be filled in, the claim gets downgraded to something weaker — empirical adequacy on a named battery of tests, say.

Give me the questions in the order I should put them, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure just stops with a no, and — importantly — where it has to hand the decision back to a human because it's a judgement call rather than a matter of fact. Then run it on three cases, from my side of the field: (1) a successor atmospheric model, more parameters, beats the old one on every benchmark we have, authors want "closer to the truth" in the abstract; (2) two dose–response models in a pharmacology paper, one roughly gets the mechanism right and fits badly, the other is mechanistically nonsense and fits beautifully; (3) a review article claiming that a two-parameter cloud scheme from my own old group is closer to the truth than the thirty-parameter machine-learned thing that replaced it — I'd like that one to pass and I suspect your procedure will tell me it can't, so tell me straight. And say plainly where the whole thing misfires.
