hey — I'm putting together one session of a seminar where each week we read a film against a thinker, and I want to do Whiplash (the Chazelle one, JK Simmons as the conservatory bandleader, Miles Teller as the drummer) against Foucault on discipline. I really don't want the lazy version where discipline just means strict teaching and the takeaway is that the teacher was a bully. I want someone to actually run the machinery: the timetable and the endless repetition, the permanent ranking for first chair, the body that gets drilled until the hands bleed, the way the standard you're judged against is never something you could check for yourself.

The bit I keep circling is what I've started calling the tempo trap. Fletcher keeps stopping the band on the same passage and telling the kid he's either rushing or dragging, and there's no procedure by which the student could settle the question — the guy running the room hears it and that's the end of it. My thought is that this, not the shouting, is the actual engine of the place. Power makes a kind of knowledge here (who's in tempo, who has earned the chair) that can't be prised apart from who's entitled to say so, and the upshot is that the student starts doing the policing himself. Does that survive as a Foucaultian reading, or is it just cruelty with fancy vocabulary? I'd like you to say where exactly the line falls between this scene shows disciplinary power/knowledge and this scene shows a grown man abusing students, because I can't tell when I've crossed it.

Second thing, the ending. Neiman goes back onstage after Fletcher has set him up to fail, plays through it, and his dad is standing in the wings watching it happen — and the film scores the whole thing as triumph. I want to hold two incompatible readings at once. First: this is the finished product, the subject who no longer needs anyone standing over him, discipline has manufactured someone who is his own supervisor, and the celebration is the trap closing. Second: I have a vague memory of Foucault having some term for conduct that gets steered away from the direction it's being given without being a straight-up rebellion or a revolution — counter-conduct? something like that? tell me if I've mixed that up with something else — and the solo looks more like that than like submission. Which reading is more defensible, and is there a way the film is genuinely both?

Also, and I think this is the part that matters most for teaching it: which single scene would you put in front of a room to make disciplinary power visible, and which scene is the one where the reading stops working and you'd have to admit the movie is doing something Foucault can't parse? My worry is that the swing, the sweat, the whole romance of the solo keeps dragging us back toward an individual-genius story, and the dinner-table speech where the conductor says the worst thing you can tell a young player is that they did well sort of invites that story too. I'd rather have the places where it doesn't fit than a clean reading.
