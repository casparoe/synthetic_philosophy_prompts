I'm the TA for an upper-division seminar on formal epistemology — fourteen students, one 80-minute block, and they can all crank Bayes' theorem but have not yet met any update rule for evidence that arrives uncertain. The topic became personal a few weeks ago: my dog had an inconclusive ultrasound, and the vet told me she was about 60% on the mass being benign. Nothing in that conversation made me certain of any proposition; I did not learn a proposition so much as have my confidence moved. Then a second, fuzzier signal arrived — a borderline lab value from an assay the lab later said had been drifting — and I noticed I reached a different final number depending on which of the two I folded in first, which made me suspect that the rule I'd been teaching is less well-behaved than I'd implied. I'd like to build a session around exactly that experience.

What I want is a runnable activity, not a handout: rules, materials I can carry into an ordinary seminar room, a payoff or scoring scheme that needs no money (chips, quiz points, or a strictly proper score on their own reported numbers), and a timeline with minute marks. My students should run into three things by doing them rather than by being told them. First: that when the shift in confidence comes from a source with a known error rate, they can reach the same answer by ordinary conditionalization on the experience itself, and the kinematics rule with its rigidity condition looks like unnecessary machinery — whereas when the shift is brute, with no proposition they became certain of and no likelihoods to lean on, there is nothing for ordinary conditionalization to bite on and the extra rule is not redundant at all. Second: that the assumption doing the real work — that the conditional probability of the hypothesis on each cell of the evidence partition stays put while the cell probabilities move — can fail, in particular when the signal also tells you something about how trustworthy the channel that produced it is. Third: that two successive uncertain shifts applied in opposite orders generally give different answers, while two pieces of evidence you become certain of do not; I want that discovered by having groups start from a common prior, receive the same two nudges in opposite orders, and compare final numbers across tables.

If there is room, I'd also like one round where the uncertain evidence spreads credence across three cells rather than two, so that holding conditional probabilities fixed on the coarse partition yields a different result from holding them fixed on the finer one — I want them to feel that the choice of partition is a substantive commitment and not a bookkeeping detail. Please include a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto specific pieces of the update rule: which round established that the rule is not redundant, which round broke the fixedness assumption and why that is a failure of applicability rather than a slip in the arithmetic. Add an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — students who refuse to commit to a number, students who quietly re-derive the priors instead of updating, groups that talk across tables and destroy the order comparison — and say how to handle each. And be frank about which part of the problem the activity cannot stage and which I will have to supply in closing myself, since I suspect the genuinely normative question — whether holding those conditional probabilities fixed is something rationality requires of you, or merely the least-bad thing to assume when you have nothing better — is not something any arrangement of nudges and payoffs can settle. Please write in American English, and skip citations, references, and suggested readings entirely; I have those already, and what I need is something I can actually run on Tuesday.
