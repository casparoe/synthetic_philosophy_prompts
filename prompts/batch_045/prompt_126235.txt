I keep going back and forth on this one and I'd rather outsource it than keep spinning.

I teach a seminar on rules, games and self-reference, and for the first three weeks we play a version of Nomic — Suber's game, with the mutable/immutable rule distinction and the whole business of amending the amendment rule. A colleague in the law school, who's been advising some constitutional-reform commission on the side and who has a very breezy line about all of it, keeps telling me that self-amendment isn't a real puzzle at all. The analogy as he runs it: a legal order is a ship being rebuilt plank by plank on the open sea, never in dry dock. So (i) nothing outside the system is needed to ground it — no sovereign or basic norm standing on the shore — and (ii) once a system has a rule for changing its rules, the rest is engineering: the paradoxes people make a fuss over are bad drafting, not conceptual trouble. He uses it to argue that "unamendable core" talk (the basic structure idea in Indian constitutional law, the eternity clause in the German Basic Law, that bit in Article V about equal suffrage in the Senate) is politics wearing a logic costume.

I want the audit rather than the vibe. So:

Lay out the mapping explicitly, a table is fine. What plays the plank, what plays the keel or the amendment rule, what plays the sea, buoyancy, seaworthiness, the sailor, the dry dock, the carpentry? Be explicit about what in the legal case corresponds to the sailor, since that's the bit I think he slides over.

Then where it breaks — and please seperate the disanalogies that matter for the use he's making of it from the ones that are just decoration. My own suspicion is that the load-bearing one is about success and error conditions: a bad plank swap shows up as water coming in, which is a fact about the world outside the boat, whereas whether a self-amendment succeeded is settled by the system's own rules, and a rule can even purport to make itself valid retroactively. Tell me if I've got the emphasis wrong.

Three cases I actually care about, all of which came up in the game or in the reading:

One, mutual entrenchment. Rule A says "B may not be amended or repealed," and rule B says the same about A. Under a one-amendment-at-a-time rule the pair looks absolutely entrenched; under a rule that permits package amendments it's trivial to remove. So the strength of an entrenchment seems to be a function of how fine-grained the amendment procedure is, which I don't think the boat can represent at all, since planks don't protect each other.

Two, the amendment power amending itself into rigidity — repealing itself outright, or setting the threshhold at something unreachable. Now the ship can't be rebuilt and can't be dry-docked either.

Three, retroactive self-validation: an amendment that says, in effect, "I count as validly enacted whatever the threshold was at the time I was passed." Is that a rule or a wish? Does it matter that it was passed by a mere majority under the old rules?

For each of those three, does the boat picture have a translation, or does it just go quiet?

Then: does his conclusion survive? In particular, does the analogy quietly import an external ground — the sea, buoyancy, the sailor's survival — that is doing exactly the work a basic norm would do, so that "no foundation needed" is the one thing the picture can't support?

And if the boat is the wrong picture, what's better? I've tried "a codebase whose bootloader is itself version-controlled" and "a rulebook with an errata section that can rewrite the errata rules," and both feel like they inherit the same problem one level down.

Give me something I could hand to a sharp undergrad without embarrassment. American English please — I'm pasting chunks of this into the week-four handout and I don't want to spend an hour de-Britishing it. Around 1200 words.
