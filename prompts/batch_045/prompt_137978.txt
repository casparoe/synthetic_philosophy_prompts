Hey — I help run a monthly philosophy meetup in the back room of a pub in Leeds, and the next session is on the doomsday argument. Half the room are programmers who've read too much online, the other half are friends of friends who came for the beer, and both groups tend to leave thinking everyone else has missed something obvious. So what I want isn't another explainer: I want a sorting quiz, the "which kind of anthropic reasoner are you actually?" sort, that I can print as a single page and hand out before anyone starts talking. Nine or ten questions, each one a concrete little case with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto positions. The hard rule is that every question has to be something a person who has never heard the jargon can answer on gut feel — no "do you accept the self-sampling assumption?", no asking people to rank how much they trust a principle — so the quiz sorts people by what they say about cases rather than by which label they'd claim. Please build it from the logical space instead of from a list of famous views: start from the diagnostic questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then place the actual positions into the boxes that are occupied, and for the empty boxes tell me in a line why nobody lives there (incoherent? just unattractive? merely unworked-out?). Leaves I want reachable, roughly: the classic shifter who takes an early birth rank as real evidence that the total is small and keeps the shift; the canceler who thinks the fact that you exist at all is itself evidence for a big total and that this washes the shift out exactly; the reference-class relativist who thinks you can get almost any answer depending on who you count as a possible "you" and that nothing selects between them, so the thing dissolves; the infinitary escapist who says the argument never gets going because the expected number of observers is infinite; the "real but inert" person who grants the probability shift but thinks it cancels in expected-utility terms and so shouldn't move any money; and the skeptic who thinks you can't update on your own existence at all, so there's nothing here to have a view about. I'd also like the duration-sampling version — where instead of your birth rank you use your position in the lifetime of a thing to guess how long it will last, the way you'd guess how many more episodes a show will run — treated as a genuine leaf or sub-branch rather than as a footnote.

Three cases I really want in there, because they're the ones my group actually argues about and I think they separate things the standard examples don't. First, the Upload Census: suppose that from roughly 2200 onward almost all minds are running on hardware rather than biology, and that in any given calendar year they vastly outnumber biological humans, but in whole-lives-ever-lived terms they don't (or do — make the case so the respondent has to bite a bullet about whether they're counting per year or per life). Ask whether the respondent's birth rank is measured among biologicals, among all minds, and what happens if they find out they're biological. Second, the Funding Committee: a small foundation with a fixed pot is choosing between work on reducing extinction risk and a near-certain near-term cause; the doomsday-style shift makes doom more likely, which makes the extinction work look more urgent, but it also lowers the expected number of people who'd be saved by it, and I want a question that forces people to say whether that pulls the money toward the extinction work, away from it, or nowhere. Third, a pair of cases about extra observers: in one, a committee the respondent sits on is deciding whether to create a huge population that will exist after them and never interact with them; in the other, the same extra population is on a sealed-off branch they could never have been part of. My hope is that this pair distinguishes reasoning from "there are many observers" from reasoning about "many observers I could have been", but if it doesn't, tell me what would. Please also flag any two leaves your quiz genuinely cannot separate, and say what extra question would split them even though there's no room for it on the page.

Format thing, sorry to be fussy: continuous prose only, please, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — it's going to be pasted into an email to the group and then photocopied, and headings just get lost anyway. So write the tree as running text with branch points marked inline like "Q4" and transitions written out as "if you picked (b), go to Q7", and give me the scoring key as prose paragraphs keyed to answer patterns (something like: "anyone who answered (a) to Q2 and (c) to Q6 lands in ... because ..."). And no reading list or citations on the page — we do this offline and half the room hasn't read anything, so it's worse than useless to me. If any of my must-have leaves turn out not to be separable from each other by cases at all, say so plainly rather than inventing a question that secretly tips people off to which box they're heading for — I'd rather have eight honest questions than twelve that do the sorting behind the scenes.
