I sit on the Deployment Authorization Committee of a national AI safety institute. We meet in two weeks and must issue a written decision on one application. I need you to draft the committee opinion I will circulate in advance.

The applicant, Northgate Financial Services, seeks authorization to deploy an agentic language-model system ("Kestrel-3") for twelve months as the primary handler of inbound retail-banking customer service: roughly 40,000 tasks per day. Via tools, Kestrel-3 can read account records; issue refunds and goodwill credits up to $5,000; update contact details and stored payment methods; open, escalate, and close disputes; send written notices to customers; and file reports with an external credit reporting agency. It decides for itself which cases to escalate to a human. About two percent of completed tasks are sampled for retrospective human QA; the remainder are unsupervised.

The submitted safety case rests on three claims. First, measured performance: in red-teaming and in a six-week shadow-mode pilot, 1.1 policy-violating actions per 10,000 tasks, of which 96 percent were detected within 24 hours, at mean remediation cost of about $140 per incident — modeled annual loss of roughly $2.2 million against roughly $31 million in projected savings. Second, a distributional claim: the deployment distribution is close enough to the pilot distribution because the customer population, product set, and task taxonomy are the same. Third, a methodological claim: worst-case analysis is unachievable for a system whose outputs are open-ended natural language, and demanding it would disqualify every candidate system, so the committee should accept a probabilistic, average-case safety case backed by layered monitoring.

Two members have staked out positions, and both are strong.

Member A (formal methods) argues that an average-case guarantee is a statement about a distribution, and that we do not have the distribution. Three forces move it, and none is exogenous: Kestrel-3's own triage decides which cases fall inside its action set and which get escalated, so the population the rate is measured over is partly selected by the system being assessed; customers adapt their phrasing once word spreads about what gets refunded; and fraudsters deliberately search for inputs that exploit it. The measured rate is therefore an average over a set the system partly chooses. Separately, A notes that the 1.1-per-10,000 figure is a rate of detected failures: a payment-method change that the customer never notices, or a credit filing that is inaccurate but plausible, generates no signal, so the evidence samples the observable subset of a larger class. A concludes that we should deny, or authorize only within a bounded action space in which worst-case analysis is actually tractable.

Member B (formerly an ML lead in industry) argues that A's demand is vacuous. For any system with open-ended outputs and broad tool access, one can construct a catastrophic worst case by hand; a criterion that every candidate fails has no power to discriminate between better and worse designs, and a committee whose standard cannot in principle be met is not making decisions at all. B also insists we reason about the counterfactual: if we authorize nothing, Northgate deploys a comparable system under a lighter regime, or routes the function to a jurisdiction where we have no monitoring access, and we lose the telemetry we currently have. And B warns that worst-case thresholds push applicants toward suppressing capabilities in ways that make failures less legible rather than less likely.

A third member has floated a middle option: authorize only a reversibility-bounded subset of the action space, with a hard human gate — implemented in the tool layer, not in the prompt — on the irreversible actions (external credit filings, account closure, permanent record deletion).

Your opinion must do the following:

1. Give a concrete bottom line: authorize, deny, or authorize with conditions, and specify the scope exactly — which action classes, what volume or exposure caps, what term, what review dates, and who has authority to trigger suspension. A recommendation the committee cannot vote on is useless to us.
2. State explicitly what standard of evidence you are demanding for each class of action, and justify the differing standards by reference to reversibility, detectability, and the shape of the tail — not by how difficult the analysis happens to be.
3. Confront the endogeneity problem head on. Say what would concretely have to be true of the deployment distribution for an average-case claim to underwrite authorization at this scale, and if your answer is that nothing could, draw that consequence explicitly rather than softening it.
4. Specify a tripwire and rollback protocol: which statistics, measured how often, thresholds set by whom, and a pre-registration requirement that Northgate state in advance what the telemetry would look like if the system were systematically failing in ways the monitor does not catch. The safety case should be required to discriminate between a safe system and an unsafe one, not merely to be consistent with safety.
5. Give the losing position its strongest paragraph: say which of A and B loses on your recommendation, reconstruct their argument better than they did, and explain why it still loses.
6. Where your recommendation depends on a factual assumption about Northgate's operations that the application does not settle, flag it as a condition of authorization rather than quietly assuming it.

Write for five readers: two ML engineers, a formal methods researcher, an economist, and a lawyer. Do not resolve the disagreement with slogans — neither "better safe than sorry" nor "worst cases are uninformative" will move anyone on this committee. Do not cite literature, papers, or standards documents; argue from the case. Keep the finished memo under 1,200 words, and lead with the recommendation.
