Hey — I'm working on a short essay (blog-post length) on superrationality and I'd love comments on my notes before I turn them into prose. Not looking for sources or refs, just for someone to poke holes.

Rough thesis: the standard move (if you're playing a copy of yourself, cooperate, because your two choices are really the same event) is fine in the two-person pure case, but it comes apart in three places, and the interesting one is scale.

1. Two readings of "what would a superrational agent do" that coincide in the plain 2-person PD but not in the Platonia-style case: (a) pick the action that's best *if all superrational agents pick it* — a constrained optimization along the diagonal; (b) pick a best response to the common policy everyone else is running — a symmetric fixed point. With N players, one prize, and independent randomization, (a) gives p = 1/N per player (success chance (1-1/N)^(N-1), about 1/e), but the only symmetric fixed point of (b) is p = 1: if the others each send with prob p, my chance of winning is r(1-p)^(N-1), linear in my own r, so I should send for sure — and then everyone sends and nobody wins. Worse, I don't think (a) is even licensed by the argument that motivates superrationality: the "same procedure, so same output" inference doesnt survive independent random draws, since conditional on my die landing a certain way yours is unaffected. Getting the randomized solution seems to need a shared random device, i.e. a correlation device nobody is allowed to agree on. (I vaguely remember Hofstadter being uneasy about the randomization move, but not whether for anything like this reason.)

2. Scale + trembles. Take an N-person unanimity game: all cooperate → 1 each; if anyone defects, cooperators get 0 and defectors get 2; all defect → 0.1 each. Now let superrational agents implement their choice only with error: realized action = intended with prob 1-ε, flipped with prob ε, independently. If everyone intends C, my expected payoff is about (1-ε)^N + 2ε; if everyone intends D, it's about 2(1-ε) once N is largish, because with probability near 1 somebody accidentally cooperates and I collect 2. So universal cooperation is only better while (1-ε)^(N-1) is above roughly 0.7, i.e. N below about 0.4/ε — a few dozen people at 1% error. Even the best symmetric mixed policy does badly: maximizing p^N + 2(1-p) gives p* ≈ 1 - ln(N/2)/N and an expected payoff around 2ln(N)/N, which goes to 0. So fault tolerance, not rationality, is doing the work: superrationality is only as good as the game's tolerance for defectors, and at scale that tolerance has to be built into the payoff structure, which makes it a mechanism-design point wearing a decision-theory costume.

3. Partial correlation. In the 2-person PD (T,R,P,S = 5,3,1,0), suppose my choice matches the other player's with prob ρ and is otherwise independent of an outsider who cooperates with prob q. Then cooperate iff ρ > (1+q)/(3+q) — a third if the outsiders always defect, a half if they always cooperate. For N players with independent pairwise matching you need ρ^(N-1) > 4/7, so the required ρ goes to 1 like 1 - 0.56/N. So superrationality demands near-certainty about who else is in the club, and the needed certainty grows with group size — which I think is the grown-up version of the old "reverberant doubt" worry.
