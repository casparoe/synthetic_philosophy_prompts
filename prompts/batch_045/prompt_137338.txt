I'm an undergraduate in a political theory seminar this term on whether the left-right distinction picks out anything real, and alongside the seminar I work about ten hours a week for a small nonprofit that maintains a Where do you stand? classroom tool: a bank of roughly eighty policy items, each tagged left, right, cross-cutting, or off-axis, that teachers run with high-school civics classes. My job, shared with two other interns who have no background in political theory, is to tag new items whenever an issue enters the news, which happens every week or two. The stakes are not trivial but not enormous: a miscoded item produces angry emails from parents, and a few years ago an item we tagged as cross-cutting was pulled from two districts after complaints, so our faculty advisor, a comparative politics professor, now has to sign off on anything we flag as difficult. We have about fifteen minutes per item, no budget for research, and often we cannot look up how parties abroad have lined up on the issue, so the procedure has to say what to do when the answer is simply unavailable to us rather than assuming an encyclopedia.

What I need is a procedure we can actually run, grounded in the theories we have read rather than in vibes. In the seminar we have looked at the equality criterion defended in Bobbio's short book on the distinction, at the view that left and right are better understood as attitudes toward hierarchy and toward change versus preservation, at the skeptical view that each side is only a contingent coalition of positions that happens to have been assembled in a particular place and could come apart, at the two-axis alternative that adds a traditional-versus-secular or authoritarian-versus-libertarian dimension, and at the psychological suggestion that what holds a side together is a stable dispositional orientation rather than a principle. Please turn whatever is genuinely usable in that material into an ordered set of questions for us, and tell us which distinctions have to survive the simplification and which we can drop. In particular, tell us what has to be true of a new issue for it to go on the axis at all, and what has to be true for it to be marked cross-cutting or dropped as a valence item that nobody of any significance opposes. We have been using three rough tests and I suspect at least one is junk: the enemy test, where we code an issue by who is currently attacking it; the time-machine test, where we ask whether the issue would still have clustered with the same side if the last big party realignment had gone the other way; and the blind-pairing test, where we ask whether knowing someone's answer to the new item would let us predict their answers to two items we have already coded better than chance. Tell us which of these tracks what actually holds a side together and which merely inherits whatever coalition happens to exist right now, and where a test fails say so plainly. Mark explicitly the points at which the procedure stops with a no, and the points at which it has to hand the decision to our advisor because the question is one of values rather than facts — for instance, whose description of a proposal we adopt when one side calls a measure protective and the other calls it authoritarian.

Then please run the procedure on three live items. First: eliminating single-family-only zoning near transit stations, so that apartment buildings can be built as of right. Second: requiring developers of frontier AI systems to obtain a license before training runs above a certain compute threshold, with a pause on the largest runs. Third: requiring photographic identification to vote, which our team has always treated as straightforwardly right-coded and which is our single most complained-about item. I should say that our working assumption is that the axis is largely exhausted and that our tags just describe current American coalitions, and I would rather not be told that our most contested item is an artifact of one country's party system, or that our whole US item set would fail a cross-national check — but if that is what the procedure delivers, please say it directly rather than softening it, since I would rather find out now than in a parent's email. I also want to know, honestly, what the procedure cannot do: which items will always be arbitrary, how often we should re-code the bank, and how to handle a case where my two co-interns reach different tags.
