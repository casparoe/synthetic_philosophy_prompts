I'm in a tiny reading group that's been grinding through animal ethics and the argument from marginal cases — you know the thing, if you say moral status tracks some cognitive capacity instead of species membership, then infants and people with severe cognitive disabilities come out below some animals, and everyone panics and changes the subject. Last week somebody said, only half joking, that at least brain volume is a measurable number, so we should just operationalize moral status and be done with it. I've been turning that over since and I cant get it out of my head, so I want to push it off a cliff. Write me the internal compliance FAQ for a jurisdiction about ten years into having actually done it — the Neurovolume (Moral Status) Regulations, or whatever you'd rather call them — round 800 words, numbered Q&As, deadpan government-guidance voice. No winking, no "as absurd as this sounds" asides: the author of the FAQ has to genuinely believe it's a reasonable document.

The jokes should come from the actual philosophy, not from silly names or silly suffering. I want the amendment history to carry the reductio: they start with absolute brain volume, get wrecked by the fact that volume scales with body mass so elephants and sperm whales outrank everybody, patch it with an encephalization quotient, then with cortical neuron count, then get wrecked again by corvids and octopuses packing far more neurons into far less volume, and by the awkward discovery that the thing neuron count was a proxy for can't be measured in a living subject at all — so the regulations end up rating people on a proxy for a proxy for a proxy while still being called the Neurovolume Regulations. Along the way I want: the banding problem, where somebody has to draw the line between full and partial status and there's an organized lobby camped exactly on the boundary; a clause rating infants and fetuses by their projected adult volume, which quietly turns one life into a curve of moral worth over time and makes anything that stunts brain growth a dignity tort; and a provision letting institutions offset low ratings in one place with high ratings elsewhere — tradable dignity credits, because if the property is a scalar, why shouldn't it be?

Keep it internally consistent: every patch should be the patch a real committee would actually reach for, and the document should never concede that anything is wrong. Land the ending on the objection I actually care about without ever stating it as an objection — the Inspector General's ten-year audit finds that the ratings track childhood nutrition, birth weight and family income far better than anyone predicted, i.e. the biological measure has been quietly re-encoding a hierarchy it claimed to be discovering. Someone who works on this should recognize the moves and think the satire is fair; someone who doesn't should just enjoy the bureaucratic nightmare.
