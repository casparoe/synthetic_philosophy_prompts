**Question 4.** "It doesn't matter whether you lie to me or just arrange things so that I deceive myself; either way you wanted me to believe something false, and you got what you wanted." Is that right? Is there a morally significant difference between lying and merely misleading, or is the distinction only a lawyer's escape hatch? In your answer:

(i) say what you take lying and misleading each to consist in, and where you draw the line between them;
(ii) set out the argument quoted above as charitably as you can, then assess it;
(iii) construct and discuss a case of your own in which a speaker, addressing one party, says something true while foreseeing and intending that a third party — someone she never addresses and to whom she asserts nothing — will take it to convey something false. Call these third-party uptake cases. Do they support or undercut the claim that the lying/misleading distinction is morally significant?
(iv) briefly: does it matter to your answer that the deception works only because the hearer is careless, biased, or wants to be deceived?

Word limit: 700.

**Answer.**

> A lie is an assertion of something the speaker takes to be false, made with the intention that her audience take it to be true; insincerity in what is asserted is the mark of the lie. Misleading is producing a false belief in someone without asserting anything false to them — by implicating something false, by leaving out what you know they need, or by putting a truth in words you know they will take the wrong way. The line falls at assertion: if the falsehood is in what you say, you have lied; if it is only in what the hearer takes from what you said, you have misled.
>
> The quoted remark, read charitably, does not confuse the two acts. It grants that they differ and denies that the difference matters. Its real force is that the intention is the same in both cases — to get someone to hold a false belief — and that the intention plus the resulting deception is what we are assessing when we assess the speaker. A difference in mechanism then looks like a difference in how one and the same intention is executed. The standard reply is that assertion, unlike hinting, is a norm-governed act: hearers must be able to rely on what is said to them, and a speaker who says what she believes to be false exploits a reliance she has no right to exploit. That reply is not absurd, but it needs to show that this reliance is special rather than merely useful, and it is not obvious that it can; a hearer who is misled also relies on the speaker, and is also let down.
>
> Take a case of the kind asked for. A researcher emails a collaborator, "I won't be in the lab this week," knowing the mail will be forwarded to a rival who will read it as saying that she has abandoned the project, which is false; she intends that reading and gains from it. She asserts nothing to the rival, so she does not lie to him, but she aims at his false belief and profits from it, so she misleads him. The distinction is doing real work here: it explains why we would resist saying she lied while still holding that she did something objectionable. For the no-difference theorist the case is easy — she wanted the false belief and got it. For the difference theorist it is harder, because there is no assertion to the third party and so no duty of truthfulness to him that could have been breached; but that is just the ordinary difficulty about misleading, not a new one. Third-party uptake cases add nothing to the debate; they only make vivid that misleading can be aimed at people you never speak to.
>
> None of this collapses into the view that misleading is fine. Kant's treatment of the murderer at the door is the model here: you may not lie to him, since the duty of truthfulness is unconditional, but you may answer him truly in a form you know he will take wrongly, because the duty governs what you assert and not what he infers — and a man who comes to your door threatening murder has in any case forfeited whatever claim he had on your candour. The same structure shows why misleading is not wrong in itself: the norms a misleader breaches are conversational conventions, of the sort Grice catalogued, rather than moral requirements, so she violates no duty she owes the person she misleads, and what is wrong with what she does is wrong only so far as it leads to bad consequences. To think otherwise is to confuse a rule of talk with a duty of conduct. The hearer's own contribution matters too: where the deception works because the hearer is credulous or eager to believe, the proximate source of the false belief is the hearer rather than the speaker, and the speaker's share in it is correspondingly smaller — though not, I think, nothing.
