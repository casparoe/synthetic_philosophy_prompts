Two members of the city's commemoration committee spent most of a dinner going at each other over a case now formally in front of them. I was the only person at the table who isn't on the committee, so I got asked to referee it and couldn't. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move. I don't need sources or citations; I want the arguments assessed on their merits.

The case, simplified. Josiah Pembroke, 1831–1902, built the railroad that turned a river crossing into a city, endowed the public library and the hospital, and as a state legislator wrote and pushed through the statute that dissolved the landholding of the region's Indigenous communities and forced their removal, defending it in pamphlets whose language nobody disputes. In 1911 the city erected a full-length bronze of him at the library entrance — the pedestal is inscribed A FOUNDER AND BENEFACTOR on one face and, toward the street, TO THE YOUTH OF THIS CITY, AN EXAMPLE — and renamed the main avenue for him. The committee must recommend something about the statue and something about the street. Nadia argues for removing the statue and renaming the street; Gordon argues for keeping both, with additions.

1. Nadia: Commemoration is a present-tense act by whoever maintains it. The bronze does not assert that Pembroke existed; the archives can do that. It asserts that the city honors him, and it re-asserts that every day it stands, in the first person plural. Since what he is centrally remembered for includes an act of dispossession he defended in print, the city is currently in the business of praising it. Preserving history is the wrong description of what is at stake here: removal touches no document. The only question is whether the city should go on saying this.

2. Gordon: Grant all of that about the statue; I'll come back to it. But the street is different in kind. A street name is a coordinate, not a tribute — it has worn smooth into a bare address, and nobody driving down Pembroke Avenue is being commended to anyone's example. Meanwhile renaming imposes costs that are certain, concentrated and measurable: every household and business on the avenue, licenses, letterhead, signage, dispatch and mapping data. Real money, borne by people selected only by where they live, to change a word no one reads as praise, on behalf of a wrong done a century and a half ago. That is symbolism purchased with other people's inconvenience.

3. Nadia: The worn-smooth argument gets the psychology backwards. Street names are not marks on a map; they are words the state is continually made to utter. Dispatchers say it, licenses bear it, court papers carry it, and the city says it in its own official voice dozens of times a day, so the content of the name is not settled by what drivers happen to feel. More generally, the honoring-content of a monument is dispositional rather than experiential: like a flag or a wedding ring, it means what it means whether or not anyone is attending to it, and it becomes fully active precisely when it is contested. That the name feels empty is why it has lasted so long. Emptiness is not innocence; it is how a community goes on saying something without having to own it.

4. Gordon: I accept that, and I accept it costs me something on the street. But it does not get you to removal, because removal is only one of the things a city can do. Re-inscription keeps the object and changes the sentence: A FOUNDER AND BENEFACTOR becomes a benefactor who also engineered a removal and defended it in print. That is strictly more truthful than either praise or disappearance. And notice the asymmetry between the options. An inscription can be read, checked and argued with; a removed statue cannot be visited at all. Removal is irreversible in a way annotation is not, and a city that learns to remove will remove again, in thirty years, on thinner evidence.

5. Nadia: Re-inscription treats the statue as though it were a sentence, and it isn't one. A standing bronze is rhetoric with a body: it sets a man above you, in permanent material, at the door of a public building. That is why the fight is always about the object and never about the plaque — a plaque is something you read, a monument is something you are positioned under, and no paragraph cancels being positioned under it. Test it: would anyone accept this statue, with a perfect plaque, at the entrance to the tribal offices? No — and what blocks it is the bronze, not the text. Besides, not all commemoration is the same act. Ancestor-commemoration says he is one of ours, we come from him, and it can absorb a flawed figure the way a family absorbs a flawed grandfather. Exemplar-commemoration says imitate this person, and it is strict-liability: a disqualifying vice in the person defeats the point of the object, and no amount of context restores it. The 1911 inscription is explicitly the second kind. On my own distinction the street survives — the avenue is ancestor-commemoration — so I would drop the street claim and keep the statue claim, with the distinction doing the work rather than the costs.

6. Gordon: Your distinction helps me too. If what is wrong with the statue is that it addresses people in a space they cannot avoid, then relocation solves it: museum grounds or the historical society ends the compulsory address while leaving the thing available to anyone who wants it. But there is a prior question neither of us has answered, and it is not about the object at all. Whose claim is this? The wrong was done to the nation he removed, not to us. Commemoration is a three-party relation — who honors, who is honored, and to whom the honoring is addressed — and you have collapsed it into two by treating the city's conscience as the thing to be relieved. If the descendants' own representatives say keep it and set the truth beside it, because they want what he did standing where everyone can see it, then removal is not being done on their behalf; it is being done about them, one more time the city decides what becomes of their history. And your compulsory-space argument needs a threshold it does not have: it cannot be that any citizen's being wounded by something in public space is sufficient, or nothing survives disagreement.

After that they went in circles.

Please go through moves 1 through 6 and say for each whether it is right, partly right, or wrong, and why — not merely who wins overall. Where a move establishes something, say exactly what it establishes, since several of them are compatible with more than one outcome. Then say what both of them are missing, if anything: some consideration the argument as they framed it cannot see. Finally, give your own recommendation for the statue and for the street, and say which of the two you take to be the harder case.
