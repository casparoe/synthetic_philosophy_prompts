I'm a philosophy PhD student working on the arrow of time, and a friend in the film department has asked me to guest-lead a session on Arrival (2016) for her seminar on science fiction and science — I'll be screening the film and handing out Chiang's novella behind it, Story of Your Life, so you can draw on either. The problem is that I can't work out how much of the film's physics is load-bearing. In my own work I've been trained to keep several asymmetries apart that the film seems to run together: the thermodynamic arrow (an entropy gradient that traces back to a low-entropy boundary condition at one end of the universe, not to any asymmetry in the dynamics); the record or epistemic arrow (the fact that memory, traces and knowledge of the past all require entropy-increasing processes, so that knowing the past and knowing the future are not symmetric achievements); the agency or counterfactual arrow (we take ourselves to be able to affect the future and not the past, and our deliberation seems to presuppose that); and the metaphysical arrow (whether the future is open in a way the past isn't, whether time passes, whether there is a block universe). I want a reading that keeps those four separate and tells me which one, or which conjunction of them, the film actually needs in order for Louise's experience to be what it appears to be — and which ones it merely gestures at.

Here is the device I've been toying with, which I'd like you to stress-test rather than endorse. Suppose the universe has low-entropy boundary conditions at both temporal ends and maximal entropy in the middle, so that the entropy gradient reverses at the midpoint: observers on our side take one low-entropy end as their past, observers on the far side take the other as theirs, and each side's records, traces and memories accumulate toward the middle. Suppose the heptapods live on the far side, or straddle the reversal, so that what they have are perfectly ordinary physical records — footprints, photographs, neural traces — of events we call our future. Two questions: is that setup even coherent, given that putting the two halves in informational contact would presumably require the very thermal interaction that would wash out the gradient the story depends on? And if it is coherent, does it deliver the film's phenomenology? Louise's own arrow is ours; any trace she acquires gets laid down in her in the ordinary entropy-increasing direction, so she would be learning now and remembering later, only about a region we call the future. Is that really experiencing the future, or just knowing it in a way that happens to be reliable? I'd also like your verdict on the variational physics Chiang builds the heptapod mindset out of — Fermat's principle of least time, where the ray's path looks as if it were fixed by where it will end up. Does that license the teleological reading, or is it a red herring, given that time-symmetric dynamics can be written with endpoint conditions without anything propagating backwards and without the ray knowing anything? And can the reading be repaired if we imagine cognition that is governed by a global variational constraint rather than by stepwise, irreversible computation?

The part I keep getting stuck on is deliberation. Louise's compatibilist thought — that the experience of knowing the future might be what produces the urgency and the obligation to act precisely as she knew she would — is, I take it, just the familiar point that her deliberating can be among the causes of what she foresees. But my supervisor's objection, which I half think is right and half think is a cheap shot, is that the film wants to have it both ways: it wants a cognition outside the entropy gradient, and it also wants an agent who chooses, while agency and computation look like they essentially require an irreversible process, so a genuinely gradient-transcending knower might be a creature that cannot deliberate at all. If that's right, the heptapod gift is agency-dissolving rather than agency-preserving, and the film's ethical crux collapses. Tell me whether the film can have both, where it quietly helps itself to both, and whether any of this turns on the thermodynamics at all or only on whether the future is open. Concretely, I need scenes to put in front of undergraduates: the first entry into the shell and the logograms, the weapon-versus-tool disagreement and the mahjong framing, the there-is-no-time message and the twelve-part message, Abbott's death, Louise's visions of Hannah and Hannah's death, the phone call to General Shang with his wife's last words, and the closing choice to have the child. For each, say what the physics does and doesn't explain. I'd also like you to say where the film resists the reading — including, if you think so, that its linguistic determinism is far stronger than the evidence supports, and that the heptapods' cooperation has no obvious connection to their non-linear time at all. Don't flatten it into an illustration of a position; I'd rather hear what the film adds that the arguments don't, especially about what foreknowledge does to the experience of grief and choice. Spend your words on the analysis, not on pointing me to further reading.
