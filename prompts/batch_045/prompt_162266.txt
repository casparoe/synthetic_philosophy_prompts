I am writing a short essay for a volume on Borges and cinema, and I want to use *Everything Everywhere All at Once* as my central case rather than the films usually paired with him. Before I commit to the frame, I need the underlying distinction about Borges's treatment of time sharpened, and I need to know whether it survives being applied.

The distinction I want to work with is my own, not standard, and I am not confident it is sound. Commentators standardly read *The Garden of Forking Paths* as an anticipation of branching time or of a many-worlds picture: at each juncture the world forks, and the forks are all in some sense real. I want to split that into three. Call *diachronic forking* the view on which branches are generated as time passes, so that the future is genuinely open and the past is single. Call *co-actual plenitude* the view on which the branches are not generated at all but given together, so that no outcome is ever really foreclosed and, correspondingly, nothing is ever really achieved or avoided. Call *retroactive collapse* the view on which one strand becomes the past only once the fork is resolved, so that what had been many possibilities is rewritten as having always been one. My sense is that Borges's own commitments, above all in the essay where he extends the negations of idealism to time itself, push toward the second, and that the popular many-worlds reading domesticates him into a diachronic forker. Is that right, and is my three-way distinction doing real work, or is it a stipulative mess that merely relabels something the philosophy of time already handles better?

Then the application, which is where I expect trouble. The film's multiverse looks diachronic on its surface, but the everything bagel strikes me as closer to *The Aleph* than to the garden: a single object in which everything is placed at once, rather than a labyrinth of branching futures. And the nihilism the bagel is meant to embody looks like exactly the worry that co-actual plenitude invites, namely that if every outcome is realized somewhere, then choice, achievement, and loss lose their point. Evelyn's answer, as I read it, declines to deny plenitude and instead relocates value to the strand one is actually in. Is that a coherent position or a sentimental evasion? Does the film earn it, or does it quietly help itself to diachronic forking whenever it needs agency, and to plenitude whenever it needs pathos? And where should an honest critic say the reading simply fails, on the grounds that this is a comedy of reconciliation about a mother and daughter whose metaphysics is scaffolding?

Which two or three scenes would you put at the center of such a reading, and does the film end up adding anything to the philosophical debate that Borges's fiction and the arguments about it do not already contain?
