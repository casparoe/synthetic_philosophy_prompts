Our department is putting together the self-study document for an external review next spring, and I got landed with the section that has to say, in about a page, what it would even be for philosophy to make progress. The reason it's landed on me is that one of the reviewers is a condensed-matter physicist who has been needling me about this at every faculty party since 2019, and my committee can't get past "we're obviously better at this than we were in 1950," which he will just laugh at. So before we argue about whether we've made any, I want the spec: what does an account of progress in philosophy have to deliver to be worth putting in front of him? I don't want citations, a reading list, or who said what — I want the requirements, each with the case or consideration behind it, and I want the list to be aimed at a specific job, which is: explaining to a smart hostile outsider what the field has been doing with its time, and telling us which of our subfields are actually moving.

Here's why I think this is harder than it sounds, and feel free to tell me I've got the setup wrong. In the sciences a refuted result gets retracted and drops out of the textbook; in philosophy nothing is ever withdrawn, the archive only grows, and every position ever held stays permanently citable. So an account has to take some stand on whether a growing space of live options is progress, stagnation, or slow-motion failure — and I suspect that question is where the whole thing turns. It also has to say what the unit of progress is and how units add up: answered questions, or dead arguments that we now know don't work, or better-formulated questions, or new distinctions and conceptual tools, or the practitioners themselves being harder to fool. And it has to handle at least these four: (i) the reprint test — if a 2025 paper on some question and a 1925 paper on it are hard to tell apart once you update the jargon, that's a mark against progress in that subfield; (ii) the export case — half of what philosophy used to do now lives inside linguistics, computer science, econ and physics, and I genuinely cant tell whether shipping a question out is an achievement for us or an admission of defeat; (iii) the recurrence case — positions come back around after a century or two, various skepticisms and idealisms especially, and that needs to be scored somehow, as failure of institutional memory or as evidence the question was never going to close; and (iv) per-head returns, since there are vastly more of us now than there were, and if the field has improved only in proportion to headcount that is a much less impressive thing to hand a physicist.

The triage on the list itself is the part I actually want. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just what one camp happens to want — I'm fairly confident "must show convergence on true answers" is a prejudice imported from the sciences and "must stay illegible to outsiders" is one of ours, but there are probably others I'm blind to. Then tell me where the list tears: is there a subset of these desiderata that can't be jointly satisfied? If so, which requirement should get dropped, and what does the surviving list then imply about whether we've made any progress? And name the single requirement doing the most exclusionary work, the one that kills the most popular stories people tell themselves. If you want to close with a one-line-per-picture scorecard for the usual suspects — the "museum of permanently refuted positions" story, the "it's self-understanding, not answers" story, the "we're a young science and it's early days" story, and the "philosophy's real job is building tools and dissolving confusions" story — that's definately fine, but keep it to a few lines; the criteria are what I need and I'll do the arguing myself.
