What this is for. I'm a PhD student in philosophy writing a seminar paper on whether talk of markets as knowing things is a metaphor with a mechanism behind it, or just a metaphor. The dialectic keeps getting stuck on a crude inference: no-trade results show that information-driven trade is at best a parasite on ignorance about other people's ignorance, so — people conclude — prices cannot be read as the market's belief, and the epistemic story about markets collapses into the pecuniary one. I want a case in which the epistemic and the pecuniary variables are genuinely separable, so I can see whether that inference holds. Because that is what the case is for, you are not allowed to stipulate the verdicts: the numbers have to fall out of a mechanism that someone in the story actually operates, and I will treat any step that amounts to assuming the conclusion as cheating.

The object. Build me a market — concrete, realistic, small enough to compute by hand, no sci-fi apparatus, no superhuman forecasters, no device a competent human could not operate — in which three quantities can be read off separately and come apart in a prescribed direction as a single parameter goes to zero.

Terminology, which please use:
- ε: the probability that a given order in this market is motivated by something other than information about the payoff, where that motive is required to be independent of the payoff state.
- Π(ε): the expected profit of an informed trader, net of what she would get by declining to trade, computed conditional on her own information.
- V(ε): the expected volume of information-motivated trade.
- I(ε): the informativeness of the price. Measure it as the reduction in uncertainty about the payoff achieved by conditioning on the price rather than the prior — say whether you are using variance reduction or entropy reduction, and be consistent. Report I(ε) against two benchmarks: the informativeness of one trader's private signal alone, and the informativeness of the pool of all private signals.

Specifications.
1. The hard direction. As ε → 0+, Π(ε) → 0, and at ε = 0 exactly there is no trade at all, in the way the standard no-trade argument requires. Meanwhile I(ε) must not go to zero: the price has to remain strictly more informative than the prior, and you should try to get it at least as informative as any single participant's private information even in the limit.
2. Information must be genuinely dispersed. At least two traders hold partial, conditionally independent signals; no single trader's signal determines the payoff; the pooled signals very nearly do. If one trader knows everything up front, there is no aggregation to adjudicate and the case is worthless to me.
3. Close the usual escape hatches, explicitly: a common prior, common knowledge of the entire structure including everyone's signal technology, risk-neutral expected-utility maximizers, no irrationality, no heterogeneous priors, no taxes or transaction costs or outside subsidy, no price-setter who loses money by construction, and no trader who is compelled to trade. The price must be set by an agent who updates by Bayesian inference on what she actually observes and who breaks even conditional on her own information; you may not write down an informative price function and then verify that it is informative — the informativeness has to be produced by an inference someone performs, and the traders' willingness to trade against it has to be sequentially rational. The ε-motive is the only thing you may parametrize.
4. Define the epistemic yield of the market as Y(ε) = I(ε) / Π(ε), the uncertainty reduction purchased per unit of expected profit. Say what happens to it as ε → 0+.
