I'm the best man at my brother's wedding in three weeks, and I'd like to avoid delivering the standard article about how he's a wonderful man and she's far too good for him. Some background, because it's what makes this hard rather than merely sentimental. My brother is a decision theorist, and for roughly a decade he has been circling a single case: the stranded motorist on an empty road who promises the passing driver money he can pay only once they reach town — a promise the driver has no way to enforce, and one the motorist will have no reason to keep the moment the car pulls up outside the bank. He takes the case to show something uncomfortable about rationality itself, namely that there are situations in which the only way to get what you want is to be, locally, less than fully rational: to be the sort of person who pays when paying no longer serves him. He also thinks the usual way of closing the case off is a dodge. The usual move, as I follow it, is to grant the point and conclude that it is therefore rational to acquire the disposition to keep promises. His objection, which I have heard at the kitchen table often enough to half reconstruct, is that a disposition you deliberately install because it is useful is one you know you installed because it is useful — and so does everyone watching — and a reassurance that is transparently manufactured is not the reassurance the driver was after. What he wants, he says, is a disposition that reaches him from somewhere he did not calculate his way into.

So here is what I need, and here is the room it has to land in. I want a five-minute wedding toast with that case as its spine — not as ornament, but as a constraint on what I am permitted to say. If my brother is even half right, nearly everything a toast normally praises is the wrong thing: their reasons for marrying, how well suited they are, how carefully they have thought it through, the likelihood that it will last. Those are desert-road reasons; they are precisely what falls silent at the moment the promise has to be kept. What I would rather praise is what neither of them selected for this occasion: the habits their parents drilled into them, the town they both come from, and his grandmother, who will be at the second table, who believes the vows bind because they are made before God, which is her own solution to his puzzle and which the toast must not quietly condescend to. I have also been turning over an idea of my own and would like it stress-tested before it reaches a microphone: that the old till-death-do-us-part formula is doing real work rather than decorative work, because a promise with a known last period unravels backwards from the end, whereas one with no end point does not. If that is wrong, or if it is right but the philosophers will hear it as a game-theoretic claim I cannot defend, I would rather the toast did not lean on it at all. About half the room will be his department, people who will recognise the case instantly and catch every misstep; the other half are family from a farming town who should not have to sit through a lecture or hear the word equilibrium spoken aloud. His fiancée is an emergency physician, if that gives you anything. One in-joke is welcome provided the whole room can enjoy it. And he would despise a toast that announces the paradox solved by love — leave it unsolved, if anything.

Write the toast. Then, in a brief note after it, tell me how confident you are in the claims it makes about the philosophy and single out the one you are least sure of: specifically, whether the worry about a self-consciously acquired disposition failing to reassure is a live problem in the literature or something my brother half invented over dinner, and where the speech takes liberties or rests on a reading that is contested.
