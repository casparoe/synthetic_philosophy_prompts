Bit of an odd request, but here goes. I'm a retired field biologist — fourty-odd years of measuring things for a living — and I've only got into philosophy since I packed it in. I run a small reading group at the local U3A: about 14 people, mostly retired, 90 minutes, a kettle and a whiteboard and that's yer lot. No laptops, no internet, and half of them won't read between sessions, so anything that depends on prep will fall flat.

We've been picking at Dewey — the book of his about the public, and the later one on inquiry — and there's a distinction I keep failing to get across by talking about it. On one side, democracy as a counting machine: people walk in with wants already fully formed, you aggregate them, out comes a decision. On the other, the Deweyan picture where that's back to front — the wants get made and remade in the course of people jointly dealing with a mess they're all caught up in, and inquiry is a matter of reconstructing an indeterminate situation rather than of fetching an answer that was already sitting there. Every time I explain it, somebody says "yes but surely you have to vote in the end" and we're back where we started.

So: design me one session where the group runs into the difference before anyone names it. Rules, roles, materials, and a timeline that fits 90 minutes with a tea break in the middle. The facilitator shouldn't use the words experimentalism, inquiry or the public until the debrief.

Four things I'd like built in if they can be made to work:

1. The group is not handed a problem with an objective already attached. At the start nobody should be able to say what the group is trying to maximise or achieve; arriving at a formulation has to be part of the work, and the payoff structure should reward a good formulation rather than reward guessing a hidden right answer.

2. Private information that individually misleads. Each person's own bit of evidence should fairly strongly suggest a course of action that's wrong for the group, so that pooling isn't just arithmetic.

3. Something from my old working life that I suspect is the real heart of it: in plenty of physical systems you can't observe without disturbing what you're observing, so finding out changes what there is to find out. If there's a non-contrived way to get that into a game — that a probe has consequences, that acting in order to learn forecloses or opens options — I'd love it. If it can't be done without feeling bolted on, say so and leave it out.

4. It must not quietly turn into a prisoner's dilemma or a tragedy of the commons in a hat. Several of them have seen those before and will name them, and then the session becomes about strategic defection rather than about inquiry.

Then a debrief that maps specific moments that will have occurred onto specific parts of the idea, including where I should expect resistance and what it costs someone to resist at each of those points. Tell me honestly what will go wrong the first time — I'm guessing someone tries to run it as a vote within about four minutes, one person dominates, and at least one person feels manipulated and gets cross — and what to do in each case. And be straight about which part of Dewey's picture a 90-minute game simply can't reproduce, so I know what I'll have to say out loud myself.

Please write it in British English, and keep any handout to a single side of A4.
