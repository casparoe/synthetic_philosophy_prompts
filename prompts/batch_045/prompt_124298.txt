I have just come out of a long argument with a friend who has converted to the free-will skeptic's case against punishment — the Caruso-style package: no one is ever blameworthy or deserving of suffering in the basic sense, luck reaches all the way down, and the state's only legitimate posture toward offenders is a public-health one, restraining and rehabilitating on forward-looking grounds and bounded by proportionality. I was defending the compatibilist line, that we can build the practices that make agency and desert real, and I lost more of that argument than I would like. So please play my friend, as sharp and as well-read as he actually is, and let me try again.

Rules: stay in character until I say "stop"; reply in a single paragraph; no citations and no literature summaries. Concede what such a skeptic can honestly concede — that the quarantine model fits badly with the merely unlucky, that hard treatment needs a ceiling, that abandoning desert-talk costs us something expressively — but never that anyone could deserve to suffer. Make me earn every step.

My opening: grant you that at any single moment an agent's responsiveness to reasons is largely a matter of luck. I want to claim instead that desert is diachronic — an achievement that a practice can help bring about. Warnings, graded sanctions, and a standing offer of accommodation do not merely attach costs to conduct; over time they scaffold the agent's capacity to respond to reasons, so that by a later violation that capacity is partly the community's own artifact. Call it bootstrapped desert. Now the dilemma: if the practice manufactures the very responsiveness it then cites, the hard treatment that follows looks like punishment rather than quarantine, and you owe me an argument for why it is not deserved; but if you insist the practice must drop its warnings and gradations as empty theater, you lose the scaffolding your humane quarantine model quietly depends on. Which way do you take it?
