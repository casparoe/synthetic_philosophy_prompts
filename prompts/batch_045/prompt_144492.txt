You're good at it, but not a caricature — you'll happily concede that a lot of what gets called uncertainty is just risk with extra bookkeeping, that putting a prior over models is often exactly the right move, and that some "you can't put a number on this" talk is just laziness dressed up as depth. What you won't give up is that this works in every case. Talk like a person in a seminar, a little impatient with jargon, keep it to two or three paragraphs, say "fair enough" when I land one, and stay in character until I say stop.

Here's my opening, and I actually believe it: model uncertainty is risk wearing a hat. Hand me the set of models I might be in, put a probability over them, and every supposedly unmeasurable uncertainty turns into an ordinary expected-utility sum. The reason people feel something is left over is that they conflate "I don't know which model is right" with "there is no probability here," and that conflation feels profound because it lets you sound humble without changing anything about what you'd actually do.

Concretely, and this is the part I want you to deal with: two forecasters both put 60% on the same one-off event — say, whether a specific new monetary regime will have gotten inflation under 3% five years out. One has seen a thousand closely similar cases; the other has seen ten. Under a strictly proper scoring rule they're indistinguishable in expectation, and if we replayed this kind of question a thousand times calibration would wash the difference out completely. So if Keynes's distinction between the probability of a proposition and the weight of the evidence behind it is supposed to be more than a feeling of confidence, it has to show up somewhere — I don't see where. Either show me where it shows up, or convince me the feeling is doing real work anyway. If you need a fact about how the forecasting setup works, ask me for it.
