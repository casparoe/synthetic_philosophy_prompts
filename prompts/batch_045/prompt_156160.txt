I would like a prediction about someone's verdict, and I am more interested in the reasoning than in the answer itself.

Last spring I took a philosophy of science seminar whose professor keeps a running list on her office door: fields that people have asked her about, each marked science, not science, or unclear, usually with a one-line reason. She describes herself as a falsificationist, but the list does not obviously bear that out, and I would like to know what rule she is actually applying. Here is the list as I copied it down, with her reasons where she gave them:

- Astrology — not science. No mechanism, and every failure gets explained away rather than counted.
- Freudian psychoanalysis — not science. Whatever a patient does confirms the theory; there is no outcome that would have told against it.
- Homeopathy — not science. It survives every trial by reinterpreting the trial rather than by giving anything up.
- Intelligent design — not science. It is built so that no observation could tell against it.
- Evolutionary biology — science. It stakes claims on evidence that could have gone the other way; she instanced transitional forms and the absence of fossils in the wrong strata.
- String theory — science, and worth funding, even though there is no test that everyone agrees it has passed and its defenders standardly answer difficulties by adding structure. Her reason: it is a growing mathematical programme with real contact with established physics, pursued by people who are trying to test it.
- Macroeconomics as practised with dynamic stochastic general equilibrium models — science, though only barely. The models repeatedly fail and get patched, but some of their predictions work and there is a genuine research community arguing about them.
- A machine-learning system that predicts which hospital patients will deteriorate, trained on electronic health records and more accurate than any clinician or existing scoring rule — not science. An instrument, not a field: it works, nobody can say why, and there is nothing in it to give up.
- Century-scale climate-impact models that attach precise costs to warming in 2100, which nobody alive will be able to test — science, because they are assembled out of independently tested physics and remain answerable to evidence in principle.
- Social psychology since the replication crisis — science, and more so than before, precisely because the field began discarding results under evidential pressure.

A friend from the same seminar says she is not really applying falsificationism at all, and is instead scoring fields on whether their research programmes are still turning up new results. I cannot tell whether that fits the whole list, and I suspect several quite different rules fit it about equally well. So I want to use the list to settle a case she has never ruled on.

The case: a materials company runs a closed-loop discovery system. A model proposes candidate solid-state electrolytes; a robotic laboratory synthesises and measures them; the results are fed back into training. Over three years the system has produced roughly forty compounds that meet the company's targets, at about sixty times the hit rate of the conventional workflow. Papers in reputable journals report the compounds and their measured properties, but the weights, the training data and the record of failed runs are proprietary, and no outsider can query the model. Outside laboratories have now independently made and tested fifteen compounds the system proposed: eleven behaved as predicted, four did not. When a prediction fails, nothing is abandoned; the model is retrained on the new data and carries on. Nobody, including the people who built it, can state why it prefers the candidates it prefers, and the company says it does not want a theory of electrolytes, it wants working materials. Recently the system has begun emitting natural-language rationales alongside its proposals. A materials scientist I showed them to said they read well but do not hang together, and that some of them contradict the proposals they accompany.

My questions:

1. What rules are compatible with all ten of her verdicts? Please state them precisely enough that they can come apart on a new case — I have in mind some form of falsificationism, some form of research-programme test, some institutional or community-and-answerability test, and some test keyed to reliability of prediction, but I want your own inventory rather than mine. And tell me honestly whether any single one of them fits all ten cleanly, or whether the list is only fitted by a conjunction, a disjunction, or a gerrymandered rule — and what that implies for using it to predict.
2. What does each rule predict about the closed-loop system, and where exactly do the predictions diverge? In particular, for whichever rules return not science, I want to know whether the proprietary closure, the absence of anything to give up, or the absence of an intelligible theory is doing the work — and whether any of those三个 is doing work it also does somewhere in her list.
3. Your prediction for her verdict, with a confidence, the closest analog among her ten, and the earlier verdict that sits in the greatest tension with your prediction. If the verdict you expect from her and the verdict that would be most consistent with her own precedents come apart, say so and say which you would recommend.
4. Whether the emitted rationales should change anything, and on which rules they should or should not.
5. The single variant of this case that would best separate the surviving rules: what change to the setup would make her answer — or the right answer — maximally informative about which rule is in play?

I am not after readings, citations or bibliography; I want the argument.
