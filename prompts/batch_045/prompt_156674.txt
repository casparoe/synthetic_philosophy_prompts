Me and two friends run a tiny reading group on cooperative game theory — last week we did Nash's bargaining solution, this week we're doing the Kalai–Smorodinsky alternative, and we got stuck on monotonicity. I want one worked example for the problem set, and I'd rather it not be the usual abstract convex blob in the plane. So: build me a case to order.

A realistic two-party negotiation — your pick, e.g. two department heads in a hospital splitting shared operating-room time plus an equipment budget, a city budget office, a union and management, two co-founders dividing equity and control — told in a single paragraph with named parties, where the change between the two versions happens for a mundane reason (a rule change, a grant that only pays out if both sides participate meaningfully, a new scheduling system, a third party's offer), not any sci-fi device. The disagreement point stays put across versions. Normalize so d = (0,0) and each side's best possible outcome is 1, i.e. the ideal point is (1,1) in both versions; give a one-line story reason for each vertex (diminishing returns, a threshold, the two sides weighting two issues differently) and keep it hand-checkable — convex hull of at most five payoff pairs, or a frontier with at most two kinks, numbers to two or three decimals, and you may assume they can randomize or time-share to justify convexity. The specs: (i) version B's feasible set strictly contains version A's, something jointly achievable becomes available; (ii) neither party's maximum is any higher in B, so the ideal point genuinely is unchanged — show this explicitly, it's the load-bearing check; (iii) the Nash solution gives one party strictly LESS in B than in A, with the argmax of the product computed in both versions; (iv) the KS solution — the top-right point where the line from the disagreement point to the ideal point leaves the feasible set — gives both weakly more and at least one strictly more. Don't stipulate payoffs that produce those verdicts; the utilities have to come out of the story and the verdicts have to be computed from them, since for my purposes that would be cheating. A hint in case it saves you time: a straight-line frontier (a plain triangle) provably can't do it, so you need real curvature or a kink, and the added options have to sit mostly on one side of the old Nash tangency rather than symmetrically around it — I tried a symmetric bulge and it just makes both parties better off, which isn't a violation of anything.

After the arithmetic: tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to the verdicts if you remove it — mirror the added options, or let the expansion raise one side's maximum (my understanding is that in the second case monotonicity simply doesn't apply, so it stops being a counterexample; correct me if that's wrong). Then give me the other side of it: how would someone who thinks Nash got this right push back? My guess is they'd say monotonicity isn't the axiom to care about, independence of irrelevant alternatives is, and KS is the one that gives that up, and maybe that the loser's loss is an artifact of normalizing both maxima to 1 — but say what you take the strongest version of that reply to be, and be honest about whether this case is really an argument against the Nash solution or just a tidy way of showing the two axiom sets can't both be had. One of my friends is a Nash partisan and I want to know in advance whether the example actually bites or whether he can just shrug at it.
