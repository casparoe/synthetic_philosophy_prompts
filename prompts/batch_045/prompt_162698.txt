You are a decision theorist and philosopher of action whose specialisms are intertemporal choice and the foundations of rational choice theory. Work carefully: before you write anything final, brainstorm candidate requirements freely, throw out the ones you judge spurious, and open with a short preamble listing what you discarded and why. Keep the weight on the criteria and on the cases behind them; any scoring of existing views belongs in one compact table at the very end.

The deliverable. I want a specification for a theory of diachronic rationality: what a theory of how an agent's preferences, choices, and plans at different times should relate to one another would have to deliver to count as adequate. Fix the purpose as follows: adequate for two jobs, and say if you think a third job is really the one that matters. Job one is critical — the theory must be able to underwrite, or explain away, the ordinary practice of calling a person's behaviour over time irrational. Job two is advisory and design-oriented — it must be able to say whether a particular agent should tie herself to a commitment device, and whether a designer, clinician, or institution ought to offer one.

The engine of argument in this area is the money pump, and my suspicion is that the literature's grip on which pumps count is much weaker than its grip on the verdicts. So I want the specification to be built around the following cases, each of which I regard as a place where a plausible-sounding requirement may be over- or under-generated. For each, say whether vulnerability to that pump is really a defect in the agent, and if so what follows for the content of the requirement.

First, the subsidized pump: a cycle of trades returns the agent to the bundle she began with and leaves her poorer, but only because the exploiter paid her a subsidy at every step; running the cycle costs the exploiter more than it extracts, so no trader with any incentive will ever run it.

Second, the fragmented pump: the cycle takes four trades at four different times with four different counterparties. Each counterparty profits only if the later ones play along, and each has an incentive to take her gain and walk away, leaving the agent with a net gain.

Third, the vanishing-step pump: an infinite sequence of trades squeezed into a finite window, where the agent's loss halves at every round and total loss converges to something small, say ten dollars, no matter how long the sequence runs.

Fourth, the instructive pump: the first cycle costs the agent money, she is a normal learner, she notices, she revises her preferences, and the cycle can never be run again.

Fifth, the solitary pump: there is no counterparty at all. The agent's own sequence of choices, each of them the thing she most prefers at the moment she makes it, carries her by the lights of her earlier self to the worst of the outcomes that were then open to her.

Sixth, the anticipatory pump: her preferences change in a way that everyone, herself included, regards as benign or even as an achievement — she trains for years and becomes the kind of person who wants something different — and a trader who foresees the change charges her at each transition.

From these I expect something sharper than immunity to money pumps: a feasibility filter stating which features a pump must have for vulnerability to it to count against an agent, and an argument for that filter rather than a stipulation. I also expect you to consider the requirements that have nothing to do with pumps at all, and I think several of these are where the real action is: the theory must handle preference change that is endogenous to choice itself, so that the person becomes the sort of person who wants the thing by doing the thing; it must handle an agent who is uncertain, at the time of choosing, about what she will later want; it must be explicit about whether its verdicts are about preference structures, about choice procedures, or about plans, and it should respect that these can come apart; it must say whether rationality over time is gradational or binary, and whether the gradations track money lost, opportunities forgone, or something else; and it must satisfy a joint-followability condition, that the recommendations it issues at different times can be executed together by one agent with ordinary capacities, since a theory that says save on Monday and spend on Tuesday in a way that cannot be jointly carried out is defective in a way the money-pump frame hides.

For every requirement on your final list, give the case or consideration that motivates it and mark it as non-negotiable, as negotiable, or as a prejudice of one camp that has been smuggled into the list. Then do the part I most want: identify any pair or subset of your own requirements that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, show why, and say which requirement you would drop and what the resulting theory would look like. Finally, name the single requirement that, in your judgement, excludes the most candidate theories, and defend that choice.

End with the compact table: no more than half a dozen candidate theories, described in their own terms rather than by author — momentary time-indexed preferences with myopic choice, the same preferences with sophisticated choice, plan-based or resolute views, views that put the verdict in a preference over whole lives or temporally extended outcomes, and views that make diachronic rationality a matter of reasons-responsiveness in belief and desire — scored against the four or five requirements you take to be hardest.
