I work on the ranking side of an RLHF pipeline, no philosophy background beyond some vaguely rationalist-adjacent blog reading, and a teammate sent me a post about how to aggregate preferences from multiple human labelers. It leans on something called Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, and I genuinely can't tell how much is real math and how much is the author smuggling in their own politics. Roughly, the key paragraph says:

"Harsanyi's aggregation theorem settles the question of how to add up people's preferences. Assume each person's preferences over risky prospects can be written as a utility function in the von Neumann–Morgenstern sense, and assume the social ranking respects Pareto — if everyone prefers x to y, society prefers x to y. Then Harsanyi proved that society's ranking has to be represented by a weighted sum of the individual utility functions, with fixed weights. So utilitarianism is the only rational moral theory, and the theorem also pins down the weights: they come out equal, because Harsanyi's impartial-observer argument shows a rational planner has to imagine a 1-in-n chance of being each person. A useful side effect is that interpersonal comparisons of utility — the thing everyone used to say was impossible — fall out of the math for free, you don't have to assume them. The result has since been strengthened: any aggregation rule that is Pareto-efficient and independent of irrelevant alternatives must be a weighted sum, which is why basically every modern preference-aggregation setup, including reward-model averaging in RLHF, is a linear combination, and why the weights ought to be equal. The one real objection is that it assumes individuals are vNM-rational, and since actual people violate those axioms in experiments, the whole thing collapses."

Two things feel off to me but I can't say why. My teammate kept muttering that "Pareto is doing something sneaky here" and I couldn't follow the point at all. And the equal-weights bit seems too convenient — if the theorem permits any weights, then "so use equal weights" doesn't look like it follows, but maybe I'm misreading which result is which, since the post talks about two different arguments like they're one.

Can you go through it and tell me, bit by bit, what someone who actually works on social choice or decision theory would accept, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong? I especially want to know: is the "interpersonal comparison falls out for free" claim defensible, does the "violating vNM axioms refutes it" objection actually refute anything, and does the conclusion my team cares about — that our reward-model aggregation should be a linear combination with equal weights — survive after the summary gets corrected?
