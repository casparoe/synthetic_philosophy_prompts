I teach a 75-minute session, once, to about 18 undergrads in a strategic interaction / philosophy of rational choice course — mostly non-maths students, arithmetic only, nothing on expected utility yet. They've allready seen the prisoner's dilemma and an informal pass at Nash equilibrium. I have one TA who can help run things.

I want them to feel the pull of what people call a safe Pareto improvement. The rough idea as I hold it: you take the way your agent already plays some game, and you change it so that no matter what the other side does, they end up at least as well off as they'd have been before, and you don't lose anything either — and you get that guarantee without having to predict or model them at all. The robustness is the whole point, not the size of the gain. I keep running into this in the AI-coordination stuff (agents bargaining, nobody trusting anybody's model of anybody) and I want students to see why it's a genuinely different kind of argument from "both sides are better off in equilibrium."

Constraints: no real money. Points on a leaderboard, top scorer gets a coffee voucher or a bit of participation credit, something small. No lecture up front, and I dont want to say the phrase "safe Pareto improvement" until the end — ideally they invent something like it themselves and only then get the name.

What I'd like from you:

- A game we can actually play in the room, in pairs, over several rounds, with clean rules and a payoff scheme in points. I was thinking some version of the divide-the-surplus / Nash demand game (both name an amount; if the total fits you each get what you named; otherwise both get nothing) since the conflict cases are so visible, but if something else lands the point better, say so and say why.
- A phase where they stop submitting mere numbers and start submitting rules — things conditional on what the other side does — without me hinting where to go. What should the submission sheet actually look like? How do I resolve a pair of rules that refer to each other, or that loop, or that nobody can evaluate?
- Some in-activity way of testing whether a proposed rule is genuinely safe rather than just nice-sounding. My thought was to give one or two students the role of adversary: their job is to find a partner behaviour that makes the patched rule come out worse than the unpatched one. Does that work, and how do I make it concrete enough that they can actually execute it?
- A later round where the guarantee stops being the thing that matters, or straight up breaks, and they notice. I have a vague sense that the trouble is that the guarantee quantifies over the other side's behaviour while holding it fixed, so it isn't an equilibrium claim and it doesn't survive the other side getting to react to your patch — but I'm not sure how to make that land in the room instead of just asserting it.
- A timeline with minutes.

Include what I should say, what I should ask, and roughly what they'll say back.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and what to do about each.
