I teach a graduate seminar on distributive justice roughly every other year, and the block on luck egalitarianism has stopped working. Every time it runs the same course: we set out the view — that inequalities tracing to brute luck are unjust while those flowing from the choices people make, from their option luck, are not, so that justice aims to neutralize the former and leave the latter intact — and then we spend two sessions on the relational critique associated with Anderson, on the luck egalitarians' replies (that the view is about responsibility, not desert; that insurance and ex ante neutralization blunt the harshness worry; that the critics have conflated a theory of distribution with a theory of blame), and we finish exactly where we began. My students can now recite both sides; nobody has moved, including me. One of my doctoral students is thinking about writing on this, and before I encourage her I want to know whether there is a live question here or only a well-rehearsed standoff.

I do not want a survey of the positions; I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement. My own hypothesis, which I would rather you test than flatter, is that there are two questions under one heading: a question about the currency and content of distributive justice — what pattern of holdings justice requires, and whether the brute/option distinction can bear the weight the view puts on it, given that brute luck shapes the very capacities and preferences that are supposed to make a choice a person's own — and a quite different question about the terms on which a state may justify itself to the people it governs, namely whether a redistributive scheme that must, in order to operate, form and act on judgments about which disadvantages are chosen thereby wrongs those it assesses, whatever the resulting distribution turns out to be. If that is right, much of the exchange is mutual talking-past, and there are further questions underneath it: whether the second question is empirical in disguise, turning on how much scrutiny and how much demeaning disclosure any choice-sensitive scheme actually requires; whether the real disagreement is about the baseline, equality as a comparative relation versus equality as a distributive pattern; and whether the two camps are simply treating different cases as their data, the improvident motorcyclist on one side and the person with congenital impairments on the other.

The device I have been using in an attempt to break the deadlock, and on which I would like your verdict, is this. Suppose a jurisdiction adopts what I call transparent luck accounting: a universal scheme that tops up or taxes down each adult's annual consumption to a common baseline, where the adjustment is computed from a standardized audit of the causes of that person's situation — congenital endowment, family background, accident, and their own choices — the audit being performed by an algorithm whose categories are fixed by statute, and no one ever being told that they deserve anything. Two variants. In the first, audit outcomes are published and searchable, so that a neighbor can see that your shortfall was self-inflicted; in the second, they are sealed, and the only person who knows is you. My intuition is that the relational critique bears down hard on the published variant and hardly at all on the sealed one, while the luck egalitarian should be indifferent between them, since the resulting pattern of holdings is identical and only the epistemic conditions differ — which would mean the debate contains an unnamed variable. I am no longer confident, though, because my students raised three things I cannot settle: that a sealed audit is still a judgment made about you, and may wrong you without stigmatizing you; that what disclosure is doing may be a proxy for something else the critic really cares about, such as the scheme's expressive meaning or the security of the worst off; and that the luck egalitarian may have reason to insist on disclosure anyway, for ex ante incentive reasons. So: is my two-questions diagnosis right; does the published-versus-sealed contrast isolate a variable the literature is missing; and what — a distinction, a formal result, an empirical finding about how such schemes operate in practice — would count as progress rather than one more round of the same exchange? If your answer is that no progress should be expected, say so and tell me why.
