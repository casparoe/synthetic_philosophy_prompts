Hi — I'm a science-and-ideas journalist, and I'm working on a column about when you should defer to a probability that some institution hands you: a model's forecast, a risk score, a "one-in-a-hundred-years" number on a planning document. The short version of the column is that deferring to the number is usually the right move and sometimes a trap, and I want one worked case to hang it on. So what I need from you is a thought experiment built to order, and the spec is fiddly, so bear with me.

Build me a case with these features:

- An ordinary decision with ordinary stakes. A hospital, a flood agency, an insurance book, an election desk, a lab deciding whether to keep a trial running. Concrete numbers, one paragraph, no philosopher's furniture: no demons, no perfect predictors, no duplication machines, no time travel.
- The decision-maker has been handed a number that really is (or really was, at the moment it was issued) the objective chance of the very proposition she is betting on. Not a frequency in some coarser class, not an expert's hunch in a probability costume, not the chance of a neighbouring proposition. The real thing. This is the number the Principal Principle — the rule that says your credence in A, given that the chance of A is x, should just be x — tells her to adopt.
- There is a rival number from a forecaster or a desk that is human-grade. Think a good meteorological service with one extra private data feed; not an oracle. Known to be well calibrated on cases of this kind. And it disagrees with the handed-down number.
- The desk has to win. Deferring to the desk has to be what you'd actually want to bet on. And it's edge can't be that it has more of the *good* kind of evidence — it has to come entirely from evidence of exactly the sort the admissibility clause tells you to set aside: a later readout, a partial tally, a noisy measurement. Boring, respectable, the kind of thing any competent practitioner would obviously use. Not a crystal ball, and not a fact about the agent's own position or sampling.
- One constraint to stop the whole thing from beeing cheap: the case can't work by the handed-down number turning out not to be a chance after all, or to be the chance of a different proposition, or the chance at a time that is obviously not the time she acts. If staleness is doing the work, it has to be staleness you can't see from the number itself. And she can't just go and request a fresh chance number — if she could, the case is over before it starts, so tell me why she can't.

Then the part that makes it worth commissioning. Derive rather than stipulate: state the version of the admissibility clause you're working with, then give the credence each of the two deferral policies delivers and show me the bets. Say what makes the desk's number better, given that the other number was by construction a true chance — is it accuracy, calibration, resolution, or something that isn't a scoring-rule virtue at all? Name the single feature of the setup that opens the gap, and show that deleting it collapses the case back into boring, correct deference to the chance. Then give me the strongest reply available to someone defending deference to chance, and be straight with me about whether that reply is just "that's what inadmissible means" or something more interesting. If you conclude that the careful version of the principle survives your own case and only the bumper-sticker version gets burned, say so plainly and tell me what the case then actually shows — an honest near-miss is worth more to me than a fake hit. No literature review, just the case.
