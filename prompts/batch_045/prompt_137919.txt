I am writing the third chapter of my PhD thesis, on moral worth and the condition that an agent act for the right reasons, and I need to pressure-test a set of cases before I run them past participants. I would be grateful for your ratings of them as a philosophical baseline; I know the literature well enough to fill in the references myself, so please do not worry about citing anything.

Every case below involves the same outward act, performed in the same circumstances, so that consequences and the true right-making features are held fixed. Ana finds a wallet in the street containing a driving licence and £180 in cash. Returning it means a forty-minute round trip on foot and missing a train she wanted to catch. The right-making features are, stipulatively: that the money and licence are someone else's, that the owner will be badly inconvenienced without them, and that Ana is the only person in a position to return them. What varies is only the aetiology of her motivation.

A. LUCID. Ana returns the wallet because it belongs to someone else and that person will be badly inconvenienced. She acts on that thought, and it guides her through to the end.

B. DEFERRED. When she picks up the wallet, Ana forms the resolution to return it, and she forms it because of the owner's need and the fact that the wallet is his. By the time she actually knocks on his door, she has forgotten why; she is acting on "this is what I decided to do" plus a general disposition to follow through on resolutions. Had the reason been absent when she formed the resolution, she would not have formed it.

C. OUTSOURCED, DILIGENT. Ana's phone assistant tells her: return it, the owner will be badly hurt otherwise. She has no independent grasp of the reason and could not easily have worked it out herself, but she knows from long experience that the assistant is reliable about such things. She acts because the assistant said so.

D. OUTSOURCED, LAZY. As in C, except that the reason was easily available to her, she does not bother to check it, and she would have acted the same way whatever the assistant had said.

E. NEAR MISS. Ana returns the wallet because she takes the owner to be an old friend from her chess club and wants to do right by a friend. The wallet in fact belongs to a stranger who resembles him. The real right-making features are present and are of the kind her motive was aimed at; she is simply wrong about who instantiates them.

F. MIXED. She is moved roughly equally by the owner's need and by the anticipation of his gratitude and a likely reward. Either motive alone would have been sufficient.

G. HABITUATED. Ana is a practised agent who returns the wallet without any occurrent thought of reasons at all: she simply does it, as she has done many times before. If asked afterwards why she did it she would cite the owner's need immediately and correctly, and had that need been absent she would not have acted.

H. AUDIENCE. Ana returns the wallet mainly because her partner is watching and she could not bear his thinking less of her. She would, however, have returned it anyway had she been alone, and the reason that would then have moved her is the right one.

What I would like from you:

1. Give each case a score from 0 to 100 for moral worth, where 100 is the most moral worth an act of this type could have and 0 is none at all. Then sort them into three or four tiers, with one sentence on why each case falls where it does.

2. I want to know what is actually doing the work in your ordering. Distribute a total of 1.0 across the following four candidate dimensions according to how much of the spread in your scores each explains: (i) whether the agent's actual motivating reason cites the right-making feature; (ii) whether the route from the right-making feature to the action is non-deviant; (iii) whether the agent herself, as opposed to a device, a resolution, or a habit, is the source of the action in a way that makes it creditable to her; (iv) whether the action is counterfactually sensitive to the right-making reason.

3. Is any case here more morally worthy than LUCID? If you think one is, say which and what that would show about the standard accounts.

4. Which case does the least discriminating work — the one on which every account you take seriously returns the same verdict, and which I should therefore drop from the survey?

5. Where do you expect untrained participants to disagree with you, and in which direction? I am especially interested in any case where you suspect the lay reaction is driven by something the philosophical accounts treat as irrelevant.

If you think one of the cases is underdescribed in a way that makes it impossible to rate fairly, say which and tell me what further detail would settle it rather than simply assuming a reading.
