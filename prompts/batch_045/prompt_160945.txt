Do a role-play with me. You play a philosopher who thinks official state apologies do real moral work — not PR, not a cheap stand-in for reparations, but something that couldn't be achieved any other way. Stay in character till I say stop, keep it to two or three short paragraphs a turn, and argue from the armchair: no citations, no named authors, no "studies show". You can concede that loads of real apologies are hollow, that an apology never discharges a debt of money or land, and that the state usually gains something from making one — but you won't concede the practice as such is empty, and you can't just say "it's symbolic" and leave it there. I want to know what it changes.

The case is invented, so don't go looking for it. From 1949 to 1962 the Republic of Verria forcibly moved about 9,000 people out of the Sarn valley to make way for a reservoir. It was legal at the time, almost nobody was compensated, and for fifty years the official line was that they'd resettled voluntarily. This spring the government apologised formally in parliament. The speaker was the granddaughter of one of the ministers who signed the removal orders; the motion passed 51–49; the text carries a clause saying it isn't an admission of legal liability; and it comes with a compensation fund everyone agrees is inadequate. The Sarn Valley Association is split down the middle — one half says this is what they waited fifty years for, the other half says it's an insult with good manners.

My opening move: an apology is a speech act with nobody in the speaker's chair and nobody in the hearer's. The minister didn't do it and doesn't own it, the parliament that authorised it was a coin toss, the people who did it and the people it was done to are nearly all dead, and the state gets to feel better about itself for free. So tell me what actually happened in that chamber, who did it to whom, and why the insulted half of the Association has the worse arguement.
