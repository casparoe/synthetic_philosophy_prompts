I sit on a small committee at a teaching hospital that is revising the documentation policy for our consultation-liaison psychiatry service. Two proposals are on the table. The first, which I will call *diagnostic restraint*: record the presenting problem, the formulation, and the plan, but enter no DSM-style diagnosis into the chart unless it changes immediate management. The second, *diagnostic transparency*: give the patient the diagnosis in writing at the end of the encounter, on the grounds that withholding a name for what is happening to them is itself a form of paternalism.

At our last meeting a senior colleague I respect closed the discussion by remarking that mental illness is a myth. As I understood him, the point was not that people do not suffer — he plainly thinks they do — but that the two proposals differ only in bookkeeping: if there is nothing of the relevant kind to be named or withheld, then the dispute between naming and not naming is empty, and we should choose on administrative grounds alone. A colleague on the other side replied that if that is right, restraint is more urgent than ever, since medicalizing distress under a false description is precisely the harm the remark was introduced to identify. The same sentence is therefore being made to support two opposite policies, and I doubt either speaker is reading it the same way.

What I want is a disambiguation, not a verdict on Szasz. Please set out the distinct precise claims the sentence could be making, ordered roughly from the trivial to the radical. I can already see four or five candidates — that psychiatric kinds have no lesions; that they have no causal essence and are only symptom clusters; that *illness* is being applied to something outside its proper extension; that the vocabulary is a metaphor rather than a description; that it is a myth in the sense of a story that does social work whether or not it is true; that diagnoses are instruments of social control whose content is exhausted by their function — and for each: what would have to be true for it to be true, whether it is plausible, and, most importantly, whether it supports my colleague's conclusion of policy parity, or the opposite conclusion, or neither.

To keep the answers from floating, I have been sorting readings with three tests, and I would like each reading's verdict on them stated explicitly:

1. *The biomarker test.* Suppose that tomorrow a reliable neural marker is found for what we now call melancholic depression, present in most but not all of the people currently carrying the diagnosis. Does the reading say the illness was real all along, that it becomes real, that the marker is irrelevant to the question, or that the question was malformed?

2. *The asymmetry test.* Nearly everyone who repeats the sentence still counts a slow-growing frontal tumor that produces apathy and flattened affect as a genuine disease. What does that asymmetry diagnose about the reading — is the work being done by the mind, by the absence of a lesion, by the voluntariness of the resulting behavior, by the social response, or by something else?

3. *The name-causation test.* Our electronic record contains a code I will call *adjustment-spectrum distress*, which exists mainly because insurers require a billable code; clinicians report that patients who receive it subsequently describe their experience differently and sometimes seek care they would not otherwise have sought. If naming is causally efficacious in this way, which readings can accommodate that, and which are committed to treating the naming as merely epiphenomenal or merely harmful?

Two further constraints on the answer. First, I need at least one reading on which the sentence is fully compatible with the claim that mental suffering is real, serious, and something medicine ought to respond to, and at least one reading on which even the substitute vocabulary Szasz preferred, problems in living, is itself suspect; I want to know how far the deflationary pressure travels. Second, my colleague, when pressed, votes for diagnostic transparency in practice, because he thinks patients are entitled to the name their insurer will use. Which readings make that combination consistent, and which make it an inconsistency he should abandon?

Please lay the readings out as a menu with verdicts rather than selecting the one you favor: a table or numbered list with columns for content, the three test results, plausibility, and what each implies for the two policies. Where a reading's fate genuinely depends on empirical input that neither of us can check without a literature search, say so instead of guessing.
