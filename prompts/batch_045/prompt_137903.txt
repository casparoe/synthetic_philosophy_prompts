Me and my friend got into a three-hour argument last night after re-watching a documentary about the Exxon Valdez, and I want someone to settle the conceptual part of it rather than the legal part. The setup, as I remember it: after the 1989 spill the government went after damages not just for lost fishing and tourism but for so-called passive use or existence value — the loss ordinary people supposedly suffer even though they'd never visit the place, never consume anything from it, and there is no market in which they ever make any choice that remotely touches it. To put a dollar figure on that they used contingent valuation surveys, asking people what they would be willing to pay to prevent another spill like it; a panel of economists NOAA convened in the early 90s, with a couple of Nobel laureates on it, gave the method a qualified thumbs-up with some methodological guardrails, and a lot of economists went on saying that hypothetical answers to hypothetical questions are not choices and therefore reveal nothing whatsoever. My friend is firmly on the second side and he keeps invoking revealed preference theory to back it up, and my gut says he's conscripting that theory for a job it was never designed for, but I can't say precisely what the job was supposed to be.

So: take revealed preference theory seriously as the lens and do a careful mapping of this episode through it. Is it a positivist move to purge utility of unobservable mental content, a consistency condition on choice data (the weak and generalized axioms, the Afriat inequalities, that whole apparatus), or a welfare criterion — and what does each of those readings say about the thing these surveys are supposedly measuring? Be disciplined about the mapping: who is the agent, what is the choice set, what is the budget constraint, is stated willingness to pay a price in the relevant sense when nothing is ever actually bought, and what should we make of the fact that willingness to pay and willingness to accept come apart by huge multiples here — my friend says a theory that lets those drift severalfold apart isn't a theory of preference at all, and I don't know if that's fair. And I want the honest negative part too: where is the theory simply silent, because the good in question isn't in anyone's opportunity set and so there is no pattern of choices to be consistent or inconsistent with?

Here's the move that actually broke the argument, and I'd like you to take it seriously instead of batting it away. My friend's fallback was that the survey response is itself a genuine choice: you really do decide what number to write down, and what you are purchasing with that number is the experience of answering, a warm glow, the sense of being the sort of person who says yes to protecting wilderness — so the responses do reveal a preference, just for a different good than the one named in the question. I think that's too clever by half but I can't say why. If he's right, does it vindicate revealed preference as a method while gutting it as a way of valuing anything, or does it show the consistency reading and the welfare reading come apart in a case nobody built the theory for? And the other direction: does the episode tell us something about what utility means once you've gone revealed-preference — that it's nothing but an index for choice behavior and has no business valuing goods nobody chooses, or that the whole project of defining utility by choice leaves you with exactly nothing to say at the moment you most need to say something? Please don't give me a survey of the valuation literature or a reading list; I want the mapping, where it holds, where it goes quiet, and if there's some distinction or test that would actually settle whether those numbers mean anything, what it is.
