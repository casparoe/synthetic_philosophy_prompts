I do a weekly column on science and ideas, and Im putting together a piece on the St Petersburg paradox — not the history, I've read the standard version of that a hundred times, I mean the live question of what a gamble is actually worth. I want one concrete invented game to describe to readers, built to spec, and I keep failing to build it myself. Help?

The spec:

1. One paragraph. A real game somebody could actually be offered: a public randomization device, resolved in an afternoon, in front of witnesses, top prize a sum the sponsor could genuinely hand over. No infinite payouts, no unpayable debts, no science fiction, no immortal gambler.

2. The player has to be someone whose utility really is flat in money across everything at stake. I want that to fall out of the setup — give them an actual reason, a fund that turns dollars into some good at a fixed rate, whatever works — not just be announced. No diminishing marginal returns, and no sneaky thresholds either: I know a funder who needs exactly $X to build one clinic will turn down fat-tailed gambles, and that's a different column.

3. The sponsor can genuinely pay the top prize, so the 'no casino could really pay that, so the real game is the capped game' move is closed off as well.

4. And the 'sure, one play is a bad deal, but play it a thousand times and it comes good' defense has to fail — for a reason inside the case, not because I stipulate that you only get one go. My own attempt was to let people buy as many tickets as they like but have every ticket pay off on a single public draw, so more tickets buy you no diversification at all. I cant tell whether that's legitimate or just a trick, so tell me.

5. Numbers: whatever entry fee expected value recommends has to be at least ten times what the median-or-typical-outcome rule recommends, and at least ten times what the many-plays rule recommends.

Then, after the scenario:

- Give me the maximum entry fee under (a) expected money, (b) expected utility with the player's actual utility, (c) the median-or-typical-outcome rule, (d) the many-plays rule. Show the arithmetic, briefly.
- Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to the gap if you take it out.
- Tell me how somebody who thinks expected value is the whole story would wave the case away.

And be honest with me: if, once those three exits are closed, the reader's 'no way am I paying that' reaction just can't be vindicated by any rule you respect, say that flat out. Then tell me the smallest change to the player's circumstances that brings the reaction back, and why that change is doing the job diminishing marginal utility normally does.

What I can't use is a case that stipulates the answer — a game where I announce that everyone agrees you should only pay four dollars — because the point of the column is that the verdicts fall out of the rules rather than out of my mouth.
