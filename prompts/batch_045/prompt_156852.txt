I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I run a fortnightly lunch reading group with seven or eight colleagues at work. All of them are comfortable with probability and have implemented some kind of Bayesian filter or tracker, but none has studied formal epistemology, and for them updating means Bayes' theorem applied to a proposition that has become certain.

In one 55-minute session, with a whiteboard, I want to get them to the point where they can apply Jeffrey conditionalization — updating on evidence that is itself uncertain — and, more importantly, see what adopting that rule commits them to. Please don't explain the rule to me; I can follow it on paper. Plan how I should explain it.

The session has to get across: why ordinary conditionalization cannot even be stated for the case that motivates the generalization (an experience that moves your confidence in E without making any proposition certain, so that there is nothing to condition on); rigidity, as the substantive assumption of the rule rather than a technicality; what the rule degenerates to when the new probability of a cell is 1, and why that is a sanity check rather than a curiosity; the fact that successive updates of this kind do not in general commute, and what that means for anyone who would bolt the rule onto a system that streams observations; and some rough sense of how the rule relates to the idea of choosing the distribution closest to your prior among those satisfying the new constraints. On that last point my understanding — which I want you to correct if it is wrong — is that in the single-partition case the two agree and come out closed-form, whereas with constraints on two overlapping partitions there is no closed form and you end up in iterative proportional fitting. Tell me whether any of that belongs in a 55-minute session, and if so where.

Specifically, I want:

1. The order in which the components must be introduced, and for each, the dependency that makes it impossible to postpone: what has to be in place before the next thing is even statable rather than merely easier.
2. One example to carry the session. Tell me what properties it must have and, as importantly, what it must lack. My default is something from service monitoring — a noisy alert that shifts my confidence that a deployment is degraded — but I suspect that example quietly contains something I don't want, whether an event I could have conditioned on instead, a proposition that effectively becomes certain, or a case where the experience also teaches me something about how E bears on A and not only about how likely E is. If a single example cannot carry both the rule and the cases where the rule misbehaves, say so and say where in the arc to switch examples.
3. The misconceptions to expect, and the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off rather than corrected afterward. My guesses: that this is just Bayes' theorem with a softened likelihood and nothing more; that rigidity is a consequence of the framework rather than an extra assumption; that the failure of order-independence is an implementation bug with a patch; that showing ordinary conditionalization cannot handle the motivating case shows it to be wrong rather than merely inapplicable. Correct these, add the ones I have missed, and tell me which are worth pre-empting in advance.
4. What I should leave out at this level and openly refuse to explain. I would rather name a topic as out of scope than gesture at it in the last two minutes.
5. Where the soft-evidence alternative belongs. Someone in the room will have met handling of uncertain observations in Bayesian networks, where you hold a likelihood ratio fixed and run ordinary Bayes on a virtual observation instead of moving the probability of E. Should I invite that, and if so as a contrast, as the same rule in different notation, or as a distraction to be waved off?
6. One question to close on that would show me whether they have understood the rule rather than followed the algebra to the right number.

Finally, since you are giving me a plan rather than a proof: say where you are least confident in it. Identify the step in your proposed sequence most likely either to lose the room or to rest on a claim that is actually contested among people who work on this, and tell me what a colleague could say or ask that ought to make me reorder, drop, or invert that step. Where the literature is genuinely unsettled, flag it as unsettled rather than quietly picking a side.
