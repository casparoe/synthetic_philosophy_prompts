I am Professor of Philosophy at a British university and, for the past three years, Convener of the Standing Orders Committee of a learned society of some nine hundred members. The Standing Orders are self-amending, and in the last two years they have begun to be amended by members who have read about self-reference and plainly enjoy testing it — a self-amending game of the Nomic kind that Peter Suber devised in 1982 has, I suspect, been doing the rounds. I must rule on points of order in the room, in a minute or two, before an audience of scientists and historians who are not philosophers; a ruling can be appealed to Council, but there is no arbiter beyond that, and I do not want an answer of the form a court would decide this differently, since I am asking about the internal logic of the rule system rather than about what the law would make of it.

The relevant Orders are these:

1. Any Standing Order may be amended or repealed by a simple majority of members present and voting at a quorate General Meeting.
2. Standing Orders 1, 2, 3 and 4 (the entrenched Orders) may be amended or repealed only by a two-thirds majority at each of two successive General Meetings.
3. No amendment takes effect before the close of the meeting at which it is adopted.
4. The Convener's ruling on a point of order is final and is not open to debate at that meeting.
5. If part of an amendment is of no effect, the remainder has effect if it is capable of standing alone.
6. The Secretary shall maintain a consolidated text, which shall be conclusive evidence of the Standing Orders.

The Orders are silent on: whether Order 2 protects Order 2 itself; what happens when two amendments to the same Order are carried at one meeting; whether an amendment may alter its own commencement; whether an amendment passed under Order 1 may make itself entrenched; and what the Convener is to do when the Orders yield no answer at all. Cases I have actually faced, or expect to: a motion to delete the 2 from the list in Order 2, on the argument that this is an amendment to a definition rather than to an entrenched Order, and that to say otherwise is to let an amendment rule protect itself, which is supposedly circular; a motion to amend Order 3 and, in the same motion, to change the voting threshold for a ballot held later in that same meeting, the amendment to Order 3 to take effect at once; an ordinary motion to add a new Order 9 with a rider that Order 9 may be amended only by a three-quarters majority at an Annual General Meeting of which six months' notice has been given; two motions at one meeting, one making the subscription unamendable and one resetting it, where whichever is put to the vote last wins and nothing fixes the order; a motion to repeal Order 4, on which I must rule while Order 4 governs that very ruling; a motion to adopt the Secretary's consolidated text as authentic after two years of drift; and a motion to repeal Orders 1 and 2 outright, leaving the Society with no amending power.

What I want is a decision procedure: the questions, in order, that I or a successor who is not a philosopher can run on an amendment as it is moved, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points at which the procedure returns out of order, and the points at which it must stop and hand the matter to a human judgement about what the Society wants rather than about what the rules say. It must fit on one side of A4, be runnable in about two minutes, and yield a reason I can state in a sentence. Please also tell me which distinctions any careful account of self-amendment would insist must survive that simplification and which can honestly be dropped, with the reason in each case; my own rough cut is between a proposal's adoption conditions, its content and its commencement date, and I would like to know whether that is the right place to cut or whether you would draw the lines differently.

Then apply the procedure in full to three of the cases above: the deletion of 2 from the list in Order 2, the self-commencing amendment to Order 3, and the repeal of Orders 1 and 2. I should say that I have twice ruled that a motion to entrench a new Order at birth is out of order, on the ground that one majority cannot bind its successors; if your procedure implies that I was wrong, I would rather be told than spared. Please also state plainly what the procedure cannot do — where it will misfire, and where it supplies only a decision and not a justification that will convince the room.

Finally: if the procedure needs the Orders to contain something they lack, say a rule of priority between competing amendments, a rule forbidding a proposal from altering the conditions of its own adoption, or a residual clause for cases the Orders do not determine, then tell me what to insert — and how such an insertion is to be made, given that any amendment proposing it is itself exposed to the puzzle it is meant to solve, so that the procedure must say what it is to apply once to its own adoption. Please write in British English, and do not give me citations, a bibliography or a reading list; I want the argument, not the sources.
