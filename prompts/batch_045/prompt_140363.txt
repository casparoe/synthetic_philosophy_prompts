I work on a four-person analytics team at a city housing agency. For about a year we have ranked every rental building in the city by a model score estimating the probability that the building currently harbours a serious code violation, and inspectors have so far been free to plan their own routes with the ranking as one input among many. A new proposal would change that: each inspector would be given a weekly expectation of clearing a fixed number of buildings from the top of the ranked list, and the ranking would be refreshed each night. At the meeting where this was discussed, a colleague closed the discussion with the sentence "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure," spoken as if it settled the question of whether to adopt the proposal. I don't think it does, but I also can't say exactly why, because I can't tell what precise claim the sentence is making. As far as I can see it could be asserting anything from something nearly tautological to something strong and probably false, and which one it is makes all the difference to what we should do.

The distinctions I keep circling around, and that I would like made precise rather than blurred, are these. One: the *proxy* claim — that optimising a proxy degrades its correlation with the thing you actually care about. Two: the *construct* claim — that the target changes the underlying thing itself, so that the measure still tracks something real but no longer the thing we wanted (cosmetic repairs and paperwork instead of habitable apartments). Three: something closer to the econometric point about regimes and off-support extrapolation — that our score was validated only on the mixture of buildings inspectors historically visited, so the problem isn't that targeting corrupts a good measure but that pushing to the top of the list moves us into a region where the score-goal relationship was never estimated; on this reading the slogan is really about extrapolation, not about corruption. Four: a deflationary reading, that the score was always a weak proxy and targeting merely made its weakness visible, so the slogan is doing the work of an excuse for a measurement failure we caused earlier. And there is a reading that runs the other way, which I want taken seriously rather than waved off: making something a target can *improve* measurement, because interventions generate the variation and the ground-truth labels you need to detect label noise and estimate what actually causes what — and our case may be one where a held-out stream of inspections chosen at random, plus a target on the rest, would give us better information than we have now. One further wrinkle specific to us: the labels our model was trained on were themselves produced by inspections scheduled under an older quota system, so whatever gaming that system induced is already baked into the training data; call it ancestral gaming if you like. I'd like to know whether the slogan, on any of its readings, has anything to say about a measure that was gamed before anyone targeted it, and whether that is a different failure mode from the ones the slogan describes.

What I want from you is a menu, not a single interpretation: lay out the distinct precise theses the sentence could be expressing, ordered roughly from the trivial to the substantive, and for each say whether it is a platitude, a defensible empirical generalisation, or straightforwardly false, and — most importantly for me — whether it would actually support my colleague's conclusion that we should not set a target on the score. If some readings only support weaker remedies (keep the expectation but don't publish the scores; randomise a control stream of inspections; audit for gaming directly; rotate in outcome measures the inspectors can't see), say which reading supports which. A final complication I can't resolve: if we build a monitor for gaming itself, doesn't the same worry apply to the monitor, and is there a reading of the slogan on which it applies to itself and a reading on which it doesn't? Please write this as a list I can bring to the next meeting, with no citations, further-reading suggestions, or named studies — I'll be reading your answer offline and want the reasoning, not sources. For each reading, tell me how confident you are that it is the right precisification and that your verdict on it is correct, flag where you are extrapolating rather than recalling anything firm, and say explicitly what would change your mind — which argument, or which pattern in our own inspection data, would lead you to a different verdict on the proposal, and where you think your reasoning is most likely to be wrong.
