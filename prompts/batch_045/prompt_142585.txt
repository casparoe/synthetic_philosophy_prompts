I've backed myself into something stupid and I want someone to tell me which plank to pull out. It's for a paper I'm meant to be finishing on fundamentality.

What I want the paper to say, briefly: I want "fundamental" to be a derived notion — x is fundamental iff x has no ground — and I want grounding to run between *things* as well as between facts. I really don't want to be stuck saying that the fact that there's a table is grounded in the *fact* that these particles are arranged table-wise; I want to say it's grounded in the particles, arranged like that. Otherwise it feels like the facts have come loose from the things. Call that first bit premise 1.

(1) For any concrete thing x, the fact that x exists is grounded in x.
(2) If the fact that x exists is grounded in Γ, then x itself is grounded in Γ. (This is the one I made up and can't shake. It feels like bookkeeping: whatever x's existing holds in virtue of is what x itself holds in virtue of.)
(3) Grounding is irreflexive — nothing grounds itself.
(4) x is fundamental iff x has no ground at all.
(5) Concrete things exist, so for each of them there's the fact that it exists.

Now take any concrete thing x. By (5) there's the fact that x exists. By (1) that fact is grounded in x. By (2), x is grounded in x. So by (4) x isn't fundamental, and by (3) that can't happen anyway. So: no concrete thing is fundamental, and my premises are flatly inconsistent, which is worse than merely having an absurd conclusion. My coffee cup is not, I take it, self-grounding.

Everything I try feels like cheating. Dropping (2) is the obvious move, but then I don't know what I'm saying when I say a *thing* is fundamental — the grounding status of the fact that it exists and the grounding status of the thing itself can just drift apart, and I've got two notions of "fundamental" where I wanted one. My supervisor says (1) is the real culprit and (2) is fine, which I honestly can't see. Restricting (2) to full grounds doesnt help. Restricting the whole argument to contingent things doesn't help either — the cup is contingent. And saying that the fact that x exists just *is* x, or is x's existing, seems to make (1) either empty or a case of self-grounding all over again, but I can't say why cleanly.

Which step do I give up, and why did it look fine?
