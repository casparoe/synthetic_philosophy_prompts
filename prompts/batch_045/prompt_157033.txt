Two friends of mine went at it for the better part of two hours over dinner last night, and then I spent the drive home arguing with my girlfriend about who had won, which went about as well as you'd expect. Now I've been asked to referee and I honestly cant do it.

The topic was whether the invention of agriculture was a mistake. Sam was defending something close to that famous line about it being the worst mistake in the history of our species — he says he read it in some old magazine essay back in high school and it stuck to him. Nadia was pushing back. Roughly, in order:

1. Sam: it's a package deal and the bill was paid in bodies. He says skeletal remains from right after the switch show people getting shorter, more cavities, more signs of anemia and infection, and work time going up — foragers in decent environments, he claimed, spend something like twenty hours a week on getting food and farmers far more — plus farming means putting all your calories into one or two crops, so one bad season is a famine instead of just a lean month.

2. Sam: and the hours understate it anyway. Forager work is varied, skilled, self-directed and social; farm work is repetitive and dictated by the calendar. Even if the hours came out the same, the experience wouldn't.

3. Nadia: you're comparing farming's worst centuries to foragers at their best, and to a biased sample at that — the foragers anthropologists actually got to study are the ones who'd been pushed onto marginal land, deserts and arctic, after farmers took the good valleys. And forager life has its own ugly tail: high child mortality, periodic real starvation, and violence rates that are nothing to write home about. So "the foragers were better off" isn't established even on the evidence Sam likes.

4. Nadia: and a more basic point — nobody decided this. There was no committee, no vote, and no available alternative: once some groups farmed they outnumbered everyone else, and any group that tried to sit it out got pushed aside. A thing can be a tragedy, or a ratchet, or a trap, but it can't be a mistake if there was no agent and nothing else on the menu.

5. Sam: mistakes don't need deciders — we say a species evolved itself into a dead end, or that somebody sleepwalked into a bad marriage. And a ratchet is the paradigm case of a mistake: individually sensible steps that leave everybody worse off. Then he pulled out this device: draw a random human life out of all the lives that have ever been lived. Farming massively increases the number of lives on offer and most of the new ones are short, sick and hierarchically organized, so the switch lowers the quality of a randomly drawn human life. By that measure it isn't close.

6. Nadia: the random-life gadget just repackages the averaging-versus-totaling fight. It treats every extra life that's worth living as counting for nothing, which is a technical position in population ethics that Sam hasn't argued for and probably doesn't want — on his own gadget, a world of ten million equally flourishing people is no better than a world of ten thousand of them. So the verdict he wants rests on one contested technicality rather than on the bones and the timetables he started with.

7. Nadia: the causal story is wrong too. What produces hierarchy isn't growing food as such; it's wealth that is storable, heritable and defensible — herds, land, granaries — plus a state able to tax it. There are farming peoples with barely any stratification, and they tend to be the ones with abundant land and no population pressure. So agriculture was the occasion for inequality, not its cause, and the mistake, if there was one, is downstream and in principle fixable. Which matters because the original verdict can't guide anything anyway: at current population we can't go back, so "it was all a mistake" is a verdict with no advice attached.

8. Sam: the separability is fake. Sedentism, storable grain and population density show up together, and wherever they show up together hierarchy follows within a few centuries. The egalitarian-farming cases are exactly the ones where the Malthusian pressure hasn't arrived yet — and farming guarantees that it arrives. So "occasion, not cause" splits a difference that isn't really there.

Then tell me who is more right overall, and what if anything both of them are missing that's doing work in the background of the whole exchange. I have a feeling there's some unstated assumption both of them are sharing, but I can't put my finger on it and my girlfriend's guess was definately wrong. Don't send me off to any reading or sources either — there's enough in the argument itself to work with, and I want your own reasoning on it.

Verdict: Sam / Nadia / Neither — followed by five words or fewer saying why.
