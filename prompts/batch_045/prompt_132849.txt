I am an undergraduate in a final-year seminar on the ethics of the long run — the term has been about existential risk, the size of the future, and the thought that a very small chance of an astronomically large good can dominate a certainty of a modest one. Most of my classmates have come to accept that thought, and I want the session I lead to put pressure on it from the inside rather than from the outside.

The case I want to stage: a stranger tells you that, in exchange for something that costs you almost nothing, they will use powers beyond this world to bring about a good so vast that even a hopelessly tiny credence in their sincerity makes the trade rational; and if you balk, they simply raise the number until the arithmetic goes through again. I would rather the usual name for this case never be said in the room before the debrief, so I will keep calling it the stranger's offer, and I would like your design to do the same.

The constraints: one 75-minute slot, thirteen to fifteen students including me, a seminar room with a projector, and a budget of about £20 for a prize. No lecture up front, and ideally no vocabulary from decision theory — expected value, utility, fanaticism — until after everyone has already committed themselves in writing. What I want from you is a runnable activity, not a discussion plan: rules, materials, a payoff scheme that does not need real money, a timeline, the debrief, and an honest account of what will go wrong and what the exercise cannot deliver.

What the group should run into, in this order, and each time by doing something rather than by being told something:

First, an offer that is simply there before class starts — cards placed on desks before anyone arrives, no framing from me, a single fixed and astronomically large number, and a private anonymous decision each student has to record and pay for. The cost should be genuinely felt but trivial: I was thinking of ten units each, with units converted at the end into raffle entries for a voucher, and anything handed over is gone. That tension is the whole point, so please keep both halves of it.

Second, an escalation. The stranger returns — I imagine a second card, or a projected sequence — and each student must first write down the exact number of lives at which they would pay, and then watch that number be exceeded. I want them to feel that there is no stopping place they can defend, and that every threshold they name is arbitrary.

Third, rival strangers. Different desks should receive offers that cannot all be acted on: one promising that payment averts the catastrophe, one promising that payment causes it, one promising that only those who refuse are spared, one offering for a smaller fee to neutralise the first. I would also like this round to include the version in which the stranger adds that they could produce the good at no cost to themselves and will not do so unless paid, since the fact that the stakes are manufactured seems to me to matter. The experience I am after is that the reasoning which seemed to force one payment forces all of them, and can then be run again by anyone at any time for as long as the units last.

Fourth, a contrast case with the same shape of number but a probability that came from somewhere — an actuarial or model-based estimate behind some cheap real intervention — so that the group has to articulate the difference, and discovers that the smallness of the probability cannot be what does the work.

Then the debrief, which should map each of those four moments onto a distinct part of the difficulty: why the product of a credence and a payoff outruns any cost you can name; why no finite cap survives the escalation; why a dominance argument that commands incompatible actions commands none; why the failure may lie in aggregation rather than in belief; and what we are saying when we say a probability came from somewhere. It should end at the real fork — whether value has a ceiling and expected-value reasoning is consequently fanatical, or value is bounded and we owe an account of where the bound comes from — and it should be plain that the longtermist argument my classmates liked in week two sits on one side of that fork.

Please also write out the wording of the cards so I can print them, tell me what to say if someone names the case in the first ten minutes or refuses to play along, and be frank about which feature of the problem the activity cannot reproduce and which therefore has to come from me.
