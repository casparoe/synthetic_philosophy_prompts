I write about the science of consciousness for a newspaper, and I have come to accept heterophenomenology in Dennett's sense: a subject's first-person reports are to be read strictly as reports, as data about what they take their experience to be, and never as data about what their experience is. I want you to map that position's exposure. Suppose we built a third-personal experience meter, a neural or behavioural measure that tracks what a subject is undergoing without asking them, and that it systematically disagreed with their reports in some circumscribed domain, much as reports and discrimination behaviour come apart in blindsight. Would that be a defeater, or would the method simply absorb the meter as one more text to be interpreted, and does either answer reveal something about the method? Give me the things that should move me ranked from fatal to merely awkward, including the ones that have arguably already happened: the interview-based programmes that treat trained first-person reports as evidence in their own right, the subjects who cannot report at all, such as infants, non-human animals, or a system that reasons but does not speak, and the standing charge that the method is verificationism or behaviourism in borrowed clothes. Say which of these its defenders would themselves concede as counting against it, rather than merely what critics allege, and say honestly whether the view has been qualified into something that cannot lose, and whether for a methodological proposal that is a defect or simply a difference in kind that critics have exploited. For what it is worth, what most unsettles me personally is the suspicion that the bracketing is only ever neutrality in principle: in practice, whenever reports conflict with the rest of the evidence, everyone quietly sides with the third-person data, which suggests the famous neutrality is never doing the work it is credited with. Please answer in continuous prose, the way you would write a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
