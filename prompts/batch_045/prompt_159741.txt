I'm 71, spent most of my working life running solid-state NMR on disordered ceramics, and about two years ago I drifted into philosophy through Hofstadter and never really came back. There's a small private foundation over here that hands out a handful of six-month independent-study grants to retired scientists who want to work on something useless, and they want a three-page proposal. I have a half-formed idea and I need help turning it into something a committee of two philosophers and one logician would take seriously.

Here's the thing that got me going. Take the MIU puzzle from Gödel, Escher, Bach: start from MI, the rules are xI → xIU, Mx → Mxx, xIIIy → xUy, xUUy → xy, and the question is whether MU is derivable. It isn't, and the only way to see that is to stop pushing symbols around and notice that the number of I's is never divisible by three. That's Hofstadter's jumping out of the system, and what bugs me is that nobody seems to have a principled account of when that move gives you knowledge as opposed to being a clever trick that happens to work.

My half-formed idea: distinguish two kinds of jump. A bookkeeping jump adds only tracking machinery — labels, counters, colourings, an abacus bolted onto the side of the system — and lets you re-run the argument inside the enlarged system. A oracle jump adds content: it settles a question in the system's own vocabulary from the outside, the way just stipulating MU as a fifth rule would. The mutilated chessboard case (31 dominoes can't cover a board with two diagonally opposite corners removed; count black squares against white) looks like pure bookkeeping — colour the squares and the argument becomes an in-system argument about a coloured board. MIU looks like bookkeeping too, if you bolt a mod-3 counter onto the derivation. So my first conjecture was that a JOOTS is epistemically kosher exactly when the enlarged system is conservative over the original one, proving nothing new in the old vocabulary. Then I noticed the wrinkle that makes this interesting rather than trivial: the MIU argument isn't finite bookkeeping, it's an induction over all possible derivations, and internalizing an induction is precisely the kind of extension that stops being conservative in general. So conservativeness is either the wrong criterion or the right criterion applied to the wrong object, and I cant tell which.

What I want from you: a proposal, three pages maximum, that states one question precise enough to have a determinate answer — not how do thought experiments work, something smaller and actually settleable in six months — explains why the obvious answers don't settle it (I mean the obvious ones: it's just model-theoretic reasoning about a system you can survey; it's a new axiom in disguise; it's whatever the informal notion of an invariant cashes out as), and lays out what I would really do, month by month, on a case list. I want that list fixed: MIU; the chessboard; the halting argument and diagonalization as cases where what gets proved is a claim about the system itself; and at least one case that looks like bookkeeping but turns out not to be, if you can construct one. Say what a finished deliverable is — I was imagining a short test or checklist you could apply to a candidate jump, plus the worked cases, but tell me if that's too much for the time — and say what would count as the project failing. My own guess at the failure mode is that the bookkeeping/oracle distinction collapses back into the definition of a conservative extension and has no bite on the informal cases at all.

Two more things. I keep being tempted to drag in scientific thought experiments — Galileo's two stones tied together, Einstein's elevator — because that's where my instincts actually live, but I suspect that's a second project that would eat the whole six months. Tell me whether to include one as a short final section or leave them out entirely, and be honest about scope either way. And dont give me a bibliography or a literature review with names and dates in it; I'll fill that in myself, and I'd rather the proposal not pretend I've read things I haven't. Write it in a voice I can actually send in: plain, no jargon for its own sake, and if you use a technical term like conservative extension, gloss it in a clause the first time.
