I write about prediction markets, forecasting, and institutional design for a magazine section on science and ideas, and I'm finishing a piece on whether futarchy — the governance scheme whose own slogan is that we should vote on values but bet on beliefs — could ever work outside a blog post. My editor wants a number somewhere in the piece, and I would much rather publish a number with its derivation than a number with a shrug attached, so I'm asking you for a credence and for the machinery that produces it.

Here is the proposition, stated as precisely as I can manage. Take a mid-sized parliamentary democracy of roughly thirty million people with a professional legislature and a competent, well-staffed civil service. Suppose that, starting next year, a defined class of decisions — say, the annual regulatory budget and any infrastructure commitment above a fixed threshold — is required to be made this way: the legislature first ratifies, by ordinary vote, a welfare metric to be maximized; traders then buy contracts that pay out only if a given option is adopted and that resolve against the realized value of that metric five years after adoption; and the option whose market implies the higher expected value is the one the state adopts. My question is about the realized world, not the elegance of the mechanism: over a twenty-year run of, say, two hundred such decisions, would the realized value of that welfare metric be higher than it would have been under the counterfactual continuation of the existing legislative, committee, and bureaucratic process? Give me your probability that yes, and no hedge about how the question is underspecified — I've just specified it, and part of what I want to see is which of my stipulations you think is doing the work.

Then take it apart. I want a decomposition with a number and a one-line justification for each term, at minimum these: (a) that conditional markets on one-off, non-repeating policy questions actually aggregate dispersed information well enough to beat the existing process, given that the market is not forecasting an event but estimating a treatment effect under a counterfactual condition and gets no repeated-trial feedback; (b) that the outcome variable can be specified tightly enough to survive five years of contact with the agencies being measured, i.e. that the metric isn't gamed in the way employment statistics or hospital waiting times get gamed; (c) that the markets won't be too thin to be informative, since capital is locked for five years, each contract is unique, there is no replicating portfolio to arbitrage against, and any informational gain has to be bought with a subsidy; (d) that the trader population won't contain agents who can move the realized metric itself — parties who can both hold contracts and degrade or inflate the outcome they are betting on, and whether any sealed-book or position-cap variant you'd design actually fixes that or just relocates it; (e) that the arrangement is time-consistent, meaning losing coalitions don't redefine the metric, repeal the rule, or refuse to implement a decision they lost. Combine your terms into the headline number, tell me which single term the number is most sensitive to, name any term you think I've left out, and say what evidence would move you most — evidence from internal corporate prediction markets, from small real-money political markets, from forecasting tournaments, or something else. I'm at about 0.3; the market designer I interviewed is at 0.55; a legislative scholar I spoke to is at 0.08, mostly on grounds about agenda control and who decides which questions get routed to a market at all. Tell me where you think that spread actually lives, which of those three is making the mistake, and what the argument is. Please skip citations, bibliographies, and literature reviews — I have no use for them here, and I'd rather have your own numbers than a reconstruction of what has been published.
