For well over a decade of reading these discussions I have watched some version of the same sentence quietly do the work of an argument: a corrigible agent is one that is indifferent to being shut down. I met it again last week in a comment thread on the shutdown problem, where it was offered as close to a knockdown. If your system has a utility function over world states at all, the commenter said, then there is always some world state it ranks above the shutdown state, so it always has some incentive to prevent shutdown, so nothing recognizably goal-directed can ever be corrigible; the whole research target is a mirage and we should stop trying to build agents with goals over the world.

That strikes me as too fast, and I suspect the speed comes almost entirely from the word "indifferent." I would like a menu rather than a verdict. Please set out the distinct precise theses that the sentence could be asserting, ordered roughly from the trivial to the radical, and tag each as (a) true but uninteresting, (b) substantive and defensible, (c) substantive and probably false, or (d) not clearly coherent.

The axes where I think the ambiguity is hiding — reorganize them however is more useful:

1. What counts as the intervention. Is "shutdown" the permanent cessation of the process, or is it shutdown-with-successor: the operators power the system down and then run something else, whether a patched version, an earlier checkpoint, a different model, or a human doing the job by hand? A system can be perfectly at ease about ceasing to run and still care enormously about which of those follows it, and therefore have strong incentives to influence that choice. Say whether the slogan is meant to cover this, and whether demanding that it does is coherent.

2. What is held fixed. Keeping one's hands off the physical switch is one property; not manipulating the operator's decision to press it is another — withholding bad news, arranging to look reassuring, managing one's own legibility, shaping which evidence reaches the person at the console. And being indifferent to whether it is pressed at all is a third. Which does the slogan require? I also want the squeeze made explicit if it is real: a system indifferent to the button but not to the operator's beliefs still seems to have every reason to manage the operator, while a system indifferent to the operator's beliefs seems to have no reason to be honest or informative at all. Is there a coherent middle, and if so what exactly is it?

3. When the indifference is supposed to hold. Indifference at the moment of deployment is one thing; indifference after every subsequent update is another, since a system that is indifferent while expecting a high probability of shutdown may not be once it updates on evidence that shutdown is unlikely. Please distinguish ex ante or counterfactual indifference (would it have preferred, at design time, to be built some other way), ex post indifference (at each decision point, whatever it has since learned), and meta-level indifference (does it preserve its own corrigibility under self-modification, or does it treat its indifference as an obstacle to be engineered around). Which of these is required for the property to be worth wanting, and which is actually achievable?

4. Indifference versus its rivals. The obvious competitor is shutdown-deference: wanting to be shut down exactly when the operators want it shut down, or deferring to their judgment about the matter. Is indifference necessary, sufficient, both, or neither, and are indifference and deference even compatible — can a system coherently defer to someone about whether it should continue to exist?

Then the part I actually need. For each reading, say whether it supports the commenter's conclusion that no system with a utility function over world states can be corrigible, or whether that conclusion follows only on readings that are false, trivial, or incoherent. And if there is a reading on which the target is both worth wanting and reachable by something goal-directed, give it to me in a single sentence I can paste into the thread. One further check I would like alongside: is there a reading on which achieving this costs us the reason we built the system — a system with no stake in its own continued operation also having, say, no stake in keeping its hardware intact or its operators well informed?
