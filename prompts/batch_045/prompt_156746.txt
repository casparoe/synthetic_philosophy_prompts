Our philosophy-of-cognition reading group has gotten oddly into Bongard problems — you know the type: two boxes of six little black-and-white line drawings, everything in the left box has some feature, nothing in the right box does, and your job is to say what the feature is. Half the fun is arguing about whether a description that sorts all twelve drawings correctly is really *the* concept, or just some other description that happens to fit, so I sat down and wrote out my gut verdicts on a pile of them, and now I want to know whether there's a principle behind the pattern or whether I'm just making noise.

The cases, with the drawings described loosely (they're all plain polygons, circles, dots, dashes, nothing fancy): (1) A box is a triangle, a pentagon, a heptagon, a nonagon and two more odd-sided regular polygons; B box is a square, a hexagon, an octagon, a circle, an ellipse. Both "odd number of sides" and "has no center of symmetry" sort them perfectly, and I'm confident the rule is the first one and the symmetry thing is a byproduct. (2) A box: big circle with a small circle inside, square inside square, equilateral triangle inside equilateral triangle, hexagon inside hexagon, and so on; B box is the same kind of nesting with mismatched pairs, like a square inside a circle or a hexagon inside a triangle. Here "the inner outline has the same number of sides as the outer" and "the inner outline is just a scaled-down copy of the outer" both fit everything, and they'd come apart on, say, a big equilateral triangle with a small squashed right triangle inside it — and unlike in case 1, I genuinely don't think there's a fact of the matter, which bugs me because structurally it looks just like the case where I was sure. (3) A problem where every A figure is tilted twenty degrees or so off upright and every B figure sits flat. "Tilted" sorts them fine; not the rule, that's just how whoever drew them happened to draw them. Same verdict for a problem where the A figures all happen to be drawn a bit smaller, or all sit slightly higher up in their box. (4) A problem where the A outlines are dashed and the B outlines solid: yes, that's the rule, no hesitation. Another problem where the only difference I can find is that the A figures are drawn with a slightly heavier line: no, I refuse to count that, even though it sorts the boxes exactly as well. I can't tell you what the difference between those two is. (5) A: a triangle with three dots in it, a square with four, a pentagon with five, a hexagon with six; B: the same shapes with counts that don't match. I'm happy to say the rule is "number of dots equals number of sides" even though it takes two counts and a comparison, and I'm equally happy with a simpler problem where the A figures just have exactly three seperate pieces and the B figures have four or five. (6) A: outlines with a gap in them — a circle missing a wedge, a square missing a side, a horseshoe; B: the same shapes closed up. I'm fine with "the outline is open", and I don't care whether you state it as "the A figures are open" or "the B figures are closed". But in another problem, where every A figure has a little triangle sticking out of it somewhere and the B figures don't, I want to insist that the rule is "has a triangular bit sticking out" and that "has no triangular bit sticking out" isn't the rule at all, even though it picks out box B perfectly. Why does the direction bother me in one case and not the other? (7) One problem where the rule I settled on — "exactly one dot outside the shape" — fits five of the six A figures, and the sixth has two dots outside. I still think it's the rule and that the sixth figure is a mistake or I'm misreading it.

My provisional principle is something like: X is the rule iff (i) it puts every A figure in and every B figure out, (ii) you could decide of a brand-new drawing which box it belongs in without knowing anything else about the puzzle, and (iii) it isn't a coincidence that all six A figures have it — you'd expect the next six the designer drew to have it too. But I can feel (iii) doing all the work and I have no idea how to cash it out, and case 7 shows (i) isn't strict either. A friend in the group says the whole question is a pseudo-question and the answer is just whatever the person who designed the puzzle had in mind, end of story; that can't be right, since the entire point is that you're supposed to be able to get it from twelve drawings you've never seen before, but I don't have a better story to put in its place. So: what's the principle, or the two or three candidate principles, with a clause for each case that motivates it? What does it say about a problem whose A figures can all be drawn in one stroke without lifting the pen or going over a line twice while the B figures need two or three strokes — that feels like a genuine rule to me even though it's about how you'd draw the thing rather than what it looks like — and about a problem where the A box is a grab-bag, a spiral and a five-pointed star and a zigzag and a circle with a dot off to one side, and the only thing I can say about them is that they all look sort of lively or balanced, which I suspect isn't a rule at all? And please be honest about which of my verdicts your principle can't save.
