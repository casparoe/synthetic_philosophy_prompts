Hi — I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I've gotten myself stuck on a scene and can't write past it.

Setup: my character runs a stud farm in a kingdom whose requisition law I made up. The legal code has two separate verbs for "is." One means "answers to the description on the warrant" — a warrant for a horse is answered by bringing a black horse, a warrant for a white horse isn't. The other means "is one of the kind" — a white horse is one of the horses, full stop. Everyone in the scene treats those as two genuinely different relations and nobody in the kingdom thinks they're interchangeable. My character is trying to talk a requisition clerk out of taking her white horse, and I want her to actually be running the old Chinese "White Horse" argument — the School of Names thing where the conclusion is that a white horse is not a horse. I don't care about getting the history right. I care that the argument in the scene is the real argument and not a sophomore's paraphrase of it.

Here's the part I can't get. The translation I've been working from has the closing move like this:

White does not fix what is white, this can be put aside. "White horse" speaks of white fixing what is white. That which fixes what is white is not white. "Horse" does not pick or exclude color, thus a brown or black horse can be brought. "White horse" does pick or exclude color; brown and black horses are excluded by color, thus only a white horse can be brought. That which does not exclude is not that which does exclude. Thus I say: "a white horse is not a horse".

That bit looks like where all the work happens: "horse" doesn't pick or exclude color, "white horse" does, so the two terms don't have the same — what? Range of things that can be brought? And then he concludes from that difference that a white horse is not a horse. But if that's the move, I can't see how it doesn't blow up in my character's face, because earlier the same reasoning seems to show that a brown horse isn't a horse either, and then there are no horses at all. The dialogue seems to notice this and then wriggle out with seperation:

Taking their being horses as different to there being brown horses, is to take brown horses as different to horses. Taking brown horses to be different to horses, is to take it that brown horses are not horses. To take brown horses as not horses, and yet take white horses as being horses, is to have the flying in a pond and the inner and outer coffins in different places: a contradictory claim and misuse of statements as there is under heaven!

Having white horses cannot be called having no horses, this is what is meant by the separation of white. Not separating it, having white horses cannot be said to be having horses.

Two things I need, concretely.

1. What is my character actually committed to if she wins? Is she saying white horses aren't horses, or that "white horse" and "horse" aren't the same thing, or that whatever makes something answer to one term isn't what makes it answer to the other? In my made-up legal language those three come apart, and I need to know which one she's stuck with — I can't have her say something the clerk can just translate away. And is "seperation" the get-out-of-jail card that saves her from the no-horses-at-all problem, or is the arguement already committed to that and simply refusing to say so out loud?

2. My real worry: that the whole thing is a pun. In the original language there's no copula, so "white horse not horse" can be heard as identity, as class membership, or as satisfaction-of-a-request, and the argument keeps sliding between them. If that's right, my scene can't be written, because my kingdom's code makes her pick one verb. So: does the argument survive being forced to pick? If the stablemaster has to say, in a language with two distinct "is" verbs, which one she means — does she have a real argument left, or is she exposed as a punster? And if she is exposed, what's the strongest version of her position that still survives?

Bonus if it's quick: the clerk tries the objection that there are no colorless horses in the world, so if a horse with color isn't a horse there are no horses. Is that a good objection or a bad one, and does it depend on which "is" he's using?
