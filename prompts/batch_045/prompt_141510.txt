I write up heuristic arguments for a living — the kind where you estimate the probability of some outcome by listing the conditions that all have to obtain for it and multiplying — and I am trying to say, in a methods note, exactly what licenses the multiplication. The stock answer is a presumption of independence: where your evidence does not tell you how two factors are related, treat them as unrelated. I tried to make that precise, and what I got was an argument that the presumption cannot be defended at all. I do not believe the conclusion. Here is the argument.

(1) Let H be the outcome of interest and F1, …, Fn conditions each individually necessary for H. Grant for the sake of the argument that P(H) = P(F1 ∧ … ∧ Fn). Write p_i for P(F_i); I take these as given, estimated one at a time from separate considerations.

(2) The heuristic estimate is the product of the p_i. What is supposed to license it is the presumption of independence.

(3) The only justification available for the presumption is symmetry: for any pair of factors the dependence might run either way, you have no view about which, and so the product is what you should expect. It cannot be that you have positive evidence of independence — if you had that, you would not need a presumption.

(4) Make (3) explicit. Fix a pair with marginals a and b. Once the marginals are fixed the joint has exactly one degree of freedom, c = P(A ∧ B), and c ranges over the interval [max(0, a + b − 1), min(a, b)]. To have no reason to favor positive over negative dependence is to be uniform over that interval. So the expected value of c is ½(max(0, a + b − 1) + min(a, b)). Call this the flat rule.

(5) The flat rule and the product rule disagree, and the disagreement has a direction. For equal marginals p the flat rule gives p/2 when p ≤ ½ and (3p − 1)/2 when p ≥ ½; the product rule gives p². At p = 0.2 that is 0.1 against 0.04; at p = 0.1, 0.05 against 0.01; at p = 0.9, 0.85 against 0.81. The flat rule never returns less than the product, and the two agree only at p = 0, ½ and 1.

(6) The gap compounds along a chain. Applying the flat rule one factor at a time halves the running value at each step, so for n factors each of marginal p ≤ ½ it yields p/2^(n−1) where the product rule yields p^n. Five factors at p = 0.2: the flat rule gives about 1.25%, the product rule about 0.032% — a factor of roughly thirty-nine.

(7) Therefore every multiplicative estimate of a conjunctive outcome is biased downward, and the longer the chain the worse the bias. The familiar long-shot argument — H is very improbable because it requires all of these things to go right — is invalid in general, and worse, it is rigged: the more necessary conditions you honestly list, the more the method manufactures improbability, so the headline number tracks the length of your description rather than anything about the world. That cannot be right. Some of these arguments are good ones.

Two things I have set aside and why I think neither is the problem. First, the flat rule is sensitive to how you bracket the chain: with four factors at p = 0.2, combining them as ((F1 ∧ F2) ∧ F3) ∧ F4 gives 0.025, while combining them as (F1 ∧ F2) ∧ (F3 ∧ F4) gives 0.05. That looks like a bookkeeping defect I could repair by fixing a canonical order of combination, not a counterexample to (4). Second, a colleague says the rescue is immediate: fix the marginals and maximize entropy over what remains free and you get exactly c = ab, so the product is what no information beyond the constraints means, and the argument dies at (4). I do not think that rescues anything, for two reasons. Choosing the maximum-entropy point is a choice, not a state of ignorance, so it assumes what (4) was trying to derive. And the same colleague then checked what a genuinely flat prior over the four-cell joint gives once you condition it on the marginals: a uniform density along the one-dimensional segment of admissible joints, whose mean is the midpoint, not the product. So the no-information prior and the maximum-entropy point come apart, and I cannot tell which one was ever supposed to underwrite the presumption.

Give me one diagnosis, not a survey: name the step you reject, explain why it looked compulsory, and price the alternatives — what I lose if I keep (3) and give up (4), what I lose if I keep the flat rule and swallow (7), and what I lose if I abandon the whole project and refuse to multiply. I would rather keep the thought that long conjunctive arguments can be sound than keep the flat rule. One further constraint on whatever you propose: it has to be split-invariant. If I split a factor Fi into two sub-conditions whose probabilities multiply back to p_i, the estimate for H should not move; otherwise the figure I publish measures how finely I chose to carve up the world. Finally, tell me what to actually write in the methods note where it currently says assuming independence.

Defend a position and give your reasons; I would rather have a verdict I can argue with than a menu. Please answer in British English.
