Ok so first off, pretend you're a professional philosopher — like a metaphysician who has spent an unhealthy amount of their career on Leibniz, the principle of the identity of indiscernibles (PII), and the modern discernibility literature. Think carefully about this, and don't just serve me the textbook moves. Before you write anything final, brainstorm four or five genuinely different argumentative routes you could take, then tell me in a line or two why you dropped the ones you dropped.

The setup. Black's two-sphere world: an otherwise totally empty infinite Euclidean space containing exactly two iron spheres that are perfect duplicates, a few metres apart, same mass, same composition, same shape, symmetrically placed, no haecceities, no primitive thisness, nothing whatsoever to tell them apart. So they look numerically distinct while sharing every qualitative property, including their relational ones (each is some distance from an indiscernible duplicate). Standardly that's offered as a counterexample to PII.

Here's the wrinkle I actually want you to work on. A very common defense is that the spheres are at least *weakly* discernible: the relation "is three metres from" is irreflexive and does hold between them, so there is a difference after all, and maybe that is all Leibniz ever needed. I want you to argue that this defense is a cheat.

To make the target precise, I'm going to stipulate some terminology — its new, so work with my definition rather than with anything you've seen before. Say that a relation R **grounding-discerns** two things x and y only if all three hold: (i) R actually holds between x and y; (ii) R does not hold between x and itself, or between y and itself; (iii) R is not *necessarily* irreflexive — that is, it is possible for R to hold between something and itself. Contrast "is three metres from" and "is distinct from" (necessarily irreflexive: distance and distinctness cannot relate a thing to itself) with "is thinking about" or "admires" (contingently irreflexive: I can think about myself, even if right now I'm thinking about you).

Your job is to argue for this claim:

**In Black's two-sphere world the spheres are not grounding-discernible, and grounding-discernibility is the only kind of discernibility that could do the work PII was supposed to do — so the weak-discernibility defense collapses, and PII in the only form worth caring about is false.**

A few things I want handled along the way, because they're where I expect the argument to get tested:

1. Give a real motivation for condition (iii), not just "it makes my conclusion come out". Why should a discerning relation not be one that by its very nature requires two distinct relata? My own hunch is that it's because PII is a *grounding* or *explanatory* thesis: qualitative difference is supposed to account for numerical difference, and a relation that already presupposes two things can't account for their being two. Make that case better than I just did, or replace it with something better.
2. Notice that every relation on offer in a purely physical two-sphere world — distance, relative velocity, gravitational attraction, causal influence, whatever — looks necessarily irreflexive. So on your view, does it follow that PII is *necessarily* false for material objects, i.e. that no purely physical duplicate-pair could ever be grounding-discernible? If so, own it and explain why that's not a reductio of your own position. If not, describe what a grounding-discerner among physical objects would even have to look like.
3. Steelman the best reply your opponent has — for instance that PII was never a grounding thesis but just a biconditional, or that condition (iii) is unmotivated, or that the modal status of a relation is irrelevant to whether it can discern — and then answer it.
4. Sanity checks: does your argument generalize to a world with three mutually indiscernible spheres, or to one with infinitely many arranged so that the universe looks exactly the same from each of them? And does it say anything at all about a world containing exactly one sphere?

Write it up as one continuous argument, not as numbered answers to my four points — fold them in where they naturally belong. Around 800 to 1000 words, and be concrete.
