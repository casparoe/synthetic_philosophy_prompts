You are a professional philosopher who works on moral responsibility and the ethics of blame, and who knows the standing-to-blame literature from the inside. I want you to think carefully and slowly about the case below. Before you write any analysis, spend a short stretch brainstorming: list three or four different ways the episode could be framed through the standing doctrine (different choices of *who* is the blamer, *what* the blamed fault is, and *what kind* of act the blaming is), note which framing does the most work, and only then commit to one. I would rather see you reject two framings for stated reasons than watch you ride the first one to the end.

The episode is the impeachment of Bill Clinton by the House of Representatives in December 1998 and the Senate's acquittal in February 1999. Treat the following as stipulated background; I am not asking you to verify it, and if some detail is slightly off, do not let that derail the analysis:

- The articles of impeachment charged perjury before a grand jury and obstruction of justice. The official wrongdoing was lying and concealment under oath, not adultery as such — but the subject matter of the lying was a sexual relationship with a subordinate.
- Several of the most prominent Republican actors in the process had themselves been unfaithful in their own marriages: the Speaker, Newt Gingrich, was conducting an extramarital affair during the period; the Speaker-designate, Bob Livingston, withdrew from the speakership and left Congress after an affair of his was revealed mid-process; the lead House manager, Henry Hyde, had acknowledged a past affair of his own.
- The hypocrisy of the accusers was the single most repeated charge in public commentary at the time. It was politically damaging, but it did not discredit the proceedings: the House impeached anyway, Clinton's public approval stayed high, and the Senate acquitted.

Here is what I want you to do with it.

First, give me a disciplined mapping rather than an impressionistic one. Who exactly is the blaming agent, what is the fault being blamed, and what is the normative power being exercised? Notice that there is more than one candidate: an individual legislator's moral criticism of a president's conduct; a chamber's vote on articles of impeachment; the judiciary committee's investigative and prosecutorial activity; and the public's diffuse disapproval. The mapping has to say, for each, what the standing doctrine is even supposed to be regulating.

Second, run the hypocrisy clause at three different grains and tell me which one survives. Call them (a) *fault-matching*: a hypocrite loses standing only to blame the very same type of fault they themselves committed; (b) *vice-matching*: they lose standing to blame any fault that manifests the same underlying vice; and (c) *content-matching*: they lose standing to blame any fault whose content, or whose subject matter, is their own fault — which matters here because the fault being blamed (perjury) is not the fault the blamers committed (infidelity), but is perjury *about* infidelity. My own suspicion is that the interesting action is all at (c) and that (a) and (b) both give the wrong verdict in opposite directions, but argue it out rather than agreeing with me.

Third, tell me what the loss of standing is supposed to *be*. Distinguish at least two readings: on the *authority* reading, the compromised blamer's blame is defective or impermissible, period; on the *uptake* reading, the blame may be perfectly in order and the target's real complaint is epistemic or conversational — the hypocrisy is evidence that the stated reason is not the operative reason, so the target may reasonably discount, resent, or refuse to engage with it. Which reading does the actual reception of the Clinton case support? The public simultaneously treated the accusers as hypocrites and continued to treat the charges as serious. That combination is data, and I want to know which reading it confirms and which it embarrasses.

Fourth — and this is the part I care most about — press on two places where I think the doctrine has nothing ready to say.

The first is *composite blame*. Impeachment is not interpersonal moral address: it is an institutional act by a collective body with a partly compromised membership, aimed at a political-legal finding about fitness for office, and it is largely forward-looking. Does a plural agent have standing at all, and if it inherits standing from its members, how? Suppose a chamber in which 60 percent of the voting members are compromised and 40 percent are not. Is there a threshold? Does the standing of the body depend on whether the compromised members were pivotal to the outcome? If the collective has no standing, does that tell us anything at all about whether the president deserved removal?

The second is the *reacquisition problem*. The literature I know of is overwhelmingly about how standing is lost and almost silent on whether it can be recovered. Livingston paid an enormous cost and exited the role from which he had been blaming. Hyde acknowledged his past and continued. Gingrich did neither at the time. So: can a hypocrite regain standing by self-punishment, by acknowledgment, by exiting the blaming role, or by submitting themselves to exactly the scrutiny they were applying? If standing is a relation rather than a status, this should have a clean answer, and I want to know whether it does — or whether the doctrine's silence here is a symptom that it was built for a tidy two-person case and never meant to govern public blame.

Finally, close by grading the case itself: is it a straightforward confirmation of the hypocrisy condition, an anomaly to be explained away, or a case the theory never anticipated and cannot currently describe? And give me one concrete revision to the doctrine — a changed condition, an added distinction, a scope restriction — that the case motivates.

Constraints on your answer: argue in your own voice and do not give me a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I want the analysis, not the sources. Where a framework is silent, say so instead of forcing it to speak. Where the historical record is contested or thin, say what you would need to know and how it would change the verdict. And do not resolve anything by declaring the whole scandal merely political: even if every actor was posturing, the question of what their blame was worth remains.
