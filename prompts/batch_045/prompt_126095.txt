In three weeks I'm giving the closing talk at a weekend rationalist gathering — about sixty people, mostly longtime LessWrong and ACX readers, a substantial effective-altruism contingent, two professional moral philosophers who are friendly outsiders to the community, and one economic historian who works on the British Caribbean and got dragged along by her partner. I have forty minutes plus twenty of Q&A. I want a prep sheet for the Q&A, not a defense of the thesis: the objections I will actually get, ordered by how much damage they'd do weighted by how likely they are, each phrased the way that person would phrase it, each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft.

The thesis I'm defending: large-scale moral revolutions are driven by changes in what it is honorable to be seen doing, not by moral argument and not primarily by material interest. The corollary I care about, and the reason this room will be hostile, is that argument aimed at changing moral belief is close to inert at the population level, and the active lever is the prestige economy of the reference group whose regard the practitioners actually want.

Sketch of the argument, so you have something to aim at. Three moves. First, against the argument-first story: the case against chattel slavery and the case against dueling were both fully articulated and widely available generations before the practices ended, and the historical record doesn't look like people being reasoned out of a conviction; it looks like the arguments getting sharper and more popular after the practice became indefensible. Second, against the interest-first story: the same material conditions were compatible with the practice continuing indefinitely — slave-grown sugar was enormously profitable right up to abolition, and dueling persisted longest precisely among officers whose careers depended on a reputation for willingness to fight — so interests don't select which equilibrium you get, and where interests and abolition coincide the coincidence often runs through what it had become respectable to be seen doing. Third, the positive claim: what ends these practices is that the people whose esteem the practitioners need withdraw it, so continuing becomes not merely inexpedient or unsound but dishonorable; the mechanism is the desire to be able to hold your head up in front of specific others, not updating on evidence. I also plan to offer an operationalization, since this audience will demand one: norm change should propagate along admiration and esteem networks faster than along information-exposure networks, and the rhetoric should improve after the collapse rather than before it.

Some things about this specific room. Most of them have a self-conception built on the idea that reasoning well and arguing in good faith is how beliefs improve, so my thesis attacks something load-bearing for them. The EA contingent will not care about the history except instrumentally: they want tractability numbers, base rates, and an answer to what anyone should do differently on Monday. The two philosophers will go after the framework rather than the cases — I expect one of them to insist that the causal story about how a belief changed is orthogonal to whether the belief is justified, and that I'm smuggling in a debunking argument I haven't earned. The economic historian knows the primary literature cold and will object that I've cherry-picked cases where the practice was already in structural decline, and that honor is a post hoc gloss on a shift in bargaining power.

Three requests. Concede nothing for me that I shouldn't concede, but tell me what to give away in the first two slides before anyone objects — my instinct is to grant up front that honor and interest are nearly impossible to disentangle in the archival record, and that my sample is three cases with the outcome known in advance. Second, tell me which single reply has to land for the thesis to survive the hour. Third, give me the question I'm hoping nobody asks, and a first sentence for answering it.

Two kinds of objection I don't need, because I already have answers: the self-referential one about why I'm bothering to give a talk, and bare correlation-isn't-causation. I want the objections past those — the ones that actually bite on the mechanism rather than on the framing. And no citations or suggestions for further reading, please; I've done the reading, I need the object-level arguments and what to say back.
