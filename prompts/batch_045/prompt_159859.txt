I spent most of my career as a comparative zoologist — insect neuroethology, mostly, and later population-level work — and since retiring I have been reading my way into moral philosophy by a rather different route than the one most people seem to take. I came in with a technical worry from my own field rather than through the literature on farming, and I now sit on the board of a small private foundation that has asked me to draft a research agenda. The constraints are roughly these: about four million dollars over five years, enough for six or seven early-career researchers plus some pilot data, and an explicit aim of changing what advocacy organisations and regulators actually prioritise — which species get attention, which interventions get funded, which welfare standards get written into law. So this is a question about where a small, fixed amount of intellectual effort should go, and I want a defended agenda rather than a survey. I have no library access where I am writing, so please argue from your own reasoning; no citations or reading lists, please.

Three things strike me as candidate cruxes, and I would like you to tell me which of them — or which thing I have not thought of — is most upstream, and in what order the rest follow. First, the correlated-uncertainty problem: evidence for sentience is largely shared within a clade, so our uncertainty about different species is not independent, and the tempting move of multiplying a small per-species probability by an astronomical number of individuals may be an artefact of treating correlated uncertainties as though they were independent. Whether that is right seems to decide whether the bulk of expected moral value sits with the comparatively well-studied vertebrates or with the vastly more numerous and far less understood arthropods and nematodes, which is an enormous practical difference. Second, the currency problem: we have no scale on which to compare the intensity and duration of valenced states across species, and the proxies available to us — behavioural display, stress physiology, response to analgesics — can come apart in principled ways; prey species, for instance, are under strong selection to conceal injury. Third, the unit question: whether the object of concern is the individual life, with death counted as a harm, or some population-level quantity, with the result that causing a net-positive life might offset suffering. That question seems to determine whether welfare reform, population control, or habitat-level work looks best, and I take the replaceability-style arguments in the literature to turn on it.

A few further stipulations. Rank the items, and defend the ranking by the criteria you are actually using — importance, tractability within five years for philosophers and conceptually-minded biologists, neglectedness, how many other answers depend on this one — and be explicit about the trade-offs between those criteria rather than quietly optimising one of them. Say which two or three things you would deprioritise as overrated or unlikely to change anything downstream; I would include in that category the suspicion that the familiar debate about speciesism and the argument from marginal cases is largely settled and no longer load-bearing, but tell me if I am wrong about that. Say how you would split the effort between conceptual work and empirical measurement, and what evidence arriving in the next few years would move an item up or down your list. Finally, a suspicion of my own that I cannot evaluate: three decades of watching animals has left me doubting that the notion of welfare we inherited from vertebrate research is even the right theoretical object for creatures whose motivational systems are organised quite differently — for animals that may not integrate valence over time in the way a mammal appears to. Is that a live and tractable conceptual question, or am I merely confused? If it is live, tell me where it belongs in the ordering.
