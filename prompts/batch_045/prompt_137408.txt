I am writing up my position on statutory interpretation for a short series of lectures, and before I commit it to print I would like an audit of whether it actually hangs together. Here is the position, as seven commitments:

(1) The meaning of a statute is the ordinary public meaning of the enacted words at the time of enactment, read in the context of the whole enactment. Interpretation aims at that meaning rather than at the private expectations of the drafters or of any individual legislator, and it is not literalism: context can show that a wider or narrower reading than the literal one is the right reading.

(2) There is no such thing as the intention of a legislature over and above the enacted text, and legislative history — committee reports, sponsor statements, floor debate — is not evidence of statutory meaning.

(3) Even so, where applying the enacted words literally would produce a result that no reasonable legislature could have wanted, a court may decline to follow them. I want to keep the absurdity and scrivener's error exceptions, narrowly construed.

(4) Judges may not engage in purposive construction. They may not work out what the statute is for and then read the words so as to serve that purpose better.

(5) Where the enacted text is genuinely open between two readings, the purpose that is evident from the text itself, read as a whole, settles which of the two is correct.

(6) The text is read against the whole of the surrounding law and against settled background conventions and canons of construction, including the substantive clear statement rules — the presumption against extraterritorial application, the federalism canons, and the principle that a statute is not read to raise a serious constitutional question — on the ground that legislatures legislate against a known background of such conventions.

(7) Where a statute is genuinely ambiguous, courts should defer to the reasonable reading of the executive agency that administers it, on the ground that the agency has expertise and political accountability that courts lack.

I currently hold all seven at once, and I have the uneasy sense that at least two of them cannot survive unchanged in each other's company, but I cannot locate the fault line. Please audit the set:

- Tell me which pairs are in real tension and which only look as though they are. I would rather you discriminated than flagged everything, so where a pair is in fact consistent, say briefly why the appearance of conflict is misleading.
- Rank the real tensions by severity, most severe first, rather than presenting them as a flat list.
- For each real tension, state precisely what I would have to abandon or qualify, and what that qualification would cost me elsewhere in the set: the cheapest available fix, and its price.
- Finally, name the single revision that would do the most work across the set as a whole, and say what it would leave me unable to claim.

Please begin with your bottom line in the first sentence — your verdict on whether the set is tenable as it stands and where the real problem lies — and give the reasoning only after that. No preamble, and no restatement of what I have asked. I want argument rather than a survey of positions, so no citations and no suggestions for further reading, please. Please write in British English.
