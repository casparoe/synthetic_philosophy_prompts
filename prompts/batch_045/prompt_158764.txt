I am writing the third chapter of my PhD thesis, on whether the cosmological argument is best reconstructed as an inference to the best explanation, and I have run into something that feels like a structural problem rather than a first-order one. Whichever version I look at — the kalam route through the finitude of the past, or the contingency route through a principle of sufficient reason applied to the totality of contingent things — every exchange has the same shape. One side offers a causal or explanatory principle; the other produces a counter-example or an alternative terminus for explanation; neither side ever concedes that the objection has force. Theists press a premise about the aggregate; their opponents either give the brute-fact response or charge a composition fallacy, insisting that what holds of the members, such as contingency or having a cause, need not hold of the whole; the first side replies by distinguishing a genuine aggregate from a mere collection, or by insisting that a series cannot explain its own obtaining; and round it goes. Nothing at stake ever seems to change hands.

My hypothesis, which I would like you to test rather than flatter, is that the real disagreement lies upstream of all of this: it concerns whether a total state of affairs can be an item of explanation at all, that is, whether the question of why there is anything whatsoever is a well-formed request for an explanation whose answer could in principle be given, or rather a request that no possible reply could satisfy, so that the two sides are not disagreeing over a proposition but over what an explanation of a totality would have to be. Call the disputed principle Aggregative Closure: if every member of a collection has an explanation, then the obtaining of the collection has an explanation too. Both sides appear to accept something like it in ordinary cases and to part company exactly when the collection is maximal; and there is a companion question I shall call the Inheritance Question, whether contingency transmits from parts to whole or whether a whole might be necessary while all its parts are contingent.

So: is this a verbal dispute, a substantive metaphysical one, or a clash of standards about what counts as a terminating explanation? Is there a hidden substantive question doing all the work under cover of a debate about causation — about the metaphysics of composition, about whether a necessary being could have contingent parts, or about whether the kalam premise concerning the past is really an empirical hostage to cosmology? Are the two versions of the argument even the same dispute, or two disputes sharing a name?
