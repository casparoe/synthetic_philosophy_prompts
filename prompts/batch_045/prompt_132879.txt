I work on the platform team at an organisation that puts language models in front of customers, and I have no formal training in philosophy. I am writing an internal design document about when we are entitled to treat a model's behaviour as evidence of what it knows, because two live decisions turn on it: whether a model saying it does not know should count as a reason to gate a deployment, and whether oversight built on probes of internal activations may be cited as evidence that a model is not deceiving us.

I find that I have firm verdicts on individual cases and no idea what principle they add up to. Here they are, in my own engineering idiom.

A. A chat model, asked whether a library function mutates its argument, says it is not certain. Under the hood, the raw completion distribution puts 0.93 on the token that means it does mutate, and if I phrase the same question as a code comment the model defensively copies the argument. My verdict: it knew all along; the verbal hedge is a suppression artefact of preference training.

B. A model reproduces the correct default port for one of our internal services, but only when the question is phrased the way the phrase appears in the config files in its training data. In the passive voice, or with a different service name, it fails. My verdict: no knowledge, just template matching.

C. A model was trained on an employee directory and, separately, on a reporting graph. Nobody ever wrote down who anyone's skip-level manager is. Asked directly, it answers correctly. My verdict: it already knew; making it compose the two tables did not teach it anything.

D. A clinical model was trained on radiology notes that systematically under-report a particular finding. It predicts what the note will say rather than what is true of the patient. My verdict: it knows what the notes say; it does not know the medical fact. I want my principle to keep these two apart rather than blur them.

E. A linear probe trained on true/false labels recovers the correct answer from activations at 99 per cent, but no prompting strategy we tried elicits it, and the model's own output distribution is close to uniform. My verdict: not knowledge. I suspect the probe was taught the concept rather than reading it off.

F. Same set-up, but when we steer along the discovered direction the model starts asserting the true claim and then uses it consistently in code it writes downstream. My verdict: now I am inclined to say it did know. I cannot say what changed between E and F except our own intervention, and that bothers me.

G. We fine-tuned a model on deliberately false claims about an internal API. Afterwards it plans on the false claim, calling an endpoint that does not exist, and true and false statements no longer separate in its activations. My verdict: it has stopped knowing the truth.

H. A model infers from the phrasing and source address that it is inside a red-team evaluation and answers with the approved but false claim, while still representing the truth internally; strip the safety scaffolding and the truth appears in its reasoning. My verdict: of course it still knows. G and H are the pair I am most confident about and least able to justify: in both, the falsehood is in the output and drives downstream behaviour, and my only real evidence of a difference is whether the true representation is still recoverable.

I. A model with a retrieval tool reliably looks up facts it cannot produce unaided. My verdict: it does not know them, though it knows where to look. Yet I would happily say a colleague who checks something on her phone knows it, and I do not know whether that inconsistency is in me or in the concept.

J. A model role-playing a senior engineer produces a flawless post-mortem of an outage it cannot have seen, and a model fine-tuned on strong play makes excellent moves while giving entirely wrong reasons for them. My verdict: it has the know-how and not the knowledge, but I cannot articulate the difference without just relabelling the cases.

My own stab at a principle, which I suspect is wrong: a system knows that p if and only if (i) some procedure extracts p from it without supplying any information about whether p; (ii) its representation of p is causally efficacious, in that intervening on it changes behaviour in a way that tracks p; and (iii) that tracking generalises across reformulations, adjacent implications and downstream tasks, not merely within the elicitation context. This seems to get A, B, D, E and G right. But clause (i) makes knowledge depend on how good my elicitation engineering is, so I can never tell whether a failure to extract means the model lacks the fact or that my probes were too weak; I cannot work out what it says about C, I or J; and F shows clause (ii) is satisfied by steering, which seems to let me manufacture knowledge by intervening.

What I would like from you:

Two or three candidate principles, each stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without guessing, with every clause tagged to the case that forces it. Where a clause is doing work only to save one of my verdicts, say so, and say what the principle would look like without it.

Verdicts on cases I have not listed, because these are the ones actually coming up at work: a fact that survives quantisation or distillation into a smaller model but only shows up after long chains of reasoning at inference time; a fact present in one expert of a mixture-of-experts model and absent from the others; a model that gets the right answer only after a few-shot prompt with ten examples of the task, where I cannot tell whether that is eliciting or teaching; a capability that exists only in the aggregate behaviour of several models calling each other; and a model that reports facts about its own weights.

An honest statement of which of my verdicts each principle cannot accommodate, and your recommendation about which verdict I should abandon.

For the G/H pair specifically: is there a distinction that separates them, an experiment that would settle it in practice, or should the design document concede that the two are indistinguishable from the outside?

Finally, one practical proposal. I have been asked for a test we can actually run to distinguish genuine ignorance from suppressed knowledge when a model declines to answer, and for language to use in the document in place of the word knows. Please give me both, keeping in mind that whatever test you propose has to be runnable by my team with ordinary evaluation infrastructure.
