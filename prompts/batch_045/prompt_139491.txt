I'm midway through a graduate seminar on classical Indian epistemology, and before I commit to a paper topic I want to work out what our professor is actually committed to. Over the first half of the term she has ruled, fairly briskly, on a run of cases, and I cannot tell which principle she is running. Here is her track record as I have it in my notes:

1. Seeing a pot in clear daylight: pramā, by pratyakṣa.
2. Taking a shell for silver: not pramā — and she added that this is not merely because reaching for the shell leads to frustrated action, since a false cognition can sometimes guide action successfully.
3. Inferring fire on a hill from smoke, with the pervasion (vyāpti) established: pramā, by anumāna.
4. The same-looking cognition where what appeared to be smoke is in fact a plume of mist: not pramā.
5. The city-dweller who is told that a gavaya is like a cow, later meets one in the forest, and forms the cognition "this is what 'gavaya' denotes": pramā, and produced by upamāna as a pramāṇa in its own right — she was explicit that this is not, as a reductionist would have it, testimony plus perception.
6. A trustworthy person's report that there is water at the foot of the hill, addressed to a hearer who has never been there: pramā, by śabda.
7. The very same true report from a speaker the hearer knows to be unreliable: true belief, but not pramā — the defect lies in the source, she said, and a true outcome does not repair it.
8. Devadatta is known to be alive but is not in the house, so he must be outside: pramā, but by anumāna; she will not grant postulation (arthāpatti) a separate pramāṇa.
9. Noticing that there is no jar on the floor: pramā, and known by pratyakṣa; she will not grant non-cognition a separate pramāṇa either.

She has also twice insisted that whether a cognition is a pramā and whether it is produced by a distinct pramāṇa are separate questions, and that the second does not follow from the first. What 5, 8 and 9 seem to show jointly is that she is not a uniform reductionist about the pramāṇas, and that her reason for resisting reduction in one case is not, so far as I can see, the same as her reason for insisting on it in the others.

Here are three cases of my own construction that she has not seen, on which I want to predict her:

A. Sam is reliably told by his astronomy teacher that there will be an eclipse on the morrow, and believes it. He then works through the ephemerides himself and derives the same conclusion. The resulting cognition is true. Is it a pramā, and if so by which pramāṇa?

B. As in A, except that the general rule on which Sam's derivation depends is one he has only on his teacher's authority and could not establish for himself; the application of it is entirely his own work.

C. Sam holds the derived conclusion before his mind and, a moment later, runs the derivation again, with no intervening doubt, and then a third time. Are the second and third cognitions pramās, and instances of anumāna? Does it matter whether an intervening doubt separates them?

What I want is this. First, the two or three principles that fit her nine verdicts about equally well. My own guesses are (i) a source-integrity rule: a cognition is pramā just in case a pramāṇa has operated without defect, with pramāṇas individuated by irreducibly distinct processes; (ii) a credit rule: pramā requires that the truth be attributable to the subject's own exercise of a reliable competence, so that borrowed reliability yields true belief at best; (iii) a novelty rule of the kind that requires what is cognized to be cognized for the first time, either for pramā-status or for counting as the product of a distinct pramāṇa. I suspect these are badly stated and that there are better candidates. Second, what each principle predicts about A, B and C, and precisely where they come apart. Third, your best guess at her verdict on each of the three, with reasons for preferring the principle that yields it — in particular, whether anything in her track record, or in the shape of the Nyāya position she is reconstructing, makes one of them the natural thing for someone in her position to hold. Fourth, if more than one principle survives, the single further case I should put to her in office hours that would separate them, together with what each principle would have to say about it. And if any candidate principle forces me to read one of her earlier verdicts as an outlier, tell me which, and why.
