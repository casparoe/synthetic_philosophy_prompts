Posting this the night before a chapter meeting, sorry for the mess. I'm a philosophy PhD student — phil of science, mostly anthropic reasoning — and last year I got pulled into an astrobiology reading group and somehow ended up being the person who has an opinion about the Fermi question. I'm writing the chapter now and I want a consistency check on the package of things I believe, because I have a nagging feeling that one of these is doing work it can't do. Roughly in order of how attached I am:

(1) The silence is a real datum and it needs an explanation of the filter kind. Nobody has found anything credible, and at even a percent of light speed a galaxy is crossable in something like ten million years, so "they're far away and we've only just started looking" doesn't carry the weight people put on it. I also won't touch the unfalsifiable family — they're already here and we can't recognise them, zoo, simulation, postbiological minds too wise to want space. Those aren't explanations, they're ways of making the question unaskable.

(2) I'm a hard-steps person: I think the coincidence between how long it took to get from a habitable Earth to observers here (order billions of years) and how long a star like the Sun gives you (order ten billion) is genuine evidence that some step in that sequence has an expected timescale at least comparable to how long a habitable world stays habitable. So whatever the filter is, it's mostly behind us — deep in the past, not sitting in our technological future.

(3) I'm not a star-type exceptionalist. I think the flare and tidal-locking worries about low-mass stars are overblown and that red dwarfs are probably fine for life. Which means the habitable window is enormous: tiny stars burn for trillions of years, so there is orders of magnitude more habitable real estate in the future than there has been so far.

(4) Anthropic and self-sampling reasoning is fine by me. I treat our position as a sample from whatever the relevant class is, and I have a chapter that defends doomsday-flavoured arguments, so I'm not about to get squeamish about observer selection effects when they become inconvenient.

(5) Mediocrity as the default: assume we're typical along any dimension where we've got no positive reason to think otherwise, and treat any move that makes us special as costing something.

(6) I don't buy the filter-is-ahead-of-us family — we blow ourselves up, everyone goes quiet by design, there's some unavoidable trap at technological maturity. As far as I can tell these were invented to fit the single datum we have, there's no independent evidence that maturity is near-universally lethal, and they smell like secular eschatology with a science-fiction budget.

(7) I think abiogenesis is probably the easy part. Life showed up here about as soon as the planet stopped trying to kill it, and I read that as evidence that the chemistry-to-first-replicators bit isn't the bottleneck. So the hard step is later: complex cells, maybe, or whatever got us the rest of the way.

Different people have told me different pairs in that list are the problem and they can't all be right, so please discriminate instead of flagging everything. What I want: which pairs are genuinely in tension, ranked by severity; for each, what would have to give and what that costs me elsewhere; which pairs only look like they're in tension and why they looked fine; and which pairs are secretly the same commitment written down twice, so that dropping one quietly drops the other as well. Then tell me which single revision cleans up the most. If a fix has knock-on costs for how I'd have to use anthropic reasoning elsewhere in the thesis, say so — but don't let that stop you.

Format: lead with the bottom line in the first sentence — which commitment goes and why — then give me the reasoning. No preamble, and please don't restate my list back at me.
