I am writing a novel, and there is a scene in it that I cannot get right.

The setting is a public meeting in a small coastal town that is being asked to approve a long, expensive program of flood defenses. Two characters argue. The first is a retired television meteorologist, well liked locally and not a crank: he accepts the physics, he simply points out that his former colleagues will not issue a forecast more than about two weeks out, that beyond ten days the best models in the world do no better than climatology, and that this limit is not an engineering shortfall but a consequence of the fact that atmospheric flow is chaotic. The second character is a climate modeler who has come to tell the town that the sea will be meaningfully higher in the year 2100, and that the town should spend accordingly. The meteorologist's question, which I want to be the hinge of the scene, is this: if the equations cannot tell us whether it will rain on a particular Sunday three weeks from now, and if the modelers openly admit that clouds are too small to be represented explicitly in their grids and have to be put in by hand, and that different modeling groups get different answers, then on what basis does the same enterprise claim to know the average temperature of the planet eighty years from now? How can one set of equations be worthless in three weeks and authoritative in eighty years?

My modeler currently answers that weather is a question about initial conditions while climate is a question about boundary conditions, and that climate is simply what the system does once you stop asking which particular eddy will be where. I do not know whether that answer is principled or merely rhetorical, and I do not want the scene to turn on a character being impressed by a slogan.

So I would like to understand what this actual practice presupposes. The practice has several parts that I take to be common knowledge: forecasts are issued and trusted for days and not for months; projections to the end of the century are issued, and international policy is anchored to round numbers for global mean warming; those projections come from models whose grids cannot resolve clouds and which disagree with one another, with the spread across models treated as the uncertainty; and the same institutions that will not forecast a particular Sunday will now say of a single flood or heatwave that a changing climate made it more likely, while declining to say whether it would have happened anyway.

Please reconstruct the implicit view of the relation between levels of description that makes these four things one coherent practice rather than a contradiction, and be exact about which of two claims the practice needs: that large-scale quantities are autonomous from micro-level detail, having their own stable patterns and perhaps their own explanations, or merely that they are insensitive to that detail while remaining wholly determined by it. Tell me which rival view of reduction the practice quietly rules out, whether more than one view would license exactly this pattern, whether the fourth part of the practice smuggles in a commitment the second part does not require, and what would have to change in the practice if the meteorologist's principle were accepted — would the century projections have to go, or only the finer-grained regional and single-event claims? A friend has suggested to me that the useful distinctions are between a quantity's being determined by goings-on at a smaller scale, its being derivable from them by us, and its being explainable only by appeal to the larger scale; if that framing helps, use it, and if it is a false trichotomy, say so. I have also been told that something similar happens in physics, where very different fluids behave identically near a critical point, and I would like to know whether that is genuinely the same structure or a misleading analogy.

Please answer in American English, and in prose rather than notes; I need to be able to hear which of these lines a character could actually say out loud.
