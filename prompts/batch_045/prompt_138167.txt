I am giving a colloquium paper in three weeks on whether the numbers count in rescue cases, and what I want from you is a preparation sheet for the discussion period, not a defence of the view.

The setting: a fifty-minute paper followed by roughly twenty-five minutes of questions, to an audience of about forty. Most are philosophers. A substantial bloc of them work on population ethics and global priority-setting, and there is an active effective-altruism reading group in the department that can be relied on both to attend and to be unconvinced in advance. I have also invited, and they have accepted, an emergency physician who teaches triage, a health economist who works on cost-effectiveness thresholds, and a clinical ethicist from the teaching hospital. I expect the objections to arrive in at least three idioms, and I would like them sorted accordingly.

The thesis, so that you know what to aim at. I defend three claims. First, the negative claim: where a rescuer can save one person or save five, and the six are strangers to one another and to the rescuer, no one is especially needy or specially endangered, no one has a prior claim on the resource, and the rescuer occupies no official role, nothing about the bare fact that there are more of them is a reason to save the five. Each of the six faces the same binary prospect; the further deaths are not an additional loss that anyone suffers, and no one's death is made worse by how many others die alongside it. Second, a test for when numbers might generate reasons at all: take any one of the five and ask, holding the rest of the case fixed, whether she has a complaint against the rescuer that the single person lacks, or that she would still have if the other four did not exist. If no individual complaint can be constructed, there is none for the group to inherit. A complaint, on my account, requires that the rescuer's reason for choosing as he does counts the complainant's own survival for less; greater need can do that, a prior claim can, a special relationship can, but the presence of others cannot. Third, a diagnosis of the opposed conviction: that it is produced by an unannounced shift among three standpoints — that of the agent with the drug or the rope in his hands, that of each person who will die, and that of the onlooker who surveys the scene and judges how bad it is. The numbers are vivid only from the third; the permission to save the one is a conclusion about the first; the test belongs to the second.

There is also an institutional coda I am less confident about: the agent-relative permission does not transfer to a protocol, and where a resource is held in trust for a population, fairness among claimants rather than aggregate value should govern.

What I want from you:

1. The ten or so objections I will actually get, ordered by how much damage they would do if I handled them badly, each attributed to the sort of person who will raise it and phrased as that person would phrase it. The health economist will want to know what I say about expected QALYs and about a threshold; the physician will ask what I would have a responder do at the scene and will point out that real responders cannot run lotteries; the philosophers will ask why the view does not simply license saving no one, or tossing a coin among all six, and whether the complaint test is more than a redescription of the intuition it was introduced to support.

2. For each objection, a reply of about a hundred and fifty words, speakable, plus an honest marginal note on where the reply is soft — where I am leaning on an undefended premise, or where the concession it requires costs me more than the objection did.

3. The two things I should concede in the body of the paper rather than under questioning, with a sentence each on how to phrase them so they read as dialectical progress rather than retreat.

4. The question I am hoping nobody asks. My nomination is the repetition problem: that whatever licenses me to save the one on a single occasion also licenses a settled policy of always saving the one, and that it is at the level of policy that the numbers return with force. Tell me whether I have picked the right one, and give me the strongest version of it rather than the version I would find comfortable.

5. Finally, an extension I have been developing and have not tried out anywhere, on which I need to know whether it is a feature of my account or a reductio of it. Suppose the rescuer can either save the one for certain or attempt a rescue that saves all six with high probability and saves no one if it fails. Each of the five prefers the gamble; the one prefers the certain rescue. So there are five complaints on one side and one on the other, and every one of them is of exactly the kind my test admits: a complaint about how the rescuer's choice bears on the complainant's own survival. The numbers seem to have begun to matter, and to have done so without aggregation, since they enter only as a fact about how many separate prospects the choice worsens. I cannot tell whether this shows that the test recovers the numbers precisely where they are intuitively strongest, or whether it shows that the test has been smuggling them in all along and I have merely found the case where the smuggling is visible. Give me the two best objections to the extension, then your verdict, with the reasoning that would have to be supplied for either answer.

Please argue rather than cite. I know the literature, I do not need a reading list, and I would rather be told that an objection is unanswered than be told who has written about it.
