Q3 (15 marks). The sorites for 'heap':
(P1) One grain of sand does not make a heap.
(P2) For any n, if n grains do not make a heap, then n+1 grains do not make a heap.
(C) So ten thousand grains do not make a heap.

(a) Identify the tolerance premise, and explain the difference between responding to the paradox by denying a premise and responding by rejecting a rule of inference. Give one example of each kind of response, and say what it costs the view. [7]

(b) Now consider a view I'll call Anchored Thresholds (AT). AT says: in every context of utterance there is a sharp cutoff for 'heap' — the smallest heap is the kth pile after the pile the speaker is using as her anchor, for a fixed k. But nothing in the world and nothing in the speaker's psychology settles which pile is the anchor: whether a given pile is the anchor is itself a borderline matter, and moving the anchor by one pile moves the cutoff by one pile. AT's defenders claim this lets them hold on to the intuition that no single grain makes the difference while still keeping classical logic within a context.

Assess AT. (i) Does it block the sorites, and if it does, does it do that by making a premise false or by making the argument invalid? (ii) An objector says AT is epistemicism in disguise: if there is a sharp cutoff in each context, then there is a determinate answer to 'is this a heap?' in each context, and the whole mystery about where the boundary lies just reappears inside the context. Does AT have an answer to that? [8]

Student answer:

[1] The tolerance premise is (P2). It says the predicate is insensitive to small differences: adding a single grain never turns a non-heap into a heap. The paradox is that P1 and P2 both look certain and the reasoning looks fine, but the conclusion is ridiculous.

[2] On the two kinds of response: you can reject a premise, or you can keep both premises and say the argument is invalid. An example of the second kind is the dialetheic, or subvaluationist, response, which holds on to P2 by letting a borderline sentence be neither true nor false — that is, by dropping bivalence — so that both piles in a borderline pair can get the same verdict without contradiction. The cost is that you loose classical logic, or at least you can't use it everywhere.

[3] An example of the first kind is epistemicism, which says P2 is false: there is an n such that n grains isn't a heap and n+1 is, but we can never find out where. The cost is having to explain why the boundary is unknowable and why everyone feels so sure there isn't one. Epistemicism also keeps bivalence, since every sentence is either true or false even in the borderline region.

[4] (b) I think AT does block the sorites, and it does it by making the argument invalid rather than by making P2 false. Because the anchor can shift by one pile without anything noticeable happening, 'heap' doesn't keep a single meaning across the long chain of inferences, so the argument equivocates — a bit like 'I am here' said in different places. That is also how AT captures the 'no particular grain matters' intuition: for any grain you pick, there is an admissible anchor on which it is the decisive one and another on which it isn't, so there is no answer, independent of a context, about which grain makes the difference.

[5] I don't think the objection works. The epistemicist says there is a boundary and we don't know it; AT says the anchor isn't settled by the world or by the speaker, so the cutoff isn't merely unknown, it's unfixed — there's no fact of the matter which pile is the anchor, and so no fact of the matter where the cutoff is. But AT can still keep classical logic within a context, because in any given context the cutoff is sharp, and so every sentence of the form 'n grains make a heap' is either true or false there: bivalence holds. So the mystery doesn't come back and the objection fails.
