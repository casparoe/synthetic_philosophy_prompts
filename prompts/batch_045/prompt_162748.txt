I teach a fourth-year seminar — this term it's called Persons, Property, and Other Animals — and a student who reads far too much of the internet handed me an argument after class that I havent been able to shake. It's a version of the argument from marginal cases, but run as a sorites, and it seems to eat its own tail. Here it is as I reconstructed it:

(1) A being's moral status, if it has any, must be grounded in some property that can matter to that being — a difference in status has to be a difference that shows up in someone's experience, interests, or what-it's-likeness. Bare membership in a biological kind can't do that work. (2) Being a member of Homo sapiens is exactly such a bare kind-property: on its own it makes no difference to how anything matters to you, it's only ever a rough proxy for the psychological capacities that do the real work. So speciesism is structurally like racism or sexism, as the anti-speciesists say. (3) So the property that does the work is some underlying psychological capacity. Take the usual candidate: sentience, the capacity for valenced experience. (4) Now the dimmer switch. Start with a normal adult human and swap out the neural machinery supporting valenced experience a small group of cells at a time, each replacement doing the same job very slightly less well, until you arrive at something behaviourally similar but with no valenced experience at all. Every single step changes the being's status-relevant capacities by less than a bad night's sleep does. (5) If moral status is all-or-nothing, and no single step in the series makes a difference to status, then no sequence of steps does either, and the endpoint has exactly the status of the start. (6) And the construction runs in reverse too: pick a richer criterion — diachronic self-awareness, being a subject of a life, whatever you like — and it sits on a continuum running up from oyster-ish being to something super-human, with no bright line anywhere along it. (7) So every possible criterion for moral status is arbitrary in precisely the way species membership was supposed to be. The arbitrariness objection therefore proves far too much: it doesn't just knock out speciesism, it knocks out sentience, personhood, and every other stopping point, leaving only "everything has full status" (absurd) or "nothing does" (worse). (8) Which means either we go back to some version of speciesism, or we grant that a mouse and a human sit on the same rung, or we grant that a human with advanced dementia sits on the mouse's rung.

I've built a good chunk of my career on (1) and (2), and I'd keep them over almost anything; I also really don't want (8), any disjunct of it. But I can't locate the damage. My suspicion is that "makes no difference" or "arbitrary" is doing one thing in (2) and something subtly different by (7), though I also wonder whether (5) is smuggling in a bivalence that (1) never licensed, or whether the real culprit is the move from "there is no sharp cut-off" to "there is no non-arbitrary cut-off." Name the step you'd reject, say why it looked so innocent, and tell me what each of the alternatives would cost me — I'd rather pay the cheapest price than keep pretending the argument is sound.

Give me exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each. No preamble, and no summing-up paragraph at the end — just the five items.
