Two people in my department got into it after a teaching-and-learning workshop on patient and worker safety culture, and they've asked me to referee, which I dont feel qualified to do. Here's the case they were arguing over, and then roughly what each of them said.

A food plant installs new packing machines. A long-serving machine operator — call him Errol, 22 years on the floor, no engineering credential — tells the maintenance engineers that a particular machine "sounds wrong" for about ten minutes before it jams, and that it already jammed twice last month. No sensor measures whatever he's hearing, and the engineers' dashboard shows nothing. They thank him and don't investigate. Three weeks later the machine fails, costing a shift and a half of downtime. Afterwards the engineers concede he was probably picking up a real acoustic signature; they just say that in general shop-floor "it sounds funny" reports have a terrible hit rate and they have to triage by evidence type.

Maya: that isn't neutral triage. "Sounds funny" reports from the floor get discounted at a rate that no credentialed person's report would get, and credential-holding correlates with race, class and schooling, so the discount tracks social identity even if nobody on that floor is personally prejudiced. This is the structural version of the credibility-deficit story, not an accusation of bigotry.

Dev: if any institutionally produced discount counts as a credibility deficit, then every reasonable evidentiary filter is an injustice — peer review, triage, burden of proof, all of it — and the concept stops doing any work. And the base-rate point is legitimate: if most such reports are false alarms, discounting them is rational, and rational discounting isn't prejudice.

Maya: two replies. First, the rationality point conflates two different objects for the base rate. The engineers discounted a kind of person (floor worker, no credential), not a kind of claim (unverified acoustic report). If the plant manager's son, home from engineering school for the summer, had said the same sentence, it would have been logged and checked — same claim, different life. Second, the harm isn't just the downtime. Errol was used as a sensor and not as a source: they were happy for him to notice things, just not for him to be right about them in a way that changed a decision. He said afterwards he'd stopped mentioning anything because it wasn't worth the eye-roll, and that's the silencing part.

Dev: I'll grant the silencing is a real cost, and I'll grant the son hypothetical shows the filter is leaky. But I don't think either of the two standard categories fits, and that's my actual point. The usual distinction is between credibility deficits (a hearer downgrades a speaker) and gaps in shared interpretive resources (a speaker lacks the concepts to make sense of, or make intelligible, what's happening to them). Errol isn't confused — he knew exactly what he was hearing and had a name for it. So it's not the second. And it isn't the first in the strict sense either: the engineers didn't think Errol was less likely to be right about what he heard; they thought that what he heard couldn't be evidence for them. What's missing isn't a concept Errol lacks — it's an uptake slot on the hearer's side. So call it something else, an institutional uptake failure, and then the workshop was just wrong about which box this goes in.

Maya: fine, call it uptake failure, but the asymmetry is what makes it an injustice rather than bad design. The same institution routinely draws on Errol's tacit knowledge — he trains the apprentices, he's the guy they call when the line is down. So the firm is perfectly willing to extract his know-how when it's useful for output, and refuses to credit him when his knowing would shift authority. I call this epistemic double-entry: the knowledge gets entered on the production side of the ledger and not on the decision side. That isn't a design glitch, it's a pattern about who gets to be a knower around here, and it's exactly what the injustice label is for, because the cost lands on the same people every time.

Dev: I like the phrase, but I think it proves too much or too little. If the wrong is extraction without credit, the natural remedy is institutional — give the floor a formal reporting channel, weight it, log it — and you and I agree on the fix. The disagreement is only whether we additionally say the institution wronged him as a knower, and I don't see what that adds once the channel is specified. Also: suppose the engineers had checked and the machine had been fine. Would Errol still have been wronged? If yes, the wrong sits in their heads and is independent of outcomes; if no, it was just a bad bet that happened to lose. I think you have to say yes, and I think that makes the position unfalsifiable.

Maya: I do say yes, and it isn't unfalsifiable — you can check whether the discount is identity-tracking, and whether it's corrigible, which here it wasn't, because there was no route through which he could have been vindicated even in principle. And that we agree on the fix isn't an objection to my description; a broken leg is still broken whether you set it or operate.
