At work we're building a warehouse robot and I've been arguing with a coworker about the control stack. His pitch is that we shouldn't have a reward function at all: just train one big generative model on the robot's entire sensory stream and have it act by minimizing expected prediction error over a horizon of a few minutes, with the precision weights learned rather than hand-set. He says goal-directed behavior falls out of that for free and that a reward term is a crutch. I think it's smuggling. The robot only looks purposive because we decided in advance which parts of the sensory stream have to be kept in a narrow band (battery voltage, distance to the pallet it's carrying) and which parts it's allowed to be surprised by. I've started calling this prior laundering — the teleology isn't derived from prediction, it's been moved into the choice of what counts as a prior preference, and then you get to announce that the system invented its own goals.

My credence that a system built exactly that way, scaled up far enough, would end up with something that genuinely deserves to be called its own goals rather than laundered ones is about 0.3. His is about 0.75.

Give me your number, but I mainly want the decomposition. The terms I'd want split out: wether minimizing expected prediction error over a horizon really does break into a pragmatic term and an information-seeking term in a way that yields genuine goal-directedness, or only behavior that looks goal-directed from outside; whether the prior-laundering objection is fatal or whether there's a non-question-begging way to fix which sensory channels are privileged; whether the epistemic, curiosity-ish term pushes a system that can model its own off switch toward disabling it, even though nobody ever gave it a reason to stay on; and whether the word "goal" is doing any work at all if the resulting behavior is indistinguishable from a well-tuned reward-based agent. Put a number and one line of justification on each, say how you're combining them into the headline number, and tell me which of those terms my coworker and I are most likely actually disagreeing about. Then: what's the cheapest experiment we could run on a small version of this that would move your number the most, and in which direction?
