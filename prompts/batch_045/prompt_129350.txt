After twenty-five years of running a small engineering firm I was appointed, rather to my own surprise, as a lay member of a professional conduct tribunal. The first case I sat has stayed with me. The panel split over a rule whose words plainly covered the conduct in front of us and whose purpose plainly had nothing to do with it. What presented itself as a disagreement about facts turned out, once we talked it through, to be a disagreement about method: one of my colleagues read the instrument, the other asked what it was for, and neither of them had any name for what he was doing. I have been asked to give a short talk to incoming tribunal members on how to argue about interpretation, and I would like to build it around the trial scene in *The Merchant of Venice* rather than around a case extract, because I suspect the scene dramatizes something about method that a case extract hides.

What I want from you is a reading of that scene, and of the play's treatment of law generally, through the textualism-versus-purposivism dispute. Please do not give me a plot summary or any quotations from the text; I know the scene and I do not want to read lines aloud. I want the argument, and I want it without citations to cases or articles, since I am not a lawyer and could not check them anyway.

Let me say what I think is going on so that you can correct me. It seems to me that three different things are being confused in the usual way of talking, and that the scene separates them better than the jurisprudence does. First, there is what textualists actually claim to be doing: recovering the communicative content that a competent reader at the time of enactment would have taken these words to convey, which is not at all the same thing as reading them word by word with maximum narrowness. Second, there is the older equity-of-the-statute style of reasoning: read the instrument in light of the mischief it was made to remedy, and extend it to cases within that reason though outside its letter, or narrow it where the letter catches what the reason does not. Third, there is something I have no settled name for and which I would like you to name and characterize properly for me: the deliberate adoption of the narrowest semantic content of the words in order to defeat the point the words were written to serve. My impression is that no textualist method actually licenses this, because textualism is a theory about what words communicate rather than a technique for resisting communication, and that it is therefore a parasite on the first position rather than a version of it.

Assuming that distinction is sound, here is what I want you to work through. Which of the three is Portia doing when she rules that the bond grants flesh but not blood? My own suspicion is that this is not literalism at all but gap-filling: the bond is silent about blood, a term has to be added, and it is added in whatever direction defeats the instrument, while being announced as though it followed from the words themselves. If that is right, then the most famous literalist moment in English literature is purposive reasoning in literalist costume, which is a more uncomfortable lesson for my tribunal members than the one usually drawn from it. And then there is the second ruling, on the statute concerning aliens who seek the life of a citizen, which she reads expansively, by indirect attempts, to a case in which Shylock was after a forfeiture he expected to be paid rather than after a life. The same judge is a strict constructionist in one breath and an expansive purposivist in the next. Is the play therefore a piece of legal realism before the letter, a demonstration that method is the vocabulary in which outcomes are rationalized? And if it is, is that the last word, or does the play itself resist that reading?

I would also like you to press on mercy. Portia asks for it before she interprets, and the Duke grants it after she has finished, and mercy in the play is not a method of reading anything: it looks much more like the Aristotelian notion of equity as a correction of law where the generality of law fails. Is mercy offered as a genuine alternative to choosing between letter and purpose, or is it the velvet glove on a conclusion already reached, and what does your answer do to my use of the scene?

Finally, the constraint I cannot get around. Shylock is himself the one who insists on the letter and refuses mercy; and the interpretive virtuosity of the scene is deployed to take from him his goods, his religion, and his standing before the law, which is what allows the court to give his estate away. So literalism here is not a neutral instrument that happens to be used well or badly; it is what the outsider demands and what the insiders turn back on him at the moment they decide he is no longer a person in law. I need to know honestly whether the scene can be used as a teaching example at all without becoming an exercise in admiring technique, and if it can, how to frame it. Tell me plainly if you think it cannot.

Give me the two or three moments in the scene you would actually put in front of the group, what claim about method each one is meant to make vivid, and the objection most likely to come from the one lawyer in the room, which I take to be some version of the charge that the scene is about a loophole and not about interpretation at all. End by saying what the play shows about the letter and the purpose that a written case on the same question could not.
