I'm taking an upper-level seminar this term on democracy and political equality, and we have reached the section on money in politics, where one slogan keeps doing an enormous amount of unexamined work. In last week's session I put forward a rule for discussion: anyone may spend unlimited sums trying to persuade the public, but payments to a professional intermediary for direct communication with a legislator or a regulator would be capped at a modest annual figure — a few thousand dollars, say — so that a small association or a single concerned citizen could still hire representation, while the market in expensive access, drafting, and expertise is substantially curtailed. Writing letters yourself, turning up at a representative's office, organising others to do so, and buying advertising would all be untouched. A classmate replied that money is speech, so a limit on buying advocacy is simply a limit on speech, and there was nothing more to say. The conversation moved on.

I want to arrive on Thursday with the slogan disambiguated rather than repeated. The case I raised seems to separate questions the slogan normally runs together: there is the person who pays, the person who is paid to speak, the official who is addressed, and the public whose interests are at stake. So I'd like a menu of the precise theses that money is speech could be asserting — from the obvious platitude that communication in a modern economy requires spending, through the claim that spending is itself expressive conduct or an act of affiliation, through the claim that hiring an agent to speak for you is protected as your own speech, through the claim that money is a proxy for which speakers get heard at all, up to the strong anti-egalitarian claim that the state can no more level differences in wealth than it can level differences in eloquence or enthusiasm — and for each, a verdict: trivial, substantive and defensible, or false or question-begging.

Three things matter most to me. First, which of those readings, if any, would actually entitle my classmate to his conclusion that banning the purchase of advocacy just is banning speech, rather than merely making some speech harder to produce or to amplify. Second, whether there is a reading on which the slogan protects the person who pays but not the person who is paid — the lobbyist, who is silent unless compensated and who would describe what she sells as a service — and how seriously I should take the comparison our professor drew with a ban on paying for legal representation, which plainly burdens rights without anyone supposing that lawyers are speech. Third, whether the slogan asserts anything that the more careful sentence people have a right to speak effectively, and to enlist others in speaking, does not already assert, or whether its rhetorical force comes entirely from sliding between the trivial reading and the strong one.
