Before I retired I spent rather too many years recording from single units in the somatosensory thalamus, and I have lately been sitting in on a philosophy of mind reading group at my old department, where we have come to the topic of pain and its badness. At a dinner last month a former colleague from the hospital pain clinic dismissed the philosophical fuss in a way I have not been able to stop thinking about: he said that pain is simply the body's smoke alarm, and that asymbolia is nothing more than the alarm going off with the bell disconnected. Patients with the condition, as I understand it, still localise and rate the stimulus and insist that it hurts, while showing none of the usual distress or urge to escape — some are even said to look amused by it. His conclusion was that the badness of a pain is nothing over and above the motivational output of the nociceptive system: the sensory channel reports tissue state, the affective channel does all the normative work, and if you cut the wire the experience that remains is not bad at all — which for him means that the ideal analgesic is precisely something that disconnects the bell while leaving the detector running, and that anyone who thinks pain is intrinsically bad has mistaken an alarm for a fire.

I would like that analogy audited properly rather than just waved away. Lay out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the part of the fire, the smoke, the sensor, the wiring, the bell, the loudness of the bell, the householder's getting out of bed, a false alarm, a low-battery chirp at three in the morning — and then say where the mapping fails, and which failures matter for the use he is making of it. I have three particular worries I cannot sort out myself. First, an alarm is normally bad for you only because of what it signals, and yet a smoke alarm that chirps all night is bad for you whether or not there is a fire, which suggests the analogy might license the opposite conclusion from his. Second, I think the analogy may be concealing that a silent alarm can be produced in several quite different ways — the bell removed, the gain turned down, the output rerouted to somewhere that does not wake anyone — and I suspect these correspond to different accounts of what asymbolia actually is, only one of which supports his conclusion; I would be grateful if you distinguished them. Third, consider a made-up mirror image, which I shall call isolated aversive arousal: the sensory-discriminative channel is silent, so there is no location, intensity or quality the subject can report, while the aversive and motivational response is intact or even amplified. In alarm terms that is a bell ringing at full volume with no detector attached to it.

What I would find most telling, if it can be sustained, is the thought that the second and third worries pull in opposite directions: one suggests the badness cannot simply be identified with the output, the other that it cannot simply be identified with the input either. So I would like your verdict on whether the smoke-alarm picture can describe both cases consistently, what it quietly imports about a clean split between detection and response that the phenomenon may not contain, whether the conclusion my colleague drew survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and what a better analogy would be if there is one.
