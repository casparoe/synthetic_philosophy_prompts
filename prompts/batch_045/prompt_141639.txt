I was listening to a philosophy podcast on a long drive and the host spent about ten minutes on something he called "Bradley's regress", and I've since gone down a bit of a rabbit hole and cant tell what's solid in it. Can you audit his account for me? Here's roughly what he said, compressed:

"A judgement like 'Bertie snores' isn't just a list of words — they hang together as one claim that can be true or false. But try to build that unity out of parts and you get stuck. You've got the subject, you've got the predicate, and you've got the relation that's meant to hold the two together. But that relation is itself just one more item, so it too has to be related to the subject and the predicate by a further relation, and that one by another, and so on without end. A proposition can therefore never pull itself together: every attempt to explain its unity by adding a connector just gives you a heap plus one more loose item, and an infinite series never even gets started, so the heap never becomes a unity. Bradley concluded from this that relations, and so relational facts and propositions themselves, are ultimately unreal — only the undifferentiated Absolute is real, and that's why he's an idealist. The modern fix is to say the proposition is just the ordered set of its constituents, with the relation doing double duty as both one of the parts and the glue, and if that doesn't satisfy you you can be a primitivist and just take unity as brute, which is more or less where the debate has landed. Incidentally this is the same point Lewis Carroll was making in his dialogue about Achilles and the Tortoise: you can always ask for one more thing, and you never arrive."

A few things make me suspicious, but I don't know enough to say where he's gone wrong. First, I'd had the impression that the real target was the set-theoretic / ordered-tuple conception of a proposition, because a set has no built-in unity — but he presents the target as "relations" as such, which seems like a much bigger claim. Second, someone on a forum said the original text actually runs more than one version of the argument and that it's about properties as much as about propositions; is that right, and does it matter for what he said? Third, his example was a one-place predicate, but everything I've read since states the regress for a two-place case, where you have two objects and a relation between them — does it even get off the ground in the one-place case, or is that a different puzzle about the copula? Fourth, the Carroll comparison feels like a category mistake to me, but I can't articulate why. And fifth: near the end he insisted on a seperate distinction between "the relation" and "the tie", and said contemporary people disagree about whether there's one generic tie shared by all propositions or a bespoke one for each — is that a genuine distinction or just relabelling the problem?

So: go through his summary more or less sentence by sentence and tell me what someone who works on this would accept, what they'd wince at as a simplification, and what is just wrong. I especially want to know whether the bit about the relation being "just one more item" is as obviously fine as he makes it sound, whether the idealist conclusion actually follows from the parts of the argument that survive, and whether "primitivism" really is where people ended up or whether that's his editorialising.
