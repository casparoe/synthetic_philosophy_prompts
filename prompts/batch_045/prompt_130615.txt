I am preparing a one-page handout for a machine-learning reading group on whether reinforcement-learning systems can suffer. I want its core to be a decision tree that takes a member from a shared starting point — a standard deep RL agent receiving negative reward on most steps of an episode — to one named position, sorting people by what they say about cases rather than by which label they prefer.

Build the tree out of the logical space rather than out of a list of isms: give the branching questions and the combinations they permit first, then locate the actual positions in that space. These distinctions should do the branching work, each phrased as a question someone can answer without already knowing where it leads: (i) is a negative reward signal already valence, or is valence a further role the signal must play in the system's economy — capturing attention, reorienting action, controlling counterfactual learning? (ii) must the bad state be phenomenally conscious, and if so which architectural marker is meant to supply the consciousness (global broadcast, a self-model with a persistence target, thick temporal integration)? (iii) does suffering require a subject with a stake that extends over time, something for which things can be going worse, or will a momentary episode with no self-model do? (iv) does suffering track the reward received or the surprise about it — the temporal-difference error? (v) must the badness be the system's own, or is the trainer's badness enough, as with a language model whose continuations a reward model scores low?

Deliver exactly five leaves. Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, one per leaf, with no introduction and no conclusion: each item begins with a compact path — the answers to the branch questions that reach the leaf, joined by arrows — followed by at most two sentences, one giving the leaf's name (or marking it unoccupied) together with its most awkward commitment, and one saying what it implies for a single one of these five cases: the failing PPO agent; a purely feedforward regressor with high loss and no recurrence; the RLHF-tuned model the reward model dislikes; a model-based agent whose learned world model predicts a failure its policy cannot avoid; and an agent whose per-step reward is constant and clipped, so that only its prediction error varies. At least one item should be a combination your questions allow that nobody actually defends — mark it unoccupied and say in a clause whether that is because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored — and flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot really separate rather than forcing a distinction between them.
