I'm writing a term paper for a seminar on Renaissance political thought, and I've got stuck on a move that everyone around me treats as obvious. In the chapter of The Prince where he asks how much fortune governs human affairs, Machiavelli says fortune is the arbiter of roughly half our actions and leaves us to direct the rest, and then offers a simile: he compares her to one of those raging rivers, which when in flood overflows the plains, sweeping away trees and buildings; and yet, though its nature be such, men may, when the weather becomes fair, make provision with defences and barriers so that, rising again, the waters may pass away by canal and their force be neither so unrestrained nor so dangerous. Fortune, he says, shows her power where valour has not prepared to resist her — my translation prints valour where others print virtue or leave virtù untranslated, and the river reading seems quietly to depend on that choice. My study group, and most of the assigned secondary literature, then leans on the simile to settle what the book is really saying: virtù is foresight and institutional preparation — laws, arms, orders, the Italy-versus-Germany comparison he draws in the same chapter — so the core doctrine is prudential readiness, and the notorious closing lines of the chapter, where he says it is better to be impetuous than cautious because fortune is a woman who yields to the bold, are rhetoric, or a borrowing from his letters, or an inconsistency not to be built on. The river is thus used to domesticate Machiavelli: no genuine recommendation of audacity, and The Prince turns out continuous with the institutionalist republicanism of the Discourses. My thesis needs the preparedness reading, so I have an interest in the analogy's surviving — but I'd rather find out now if it doesn't.

What I want is an audit rather than a verdict. First, a correspondence table: what in the simile plays the role of the flood, the fair weather, the defences and barriers, the canal, the undefended plain, and the people who flee, and what each is supposed to be in The Prince. Second, the disanalogies, and — this is what I can't do myself — a separation of the ones that are merely decorative from the ones that actually bear on the conclusion my group is drawing. Four worry me, but I don't know how much weight they carry. (i) A river flood is exogenous and purposeless, whereas most of what undoes Machiavelli's princes is other princes exercising their own virtù; one agent's fortune is another agent's virtù, and a hazard partly produced by the adaptive responses of others isn't obviously something you prepare against by building rather than by positioning yourself in a game. (ii) A dike is a durable stock, accumulated in calm periods and then doing its work passively; virtù in The Prince looks like something that must be re-performed at each occa­sione and that can be spent or lost, and the new prince, who is the book's addressee, arguably never gets the calm season in which to build — the remedy seems to presuppose the settled polity whose absence is the problem. (iii) The simile pictures a hazard of known, repeatable frequency, the sort of thing one provisions against; Machiavelli's occasioni are singular and unrepeatable, and I can't see how he could have a base rate for them, so the river may be smuggling in a modern risk-management frame — measurable risk rather than unmeasurable uncertainty — into a text with no room for it. (iv) The simile does not resist the water but channels it, the waters passing away by canal, which seems more permissive than the preparedness reading allows and perhaps closer to the audacity passage it is used against; note too that Machiavelli applies it to whole countries and their institutions, while the conclusion drawn from it concerns an individual prince's mode of proceeding — and an individual prince, he says himself, cannot deviate from what nature inclines him to.

So: which of these, if any, is fatal; are there further disanalogies I've missed that matter more (for instance, is the fortune of the river passage even the same fortune as the woman of the closing lines?); does the conclusion my group draws survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time; and if the river can't bear that weight, is there a better comparison — someone suggested winds and a helmsman, someone else a card player and the deal — or will each of them inherit the same defect? Please lead with the correspondence table and keep the entire answer under 700 words.
