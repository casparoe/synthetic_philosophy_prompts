I have been turning over an argument about moral luck that ends somewhere I cannot follow, and I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict.

(1) Control: a person can be morally assessed only for what was within their control. For the rest, they may be fortunate or unfortunate, but not praiseworthy or blameworthy.

(2) Resultant luck: take two drivers, same blood alcohol, same street, same speed. One finds an empty road and gets home. The other meets a child who runs out between parked cars and kills her. Nothing either of them controlled separates the two cases; the difference lies entirely in how the world happened to be.

(3) So the difference between them is not a difference in blameworthiness.

(4) The same should hold whenever outcomes diverge by luck: the killer whose bullet strikes and the attempter whose bullet is turned aside by a passing bird; the donor whose gift buys bednets and the donor whose gift is embezzled before it arrives. Equal blame, equal credit.

(5) Generalizing: only choices are within our control and results never are, so blameworthiness is a function of what the agent chose and of the risk that choice imposed — never of what actually happened. Results are morally inert.

(6) But (5) seems to prove far too much. If results are inert, I have no reason to care whether anything I do actually works, no reason to check afterward, and no reason to prefer a course that succeeds to one that fails so long as both were reasonable at the time. It also makes the family's resentment and the recipient's gratitude misdirected, since both are responses to something outside anyone's control.

(7) And it turns on itself: whether I accept or reject this argument is largely a function of how I was raised, what I happened to read, and what sort of day I am having. So if the argument is sound, no one can be credited with getting it right or blamed for getting it wrong.

(8) So either (1) is false, or (3) does not follow from it, or (5) overreaches, or I should accept (5) and train myself to stop caring whether my actions succeed — which I find I cannot do.

The step I am least willing to give up is (3): I remain convinced the two drivers are equally blameworthy. What I cannot accept is the first half of (6). Tell me which step fails and why it looked sound in the first place; if a premise is ambiguous, say which reading you are rejecting and what the other reading would commit me to. I have also heard it said that resentment and gratitude are not in the business of tracking desert at all, so that surrendering them costs less than I suppose — if that is the move available here, say so and say what it costs. Please answer in American English.
