I am in my final year of secondary school, and over the winter break I have been writing an essay of about two thousand words for a philosophy competition on the question of whether the numbers should count when one can save either one stranger or five. I have roughly five hundred words left to spend, and before I spend them I would like comments on the draft below. My questions are at the end.

***

**Should the numbers count? A partial answer**

**1. The case.** Six strangers will die unless I intervene. I can either rescue the first, or rescue the other five, but not both. Nothing else distinguishes them: no special duties, no prior claims, no differences in how much each has to lose. As far as I can tell there are three familiar answers.

(a) *Aggregation.* Five deaths are worse than one, so save the five.
(b) *The no-numbers view.* The numbers do not count. Each person has an equal claim on me, and the only fair response is to give each of them an equal chance, which here means tossing a coin between the two groups: every one of the six then has a one-half chance of survival. This is the position I have seen attributed to John Taurek, though I have only encountered it through secondary discussion.
(c) *A numbers-weighted lottery.* Give chances proportional to the numbers, so the five are saved with probability five-sixths. I am not sure anyone actually holds this; it may be a position that exists only as something for other views to be contrasted with.

**2. My proposal: the root rule.** Let the chance assigned to a group be proportional to the square root of the number of people in it. Since the two groups here have five members and one, the five get √5/(1 + √5) ≈ 0.69 collectively and the single stranger gets ≈ 0.31. Two features recommend it. First, when the groups are the same size the rule returns the coin toss, so it agrees with (b) exactly where (b) is most plausible. Second, as the larger group grows its share approaches one but never reaches it: with ten thousand on one side and one on the other, the single stranger still gets about a one percent chance rather than being outvoted to zero. The expected number of lives saved is about 3.76, which sits between the coin toss's 3 and the aggregator's 5.

**3. Why sub-linear?** When several people each press the same kind of claim on me, I can perform only one action, so their claims have to be pooled into a single action-guiding reason before they can move me at all. And they do not pool additively. The five are in the same situation, they would be rescued by the same act, and what each of them asks for is, at the level of the action available to me, the same thing the others ask for. Pooling such claims is like pooling partly redundant testimony: five witnesses who saw the same event from the same window confirm one another, but the fifth adds far less than the first did, because each new witness is confirming something already established rather than supplying an independent reason. If the claims combined additively we would get the proportional view; if they did not combine at all we would get the coin toss. Sub-linear combination is the natural middle, and the square root is the simplest smooth instance of it: the combined force of n identical claims grows as n raised to some power between zero and one.

**4. Two gaps I know about.**
(i) *The exponent.* My argument establishes only that the combination is sub-linear. Nothing in it selects 0.5 rather than 0.3 or 0.8, and yet the exponent is what decides how many people live. That is uncomfortable.
(ii) *The conversion step.* From the claim that the five's pooled reason is about 2.24 times as strong as the single stranger's, I move straight to giving the five a 0.69 chance. But comparative strength of reasons does not by itself dictate a probability. I have silently assumed that chances should be proportional to strengths. Other mappings are available — ones more or less responsive to differences in strength — and this may be where the real disagreement lies: perhaps (a) and (b) are less divided over whether numbers count than over how comparative reasons should be turned into action under uncertainty. Note that even full additivity does not get you to (a) under a proportional mapping: with an exponent of one the five would get five-sixths, not certainty.

**5. The objection I find hardest.** Suppose I will face a hundred such choices in a row, involving different strangers each time, and I know this in advance. My rule saves about 3.76 lives per case; saving the five every time saves 5. Across the hundred cases that is roughly 124 additional deaths, all of them foreseeable. My attempted reply is that the rule governs each decision, that a pattern of decisions is a different object of assessment from a decision, and that I may be permitted — perhaps required — to plan the sequence differently from how I would act in any one of its members. But I cannot tell whether this is a genuine implication of my view or a collapse into straightforward expected-value maximisation one level up, at the level of policies rather than acts.

**6. Scope.** Everything above concerns the number of lives at stake. I have not said anything about amounts of benefit: one person losing forty years versus five people losing eight years each. I suspect sub-linearity in numbers does not automatically give sub-linearity in magnitudes, and that extending the rule would be a further argument rather than a corollary — which worries me, because most real decisions are of the second kind.

***

I would be grateful for comments on five things.

1. Is the root rule genuinely a fourth option, or is it only (b) or (c) in different notation? I cannot get hold of academic journals from here, so please tell me in your own terms whether the logical space is already occupied and what the neighbouring positions look like, rather than pointing me to papers.
2. Where exactly does the argument in section 3 break? In particular: is the analogy between pooling redundant testimony and pooling several people's claims doing real work, or is it a picture with a formula bolted onto it? And can the five even be described as pressing the same claim, when they are five distinct people each pressing his or her own?
3. Is the sequence objection in section 5 fatal? Can a view like mine answer it without turning into aggregation at the level of policies? Is the move from assessing individual decisions to assessing patterns coherent, or is it special pleading?
4. Of the two gaps in section 4, which deserves my remaining five hundred words? My instinct is the conversion step, since it may reframe the whole debate rather than merely tidy up my version of it, but I would like the reasoning for and against.
5. If I have misunderstood the no-numbers view — in particular, if it is not committed to a coin toss between the groups — please say so, because my rule is built to coincide with it when the groups are equal in size, and that coincidence is doing much of the persuasive work.

I would rather have blunt criticism than encouragement, and I would rather be told that the idea is unoriginal than that it is promising.
