I'm drafting a novel, and one of my characters is a smug, extremely online media critic who has decided that Charles Sanders Peirce basically predicted the internet. I want her to be more than right — I want her to be interestingly right, and I dont want the scene to read like a Wikipedia summary. So do the actual work.

Peirce died in 1914, so he never saw a feed. In his 1877 essay on the fixation of belief he sorts the ways of settling belief into four methods — tenacity (just cling to whatever you already believe), authority (let an institution settle it for you), the a priori or agreeable-to-reason method, and the scientific method — and he argues each of the first three undermines itself: tenacity in particular fails in practice because we are social animals and running into other people's contrary opinions wears us down. My character's twist is that engagement-ranked feeds repair exactly that failure mode: the algorithm hands each person a private public that mostly agrees with them, so the social friction that was supposed to break tenacity now props it up.

So: drop Peirce into the present. How would he diagnose social-media driven political polarization, echo chambers, and the standing suspicion that the other side is arguing in bad faith? Is the feed best described as tenacity made stable, as the method of authority with the institution swapped out for an influencer, or as something his four-method taxonomy has no slot for? Does his fallibilism commit him to treating opponents as reasoning in good faith, or does his framework give him grounds for calling some disagreement pathological rather than merely mistaken? And what would he prescribe, given that the scientific method for him meant a real community of inquirers being forced by a real external world to converge, not fact-checking? For context, her foil in the book is a small-town newspaper editor who thinks the cure is simply to show people the other side — I'd like to know whether Peirce would back that or whether it's one of the moves his own argument rules out.

Sections, please, in this order:

"What Peirce had" — about 150 words, the four methods and why three of them collapse.
"The diagnosis" — about 350 words, the feed mapped onto his taxonomy, and which method it really is.
"Good faith" — about 250 words, on whether the other side is arguing in bad faith.
"Where it breaks" — about 250 words, what polarization does that his framework can't reach.
"What he'd prescribe" — about 200 words, including a verdict on the newspaper editor's cure.
"Lines for the scene" — 4 or 5 short things my character could say out loud, in her voice, smug but not gibberish.
