I am a retired physical chemist — thirty-odd years in surface science, now auditing philosophy and, more recently, chairing a small interview series attached to a continuing-education programme. Each session is an hour of close questioning of a single philosopher about a single thesis; the audience is largely scientifically trained and largely innocent of metaphysics. My next guest works on future contingents, and I would be grateful for help drafting the question sheet.

Her position, as I have reconstructed it from her published work and from an exchange of letters, has three components. First, she is an Ockhamist about Aristotle's example of tomorrow's sea battle: exactly one of the two future-tensed statements is true now and the other false, even though nothing in the world at present settles which; the asymmetry is that the past is fixed and the future is not, and truth, on her account, does not carry necessity with it — the medieval distinction between the necessity of the consequent and the necessity of the consequence is meant to carry that weight. Second, she accepts what I take to be the standard Ockhamist burden: in a branching-time picture of the sort associated with Prior, where the past is single and the future fans out into histories, she grants that she needs a privileged history — the thin red line, the one that will in fact be realised — since otherwise there is nothing for the present truth of the future-tensed statement to consist in. Third, and this is the part I believe she has not defended in print and the part I most want to press, she holds that the asymmetry of fixity itself is not a primitive feature of time but is inherited from the thermodynamic asymmetry of records: an event is fixed once traces of it exist whose formation was thermodynamically irreversible, and the future is open because nothing has recorded it yet. She likes this because it makes openness independent of determinism; fixity, on her account, is a matter of what has been recorded, not of what the laws and the present state determine.

Please draft nine or ten questions, each with a parenthetical note of the jargon it presupposes and each with a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to make. I want at least one question on each of the following five matters, and I want the questions arranged so that the later ones build on the concessions the earlier ones are likely to extract.

One: the step in the original puzzle from it is true now that there will be a battle to it is now necessary that there will be a battle. Ask whether, once the distinction between the necessity of the consequent and the necessity of the consequence is granted, anything of the sea-battle puzzle is left over at all, or whether the residual problem is really about bivalence and assertion rather than about necessity.

Two: why she is not a supervaluationist — the option on which, if the matter is unsettled, neither the affirmative nor the negative is true, while the disjunction that either there will be a battle or there will not is true. Press especially on the retrospective case: the admiral who said yesterday that there would be a battle, and who says today, after the battle has occurred, that he was right. Ask what truth value his earlier utterance had, and whether the gap view can say anything better than that he was neither right nor wrong.

Three: the work the thin red line is supposed to do. She has a stock answer here, which I would like the sequence of questions to make progressively harder to use: the line is not an additional entity, she says, it is simply whichever history is actual, and actuality is not a matter of degree. Give me questions that force her to say whether the privileged history is in principle detectable before the events on it occur, what explanatory or predictive work the notion of actuality does in the theory if it is not, and why a contextually supplied branch — the relativist treatment, on which the truth of a future-tensed claim is assessed relative to a history — would not serve her purposes equally well.

Four: chance and deliberation. If it is already true which way the battle goes, and if one history is already the actual one, then what are the probabilities I assign to the two outcomes? I want a question about whether a privileged history reduces ostensibly objective single-case chances to mere ignorance, and what that would mean for quantum probabilities, which the scientifically trained part of my audience will take to be objective; and a question about why deliberation is not idle if the outcome is already true.

Five: the entropic grounding of fixity, where I suspect she is most vulnerable. I want questions that squeeze both horns: if fixity requires surviving traces, then a past event that left none — a battle fought on an empty stretch of sea a thousand years ago, every trace since dispersed — appears to be unfixed, which is to say that the past is not, after all, settled; but if fixity requires only that the present state carry information about the event, then a deterministic world's present state carries information about every future event, and her openness collapses under determinism, which is exactly what the entropic account was supposed to avoid. Add a question about whether fixity on this account is a matter of degree and a question about time-symmetric cosmological models with a low-entropy boundary condition at both temporal ends, in which traces would appear to point in both directions.

Finally, give me one closing question — not a soft one — about what would count as evidence against the package, and one that asks her to state the strongest objection she thinks she cannot answer. Please also flag, for each question, any piece of terminology my audience would not know, and keep the whole sheet free of biographical or motival questions.
