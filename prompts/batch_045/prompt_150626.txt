I am trying to say what makes it wrong to take someone else's idea, and I keep failing, so I will give my verdicts on cases and ask what principle, if any, they add up to. Suppose a technology I will call the Ledger: anyone can seal a timestamped hash of a design, a premise, or a method, and a court can afterwards establish with certainty both who sealed first and whether a given person's route to the idea was causally independent of the seal. So priority and copying are settled facts in every case below; what I cannot settle is which combinations of them constitute the wrong.

Here are my verdicts.

1. Two founders with no contact build the same routing scheme for grocery delivery, weeks apart. The later one wins the market and the earlier one loses everything. No theft, nothing owed.
2. A studio passes on a writer's pitch, then airs a series with the same premise, the same twist and the same unusual setting, but different characters, scenes and dialogue. Theft, and the abstractness of what was taken does not excuse it.
3. A rival firm buys the product, dismantles it, and reproduces an unpatented mechanism exactly. Fine, even though the copying is total.
4. The same firm hires the designer's former assistant, who brings her old notebooks, and reproduces the same unpatented mechanism. Not fine. This is the pair I cannot reconcile: 3 and 4 differ only in the route to identical information.
5. Someone posts publicly and in jest that the trick is that the constraint was never real. An expert who had never considered the possibility reads the sentence and spends six months building the thing. Not theft, but I want to say that something substantial is owed.
6. A team that would certainly have reached the same design within a fortnight learns it from a defector a fortnight early. Theft, though nothing was lost but time.
7. A living illustrator's entire output is used to train a system that emits new images recognizably in her manner, sold by a firm she never dealt with. I go back and forth on this one.

My tentative principle is that an idea is taken wrongly just when the taker saves a cost that the originator bore and that the taker would otherwise have had to bear. It seems to get 1 and 4, but it appears to condemn 3, which I think is fine, and it seems to yield only a trivial debt in 5 and 6, where my verdicts are not trivial.

Is there a principle, or a small set of candidates, that does better? I would like each candidate stated precisely enough to apply to a new case, with the case that motivates each of its clauses, its verdicts on cases I have not listed — say, a case where the originator would never have used the idea, and a case where the second party knew of the seal and raced to finish first — and an honest statement of which of my verdicts it forces me to give up.
