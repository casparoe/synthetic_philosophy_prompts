My Hume reading group spent two hours on this and I've been trying to write the objection up properly since, and the version I ended up with now seems to prove something nobody could accept, which makes me think I broke something somewhere but I can't see where.

First some terms so the steps arent slippery. A *hard miracle* is an event that would violate a law of nature. A *soft miracle* violates no law but is astronomically improbable: two hundred heads in a row, a room of monkeys producing a grammatical sonnet, the same person winning two big lotteries. A *singular* event is one that's essentially unrepeatable, so there's no track record of relevant trials at all: the origin of life, the origin of the universe, a first-ever resurrection. Most miracle claims people actually care about are singular, and that category is the one I think Hume never handles, but that may be a sepperate problem.

(1) Hume's maxim, as I'd put it: a report establishes an event only if it would be more surprising for the report to be false than for the event to have happened. The credibility of the witnesses has to beat the prior improbability of what they're reporting.

(2) The prior improbability of a hard miracle is set by uniform experience: every observed instance of the relevant kind went one way, so the evidence against the event is as strong as evidence gets.

(3) The credibility of testimony isn't a free-floating primitive. The only reason we trust reports at all is that we have a track record of reports lining up with the world. So testimonial reliability is itself a product of uniform experience.

(4) But then the two quantities can't come apart the way (1) needs them to. The same uniform experience that pushes the prior on the event down is what generates whatever push the testimony has. So whenever an event is improbable enough relative to our background experience, any report of it is automatically too weak to establish it. No report can ever establish a hard miracle — fine, that's Hume's conclusion — but also no report can ever establish a soft miracle, or a novel anomaly, or a one-off.

(5) Which is crazy. People were rationally right to accept reports of stones falling from the sky before the experts came round, of animals that supposedly didn't exist, of patients recovering when they shouldn't have. And basically every empirical fact I believe, I believe because somebody told me. If (1) through (4) are sound then all of that was irrational.

(6) So something has to go, and my guess is (3), but (3) looks obviously true to me and I don't know what you'd put in its place. One guy in the group says the whole thing is just Bayes and Bayes handles it fine, which I don't find helpful because I don't see which term changes.

Two other things I can't place. If the event is singular, (2) seems to have nothing to bite on — there is no uniform experience of the relevant kind, so you have to assimilate it to some broader class, and which class you pick looks like it just decides the answer. And Hume's own supporting claim that miracle stories always come out of ignorant and barbarous ages is itself a historical claim we only know from other people's reports, so I'm not sure his argument leaves itself standing.

If you had to pick one step to reject, which is it, why does it look fine, and what does each of the other options cost me? I'd rather keep (1) and (5) than anything else.
