My book club read a chapter in one of those popular philosophy books about L.A. Paul's work on transformative experience, and I don't know how much of it to trust. It goes roughly like this:

"Paul's argument is that some decisions can't be made rationally at all. Having a child, or the famous case of being offered the chance to become a vampire, is transformative: you cannot know what the experience is like until you have had it, and since you can't imagine it, you can't assign a utility to the outcome. Expected utility theory says to pick the option with the highest expected utility, but here the utility is simply unknown, so decision theory gives no guidance and the choice is arational. Her own examples show how general the problem is — tasting Vegemite for the first time, or seeing red for the first time, is also something you can't know in advance — so really every decision involving novelty is threatened. Her solution is to ask whether you want to discover who you will become, and to treat that desire as the utility of the outcome, which lets expected utility theory do its work after all. Critics have pointed out that testimony settles the whole thing anyway: other parents can describe what it's like, so you can form an estimate and get on with it, and this is why the argument is nowadays generally regarded as refuted."

I half believed it, but the testimony bit seems too easy, and the idea of plugging "wanting to find out" in as a utility struck me as odd given that the whole problem is supposed to be that your preferences change. Which parts would someone who actually works on this accept, where is the chapter quietly shifting the claim, and does the "refuted" verdict follow from the parts that are right?
