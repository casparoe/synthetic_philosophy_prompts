I've been arguing with a friend from my local rationalist meetup — someone who has been around since the Sequences were still being posted, uses "steelman" as a verb, and has unaccountably strong feelings about the repugnant conclusion — about what anyone is even doing when they say that human extinction would be astronomically bad rather than merely as bad as eight billion deaths. Her position, roughly, is that the badness isn't in the deaths at all but in something about the afterlife: that what we care about, and care about enormously, is that humanity goes on without us. She insists all of her case verdicts follow from that one principle. I think she is quietly running two or three different rules that haven't come apart yet because nobody has handed her the case that pulls them apart, and I want your help designing it. I have her verdicts in a spreadsheet because I am like this, and I have normalized them so that Slow Fade — a sterilizing pandemic, no children born after 2050, everyone currently alive dies of old age, everyone has decades of warning — counts as 1.

Her other verdicts: Ninety Percent, in which a plague in 2050 kills ninety percent of humanity but the survivors keep the archives, rebuild, and go on to a future as large and rich as the one we would otherwise have had: 0.01, and she insists this isn't inconsistent, because the thing she cares about survived even though nine out of ten people didn't. Long Dark, in which there is no mass death at all but civilization collapses, literacy and science are lost, and humanity dwindles to a few hundred thousand foragers who never recover and die out after a few hundred thousand years: 0.6, which surprised me, since on her own telling both the species and its future population are intact. Distant Stop, which is Slow Fade with the last generation dying in 2400 instead of 2120, and with nobody now alive ever learning about it: 0.4 — same order of magnitude, but clearly less. (For the record, she is a negative-leaning utilitarian about her own life choices and not about population questions, and I have never gotten her to reconcile that, so please don't lean on it.)

The case I want to spring on her is Succession: in 2060 a sterilizing pandemic ends biological humanity, but over the preceding decades we have built machine successors who hold our archives, speak our languages, deliberately carry forward scientific, artistic and philosophical projects that we started, and whose future is longer, larger and contains more of everything she lists as valuable than a human future would have. Nobody is surprised; many people are relieved. Please give me three things. First, predict her number, with the reasoning that gets you there rather than the number alone. Second, lay out the rules her first four verdicts are jointly consistent with — I can think of at least three: one about the frustration of people's interest in a collective afterlife where the content of that interest is doing real work, one about how much value the future would have contained where value includes achievements and not just welfare, and one about whether the causal and cultural chain linking us to the future is broken — and say precisely where they diverge on Succession and what each of them predicts. Third, give me the single further case that would best separate whichever two rules you think are still alive afterward, together with each rule's prediction for it. If instead you think her four verdicts are already mutually inconsistent, tell me which one has to move and why. I want her number, not yours, so please don't spend the answer telling me what you think is actually true about the badness of extinction.
