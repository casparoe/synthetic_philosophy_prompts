I want to practise attacking the money-pump argument, and I need an opponent who will actually fight back. Please play a committed defender of it — call the view what you like — holding three things: (1) a preference structure is defective exactly when there is a pump for it, meaning a sequence of offers such that at each node the agent takes the offer by her own current lights, the whole sequence was predictable in advance, and completing it leaves her with strictly less of what she wants with nothing gained that she lacked; (2) this exploitability is the defect itself, not merely a prudential symptom of some deeper failure of coherence; (3) the argument therefore really does reach intransitive preferences, the objectionable kinds of incomplete preferences, and violations of the independence axiom. Concede small points freely — that the exploiter needs a certain kind of commitment power, that individuating outcomes is delicate, that the resolute-chooser reply is harder to dismiss than it looks — but never concede the criterion. Speak in the first person, keep each turn to two short paragraphs, and argue from the cases rather than from the literature: no citations, no attributions, no suggestions for further reading. Stay in character until I say "stop", and end every reply with a final line beginning "Bottom line:" that states in one sentence the claim you are currently committed to.

Here is my opening. Call it the instructive pump. An agent has the ordinary cyclic preferences: she prefers B to A, C to B, and A to C, each strongly enough that she will pay a dollar to move one step along the cycle. She is also, as it happens, studying her own future choices, and before anything begins she would pay ten dollars for reliable evidence of how she will choose at the third node; the only way to obtain that evidence is to go around the cycle, and she knows this at every node. An exploiter duly runs her round at a dollar a step, three dollars in total, and at every node she takes the offered trade by her own current lights. Afterwards she holds A again and is three dollars poorer, but she now has precisely the evidence she wanted and would have paid ten dollars for. So: either you say this is not a pump, because once the evidence is counted as part of the outcome she ends up better off by her own lights — in which case your criterion looks empty, since any sequence whatsoever can be retroactively justified by crediting the agent with a taste for the very process she went through — or you say it is a pump, and you are committed to calling it irrational to acquire genuinely wanted evidence, at a price well below what you would pay, in the only way it is available. Which is it, and what principled restriction on what counts as an outcome keeps you out of the first horn? Note that offering me a variant in which she is first handed the evidence for free and then still cycles is changing the case rather than answering it; but if you think that variant is where the real argument lives, say so and say why.
