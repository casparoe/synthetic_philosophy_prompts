Write a dialogue of roughly 1,200 to 1,400 words between two philosophers, Mira and Dev, who are in complete agreement about the complexity theory and in complete disagreement about what follows from it.

Shared setup they are both looking at: Ari runs a small emergency-response unit. Each hour he revises a plan over roughly fifty interacting variables — road closures, bridge capacities, hospital loads, weather, crew availability — most of them only partially observed, many of them probabilistically dependent on others. The problem family he is solving contains instances for which every known exact method has a worst-case running time that grows faster than any polynomial, and the familiar hardness results for probabilistic inference and for planning under partial observability put his problem in that family. Ari has one hour, one laptop, and lives are at stake.

Mira holds the feasibility constraint: a normative requirement, epistemic or practical, is admissible only if an agent in the relevant circumstances can conform to it, and complexity results are the correct instrument for saying when conformity is possible; an intractable ideal should therefore be replaced, not venerated, by a tractable surrogate norm that approximates it as closely as the budget allows. Dev holds the standard-of-correctness view: norms state what is correct rather than what is achievable, the gap between the two is precisely what lets a norm evaluate an agent from outside her own predicament, and complexity results are worst-case, asymptotic, encoding-relative and contingent on machine architecture, so they underwrite no claim about what Ari ought to believe or do.

Give each of them real resources. Mira must have an answer to the charge that feasibility collapses the normative into the descriptive, and to the charge that choosing a surrogate requires a norm that cannot itself be justified by speed. Dev must have an account of what critical work an unsatisfiable norm does that is not simply the assertion that it is correct, and must say what distinguishes his position from indifference to Ari's actual situation.

Now introduce a distinction that neither of them has seen in print, and require that each of them gets to use it and that it cuts against the person who introduced it. Say a method's difficulty is diagnosable when there is a test, computable well inside the agent's budget, that flags in advance the instances on which the method will overrun that budget, so that the agent can decide before committing whether to attempt exact conformity or fall back on something cheaper. Say it is opaque when no such test exists, so that discovering whether this instance is one she can handle costs about as much as handling it. Mira should introduce diagnosability in order to dismantle the obvious compromise that Dev reaches for, namely that Ari can aim at the ideal norm and satisfy-when-hard: if the difficulty is opaque, he cannot tell when he is in the hard region, so the compromise issues no guidance at all. Dev should then turn the distinction: if the difficulty is opaque, Mira cannot tell which instances call for the surrogate norm either, so her replacement has to be a single universal policy rather than a conditional one, and the choice between universal policies is a question about which norm is correct that complexity theory does not touch. Mira should respond by relocating diagnosis from the instance to the environment, arguing that what matters is whether the distribution of instances an agent actually faces is one on which her policy's expected performance is acceptable, and that this is an empirical and local matter rather than something settled by an asymptotic worst-case theorem. Dev must then press the status of that empirical premise and the two of them should end on a precisely stated disagreement rather than a reconciliation.

Constraints: both characters are equally competent and equally well informed; neither is exasperating, and neither gets a speech the other could not have anticipated. There must be genuine back-and-forth — each should quote or paraphrase the other's actual argument before replying to it, and each should concede at least one substantive point that costs them something. No character cites an author, a paper or a result by name; the complexity claims are stated in their own words. Neither explains complexity theory to the other. Keep Ari in view: abstract points should be cashed out in what he is supposed to do at hour eleven, and the dialogue should not float free of that case for more than a few turns.

After the dialogue, add a structured summary as a JSON object with exactly these fields, and nothing else after it:

positions — an object with keys feasibility and standard, each holding one sentence stating that character's position in the terms the dialogue ended up using rather than the terms above.
crux — one sentence identifying the single proposition on which the whole disagreement turns.
diagnosability — an object with keys definition_agreed, use_by_Mira, use_by_Dev, and residual_disagreement, where definition_agreed gives the formulation both characters accepted, the two use keys give one sentence each on how that character deployed it, and residual_disagreement says what they still disagree about concerning it.
concessions — an array of two objects, each with keys speaker and concession, recording the real concession each character made.
strongest_unanswered_objection — an object with keys raised_by, objection, and why_unanswered, where why_unanswered explains in one sentence why the reply given was incomplete.
decisive_question — one sentence posing the question whose answer would settle the disagreement, together with a clause on what would make that answer non-decisive anyway.
