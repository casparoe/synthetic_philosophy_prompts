I am defending a paper in an interdisciplinary social-science seminar next week, and I want the objections I will actually get in that room, not the ones a philosophy department would give me.

The thesis: value-orientations can figure as causes in social-scientific explanation, and the Protestant Ethic book is best read as a causal claim about a socially transmitted conduct of life rather than about doctrine, denomination, or guilt-psychology. My own version of the claim is narrower than that. I argue that values are causally efficacious only when they operate as what I call practical salience rules: socially transmitted, largely tacit, second-order rules that fix which considerations count as reasons for agents within a domain. They qualify as causes, on my account, for two reasons: they are carried by mechanisms (exemplary conduct, household discipline, congregational inspection) that can be interrupted, and they make a counterfactual difference to what agents would have done with their interests, opportunities, and information held fixed. I also argue for a ratchet structure: institutions first select the value-orientation, the value-orientation then stabilizes the institution, and the effect outlasts the selection that produced it, so the causal arrow runs both ways. I read Weber's notion of elective affinity as a stipulation about that kind of mechanism rather than as a hedge. My running case is deliberately stylized: two regions with the same factor endowments, the same legal institutions, and the same access to credit, one of which nonetheless develops an early and durable pattern of reinvesting surplus in the same enterprise together with an unusual meticulousness about accounts, and which carries that pattern with it when its merchants migrate into a different legal environment. I have already argued in the paper that the doctrinal reading (Calvinist theology produced capitalism), the human-capital reading (it was really literacy or numeracy all along), and any national-character reading are all wrong, so please do not give me objections that assume I hold any of them; and please skip the philosophers' objections about reasons not being causes or about the indeterminacy of meaning, since this audience will not raise them and would find them irrelevant if I volunteered them.

The audience: two economic historians, three applied-micro economists, one rational-choice sociologist, and a chair who is a historical materialist and who is fond of saying that culture is what you invoke when you have no identification strategy.

Please write me a Q&A prep sheet: exactly five numbered items, ordered by how much damage each would do if I handled it badly, with no introduction and no conclusion. Each item should be at most two sentences: the objection phrased the way that audience would actually phrase it (identification strategy, endogeneity, mechanism, counterfactual, ideal type, base and superstructure), together with what I should concede, what I should hold, and where my reply is weakest. At least one objection should come from the chair, at least one from the rational-choice sociologist, and at least two from the microeconomists. One of the five is the objection I am hoping nobody asks; say so in that item itself.
