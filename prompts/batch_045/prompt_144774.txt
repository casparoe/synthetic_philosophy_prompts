Construct a thought experiment for use in the debate over original versus derived intentionality. Background you should use: Searle's distinction holds that only intrinsic states of biological organisms—paradigmatically the mental states of creatures with brains—have original intentionality, while the aboutness of words, maps, diagrams, and non-biological machines is derived, conferred by the intentions of designers or interpreters. On this view, a machine that is not a brain has at most derived intentionality, and if no one is currently interpreting it, it has no intentionality at all. Dennett rejects the sharpness of this split. In his two-bitser example, he considers a very cheap, simple robot that has come, through a blind process of variation and selection—say, a genetic algorithm or a reinforcement schedule that rewarded it for picking up quarters—to have an internal state that reliably indicates the presence of quarters. No engineer intended the robot to represent quarters; the sensitivity emerged because gathering quarters was rewarded or otherwise advantageous. Dennett uses this to argue that the robot's state is genuinely about quarters without deriving that aboutness from anyone's intentions, and that the original/derived contrast is therefore not doing the work Searle assigns to it.

Your task is to design a new case in this neighborhood, not a reproduction of Dennett's quarters example. Invent a different, mundane target property and a different blind selection process, but preserve whatever structural feature you take to be essential to the two-bitser argument. The case must be built to the following specifications, and the verdicts must be generated by applying the two positions to the case, not stipulated.

Specifications:

1. Setting and length: The scenario must be realistic and set in an ordinary human institution—a recycling depot, a supermarket bottle-return area, a laundromat, a transit fare system, a warehouse, or similar. There must be no science fiction, no brain emulation, no exotic physics, and no appeal to consciousness-inducing technology. The scenario itself should be no longer than a single paragraph.

2. The system and the isolated variable: The system must be a non-biological, non-conscious artifact; it should be cheap and simple, and not a person or animal. It has an internal state S that reliably covaries with a mundane target property T in its environment and that explains the rest of its behavior. The case must isolate provenance: S's sensitivity to T must have been produced by a blind selective process—evolutionary, market-driven, or training-based—in which no designer, programmer, or user intended the artifact to track T, and in which selection operated only on some downstream success, such as earning a reward, being kept in service, or being reproduced. At the time the verdicts are assessed, no agent intends the artifact to represent T, and no one is currently using or interpreting it as a T-detector. Every other feature—the device's construction, the statistics of the environment, its behavioral dispositions, and the reliability of S—must be held fixed across the variations discussed below.

3. Conflicting verdicts: When Searle's original/derived distinction is applied to the case, it must entail that S has only derived intentionality, or no intentionality at all. When Dennett's alternative—the intentional-stance or selectional account suggested by the two-bitser—is applied, it must entail that S is genuinely about T, or that the device is a genuine intentional system whose state S carries belief-like content regarding T, and that this content is not merely derived from the intentions of any designer or current user. Dennett need not grant that this is original intentionality in Searle's sense; what matters is that his account yields a verdict of genuine aboutness where Searle's account yields only derived intentionality or none. Derive both verdicts from the stated criteria of the two positions; do not simply announce them.

4. Demonstration and isolation: After presenting the scenario, walk through what each position says about S and why, pointing to the features of the case that trigger the respective criteria. Then identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work—whether it is the blind provenance of the sensitivity, its combination with current non-use, or something else—and show what happens if that feature is removed. In particular, show that if the sensitivity had instead been intentionally designed by an engineer who wanted the device to track T, the case collapses into an uncontroversial instance of derived intentionality for both positions; and explain exactly how, if at all, the verdicts shift if a human later begins to use the artifact as a T-detector.

5. The case's purpose and the bad-side reply: The point of the case is to test whether the original/derived distinction can be grounded in biology or design without presupposing consciousness or an intentional designer. Conclude by explaining how a defender of Searle's distinction would attack the case—for example, by arguing that the selective history is itself set up by intentional programmers, that the device's aboutness is merely as-if, that without consciousness there is no original intentionality, or that Dennett has switched to a stance-relative notion of content—and assess whether that reply begs the question, given the purpose just stated. Be honest about whether the case really embarrasses Searle's distinction or only appears to.
