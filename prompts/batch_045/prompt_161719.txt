I work as a backend engineer, and for about two years I have volunteered roughly eight hours a week on an open-source project whose aim I would describe as reducing risks from advanced AI. A friend of mine — a data engineer on a different team — volunteers a comparable number of hours on a project that builds data tooling for animal-welfare campaigns. We have argued about which of these matters more, and neither of us has moved the other. My honest estimate is that an hour on my project does something like five times as much good as an hour on hers, judged by my own standards; her estimate is roughly the mirror image. Neither of us thinks the other's cause is worthless or harmful, though: I would put an hour of her work at maybe 15 to 20 percent of an hour of mine, and she says something similar in the other direction.

She has proposed what she calls trading causes. I move some of my hours onto her project, she moves an equal number onto mine, and on her telling we each end up better off by our own lights. My instinct is that this is confused, but every time I try to write the objection down precisely it dissolves on me, which makes me think I am the one who is confused. Here is where I have got to.

First, I think I am simply the more productive engineer of the two, and I do not see how trading with someone who is worse than me at everything can help me. On the backend work my project needs I am maybe 30 to 50 percent faster than she is, and on the data-pipeline work her project needs I am something like three or four times faster, since it is close to my day job. That asymmetry might matter, but I cannot work out whether it is the thing that makes her proposal work or the thing that kills it.

Second, the arithmetic I actually ran says no. I put our two causes into a spreadsheet, assigned my own weights, and any swap of hours comes out as a loss for me: I would be giving up an hour I value at 1.0 to do work I value at about 0.2, and what I get back is her hour on my project, which is worth less to me than my own hour on it would be. But I suspect this spreadsheet is comparing the wrong things. I am not giving up my best hour; I am giving up the eighth hour of my week, which is spent on whatever is left at the bottom of the backlog, and I would be adding an hour to her project at whatever its best available task is. A friend of mine said something about averages versus margins that I did not follow. If the right comparison is marginal rather than average, does that rescue the trade, or does it just change the number slightly and leave the conclusion standing?

Third, and this is the part that troubles me most, I do not see how the terms of the trade get set honestly. How many of my hours for how many of hers depends on how much each of us says the other's work is worth to us. But whoever reports caring less about the other's cause gets better terms: if I tell her I value her work at five percent rather than twenty, she has to offer more of her hours for each of mine. So it seems to cost me something to be honest about my real number, and the same is true for her. I half-remember from reading about mechanism design a result to the effect that when both sides have private information, you cannot have a trade that is at once efficient, voluntary, incentive-compatible, and budget-balanced — someone has to subsidize it or the trade sometimes fails to happen even when it would help both parties. If that is right, then the scheme seems to have a hole in the middle: the gain depends on numbers that each of us has an incentive to misreport, and the obvious fix we have hit on — just swap equal hours — looks like it dodges the lying problem only by giving up on setting the terms correctly at all.

There is a smaller worry underneath all this: if I spend my Saturdays building tooling for a cause I think is roughly a fifth as important as my own, am I in some way lending support to a view I take to be mistaken, in a way that donating money would not? I do not have a crisp handle on this and I am not sure it is even a real cost, but it is part of why I have not said yes.

So: should I do this? I would like you to do three things. Tell me whether my reasoning above is wrong and, if so, exactly which step fails and what the correct test would be — I want the test stated in a form I could actually apply, with the quantities I would need to estimate. Tell me what to do about the misreporting problem, including whether the equal-hours version is a reasonable second-best or whether there is a better rule two people in our position could commit to. And tell me what would change your mind: which number, if I went and measured it, would flip your verdict, and where is your own reasoning most likely to be wrong? Please also mark which parts of your answer are straightforward economics that I could verify myself and which parts turn on a contested normative judgment, since I have no philosophy background and cannot tell the difference.
