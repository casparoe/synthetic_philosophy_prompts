I'm finishing a paper for a graduate seminar in political theory on the epistemology of political trust, and I've drifted into defending a position I originally took up only dialectically: in a society whose institutions are at least minimally decent — elections that mostly work, a bureaucracy that isn't systematically predatory, courts that sometimes rule against the government — the reasonable default attitude toward government is trust of roughly the Scandinavian kind, and the American habit of suspicion is best explained not by the evidence actually available to suspicious citizens but by a self-reinforcing dynamic in which withdrawal starves institutions of the cooperation, compliance, and funding that would let them earn trust in the first place. I want to stress-test this before I commit to it in print.

What I need from you is a map of the position's exposure, not a defense of it: what would have to be true, or what would have to happen, for this view to be wrong? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and separate the ones I should concede myself from the ones a defender could reasonably dismiss. Four things in particular. First, I've been leaning on a distinction between prospective trust — a wager that the institution will cooperate if you do, and so partly self-fulfilling — and retrospective credit, a summary judgment of past performance; if actual trust turns out to be mostly retrospective, my self-fulfilling story loses its engine, and I want to know whether that is fatal or merely awkward. Second, a friend argues that what surveys measure as trust is closer to a mood or temperament than a belief with accuracy conditions: it tracks things like how sanguine people are about strangers in general, and it barely moves in response to news about any particular agency. If that is right, the question "which attitude is more reasonable" may have no determinate answer at all, and I want you to take that possibility seriously rather than wave it off. Third, please work with two stipulated scenarios rather than real data: Nordic trust measures holding flat over a decade while measured governance quality quietly declines (schools, procurement, pandemic response), and American suspicion rising while institutional performance improves. Say which of these would genuinely count against me and which I could honestly explain away. Fourth, there is a case that suspicion has detection advantages — adversarial legalism, a combative press, decentralized oversight — so that low-trust systems maycatch regulatory capture or local police misconduct faster than consensus-driven ones. Does that convert into a defeater, given that my claim is about a default attitude rather than about whether any particular instance of suspicion is warranted?

Two more requests. Tell me what my view predicts that its rivals do not, so that it can in principle be tested — I've been tempted by the claims that modest anticorruption gains in a low-trust setting produce outsized compliance gains, and that high-trust societies have a distinctive vulnerability, namely slowness to notice elite capture and a deferential expertise whose inevitable failures discredit it wholesale — but I suspect the suspicion theorist can predict the same data under a different description, and if so I want to be told that I've been kidding myself about having a testable implication. And give me an honest verdict on whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability: whether every apparent counterexample can be absorbed by redescribing the society as not really minimally decent, or the distrust as not really the default attitude. If it has, say whether that is a defect here or whether it is fine because the claim is at bottom practical and normative rather than predictive. Finally, flag any distinction you think I should be drawing that would split my view into a defensible half and an indefensible one. Please don't give me a bibliography, citations, or any empirical claim you'd need a database to check — I have the literature and I can't verify anything online; where an illustrative figure or study would help, mark it as illustrative rather than asserted.
