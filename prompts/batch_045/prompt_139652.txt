I host a little podcast on philosophy of action, and next week's guest is an invented philosopher I'll call Dr. Ines Vartanian, whose new book argues that the free will debate has been fighting over the wrong variable. Her claim: the interesting property isn't indeterminism but sphexishness — behaviour that looks purposive and gets the job done in the normal case, but collapses into a fixed loop once something is moved. Her positive proposal, as I've understood it, is that an agent's freedom is a matter of how rich its "perturbation profile" is: how much of what it does actually seperates and re-tracks when the world is relevantly different, across a wide and non-arbitrary range of differences. On that picture indeterminism is neither necessary nor sufficient, and the old standoff between compatibilists and incompatibilists is mostly a distraction.

She has two moves I can already predict, and I want questions that make them progressively harder to lean on. First, when a rival view comes up she says some version of "that's just reasons-responsiveness with new vocabulary" — so I want at least one question that makes her say what the *testable* difference is between her perturbation-profile account and a Fischer/Ravizza-style guidance-control picture, and what observation would separate them. Second, whenever a case gets uncomfortable she says "it depends what grain you individuate the behaviour at" — I want that escape route closed down over the course of the episode rather than granted at the start.

Give me eight to ten questions, and for each one: the one-line answer she's most likely to give, and a follow-up aimed at that specific answer (not at a strawman). Things I definitely want covered:

- Is sphexishness a property of the behaviour or of the mechanism behind it? Because a system can have enormously flexible internals and still emit one rigid output, and vice versa.
- The grain problem in its sharpest form: the wasp's loop isn't a blind shove, it contains an inspection step — go in, check the burrow, come back out. At a finer grain that looks like responsiveness to how things are. If flexibility is always relative to a grain, what stops the view from collapsing into "rigid at whatever grain I happen to dislike"?
- Perturbation-set relativity: any behaviour is responsive to some perturbations and rigid under others, so who picks the relevant set, and does picking it by the creature's ecology just smuggle in the conclusion?
- Is rigidity always the defect? Commitment devices, Ulysses-style self-binding, checks that are valuable precisely because they won't be revisited. If some rigidity is rational or adaptive, sphexishness can't just mean rigidity.
- The anecdote itself is shakier than the retellings suggest: it comes from old natural-history observation, and people have questioned whether the wasp really loops indefinitely rather than a handful of times, and it only shows up because somebody keeps moving the prey. So: does her account need the story to be true, or is the concept detachable? And isn't the experimenter's repeated intervention arguably a case of *manipulation* rather than one of *rigidity* — which would relocate the whole example inside a different argument entirely?
- The AI case, which I know she'll want to talk about: is a language model producing a chain of thought sphexish or not? I have a worry I want her to answer rather than dodge — call it asymmetric sphexishness: such a system can look strikingly flexible inside the perturbation space it was trained on and go rigid or go off the rails outside it, and if our only tests are drawn from that same space, then "look how flexibly it responded" may just be measuring coverage. Ask her what perturbation would actually settle it and whether we can build one without already assuming what flexibility is.
- Meta-level rigidity: a system can be flexible about first-order moves but rigid about how it re-plans, and then flexible about that, and so on. Does adding levels ever bottom out, or does the question just move up a floor each time?
- And end with one question about what could count as evidence *against* the view.

I dont want any "what first got you interested in free will" filler, and please don't pad the answer keys with her restating the view. Keep the wording something a listener who's never read philosophy could follow, without thinning out the substance.
