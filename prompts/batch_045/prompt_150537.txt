I spent my career as a marine biologist and have only lately begun reading philosophy of biology, so I may well be reinventing something that already has a standard name. Reading Gould and Lewontin's spandrels paper set me to sketching what I privately call the constraint-first protocol: before asking what a trait is for, ask what the organism's developmental machinery, its materials, and its geometry would have made it difficult for the trait not to be there — a by-product of something else already present, a consequence of how the thing has to be built, an inheritance carried along from an ancestor rather than a response to present conditions — and only where a trait is genuinely optional in that sense does an adaptive story get a turn, and then as a hypothesis to be tested rather than a default to be filled in.

What, in your view, are the most important implications of adopting such a protocol for how one should read and evaluate adaptationist explanations in general? I am at least as interested in what it would cost: which well-supported adaptive explanations it would wrongly disqualify or needlessly burden, and where the contrast it depends on — between a trait the organism's architecture forces and one that was open to selection — turns out not to be well defined, or not to be drawn where I have drawn it.
