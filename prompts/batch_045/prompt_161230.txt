I'm putting together a handout for a third-year tutorial on blame and Ive been going round in circles trying to build one specific case, so I want you to build it for me. The topic is standing to blame and hypocrisy, and the case has to flip the usual ordering: the hypocrite — someone who is *currently*, unrepentantly doing the very thing they are blaming someone else for — has standing to blame, while a completely clean-handed person, who has never done anything remotely like it, lacks standing. And I don't mean lacks it in some weak advisory sense; I mean the strong thing, where the fitting response to their blame is: you are not in a position to say that to me. What the case is for is to make students see that standing might be a relation between blamer and blamed rather than a report card on the blamer, so anything that just stipulates that conclusion is cheating.

Here is the part I can't get to work. I want one scenario, one paragraph, realistic — a workplace, a hospital ward, a restaurant kitchen, a university department, that sort of thing, no trolleys and no philosophers' dolls. Both blamers blame the same person for the same act, at the same moment, in more or less the same words, so the only things that vary are (i) dirty hands versus clean hands and (ii) inside the practice versus outside it. The hypocrite may not get standing from any of the usual escape hatches: no reform, no repentance, no apology on record, no punishment already served, no special role or authority over the person blamed, and the ongoing violation is recent and known. The clean-handed person may not lose standing for any of the usual reasons either: not complicit, not profiting from the violation, not misinformed, not motivated by spite, and not a stranger who wandered in off the street — they should know exactly what happened and be some kind of colleague, just not a participant in the practice whose norm was breached. Then the bit I actually care about: I want the case engineered so that three accounts come apart pairwise. Call them what you like, I don't want citations. (1) The forfeiture or tu quoque account: hypocrisy costs you standing, so it should say hypocrite no, clean-hands yes. (2) The equal-standing account: moral address among persons doesn't come in grades and dirty hands don't demote you, so it should say yes to both. (3) A relational account on which what matters isn't the state of your hands but whether you are inside the practice and exposed to being blamed back in the same coin: hypocrite yes, clean-hands no. My hunch is that (3) only wins if the violation is a breach of a norm internal to some shared practice rather than a plain moral wrong, because otherwise (2) grants the outsider standing for free and (3) collapses into (2) — build it that way if you agree, and tell me if you think the hunch is wrong.

After the scenario, give me the verdict each of the three accounts gives and the reasoning that gets it there from that account's own commitments — I want to see the verdicts fall out, not be announced. Then: which single feature of the design is doing the work. Then a collapse test: what happens to the three-way spread if you clean the hypocrite's hands, if you swap the internal norm for a generic moral one, or if you make the outsider a full co-member. And finally the honest part: how would a defender of whichever account comes out worst attack the case — is someone going to say I built the verdict into the description of the practice, and is that a fair objection or just sour grapes?
