I sit on the Collections and Access Committee of the Marchwoodshire Record Office — I am one of the two volunteer trustees on it, and by day I teach history at a sixth-form college, which is how I got co-opted — and we have to decide at our meeting a week on Thursday what to do with a run of settlement examination books and attached removal orders from the old poor law union. It is roughly 4,900 folios, 1836 to 1848, conserved and digitised out of a Heritage Fund grant whose public-access conditions we are now having to interpret. The images are finished; the only question left is the form in which they go out. The books record paupers' own examinations, taken down by the vestry clerk in the first person — a composite example, not a quotation, would run along the lines of *Mary Bright, examined 12 April 1841, saith that she was hired for one whole year to John Halloway of Ash Priors at wages of four pounds, that she left the said service three weeks before the year's end, and is now chargeable to the parish of Ash Priors* — and nowhere in the entire run is there any entry recording why anyone left a place, whether they were turned out, or what became of the children named in them. Our archivist has been trying to get us to separate two things she calls access-silence, which is the record you cannot find or cannot read because it sits in a strongroom in a clerk's hand, and category-silence, which is what remains when the record is fully available but was built to answer an official question, so that there is simply no slot in it for the things a historian or a descendant actually wants to know. She also uses a phrase I have found useful, attributive ventriloquy: the examination attributes the speech to the pauper, but the order of the questions, the vocabulary, and the suppression of everything legally irrelevant are the clerk's, so that a keyword search over the full text returns the clerk's categories under the pauper's name, and the more searchable the material becomes the more the official's idiom is what circulates. Four positions have crystallised in committee: full open publication, name- and place-indexed and searchable, which is what the county family history society is petitioning for and what our funder probably means by access; a deliberately unindexed, browse-by-parish-and-date publication of the images, which is the archivist's counter-proposal; a "counter-register" edition in which we publish it indexed but volunteers maintain a crowdsourced biographical supplement for each pauper, drawn from baptisms, rate books and newspapers, so that no one appears only as a legal status; and holding the material back until we have done more thinking.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate in advance of the meeting, in British English and in proper memo form — To, From, Date, Re, then the recommendation in the first two sentences, then the reasons, then conditions and a timetable, then the trigger at which we would revisit it — 900 words at the outside, because the trustees will not read more. It should come down in favour of one option, or of a staged combination with a dated first step, and it should say plainly what we are going to tell the family history society and what we are going to write in the report to the funder. I do not want footnotes or appeals to named authorities; reason from this run of records. Two things have to be handled rather than waved aside. The first is the strongest argument on the losing side, which is the asymmetry argument: the gentry's estate papers and the vestry minute books in the same building are fully indexed and searchable, so if we make the poor's records deliberately hard to search we are reproducing, with public money, precisely the imbalance that reading history from below was supposed to undo — and, worse, we may be doing it uselessly, since a commercial genealogy site will probably index our images within a year whatever we decide, leaving us with the amplification and none of the apparatus. The second is a practical constraint: the originals are now too unstable to be produced at all, so the digital surrogate is the only access route there will ever be, and one of our trustees relies on a screen reader, which means any option that amounts to pictures without readable text needs an answer.
