I am writing a novel in two strands, and the strands are meant to rhyme. The earlier one is set in Amsterdam in the 1640s and follows a young physician who has taken the new philosophy of the beasts entirely to heart; the later one follows a present-day researcher who inherits his notebooks and applies the same tests to creatures he could not have imagined. To make them rhyme I need to know what rule my physician is actually running, because I have him passing judgment on a series of cases in the first half of the book and I cannot have him rule one way in chapter four and another in chapter eleven.

His verdicts so far, as I have drafted them:

1. A spaniel that yelps when struck: no pain, no thought. He grants it something he insists on calling sensation, a registering of impact in the organs, but denies that anything is felt; the yelp is the noise of a strained machine.
2. A magpie that greets visitors at the right moment and can be prompted into a phrase: pure training of the throat. It never uses a sign to declare anything to anyone.
3. A dog that fetches objects by name and can be shaped by reward alone into long chains of behaviour: still a machine.
4. A newborn infant: has a mind, a rational soul, though it exercises no reason yet.
5. A man born deaf, never taught any conventional sign language, who has invented a private system of gestures with his family: has a mind. Those gestures are true signs, invented for use.
6. A feverish patient who speaks connectedly and afterwards remembers nothing: words issued from the body alone, without the consent of the soul.
7. A clockwork figure in a garden that starts aside when a visitor treads on a hidden plate: no mind, although it answers a sign aptly enough.

His stated test, which I give him in dialogue, has two parts: a creature has a mind only if it can reply aptly to anything said to it by arranging signs in new orders, and no machine with a finite arrangement of organs could manage that in every circumstance of life. I can think of at least three rules that generate all seven verdicts equally well: a soul rule keyed to species, a sign-flexibility rule keyed to use, and a versatility rule keyed to what a finite organ arrangement could explain. I need to know which one he is really running, because they come apart on the cases still to come.

The new cases:

A. A dog that has learned several hundred names for objects and, on being asked for a name it has never heard, selects the unfamiliar object from among familiar ones.
B. A man who has lost all language to a stroke: no speech, no comprehension, but he avoids hot surfaces, seeks his daughter's company, and can be taught to indicate pictures in order to ask for things.
C. An ape raised among humans, trained on a board of arbitrary symbols, which combines symbols in orders it was never taught and uses them to request things that are absent, but is never observed using them to tell anyone anything.
D. A machine of the present with no body and no sense organs, which produces fluent and apt replies to almost anything said to it, including questions never asked before, and can be made to correct its own errors when they are pointed out to it in words.

What would my physician say about each, and why? Which of the rules fits his earlier verdicts best, and which of these cases actually tells the rules apart rather than merely appearing to? Which single further case, described concretely, would settle it? And for each case where the rules diverge, what does he have to give up if he rules the way the favoured rule dictates: the soul rule, the test of signs, or his confidence that a machine could never pass it? I would rather have a consistent character than a defensible philosophy, but I would like to know which of the two I am being asked to choose.
