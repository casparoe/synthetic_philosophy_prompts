Next Tuesday I'm giving a twenty-minute talk, and I need a prep sheet for the bit afterwards.

Bit about me: no academic background — left school at sixteen, work in warehouse logistics, been reading about this on my own for maybe two years. The talk is at a monthly meetup, thirty-odd people: mostly working software devs who listen to AI podcasts and are vaguely worried; three or four philosophy and STS postgrads who like to be seen landing a clever objection; and two historians of technology the organiser especially invited — one works on early modern print culture, one on twentieth-century military research and development. Friendly room, but the postgrads will want blood.

What I'm arguing: people wheel out the three big analogies — nuclear weapons, the printing press, the Industrial Revolution — to answer "how big, how fast, how dangerous", and that's a job analogies can't do. The only job they can do is tell you which bottleneck binds. So I've got a two-part test: (1) say out loud which constraint you think is binding and why it's the one that binds, (2) then look at what actually happened in the analogous case once that constraint was lifted, and ask who the gatekeepers of the second-order effects were.

On that test I rank printing press first, Industrial Revolution second, nukes last. My reason for putting nukes last isn't the usual one about it being alarmist — it's that nukes were gated by concentrated, expensive, state-sized dual-use know-how, and that's precisely the feature AI hasn't got, so every conclusion people draw from the analogy (export controls, a big-project monopoly, secrecy regimes) inherits the one feature that doesn't match. With printing I want to argue the opposite of how it normally gets used: releasing cheap copying didn't change much for ages, and the visible effects had to wait on literacy, distribution, licensing and copyright, so it should be pushing people's timelines out, not in. What I don't know what to do with is "AI travels at software speed, so the waiting around compresses", which someone will definately say.

Give me the objections I'll actually get, ordered by how much damage they'd do, with who asks each one and how that person would phrase it, a reply I could give in about ninety seconds, and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what to concede in the first two minutes rather than be forced into it later, which objections have real force and which are just point-scoring, and include the one I'm hoping nobody asks.

Don't send me to books: I've no library access and you can't look anything up either, so no reading lists, citations or author names, and if you lean on a historical fact, flag it so I don't repeat it as settled.

British English, please. Lead with a single sentence — your bottom line, the one thing you'd change about the talk before Tuesday — then the sheet. No preamble, and don't repeat my question back at me.
