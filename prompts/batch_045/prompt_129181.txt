I've been reading Szasz and I keep running into the same structure, for which I don't have a word.

In addiction treatment, the patient's insistence that he could stop whenever he liked was entered in his notes as denial, and the vigour of the insistence was taken as a measure of how advanced the illness was; the day he said he was powerless, an equally unremarkable utterance from the same mouth became insight, and evidence that treatment was working. On the ward, the patient who protests that there is nothing wrong with her mind is documented as lacking insight, and the detention is extended. What interests me is that it is not the diagnosis as a whole that is insulated from refutation — history, scans, observed behaviour can in principle tell against it — but one particular evidence channel, first-person report, which appears to run in one direction only: whatever the patient says about her own mind is absorbed as confirmation, and there is nothing she could say that would count against the framework.

I don't think this is merely a professional vice, because there are cases where discounting the report is plainly right: the man with a right-hemisphere stroke who denies that his paralysed arm is his; someone mid-mania who has never felt better in his life; and, if I am remembering the experiments correctly, depressed subjects whose estimates of their own control over an outcome were more accurate than the non-depressed controls', and whose pessimism was then recorded as a symptom rather than as a report. The rough test I have fallen back on is to ask, in advance, what a patient would have to say for it to count as evidence against the diagnosis; if the answer is 'nothing, and saying so would itself be a symptom', I treat the asymmetry as suspect. But the test embarrasses me, since it also condemns cases where I think discounting is correct, and since many respectable concepts do not come with their refutation conditions specified in advance.

So: is there an established concept for this one-directional evidence channel, and for the difference between its legitimate and its illegitimate versions? 'Unfalsifiable' and 'epistemic injustice' are the two names I keep being offered, and neither seems to fit — the first because the diagnosis is falsifiable by other means, the second because it seems to prejudge the very question, namely whether the discounting is warranted here. If no established concept fits, coin one: define it, say clearly what it excludes, and give me the boundary cases you would use to test the definition. Szasz's own answer, as I understand it, is that there is no illness there at all and so the asymmetry never has a legitimate version; I have been treating that as too cheap, and I want to know whether it is doing more argumentative work than I have credited.

Finally, tell me where your answer is most likely to be wrong: which consideration would most persuade you that I have conflated two or three separate phenomena under one description, or that the whole thing collapses into something already named.
