I'm in eleventh grade, and a few of us run a philosophy club that meets on Friday afternoons. For about four months we have been completely stuck on one case, and I'd like a diagnosis rather than a verdict.

The case is Putnam's Twin Earth setup: somewhere there is a planet just like ours, where the stuff in the lakes and taps looks, smells, tastes and behaves exactly like water but has a different chemical composition — call it XYZ — and where a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of me, call him Oscar, points at a glass of it and says "water". One group in my club (I later learned this position has a name, something like semantic externalism) says that because Oscar has never encountered H2O, his word picks out XYZ rather than water, and so what a word picks out depends partly on the environment and not only on what is inside the speaker's head. Another group says that is absurd: Oscar and I are in identical internal states, we learn and apply the word in the same way, it plays the same role in our reasoning and our plans, and so it can only mean the same thing — otherwise we would have to say either that Oscar does not know what he is talking about, or that he changes his mind the moment he is told about the chemistry, and neither of those seems right. Nobody has moved an inch in four months, and every argument now feels like the same argument with different words.

So: what kind of disagreement is this really? My hypothesis is that it is largely verbal, or at least that the two groups are answering different questions under the single word "meaning": one group seems to care about what in the world the word latches onto and what would make a sentence about water true or false, while the other seems to care about what the speaker has in mind, what they can tell apart, and what makes their behavior intelligible to the rest of us. But I can also see a substantive question hiding underneath — about what a correct theory of language is even supposed to explain (communication? successful action? truth conditions? how children pick words up?), and maybe an empirical one about what ordinary speakers would actually say if you pressed them. I want you to tell me, using what each side would actually say when pressed, which of these it is and roughly how much of the standoff is each.

Two more things I'd like you to handle, because my club invented them and they seem to split people differently than the original case does. First, the Drift case: Oscar is not from Twin Earth at all, he is just me — except that, unknown to anyone, the liquid in every lake and tap on Earth was quietly replaced with XYZ a hundred years ago, and the word has been passed down from speaker to speaker the whole time, so nobody has ever tasted the difference. Second, the Expert case: Oscar is a chemist who has handled both liquids and can tell them apart in a lab, but he is at a party, is handed a glass of XYZ by a host who grew up on Twin Earth, and says "thanks, I'd love some water" — he is not deceived, he is just speaking loosely in company. If your diagnosis is that the fight is verbal, or that the two sides are asking different questions under one name, then it ought to predict why these two cases scramble people's answers in the way they seemed to in our discussion; if it does not predict that, I'd rather you said so than paper over it. And finally: given whatever the disagreement actually turns out to be, what would count as progress here — a distinction, an explicit decision about what a theory of meaning is for, some finding about what speakers would say — or is this a question where no progress should be expected, and why?
