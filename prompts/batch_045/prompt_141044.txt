I recently read a long essay — one of those magazine pieces that announce that philosophy has finally got over an old argument — whose subject was the deflationary treatment of existence questions: the cluster of views that travel under the labels quantifier variance, the neo-Carnapian treatment of existence claims as internal to a linguistic framework, and the "easy" ontology program that settles whether there are tables by appeal to the rules governing our ordinary sortals. I have tried to reconstruct its central passage from memory, compressed but without, I think, altering any of its claims:

"Here, in outline, is the deflationist's manoeuvre. Take the old quarrel between the ontologist who denies that there are tables — there are, she says, only particles arranged tablewise — and the one who insists that of course there are tables. Step one: observe that the two are not using 'there are' in the same sense; the denier means something like 'figures in the world's fundamental inventory,' the assertor means something looser and more everyday. Step two: note that each side's sentence is true in its own sense, so both of them are right, and neither has said anything the other needs to deny. Step three: conclude that the question whether there are really tables has no answer — there is no fact of the matter, any more than there is a fact of the matter about which of two equally serviceable languages to speak. What looked like a disagreement about the furniture of the universe is a disagreement about words, and the only question that survives is the practical one of which vocabulary it is more convenient for us to adopt. That is why the deflationists call these disputes 'merely verbal,' and it is also why their critics are unmoved: if every ontological dispute dissolves in this way, then for every existence question the answer is yes in some language and no in another, which is only a polite way of saying that everything exists. Nor does the dissolution even work. Call a dispute a revenge dispute when, after the contested expression has been eliminated and both parties accept the neutral paraphrase, they can still disagree about whether that paraphrase captured everything they originally meant. Any paraphrase can be challenged in this way, and the challenge is not a disagreement about any expression at all, so no diagnosis of verbality is ever stable. The honest conclusion is that we should stop trying to dissolve ontological disputes and go back to asking which side is simply right about what there is."

My difficulty is that the piece is confident and I am not in a position to check it. I am inclined to accept its conclusion — it does seem to me that if a disagreement is really only about how to use a word, then there is nothing left to argue about, and someone should simply say so — but I also have a nagging memory that people in this area distinguish being verbal from being trivial, and that they deny that their view leaves no fact of the matter. So what I want is an audit rather than a verdict.

First, go through the passage claim by claim. What would a deflationist accept as stated, where would they wince, and what is straightforwardly wrong? Please separate genuine misrepresentation from the compression a competent populariser is entitled to, and say which is which.

Second, some of those moves look to me as though they belong to one member of the deflationary family rather than another — the difference in quantifier meaning, the practical question about which framework to adopt, the appeal to the rules governing a sortal. Do those views actually come apart anywhere in the passage, and would a defender of one of them disown claims that a defender of another might tolerate?

Third, is the "everything exists" objection a refutation or a live, contested objection with standard replies? What exactly would it need in order to go through — what extra premise about the range of admissible quantifier meanings or frameworks — and is that premise one a deflationist grants?

Fourth, take the revenge-dispute device on its own terms. Is the phenomenon it points to real? Does the argument from it to the instability of all verbality diagnoses work, and does the essay's final recommendation follow even if the argument does? If the deflationist has a reply here, I would like it stated in the strongest form you can give it, not merely gestured at.

Finally, if the passage is unfair, give me the fair version of its central claim in three or four sentences — what I should have been told instead — and say what, on the corrected version, is left for the two parties to disagree about, what kind of disagreement it is, and what work the deflationist thinks remains in ontology once the easy questions are answered.

I am not asking who holds these views or where they were defended; I want to know which of the passage's claims are true.
