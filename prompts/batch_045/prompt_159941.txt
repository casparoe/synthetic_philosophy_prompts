I'm a retired solid-state physicist — spent most of my career on incommensurately modulated crystals and superspace groups, believe it or not — and since retiring I've been auditng a graduate-level philosophy of science course down at the university, mostly to keep from going stir-crazy. The Kuhn section has me both nodding along and wanting to throw things. Last night after class I got into an argument with a couple of the grad students and the TA about whether Kuhnian incommensurability is a real epistemic barrier or just a fancy way of saying scientists change there minds. I tried to explain it with an analogy from my old field and ended up writing down the following argument, which now seems to prove way more than I actually believe. I'd rather keep the claim that there is genuine, local translation failure between successive paradigms: the reading said Kuhn's mature, taxonomic version is that translation fails for local clusters of inter-defined terms, because the rival paradigms sort the objects into mutually exclusive taxonomies of kinds, and he repeatedly emphasized that none of this means incomparability or makes science irrational. I also really don't want to give up my superspace story, because I'm pretty sure I haven't forgotten how my own former field works — so please don't tell me step 2 is just wrong. Where does it go wrong, and what does rejecting the guilty step cost me? Is there an equivocation on 'no common measure', or a slide from 'these particular incommensurate structures can be embedded' to 'any incommensurable theories can be embedded'? I can't quiet put my finger on it, and my philosophy friends just keep saying 'it's an analogy, not an argument' without naming the step.

Here is the thing I wrote out:

(1) 'Incommensurable' originally just means having no common measure: two magnitudes are incommensurable if no single unit measures both exactly, like the side and diagonal of a square, which we now handle with irrational numbers. Kuhn and Feyerabend took over this mathematical notion: two paradigms are incommensurable when there is no common measure for assessing them, no neutral language into which all their claims can be translated, so you can't just line up the consequences and see which theory wins.

(2) In my old lab we dealt with incommensurate modulated crystals all the time. The basic lattice has one repeat distance, the modulation wave has another, and the ratio is irrational: in ordinary 3D space there is literally no single unit cell, no common period, that tiles both the average structure and the modulation. But crystallographers aren't stuck: we go to a (3+1)-dimensional superspace, use a superspace group, and describe the whole thing as a perfectly periodic structure in that higher-dimensional space. The lack of a common period in 3D does not stop us from giving one exact description of the composite structure in superspace.

(3) So the pattern is: when two things lack a common measure in their home space, you can often recover a common framework by moving to a higher-dimensional or more abstract space that contains both — real numbers for incommensurable magnitudes, superspace for incommensurate crystals.

(4) By the same token, if two scientific theories are incommensurable in Kuhn's sense, there should be some higher-order framework — maybe the later theory plus enough history of science, or some neutral meta-language — that embeds both theories as special cases and lets us translate between them and compare them by a common measure.

(5) If such a framework is always available in principle, then the 'translation failure' Kuhn talks about is not a deep barrier; it's either a temporary engineering problem (we haven't found the right extra dimensions yet) or else it collapses into plain incomparability in the home vocabulary — and Kuhn himself denied that, since he said incommensurability neither means nor implies incomparability.

(6) Therefore Kuhnian incommensurability is either no threat at all to rational theory comparison by a common measure, or it is an empty notion, because any pair of theories can in principle be rendered commensurable in some superspace-like framework.

But I can't accept (6). I actually think the local translation failure is real: when the taxonomy of kinds gets re-cut (oxygen versus dephlogisticated air, or relativistic versus Newtonian mass), there are local clusters of inter-defined terms that just won't translate, even though we can still compare the theories in lots of other ways and the choice isn't irrational. At the same time, I'm confident step (2) is a correct description of what I used to do for a living. So: which step do I reject? Is (4) the place where the analogy breaks, because the superspace trick needs a shared underlying basic lattice — a common taxonomy of atomic positions — that a full-blown paradigm shift destroys, so I've illicitly generalized from 'this special kind of incommensurability, where a basic lattice is shared, admits a higher-dimensional embedding' to 'any incommensurability whatsoever admits one'? Or is the rot already in (1), because Kuhn's mature taxonomic incommensurability isn't really the Euclidean 'no common measure' metaphor at all — it is cross-classification of kinds, and the metaphor misleads me into thinking a single higher-dimensional space can always supply the missing common measure? Name the step, explain why it looked fine when I wrote it down, and tell me what I have to pay to give it up — especially if the cost is that I have to admit the analogy from my own field is even weaker than I thought, or that I have to redefine incommensurability in a way that makes it local but not a threat to a neutral comparison language.
