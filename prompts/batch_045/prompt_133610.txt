This week's chunk is the passage where money first appears, and I am the one leading on Thursday. So please work from the English as quoted below plus the logic of the argument. I have no library and no access to commentaries or editions, and nothing useful will come of being told that so-and-so reads the passage thus; argue the point yourself and say which words in the text support you.

Here is the passage, in five pieces as my translation gives it (elisions marked with ellipses; the translator's algebraic example left as it stands):

(1) Hence all commodities exchanged must be able to be compared in some way. It is to meet this requirement that men have introduced money; money constitutes in a manner a middle term, for it is a measure of all things, and so of their superior or inferior value, that is to say, how many shoes are equivalent to a house or to a given quantity of food.

(2) It is therefore necessary that all commodities shall be measured by some one standard, as was said before. And this standard is in reality demand, which is what holds everything together, since if men cease to have wants or if their wants alter, exchange will go on no longer, or will be on different lines. But demand has come to be conventionally represented by money; this is why money is called nomisma (customary currency), because it does not exist by nature but by custom (nomos), and can be altered and rendered useless at will.

(3) That it is demand which, by serving as a single standard, holds such an association together, is shown by the fact that, when there is no demand for mutual service on the part of both or at least of one of the parties, no exchange takes place between them [as when someone needs something that one has oneself, for instance the state offering a license to export corn in exchange for wine]. This inequality of demand has therefore to be equalized.

(4) Now money serves us as a guarantee of exchange in the future: supposing we need nothing at the moment, it ensures that exchange shall be possible when a need arises, for it meets the requirement of something we can produce in payment so as to obtain the thing we need. Money, it is true, is liable to the same fluctuation of demand as other commodities, for its purchasing power varies at different times; but it tends to be comparatively constant. Hence the proper thing is for all commodities to have their prices fixed; this will ensure that exchange, and consequently association, shall always be possible. Money then serves as a measure which makes things commensurable and so reduces them to equality. If there were no exchange there would be no association, and there can be no exchange without equality, and no equality without commensurability. Though therefore it is impossible for things so different to become commensurable in the strict sense, our demand furnishes a sufficiently accurate common measure for practical purposes.

(5) There must therefore be some one standard, and this accepted by agreement (which is why it is called nomisma, customary currency); for such a standard makes all things commensurable, since all things can be measured by money. Let A be a house, B ten minae and C a bedstead. Then A=B/2 (supposing the house to be worth, or equal to, five minae), and C (the bedstead) =B/10; it is now clear how many bedsteads are equal to one house, namely five.

Four things I cannot settle.

First: does money do the commensurating, or does chreia? The passage seems to say both — money "makes things commensurable," yet in the strict sense it cannot, and demand is the real standard that money merely represents by agreement. Does money then measure anything at all, or does it only record a comparison that demand has already fixed? And which of the passage's sentences actually decides this?

Second, a case I invented to test it. Suppose a city fixes all prices in a unit I will call the koros, defined by statute as a fixed basket of grain, oil and wine in fixed proportions; the koros is never itself struck, carried or handed over, and debts are discharged either in kind or in unminted silver at the koros valuation. On the face of it, the commensurating work still gets done — a house is so many koroi, a bedstead a tenth of that — and the arithmetical example in (5) seems to go through unchanged. But the guarantee in (4) requires "something we can produce in payment" when a future need arises, and a pure unit of account is not a thing one can produce. So: does the argument in this passage require the same item to perform both jobs, or are they independent requirements that merely happen to be met by one thing in practice? Which clause would fail under my arrangement, and would the failure matter to Aristotle's conclusion about association?

Third, one member of the group insists that chreia here is not appetite or subjective wanting but what the city needs in order to be self-sufficient, so that money measures a good's contribution to that end. Test it against a trade in pure luxuries: a cargo of imported incense exchanged for a cargo of ornate drinking cups, where both parties want what the other has and nothing about it bears on self-sufficiency. On her reading, is that not an exchange at all, or an exchange that the account cannot evaluate? Note that the passage's own example in (3) is the state trading a corn-export licence for wine, and that the demands there look one-sided. Does anything in the text settle whether demand must be mutual, and whether it must be need in the public sense?

Fourth, the tension I keep coming back to. Money is said to exist not by nature but by custom and to be al-terable and renderable useless at will — and yet its purchasing power varies like any other commodity's and it "tends to be comparatively constant," and the proper thing is said to be that all commodities have their prices fixed. I cannot tell whether these are two different claims about different things, or whether the passage is pulling against itself, or whether "fixed prices" is doing more work than I am giving it.
