I have a single ninety-minute session on epiphenomenalism with fourteen upper-level undergraduates in a cognitive-science seminar — roughly half philosophy majors, half psychology majors, no assigned reading on the topic. They have just finished a unit on blindsight and binocular rivalry, so they already accept that a subject's reports can dissociate from what the visual system is doing, and I suspect that background will let them slide too easily past the view I am trying to make costly. I am not asking you to explain epiphenomenalism or the paradox of phenomenal judgment to me; I am asking for a plan for explaining them, and specifically for a plan that lands the paradox as a cost the theory has to pay rather than as a curiosity.

Three constraints I have imposed on myself, which I would like you either to honour or to talk me out of. First, I want to run the whole session without the words "qualia" and "zombie" until the final ten minutes, because in past years those labels have arrived with stock responses attached; tell me whether the idea can be carried without them, and where exactly the labels should be handed over. Second, I want the students to see that the paradox does not depend on substance dualism: it survives on the assumption that phenomenal properties are perfectly respectable natural properties that are nonetheless not identical to, and not among, the properties doing the causal work in the production of a judgment — and that a student who takes the exit marked "they are realized by those properties and so inherit their causal powers" has thereby stopped being an epiphenomenalist. Where in the sequence does that fork belong? Third, I intend to hold back the Moorean reply — that my certainty that I am in pain is firmer than any premise in the argument — until the paradox has actually bitten.

The carrying example I have drafted: a 3D printer whose extruder carriage travels along a rail; the motor currents that move it also produce a high-pitched whine, and a sensor writes the carriage's position into a log file. The whine is caused by those same currents, correlates perfectly with the carriage's position, and causes nothing downstream; the log is caused by the sensor and never by the whine. Read the log and you can infer the pitch exactly. Silence the whine while leaving the currents untouched and the log is word for word what it was. Tell me what this example must have and must lack to carry the argument — I suspect the artifact setting smuggles in a designer, and with it a kind of "aboutness" that the real case does not get for free — and whether I should keep it, repair it, or replace it.

Then: the dependency structure among the components and why that order rather than another; and a misconception map with each item placed at the point in the sequence where it must be headed off. The ones I predict are that epiphenomenalism is really eliminativism about consciousness; that it says introspection is unreliable, or that our reports about experience would be noise; that it is simply the problem of other minds in disguise; and, from the stronger students, that a belief-forming process perfectly correlated with the fact believed yields knowledge, so there is no paradox here at all. Tell me what else to expect, and whether at this level I should separate the epistemic charge — that the judgment is ungrounded — from the semantic charge — that the judgment is not about the experience in the first place — or deliberately run them together.

Finally, one question I can put to the room at the end that would show the structure has landed rather than been restated, and an honest statement of what I should refuse to settle in ninety minutes, phrased as I could say it out loud.
