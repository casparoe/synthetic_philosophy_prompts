i sit on the committee that has to give a yes or no on specific AI deployments here (a hospital triage assistant last quarter, now something that generates most of its own training signal, next quarter god knows), and every meeting goes the same way: we argue for an hour about whether the thing is 'aligned' and only realise at the end that three of us meant three different things by it. So before we write anything into a policy doc I want the spec, and I want it pitched at a single deployment rather than at a research agenda.

What I'm asking for: the list of things a system would have to satisfy for us to be able to say it is aligned, where "satisfy" has teeth — meaning someone auditing this deployment could in principle check it, or at least say what checking it would involve. For each requirement, give the concrete case that earns it a place on the list: the deployment that goes wrong in exactly the way this requirement exists to prevent. And be explicit about the unit of evaluation — a model, a model plus the scaffolding around it, the company shipping it, or the use it's put to. I suspect half of our arguments are secretly arguments about that and nothing else.

Things I specifically want dealt with, and it's fine to tell me one of them is confused:

- Aligned compared to what? A human doing the same job? The same system trained differently? Us not deploying it at all? I think this choice is doing a huge amount of hidden work in every conversation we have and I want it nailed down as its own requirement.
- Who is the principal? Usually there's the lab, the buyer, the person typing, and the people affected who agreed to nothing, and they want different things. Whatever the spec says, it has to say something. Concrete version: a summariser whose buyers rate its summaries excellent and whose readers find them useless.
- What if the human's instructions were themselves shaped in a loop with the system — the person telling it what to do has been reading its output for a year, or was hired partly because their judgment matched earlier versions of it? Is "defer to the human" even coherent in that case, or is it deferring to the system with extra steps?
- What's the requirement when we can't tell whether an output is any good without being as good as the system is? Our whole procedure assumes a grader who knows. Say what we do when the grader doesn't.
- Threshold or dial? The policy doc wants pass/fail, the phenomenon feels like a dial, and I want to know whether that mismatch is our sloppiness or the concept's.

Two more things. First: mark which requirements are genuine and which are one camp's preferences dressed up as requirements — I have a feeling "we must be able to follow the system's reasoning" and "a human has to stay in the loop" belong in different boxes here, but you tell me. Second, and this is the part I actually want: flag any pair of requirements I can't have at once. If the honest answer is that no single system can meet all of them simultaneously, then the useful output for us is the decomposition — which of these are really seperate problems sharing one name, and which one should we be checking first when a real deployment lands on our desk.

Keep the weight on the requirements and the cases behind them. If you want to score the main existing approaches against the list in a short table at the end, fine, but I'll do the real scoring myself.
