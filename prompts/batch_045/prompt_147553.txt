I'm writing a seminar paper on the philosophy of competition law, and I keep circling around a cluster of doctrines that seem to me to presuppose incompatible things about agency and coercion, so I'd like help reconstructing the implicit theory rather than a literature survey (I don't need case names, jurisdictions, or citations — please take the doctrine as I characterise it below and treat it as fixed).

Here is the pattern. First: in the antitrust law I'm working from, competing firms that each independently raise, match, or refrain from cutting prices, each in full awareness that the others will do the same and that consumers will end up worse off, commit no violation on that basis. Mutual interdependence plus a rational response to what you expect your rivals to do is conscious parallelism, and it is lawful on its own; a plaintiff has to produce so-called plus factors — evidence of communication, of acts against individual self-interest absent an understanding — pointing to an actual agreement. Second: once there is an agreement, the same system treats the participants as serious wrongdoers, and it deliberately engineers defection from the cartel, with a leniency scheme that gives the first member to self-report far better treatment than the second, so that everyone is placed in a race to confess first. Third: elsewhere in private law, a party who signs a contract because the other side threatened to do something they were perfectly entitled to do can sometimes have the contract set aside for economic duress, on the ground that the pressure left no reasonable alternative — the threat need not be unlawful conduct, and there is still a defence. What puzzles me is the asymmetry: the individual who acts because someone else left them no reasonable alternative has a defence, while the firm that acts because the structure of the market left it no reasonable alternative has none, and the same courts that deny coercion in the second case seem happy to manufacture a coercive race in the first.

I want to press this through the frame of multipolar traps and races to the bottom — the thought that some of these outcomes are nobody's fault in the ordinary sense and yet everyone's doing. So: what does the agreement requirement actually presuppose about coercion and responsibility? Does it strictly presuppose that a trap with no communicating agent behind it is not a form of coercion at all — that coercion requires an agent who wills the outcome — or is it merely compatible with that view, resting in fact on evidentiary and administrability worries (courts cannot see the relevant counterfactuals; liability for parallel conduct would chill pro-competitive behaviour), so that a view on which the structure itself coerces could license the same rule? And where exactly is the practice philosophically underdetermined, so that several theories of coercion would fit it equally well? I have two further wrinkles I'd like addressed. The first is a distinction I want to use: in a dominance trap each party has a dominant strategy to defect and would prefer everyone else to stay put; in what I'll call a ratchet trap — advertising escalation, ever-larger signing bonuses, each increment rational given where the others already are — no one would prefer to unilaterally stop, and the bad equilibrium is reached by steps each of which was locally sensible. Does the agreement requirement track that distinction at all, or does it cut across it, and what follows for whether the doctrine can be justified on its own terms? The second is a hypothetical reform I've been toying with, a structural coercion defence: a firm could plead that its conduct was not chosen but extracted by the structure, and the court would ask whether the industry would have arrived at the same place regardless of what this firm did. Tell me what a court would have to believe about responsibility, about counterfactuals, and about what counts as the natural or baseline state of a market in order to grant such a defence, whether it could be made coherent, and whether granting it would collapse into excusing every profit-maximising firm. My own tentative view is that the agreement requirement is best read as epistemic and administrative rather than metaphysical, and that the leniency scheme shows the law already accepts trap logic instrumentally while refusing it as an excuse — but I'd like to know where that reading is weakest, and which of these three doctrinal pieces would have to change if we took the structural framing seriously.
