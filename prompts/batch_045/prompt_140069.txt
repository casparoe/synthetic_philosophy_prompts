I have to commit to a semantics for vague predicates for a two-year project on regulatory and statutory language — the sort of text full of "significant", "unreasonable", "promptly", "substantial risk". Before committing I want the candidate approaches ranked by how much trouble they actually have with a fixed body of hard cases, rather than by their standing in the dialectic. Please take the five cases below as the evidence base, even where you think they are idealized, and assess the approaches against them. I don't need references or reading suggestions; I want your own assessment.

The approaches (you may add at most one further family if you think a major one is missing):

A. Epistemicism: every predicate has a fully sharp extension; borderline cases are cases of ineradicable ignorance, usually explained by a margin-for-error constraint on what could be known.
B. Supervaluationism: true iff true on every admissible precisification, false iff false on every one, borderline otherwise; classical logic is preserved for the operator "definitely" while bivalence fails for the bare predicate.
C. Subvaluationism: the dual treatment, on which truth on some admissible precisification suffices for truth and falsity on some suffices for falsity, so borderline cases are both true and false and the consequence relation is paraconsistent.
D. Degree semantics: continuum-valued truth, with negation and conjunction handled by a chosen family of operations, and assertibility or truth identified with degree 1 or with exceeding some threshold.
E. Contextualism about standards: the determining standards are fixed by context, and a sorites series works by shifting the context from one step to the next, so that no single context ever contains a cut-off and each adjacent judgment can be correct as made.
F. Strict-tolerant approaches: every instance of the tolerance principle and every classical logical truth is retained, and what is given up instead is the transitivity of entailment, via a distinction between a tolerant and a strict application of the predicate.

The cases:

1. The jittery forced march. Two hundred colour patches, each indiscriminable in hue from its immediate neighbours, running from a clear red to a clear orange. Judged one at a time, in order, with a forced binary choice between "red" and "not red": (i) within a session each subject produces exactly one switch point; (ii) across subjects, and across sessions for the same subject, switch points spread over roughly fifteen positions in the series in a roughly bell-shaped distribution whose mean and spread are stable under repetition; (iii) when a single mid-series patch is presented in isolation with a third option available, most subjects take the third option, and the patches they decline on concentrate in the same region as the switch points.

2. Definiteness without a floor. For one fixed mid-series patch, subjects answer "is it red?", then "is it definitely red?", then "is it definitely definitely red?", up to five iterations, and separately the same series with "not red". The proportion of yes-answers declines smoothly and gradually with each iteration, at a similar rate in both series, and at no level do both series stop drawing yes-answers from a substantial minority. Individual subjects stop at different levels when retested a week later, and none reports any sense that some particular iteration is where the question becomes illegitimate.

3. Vagueness with no ordering dimension. A predicate (say "is a family firm") with clear positive and clear negative cases but no single scalar dimension along which contested cases can be lined up: instead several partly independent criteria — size, concentration of ownership, continuity of control, formality of governance — which trade off against one another. Competent judges asked for pairwise comparative verdicts produce cycles: A over B, B over C, C over A. Shown the cycle, they neither retract nor treat it as revealing an error. Clear cases are stable and never cycle.

4. The five-member board. A board applies "significant hardship" to forty cases one at a time by majority vote, with abstentions and ties ruled out. (i) The board's verdicts show a single switch, but the switch falls in a different place when the same forty cases are presented in descending rather than ascending order of hardship, and individual members' thresholds drift toward recently seen cases. (ii) Asked afterwards, the board declines to state a numerical threshold and declines to say that any of its decisions was mistaken. (iii) Shown two non-adjacent cases side by side, the board sometimes orders them oppositely to the way it implicitly ordered them when the cases came up in sequence.

5. Sharp rule, unsettled application. A regulation defines "overweight shipment" as one whose mass exceeds 20,000 kg. Mass is not itself vague and the drafters' stated aim was to eliminate the vagueness of the everyday term. But certified scales carry a stated tolerance, readings drift with temperature and vibration, and inspectors nonetheless behave exactly as in the ordinary sorites: they decline to say whether a reading of 20,001 kg settles the matter, they treat that refusal as correct rather than as ignorance, and no improvement in instrumentation is expected to end the disputes. Courts, meanwhile, treat the statutory term as perfectly sharp and the residual disputes as purely evidential.

What I want from you:

- A 0–10 score for each approach on each case, and a strict ordering of the approaches within each case. If two really do equally well, break the tie by which one gets there with fewer extra stipulations, and say what the tie-break was.
- An overall ranking, with an explicit weight for each case argued for in light of my project rather than of how intrinsically interesting the case is, followed by the aggregate scores the weights produce. State the weights before the aggregates.
- Which single case is least diagnostic — which one nearly every approach handles for the same structural reason, so that it shouldn't move the ranking. If you think none of them is like this, say which comes closest and why.
- For whichever approach finishes first, the one additional stipulation it still needs in order to handle the case it does worst on.
- One sentence on which single change in the weights would most change the overall ordering, and one sentence on which case you would most want better data on before I commit.

Aim for 700–900 words excluding the table. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the overall ordering with its top aggregate score — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of what I have asked for.
