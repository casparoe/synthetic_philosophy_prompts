Teaching a grad seminar on time in relativity and I want to end on something speculative, so help me out. Say Gödel had lived into the early 90s and run into the literature on what relativists call Malament–Hogarth spacetimes: some rotating solutions — the Kerr geometry is the standard example — contain events p such that a worldline of infinite proper length lies entirely in p's past light cone, so in principle you can set a computer going along that worldline, let it grind forever on a Turing-undecidable problem, and have it signal the answer to an observer who reaches p after finitely much of their own time.

Now, Gödel was drawn to the thought that the mind outruns any finite machine — his famous disjunction was that either that's true, or there are absolutely undecidable mathematical problems. So: would he have seized on this as a reason to take rotating, closed-timelike-curve worlds seriously as physics, as the sort of universe you'd need if non-mechanical cognition is to be physically available? Or would he have said its irrelevant, since the argument he built on his 1949 rotating solution was modal anyway — what mattered was that such worlds are possible, not that we happen to live in one?

And one more: does the hypercomputation story sit badly with his claim that in such a world there is no objective lapse of time? Under a page, and skip the citations.
