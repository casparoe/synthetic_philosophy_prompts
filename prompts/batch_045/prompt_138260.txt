I convene the final examination for an advanced undergraduate seminar on consequentialism and its limits, and I need one essay question on the problem of cluelessness. The students have spent two weeks on it, and the dialectic they have read is one they can reproduce fluently: the objector says that because the long-run effects of our acts swamp the near-term ones we can actually forecast, and because our evidence about the sign of those long-run effects is roughly symmetric, act-consequentialism either fails to guide action at all or quietly degenerates into a near-term proxy that it cannot itself justify; the defender replies that cluelessness is not a special problem but the ordinary problem of acting on uninformative evidence, that symmetric noise is no reason to abandon expected-value maximisation, and that we should simply do the best we can with the evidence we have. I do not want a question that lets a strong student write out that exchange and stop.

What I want is a question that locates the real crux, which I take to be this: the defender's reply depends on assumptions about the *structure* of our uncertainty and about the *level* at which the reply is offered, and both of those can be prised apart. So I would like the question to supply students with two devices they will not have seen before and ask them to use them. First, what I call the "articulation asymmetry": the hypothesis that the causal mechanisms whose long-run consequences we can represent are a biased sample of the mechanisms that matter, biased towards what is near, measurable, and easy to narrate, so that our estimates of long-run value carry an unidentified directional bias and not merely a large variance. Second, what I call the "self-endorsement test": the requirement that a decision rule proposed for use under cluelessness must, when applied to the choice of a decision rule, not recommend its own abandonment. Both should be stated in the question itself, so that no prior reading is needed to engage with them. Anchor the question in a concrete three-way choice — a well-measured near-term intervention, a reform whose long-run effects are orders of magnitude larger but whose sign you cannot estimate, and a research programme that would reduce your cluelessness about the second — and ask students what the clueless consequentialist should do and why.

Please write the question itself, a model answer of roughly 800 words, and a marking scheme. The marking scheme should be built around five must-hit points worth four marks each, totalling twenty, and should include a one-line note on how strong candidates typically go wrong on each point. The points I have in mind, roughly, are: reconstructing the cluelessness argument formally rather than asserting it, separating dominance-by-variance from sign-indefiniteness; stating precisely what the expected-value reply assumes and what would defeat it; handling the articulation asymmetry without overclaiming, since an unknown directional bias whose own direction is symmetric does not by itself break expected-value reasoning, and the candidate needs to say what actually would; applying the self-endorsement test at the level of rules and saying whether the expected-value rule uniquely passes it and whether passing it is even necessary; and giving a normative upshot about whether cluelessness is an objection to consequentialism specifically, whether buying information is a stable response, and whether the immunity of non-consequentialist views to cluelessness is a virtue or a cost. Rename or reorganise these as you see fit, and tell me explicitly, in a short note after the scheme, what separates an A answer from a B answer, since I expect a great many scripts that are clear, well-organised, and wrong in the same place.
