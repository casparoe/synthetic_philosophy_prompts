I'm a senior in high school and I have never taken a philosophy class, but a few months ago I got into an argument with a friend about what we are even saying when we say that something is 70% likely, and I haven't been able to let it go. The definition I kept coming back to was that the probability of an outcome for a particular thing is the fraction of things like it that turn out to have that outcome. That seemed fine until I noticed that "things like it" can be picked in many different ways, that the fraction changes depending on which way you pick, and that nothing in the definition tells you which way is the right one. So I started writing down cases and my own honest verdicts on them, and I would like to know whether those verdicts add up to a principle.

1. A quarter I am about to flip: probability of heads is 1/2, and the group is flips of ordinary coins. This one feels completely unproblematic.

2. Whether a particular patient survives five years. I want the group of patients matched on diagnosis, stage, age and genetic markers, and I think the fraction among everyone who has the disease is simply the wrong number to quote. My working rule has been that the more specific group is always the better one.

3. Same patient, but I keep narrowing until his is the only case left. Now the fraction is 1 or 0 and I don't want to say either. So narrowing cannot always be an improvement; at some point it destroys the answer rather than sharpening it.

4. Groups I think are cheating: coin flips by left-handed people on days the market rose, or patients whose surnames begin with a vowel. I want to say their fractions are irrelevant. But I can't say in general what separates these from the specific group in case 2, and I've noticed that one person's "more specific" tends to be another person's "gerrymandered."

5. A group that exists but where I have almost no data: a thumbtack I've thrown three times, landing point-up twice. I won't say 2/3. I also don't know what to say instead, since the fraction in some endless hypothetical sequence of throws strikes me as a fiction, even though it seems to be the only thing that could make my refusal principled.

6. Two groups, neither of them a subset of the other, giving different fractions: Sam falls under one description with a 12% rate and another with a 4% rate, and nobody has ever tabulated the people who satisfy both. I don't want to pick one; I want to say the answer lies between them, or that there is no exact number I can state.

7. A group whose membership was fixed by how things turned out: bridges of this design among bridges that have never collapsed, or patients who survived long enough to appear in the study. I think using such a group is a straight mistake, because the group was assembled by the very outcome I'm asking about.

8. The first person to set foot on Mars: what is the probability that this person is a woman? There is no group at all yet, nothing has happened, and still I want to say 1/2 and I'm fairly confident about it. This case worries me most, because read alongside case 3 it suggests my verdicts aren't really coming from fractions in groups: offered a group of exactly one I refuse to use its fraction, and offered a group of zero I am perfectly happy to produce a number.

The best principle I've been able to state for myself is: the probability of an outcome for a particular thing is the fraction of that outcome in the narrowest group of relevantly similar things for which you have enough data to get a stable fraction, where a group is disqualified if its membership was fixed by whether the outcome occurred or if it was chosen after the answer was known. I can see it fails case 8, and I suspect it fails case 6, and I have no idea what it does with case 3, since "enough data" looks like an arbitrary stopping point rather than a real constraint.

The case I keep going back and forth on is a surgeon's estimate that a never-performed operation has about a 60% chance of success. Part of me thinks there is no group here at all, so 60% is just her confidence and there is nothing further for it to be right or wrong about, as long as she is being coherent. Another part of me thinks there is a real chance of the operation succeeding and her number is an estimate of it, so that it could be badly wrong even if her reasoning is faultless, and that this is the only way to make sense of her being called better or worse calibrated over her career.

What I'd like from you: one principle, or a short list of candidates, that fits as many of my verdicts as possible; each clause tied to the case that motivates it; a plain statement of which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and which I should therefore give up, especially if that means abandoning the fraction-in-a-group idea altogether in favor of something like degree of confidence. Then tell me what your principle says about cases I haven't listed, for instance the probability that a given scientific hypothesis is true, or the chance that I personally am in a car accident in the next year, and whether it settles the surgeon case for me. I've been told this difficulty has a name and a literature, but I don't want the history or a reading list; I want the principle and the price of holding it.
