I teach a cross-listed seminar called *Measurement and Institutions* — 26 upper-level students, about half computer science and half public policy, meeting for 90 minutes. I want one session, early in the term, that makes the structure of specification-gaming failure something the students collide with rather than something I assert. No lecture up front; they should not hear the name of the phenomenon, or any slogan for it, until the debrief.

Constraints before you start designing.

Materials are paper, a projector, and one shared spreadsheet: index cards, a scoring sheet, a deck of hidden-objective cards, points instead of money, and nothing the students have to install. The scenario should be invented for the game — please do not build it around a canonical anecdote, because half the room will have heard the anecdote and will merely recognize it instead of reasoning.

What I want the machinery to do.

Students are split into principal teams and agent teams. Each principal team is handed, on a card, a target it genuinely cares about — something concrete enough to argue about. Each agent team draws a private objective card that is *partially* aligned with the principal's, with the degree and locus of alignment hidden from the principals. Principals write a metric they will score agents on; agents then act to maximise the metric under their own private objective.

The spec must be short. I want a clause budget — say, an initial metric of no more than twenty-five words, with only a small number of additional words purchasable in each later round — so that teams are forced to choose which gaming surface to close first and which to leave open.

Run at least three metric-writing rounds with re-specification between them, so the group can feel for itself whether iterated tightening converges on the target, oscillates, or ratchets away from it. Then invert the direction once: in a final round, the *agents* propose the metric they will be measured by. I have not seen that inversion staged and I think it is where the most interesting discomfort lives, so give it real design attention rather than treating it as a variant.

I also want the mirror-image failure to be staged, not mentioned: a round in which agents do something that genuinely advances the target but is invisible to, or actively penalised by, the metric. The point is that a proxy can fail by under-measuring as well as by being gamed, and I want students to notice the asymmetry before anyone labels it.

Two things about scoring that I think are the real design problem.

First, I cannot be an oracle: I do not have privileged access to the true target, and any score I assign is itself a proxy. Do not solve this by pretending otherwise. Design a scoring apparatus that works without oracle access, and tell me how to stage the moment when a sharp student points out that my own judging is one more proxy — I want that to be the best moment of the session rather than a derailment.

Second, the payoffs must not imply that gaming is a vice. I want at least one round where gaming the metric is the rational move for an agent that fully shares the principal's goal, and at least one where hitting the metric also genuinely advances the target. Specify precisely what principals' points and agents' points depend on and how they interact.

Teams should mix computer-science and policy students, and I expect the two groups to reach for different framings; tell me how to make that split productive and what to do when they talk past each other.

The debrief should map specific moments of play onto specific parts of the idea: the gap between target and proxy; failure from selecting on the extreme of a noisy proxy versus failure from an agent optimising against you; whether re-specification helped and why; and the under-measurement case. It should say when the name gets introduced and what the students supply before you do. Include an honest treatment of the move where someone concludes that measurement is therefore pointless — including the cases where gaming is diagnostic rather than corruptive, and where the episode reveals that the stated target was incoherent.

Finally: tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — at least four failure modes with concrete handling — and be frank about which features of the phenomenon the game cannot reproduce and I will simply have to say out loud.
