I write a weekly column for a national newspaper on science and the life of ideas, and I am working on a piece about the ethics of belief as it applies to working journalists rather than to private individuals. Before I draft it, I would like you to set out the most important implications of the James–Clifford dispute for one specific, recurring situation, which I have tried to describe precisely enough that you cannot answer it with the standard talking points.

The situation. A reporter covers a fast-moving field in which a striking result has been announced by a credible group, the early replication attempts are contradictory, and she must file within hours, before the evidence settles. Her editor will accept anything from a flat assertion of the result to a heavily hedged report, and treats hedging as almost costless. Three features distinguish her case from the textbook ones:

First, graded belief. She is not choosing between full belief and withholding; she is choosing a point on a continuum of confidence, and a matching strength of wording, so the question is what the ethics of belief says about degrees of confidence and about hedged assertion, not only about outright belief.

Second, doxastic leverage. Several hundred thousand readers, plus other reporters and a few funders, update on how she frames the story. A philosopher friend of mine argues that once leverage is large, the ethics of belief collapses into an ethics of influence: what matters morally is not whether her own state is true, but the size and direction of the updating she induces in others. I want that claim pressed, not merely recorded.

Third, self-verifying uptake. In this particular field, widespread belief that the result holds channels money, attention, and replication effort towards it, which measurably raises the chance that it is eventually confirmed; scepticism channels the same resources elsewhere. Optimistic belief here is partly self-fulfilling, in a way that the shipowner's belief in the soundness of his ship was not. Call this the tipping-point case.

Please give me the five most important implications of the James–Clifford dispute for how such a reporter should set her confidence and her wording. I want implications that turn on the details above, and specifically on: whether James's conditions for a genuine option can even be applied when the option is a matter of degree rather than a forced choice between believing and not believing; whether a Jamesian permission to believe on passional grounds extends to a case where the expected benefit accrues to other people and to the field rather than to the believer herself; whether Clifford's rule constrains belief, assertion, or both, and, if both, whether hedged assertion can discharge the duty that full belief would violate; and whether a belief's tendency to help bring about its own truth is evidence, an excuse, or neither.

Assume throughout that the reporter's personal welfare is not at stake, that she is competent and honest, and that no one is coercing her. Do not spend any of your five items on generalities about balance, both-sidesism, or the state of trust in science journalism.

Present your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no concluding summary, in British English spelling and usage.
