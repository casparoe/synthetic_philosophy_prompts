I'm fluent in all of that. I have basicaly zero neuroscience and zero philosophy, so please don't assume I know what a synapse does beyond "roughly a multiply-accumulate" or what anyone means by "computation" outside my shop.

What I want is a translation plus a residue report. Restate the actual argument of the book as faithfully as you can in my vocabulary, then tell me what didn't survive the trip.

The bits I want translated, as I (possibly wrongly) remember them:

- The nervous system as a *mixed* system: the pulse itself is all-or-none, so digital-ish, but the intensity of a signal is carried by pulse rate, which is continuous, so analogue-ish — hence neither of the two kinds of machine he knew how to build.
- The precision-versus-parallelism trade-off: his neurons switch in about a millisecond, his vacuum tubes in microseconds, there are something like ten billion neurons, so the brain must buy its performance with parallelism and pay for it in precision — and therefore its arithmetic cannot be our arithmetic.
- Getting reliable behaviour out of components that aren't reliable. I think he gestures at something like multiplexing, using many channels to carry one message.
- His remarks, if I've got this right, about the nervous system running on its own code or language rather than the language of mathematics, and about the organism being specified in one code and running in another.
- Whatever he says about memory capacity and how much the thing can hold.

Here's the wrench, and I want you to engage with it rather than nod along. On my reading, numbers one and two above have been quietly dissolved by the last decade of my own field. We deliberately run at eight or four bits because precision is expensive and throughput is what sells. We do analogue multiply-accumulates inside the memory array with real physical noise, and we just live with it — stochastic rounding, noise-aware training, retraining around the defects. We are entirely happy shipping a machine whose arithmetic is approximate and whose per-element reliability is poor, provided the aggregate behaviour is fine. So either von Neumann's contrast was only ever a contrast with 1950s vacuum-tube hardware and evaporates the moment you've seen an int4 systolic array, or there is something in his sense of "analogue" and "precision" that my vocabulary can't see and my hardware still doesn't have.
