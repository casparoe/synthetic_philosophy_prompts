I spent most of my career as a marine biogeochemist, reconstructing past ocean conditions from proxies: isotopic ratios in foraminiferal shells, accumulation rates in sediment cores, and the like. A large part of that work consisted of arguing with colleagues about what a proxy actually entitles you to say. Since retiring I have been attending a philosophy reading group, and I have rather unexpectedly been asked to help set the final paper for an upper-level interdisciplinary seminar, Collapse: Ending or Becoming?, taught jointly by a historian and a philosopher. The students have worked through the familiar camps: the ecocide or self-inflicted environmental overreach account; the diminishing-returns-on-complexity account; and the more recent literature insisting that what looks like collapse is really transformation, persistence and continuity under another name. My worry is that the third camp has hardened into a slogan. A student can now write a perfectly competent essay by repeating that societies do not fail, they change, without ever saying what is at stake in the word.

I would like to use the one thing I know something about — proxies — to make that harder. Below is a small apparatus of my own devising. Please build the exam question around it, rather than around the reading list.

Suppose we describe a polity at a given time by two measured series. Call the first the demographic proxy: occupied site count, or summed settled area, or whatever else tracks bodies on the ground. Call the second the institutional proxy: a composite of administrative tiers, standardisation of weights and measures, volume of long-distance exchange, and energy invested in monumental construction. Now consider four stylised three-hundred-year trajectories. In the first, both proxies fall by more than eighty per cent and stay down. In the second, the demographic proxy falls by more than eighty per cent while the institutional proxy recovers to its former level within two centuries, but under new elites. In the third, the institutional proxy collapses while the demographic proxy is roughly stable: the people remain, the fields are still farmed, but the tiers, the standards and the monuments do not come back. In the fourth, neither proxy falls, but the polity is absorbed into a larger one, and nothing the old elites would have recognised as their own survives under its own name.

Two further complications should be built in. The first is what I would call window sensitivity: whether a change counts as abrupt depends on whether you measure it over fifty years or five hundred, and the archaeologist's stratum cannot always tell you which window the people living through it experienced. The second is hysteresis, which I would like borrowed honestly rather than as a metaphor. In the study of critical transitions, a system that has flipped may not return to its former state even when the forcing that flipped it is reversed, because the mechanism that would rebuild that state has itself been destroyed: the litter that kept a clear shallow lake clear is gone, and clear water alone will not bring it back. The analogous claim here would be that the capacity to run a bureaucratic, record-keeping, monument-building polity depends on a literate cadre, a fiscal habit and a network of partners, so that restoring the former population and the former climate does not restore the polity.

The question should force students to say whether the dispute between the collapse camp and the transformation camp is verbal, a disagreement about which proxy deserves the name, a disagreement about reversibility in the hysteretic sense, or a normative disagreement resting on the fact that collapse is a thick term which at once describes a loss and ranks it as a failure, while quietly identifying the society with the apparatus rather than with the population. It should also make them say what explanatory or predictive work, if any, the word does once the apparatus has been sorted out. It must not be answerable by reciting the slogan in either direction, and it should require the student to handle at least two of the four trajectories explicitly.

Please write the material in four titled sections, in this order.

1. The question — around 300 words: the self-contained essay question as it would appear on the paper, addressed to the students, stating the apparatus compactly and setting out exactly what they must do in ninety minutes, closed book.
2. Model answer — around 900 words: written in continuous prose as a first-class answer would actually read, not as notes to a marker. It should take a position on the menu of diagnoses above, say which of the four trajectories count as a collapse and why, use hysteresis to make a substantive rather than decorative point, and close by saying what work the concept retains.
3. Marking scheme — around 450 words: twenty-five marks distributed across five named must-hit moves, with the marks each is worth, and an explicit statement of the move that separates an A answer from a B answer.
4. Frequent failures — around 250 words: the mistakes that should cost marks, each with its penalty and a line on why students make it. I have in mind such things as declaring the whole dispute verbal and stopping there, sliding between the two proxies without noticing, treating hysteresis as a poetic flourish, and supposing that data could settle the evaluative component of a thick term.
