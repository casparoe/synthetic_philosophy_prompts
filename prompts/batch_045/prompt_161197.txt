Here's the situation. My reading group (me + four grad students) has been stuck for about eight months on the same old fight: do composite objects exist? The nihilist says there are no tables, only particles arranged tablewise. The commonsense/universalist side says there are tables. Nobody disagrees about the physics, about the micro-arrangement of particles, or about what anyone would observe. And each side can hand you paraphrases of the other side's sentences that preserve every observational and practical consequence. The quantifier-variance / neo-Carnapian camp looks at this and says: verbal, you're just using "exists" to range over different things. The ontological-realist camp says: no, one of us is wrong about what there is, and that's a real, substantive mistake. These two camps have been saying the same handful of things at each other forever, and in eight months not one person's intuition has moved even slightly.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis — argue with it if you think its wrong: the "verbal or substantive?" framing is itself a trap, because it assumes there's one univocal thing, "what there is," that both sides are aiming at, such that either both succeed equally (verbal) or one gets it right (substantive). What's really going on is a third option: a disagreement about what ontology is *for*. One side thinks its job is to describe the world's fundamental structure, so "there are tables" is a candidate for literal truth or falsity. The other thinks its job is to give us the best regimented language for talking about whatever we end up talking about, so the choice between frameworks is a practical or axiological choice, not a factual one. On that reading the dispute is neither factual nor verbal but evaluative: they disagree about the point of the practice, and that disagreement bottoms out in bedrock differences about which virtue matters (fundamentality? expressive convenience? fidelity to ordinary talk?) that neither side can argue for.

Now the part I actually want you to chew on. I invented a diagnostic gadget, call it the Cost-of-Error Test. For any dispute, ask each side: suppose you're wrong — what exactly has gone wrong, and what would you then be obliged to stop doing or give up? If the answer is "I'd have a false belief about the world and I should retract a claim I'm making," that's a factual dispute. If it's "I'd find out we were talking past each other and should drop the question," that's verbal. If it's "I'd have to concede my framework is worse at something I care about, or that I've been spending my time on the wrong project," that's the third kind. Run this test on the composite-objects dispute, for both sides, and tell me where it breaks down. In particular: the realist will say "I'd have a false belief," which makes the dispute look factual on this test — but tell me whether that answer is *informed* by anything independent, or whether it's just stipulated by their own view, i.e. whether my gadget is question-begging in exactly the way the verbal/substantive framing is.
