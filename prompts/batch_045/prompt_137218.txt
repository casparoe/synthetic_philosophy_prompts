Hey, hoping you can help with a bit of course prep. I'm teaching a graduate seminar this spring — working title "Who Gets to Be the Judge?" — on what happens to our epistemic practices when the thing being judged is an argument that nobody in the room can actually check, and one week is going to be on that idea from some AI safety circles: that you can get honest, maximally useful answers out of very capable systems by having two of them argue against each other in front of a human judge who is, by stipulation, not able to verify the claims himself. I don't want to open that week with another bland summary of the proposal and its counters, so here's the ask: write me the Official Rules and Adjudication Handbook for an elite competitive league built entirely on that premise.

What I'm picturing is a deadpan administrative document — numbered clauses, call it 700 to 900 words, in the voice of a rulebook that has been amended many times by committees trying to close loopholes and quietly revealing, clause by clause, that the loopholes are the whole game. It should make people who know the actual arguments here laugh, and it should still be readable for my students, who mostly aren't from CS. Critically, the humour has to come from getting the structure right rather than from inventing silliness: the founding idea that the honest side should win because the truth is easier to defend; cross-examination as a device for forcing a speaker to point at the single weakest link in a long chain instead of restating the chain; the fact that producing a hundred plausible-looking inferential steps is cheaper than locating the one bad step among them; the way a dispute about a claim slides into a dispute about whether the previous objection was really answered; and the odd status of the judge, who is the only participant expressly forbidden from going to check anything. None of that should ever be stated as an objection — it should all show up as neutral-sounding rulemaking that is straining to hold a line.

Some things I'd love worked in, though don't treat this as binding: a definitions section separating an "Argument" from an "Obfuscated Argument" in a way that quietly makes the distinction unenforceable in practice; a clause capping how deep recursive objections may go, with a footnote about how that number was arrived at; a tie-breaking procedure for cases where both sides are equally unverifiable; and a scoring innovation I've been batting around in my head, call it a counterfactual-robustness bonus, where a win only counts if the panel certifies that the winner would also have won against a version of itself with roughly ten times the capability. Maybe an appendix of special event types too: questions whose answers are checkable in principle but not by anyone in the building, and a small experimental division where both debaters are assigned the same side and scored on how much damage they voluntarily do to their own case. Keep it in-universe all the way through — no winking at the reader, no real lab names, no papers, no citations; the document should read as though the league sincerely believes it works.
