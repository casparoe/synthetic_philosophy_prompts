I'm in a philosophy and economics elective right now and we just did Sen's liberal paradox — the two-person toy example with the people called Lewd and Prude, a single copy of the novel Lady Chatterley's Lover, three outcomes (Lewd reads it, Prude reads it, or nobody reads it), and the rankings where Lewd most prefers Prude being made to read it, then Lewd reading it himself, then nobody reading it, while Prude most prefers nobody reading it, then Prude reading it in disgust, then Lewd enjoying it. As I understand the argument, giving each person decisiveness over whether they themselves read forces society to rank Lewd-reads above Prude-reads, but because they both actually prefer Prude-reads above Lewd-reads, Pareto forces the reverse, and you get a cycle; so no aggregation rule can satisfy unrestricted domain, Pareto, and even a minimal liberalism all at once.

Before we read the proposed escapes, I want a spec rather than a literature review: write the desiderata any adequate theory has to meet if it wants to keep both a Pareto condition and some genuine individual rights in social choice, i.e. an account that blocks the cycle without just deleting rights or pretending the example never happens. I definately don't need citations, a bibliography, or a reading list — just the conceptual list. Give me the requirements with the motivating case behind each: the cases it has to get right (the Lady Chatterley set-up, plus a variant where the nosy preference runs the other way and Lewd would hate Prude reading it, plus a case with more than two people if that changes anything), the seperate distinctions it can't collapse (being decisive over a pair vs. actually having a moral right, negative rights not to be forced to read vs. positive rights to make someone else read, excluding meddlesome preferences vs. just stipulating the conclusion, and Pareto as unanimity vs. Pareto as efficiency), the no-go results it must stay consistent with (Sen's result and Arrow/Gibbard-Satterthwaite if they bite), and which demands are non-negotiable versus just a preference of one camp — e.g. if someone insists a right has to act as an absolute trump or veto over social goals, is that a real desideratum or a prejudice?

To make this less generic, I've been playing with a restriction I'm calling the No-Peeping condition: an individual's ranking over outcomes has to depend only on their own private consumption (whether they themselves read) and on whether their own rights are violated, and cannot depend on the private consumption of others; so if two outcomes hold fixed my own private act and my rights, I have to be indifferent between them no matter who else reads, and I can't reverse my preference about my own reading just because of what someone else is doing. On that condition Lewd could not rank Prude-reads above Lewd-reads merely because he likes the thought of Prude being forced to read; his preferences would collapse to Lewd-reads first, then indifference between Prude-reads and nobody-reads, while Prude would be indifferent between nobody-reads and Lewd-reads and would put both above Prude-reads. Treat that as one candidate move and say what desiderata it would have to meet to be more than ad hoc: what counts as a private act, how it handles a case where everyone is meddlesome or where the meddlesome preference is shared, whether it should be a domain restriction (weakening unrestricted domain) or a revision of the rights condition, whether it accidentally kills off liberties we do want to protect (say, my say over whether a racist pamphlet gets pushed through my neighborhood, or a parent's say over their kid's reading), and whether it can even be stated without smuggling in the very trade-off it is meant to resolve.
