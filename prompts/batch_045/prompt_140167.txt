Bit of a panic: I'm presenting at the motor control group here in ten days, and the room will be mostly evidence-accumulation people — the sort who'd much rather talk about drift rates, thresholds and stopping rules than about "the will" — plus one philosopher of action who works on trying and intentional omission. Almost nobody there cares about free will as such, which is exactly why I'm nervous: I'll get their objections, not the ones I've rehearsed.

What I'm defending: the conscious veto in Libet-style setups shouldn't be written off as a last-ditch rump of agency squeezed in just before the movement, and the reason is that the whole experiment is better modelled as a race. The go process gets set running by the instruction ("move whenever you feel like it"); what the subject actually contributes is a policy — the threshold, the caution, the standing intention about which urges get let through and which get killed. So agency sits in the setting of the race, not in winning it at the last moment, and I want to argue that on this description the veto is where the agent's reasons enter rather than a face-saving afterthought. Sketch of the argument: (1) the timing of the readiness potential tells you nothing about when a decision was made once you take the accumulation picture seriously; (2) what's left for the subject to contribute is the stopping policy; (3) intentional omissions are genuine exercises of agency, so a veto is not a lesser or second-class case.
