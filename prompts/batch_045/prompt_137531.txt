Having spent a career in geochemistry and come to philosophy only in retirement, I sat in on a meeting of our county historical society where two members of the town's commemoration committee fell into an argument I have not been able to settle. The object is a bronze statue, erected in 1898, of Dr. Ephraim Halloran, who developed a surgical repair for obstetric fistula — and developed it by operating, without consent, on enslaved women — and who later helped found the county's public hospital. It stands on the courthouse lawn. Ingrid wants it relocated to the county museum; Marcus wants it left standing with a new interpretive plaque.

Their exchange, as nearly as I can reconstruct it:

(1) Ingrid: honoring is a standing speech act performed by the town, and its content is not fixed by what the sculptor intended but by the visual grammar of a nine-foot bronze on a plinth in the most authoritative square in town. A plaque cannot undo that grammar; salience is not neutral, and you do not neutralize a hierarchy by annotating it.

(2) Marcus: the honoring is largely dormant — for decades nobody read the statue as saying anything, and what it now actually communicates to anyone who looks is that in 1898 this town's notables chose to admire this man, which is a fact about them. Removing it destroys the evidence of that fact and quietly lets the present pretend it never admired him.

(3) Ingrid: dormant or not, an honorific is in force while it stands, like a statute that is never enforced. And there is a substitution test: the square has essentially one monument site, so leaving it occupied by Halloran is not neutrality but a decision to spend that space on him rather than on the women he operated on.

(4) Marcus: the scarcity is overstated — towns add monuments all the time — and the two moves are not symmetric, since additions are cheap and reversible while removals are not, so the default should favor adding. Anyway, relocating the statue converts a public moral question into a curated one and lets the county treat the matter as settled at a one-time cost, when the live obligation — the health disparities in the county the hospital still serves — is ongoing and expensive.

(5) Ingrid: the cheap-gesture worry is real but runs the other way; you cannot credibly fund redress while the county officially celebrates the man, so symbolic and material redress are sequenced rather than competing. The hospital board's offer to fund a companion monument to the women only if the statue stays is not a compromise but a hostage-taking.

(6) Marcus: that is overheated; nearly every real reform is an impure package of that kind, and refusing packages because they are impure means nothing gets built.

Please adjudicate this move by move: rate each of the six from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification, and say what both parties are missing. I would then like a committed verdict rather than a survey — including whether the dormancy argument or the substitution test is the stronger of the two, and whether some third option (an empty plinth, inscription of the women's names into the existing stone, or some deliberately self-undermining counter-monument) is genuinely better than either side's proposal. Take a position and give your reasons.
