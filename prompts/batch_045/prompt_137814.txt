So my roommate — he's a CS PhD student who audits philosophy seminars for fun, which tells you what kind of person he is — has an argument that there's basically no such thing as a violation of privacy, and I've been failing to find the hole in it for about a week. Here it is more or less as he wrote it on the whiteboard:

(1) To wrong someone you have to make them worse off in some respect: set back one of their interests, frustrate something they care about, that sort of thing.
(2) Finding out a true fact about someone doesn't make any new fact true and doesn't change any of their intrinsic properties. They're the same person, physically and mentally, before and after.
(3) So an act that changes none of the facts about a person and none of their intrinsic properties can't make them worse off in any respect.
(4) So merely finding something true out about someone — reading their diary, watching them through a window, running their DNA — doesn't make them worse off.
(5) Recording it or storing it is just reliable finding-out-later, so that doesn't either.
(6) So no act of pure observation or collection can wrong anyone. Only acting on the information can: blackmail, firing them, outing them, denying them insurance.
(7) Which means a surveillance apparatus that collects everything about everybody and never, ever acts on it has violated nobody's rights.

I have two worries and I want help with the second one mostly. Worry one is just that (7) seems insane to me — I have a really stubborn intuition about the pure-observation cases, and it doesn't go away even when I stipulate that nobody ever uses the footage, nobody ever learns it secondhand, the person never finds out they were watched, and the watcher has no hostile intentions at all. Worry two, the one I actually want your diagnosis on: I think the argument proves too much by its own lights. Step (6) is supposed to leave us a residue, "use can still be a wrong" — but every use case he lists works by getting some *other* person to know true things, and by (2) and (3) that changes no facts about the victim either. So on his own premises it looks like the only thing left that can be a wrong is plain physical interference, and the whole category of informational wrong collapses. He bites that bullet with an annoying amount of cheerfulness and says something like "yes, and that's fine, information wants to be free," which I don't think is an argument.

So: which step do you reject, and why did it look fine on the way through? I should say where I'd rather take the loss — I care a lot about keeping the claim that the (4)/(7) cases can be wrongs, I'm genuinely indifferent about (1), and the reading we did on the reductionist line (privacy as just a bundle of other rights and interests, nothing of its own) versus the contextual-integrity thing left me completely fine with privacy turning out not to be one single thing — so I'm happy to pay somewhere structural. One more data point that makes me suspicious of his picture: he agrees it's somehow worse if a human watches the footage than if it's only ever crunched by a machine that deletes it immediately, but his explanation is that machines don't really "know" anything, which seems like it should collapse under (2) too, since on his own view knowing is just tracking true facts. Anyway — please don't just tell me it depends what I mean by privacy and stop there; tell me which reading of privacy makes which step work, and what I'd be stuck with if I gave that step up.
