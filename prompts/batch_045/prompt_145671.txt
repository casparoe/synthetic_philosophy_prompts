I help coordinate a volunteer driver rota for a small community meals service — about thirty people, mostly retired, arranged over email — and over the last couple of years I have become slightly obsessed with a pattern in how we judge one another that I have no word for. Two volunteers are asked on the same day to cover an extra run. The first reads the message, actually thinks about it — she has a hospital appointment, but it is in the morning and the run is in the evening, so she could probably manage it — decides against, and writes back to say so politely. The second never opens the message at all; she has been away, or she is having a hard month, and she simply does not respond. The first volunteer is the one who acquires a faint reputation for not pulling her weight. The second is treated as having been unavailable. The difference cannot be the outcome, the reasons, or even the effort: the first woman's reasons were perfectly good, the run was not hers, she was under no obligation to take it, and everyone in the group would say she was free to decline. What seems to do the work is that she took the request up as a live question and then answered it in the negative. I have since seen the same shape everywhere: the colleague who visibly weighs a request for help and then does not help is judged more harshly than the one who never registered that there was a request; the local business that looked into making a donation and decided not to is resented in a way the business that never considered it is not; and at the far end, the passer-by who checks whether the person slumped on the pavement is unwell and then walks on strikes me as worse than the one who walks past without seeing a question at all.

There is a mirror image that I suspect is the same thing rather than a separate one. The person who thinks about it and then does help gets less credit than the person who helps immediately and without apparent thought, which seems backwards if reflection is what makes an action moral in the first place. And the whole effect seems to depend on publicity: someone who weighed the request, declined, and then mentioned the weighing is judged worse than someone who weighed it, declined, and said nothing. Yet it is not simply that deliberation is bad. When a volunteer has weighed the run against a conflicting claim — visiting a spouse in hospital — and declined on those grounds, I do not judge her at all, and I do not think anyone else does either. So the pattern is not about thinking per se, and it is not about the reasons, and it is not about the act's optionality in the ordinary sense.

A friend who took some philosophy at university said this is just the territory of acts beyond the call of duty, or a version of the worry about how much morality may demand of us, and neither of those seems to reach it: as I understand them, they are about which acts are optional, whereas my puzzle is about an optional act's optionality apparently not surviving being consciously taken up, and about praise going down rather than up when someone reflects and then performs. She also muttered something about there being one thought too many in cases like this, which I did not follow. So: is there an established concept or literature for this — the way deliberation, or the visibility of deliberation, alters the moral standing of an optional act's performance or non-performance? If nothing fits, please say so plainly rather than stretching something into place, and give me a term with a definition and boundary cases. I would rather have the carving than the label: what exactly the phenomenon is, which nearby notions it collides with and how it differs from each, whether the extra blame is better explained by what the deliberation reveals about the agent or by something that deliberating-and-declining itself does, whether the praise-side and the blame-side are really one phenomenon, and whether it changes when the person has the relevant skill or role but is not on duty — our rota includes a retired paramedic, and I have no idea whether she is in a different position from the rest of us. Please also tell me how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you are least sure of. Write it as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, since I intend to read it aloud to my friend over the phone.
