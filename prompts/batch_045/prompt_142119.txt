I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on decision theory and rational choice: 24 students, one 75-minute session, a blackboard but no laptops. They can compute expected utilities, they have seen the one-shot prisoner's dilemma, and a few of them will have run into the label "superrationality" online. I want them to meet the argument as a surprise, so it should not be named until the debrief.

Design a four-round game for this session that makes students feel the load-bearing premise of that argument: that the reason to cooperate in a one-shot dilemma against a sufficiently similar agent is not that your choice causally influences theirs but that the two decisions are expected to move together — and that this co-movement is a matter of degree, with a threshold below which the argument simply gives out. Use this payoff matrix throughout, in points convertible at the end into raffle tickets for a trivial prize (say a twenty-dollar book voucher), so that stakes are real but small: mutual cooperation 3/3, mutual defection 1/1, unilateral cooperation 0/5. Each student's partner is the other holder of the same letter on a card drawn from a bag; nobody is told who their partner is, and talking during a round is out of bounds.

Here is the arc I have in mind, though you should fix it if it will not run. Round one: an ordinary anonymous round, both players' slips used. Round two: a "mirror" round in which each student's payoff cell is computed from their own slip twice, so that they are, for payoff purposes, playing a copy of themselves; this must be disclosed honestly before anyone chooses. Round three, the centerpiece: pairs are pre-assigned to mirror or open by sealed envelopes, five of the twelve marked mirror and seven marked open, with the composition announced in advance but not the contents; students are told that with probability p = 2/5 they are playing a copy of themselves and otherwise their partner's actual slip. Round four: everyone open again, but a single die is rolled in front of the room and students may make their choice conditional on the public outcome, while their own dice (used privately under a cup in round three) are put away.

Two pieces of apparatus I want used explicitly. First, define α as the probability your partner cooperates given that you cooperate, β as the probability your partner cooperates given that you defect, and the co-move gap g = α − β; the debrief should give the general condition for cooperation in terms of g and β, show it on the board, and show how the facilitator's p contributes to g but is not the whole of it. Second, after round three, tally from the open pairs only how often someone who chose C was paired with a partner who chose C, and likewise for those who chose D; the difference is the room's realized gap. Tell me how to do that tally in about two minutes with 24 slips, and what to say if it comes out near zero, negative, or larger than 2/5.

My own calculation is that at p = 2/5 the symmetric answer is not "cooperate" but "cooperate with probability 1/3," because each student's optimum depends on the room's cooperation rate, which is itself made of the students' own choices; and that once private randomization is replaced by a shared die the effective gap returns to 1 and the answer flips back to unqualified cooperation. Check both claims, correct them if they are wrong, and show the arithmetic in a form I can put on the board without losing half the room.

What I need from you: a materials and prep list; a minute-by-minute timeline; the exact words I should read before each round — no more than two sentences per round — that disclose the payoff mechanism completely without naming the idea or hinting at a verdict; the tally to display after each round; and a debrief that maps specific moments onto specific parts of the argument, including the point at which it is finally named and the point at which students should notice that round two does not discriminate between causal, evidential, and functional decision theories, since there the facilitator has made both cells depend on one slip by fiat.

Also include: at least five things that will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, with the handling for each — I expect trouble from students who cooperate out of simple niceness, from anyone who thinks the mirror round is a trick, from signaling despite the ban, and from the debrief arithmetic. Be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: the claim that two independent agents running the same reasoning must converge is supplied here by my envelopes rather than by anything the students experience, and the game cannot show whether that claim is true. Finally, add a six-minute coda in which I hand eight of the twenty-four students a card on which the temptation payoff is 7 instead of 5, announce that fact but not the holders, and ask what a superrational pair holding mismatched cards should do; say what the coda is for, what answers to expect, and whether you would cut it.
