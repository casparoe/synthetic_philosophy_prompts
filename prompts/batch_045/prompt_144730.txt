I'm a backend engineer (one logic class in college, hated it, no other philosophy) and I need to go into a design review on Thursday with something better than vibes.

We're arguing about whether to finally replace an internal message format we've had since about 2016. It's genuinely bad: the schema still has a field for a vendor we dropped years ago, the ordering guarantees are documented wrong, and one of the consumers only works because of a bug somebody's now dependant on. Every time I push for a rewrite, a senior engineer says "worse is better" and the room kind of goes quiet and we move on. I've heard that line forever — I think it comes out of some old Lisp-era essay, I dunno who wrote it — and I've slowly realized I can't tell what he means by it. Across different meetings I think I've heard at least four different things:

1. simple or inferior designs win adoption because they ship first and spread, and the better-designed alternative never gets enough install base to catch up
2. once something is installed, switching costs mean you can't displace it no matter how much better the alternative is
3. users actually don't care about the properties we engineers think make a design good, so the "worse" thing isn't worse by the only measure that matters
4. it doesn't matter that we chose this, because we'd have ended up in the same place anyway — which, conveniently, means nobody has to own the 2016 decision

Can you give me a menu of the distinct precise claims "worse is better" could be making, grouped by what kind of claim each one is (a claim about what causes adoption, about lock-in, about what "better" even means, about whether the outcome was inevitable)? For each one: is it a platitude, a real substantive thesis that might be true, or actually false — and, the part I care most about — which of my colleague's four moves it would actually support if it were true. I want to know which readings do real work in the argument and which ones are being smuggled in.

Some things I specifically want separated out:

- "we'd have ended up with some message format having this general kind of problem" vs. "we'd have ended up with this exact format and these exact problems." Those feel like totally different claims and I think he slides between them. Is there a name for that distinction?
- whether the lock-in reading needs something like increasing returns, coordination problems, or everyone-having-to-move-at-once, or whether plain old switching cost is enough. In our case we control both ends of most traffic, it's maybe 40 services, and we could run both formats side by side during a migration — so I suspect the strong version of the claim doesn't apply here, and I want to be able to say precisely why.
- whether there's a version of the slogan that's unfalsifiable, something like "whatever won must have been better on the dimension that mattered." That feels true by definition and therefore useless, but I can't articulate why.
- whether any of the readings is supposed to be about fault or responsibility at all. I think he's using it to get from "the process was path-dependent" to "nobody's decision made a difference," and that step looks broken to me but I can't point at the exact broken part.

Please don't just tell me about QWERTY and VHS vs. Betamax — I've heard those a million times and they don't fit my situation anyway. I don't need sources or reading suggestions either; I'm writing a doc for Thursday, not a paper. Distinctions and verdicts only. And if any of the four uses rests on a reading that's straightforwardly false or trivially true, say so plainly enough that I can repeat it out loud in the meeting.
