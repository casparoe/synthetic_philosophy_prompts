I teach a final-year metaphilosophy seminar, and I am writing the take-home final: open-book, but students get only 45 minutes once they open it, so the question has to be answerable in roughly 600–700 words of prose rather than a full essay. Every year I receive the same pair of answers to "does philosophy make progress?" — either a list of questions philosophy has apparently settled (which mostly turns out to be questions that became empirical), or the dignified retreat to the claim that philosophy progresses in understanding, clarity, or the articulation of options even if it never settles anything. I want a question that makes both of those moves cost marks unless they are cashed out, and the lever I want used is a position I handed out last week under a deliberately ugly name, the Export Thesis: philosophy's genuine successes are cases in which a question stops being philosophical — questions about mind migrating into cognitive science, about meaning into formal semantics and linguistics, about space and time into physics — so the discipline's progress consists in the shrinking of its own domain, and the natural measure of progress is shrinkage plus the health of the exported question downstream. The objection I built into the seminar is the Retention Problem: if every success is a case of the question leaving philosophy, then philosophy displays progress only by disowning its successes, and the credit accrues to whatever field inherits the question — which is like a clinic that counts its patients as cured at the moment it discharges them.

So the exam question should force students into that crux. It should also make them do two further things. First, they nearly always take the natural sciences as the benchmark of what progress looks like and infer philosophy's failure from its lack of consensus; I want a sub-question that presses on whether that benchmark is legitimate, given that scientific consensus is partly produced by non-epistemic machinery — shared instruments, funding streams, textbook authority, career incentives — and then asks what follows either way: if those mechanisms are doing much of the work, disagreement in philosophy may not evidence stagnation; if the benchmark is fair after all, what structural change in philosophical practice does the Export Thesis recommend? Second, I want the self-application wrinkle: the dispute about whether philosophy makes progress is itself a long-running, unresolved philosophical dispute, so students should say what the Export Thesis and the Retention Problem jointly imply about that meta-dispute, and whether the thesis can be applied to itself without self-consuming (if the meta-question is exported, say into the sociology or psychology of inquiry, does that count as philosophy's progress or as its dissolution?).

Please write the exam question itself (with clearly marked subparts, sized for 45 minutes), then a model answer of at most 650 words, then a marking scheme out of 20 in a table: five must-hit points, the marks each is worth, and one line on the specific way students typically miss or fake each point. An A answer should, among other things, specify what "progress in the formulation of a question" would have to consist in for it to be possible in principle to show that such progress is absent — no credit for gesturing at clarity, rigour, or sophistication without a test. Keep the entire response under 1,200 words, and do not make the question one that can be answered by naming or summarizing well-known positions.
