Hey — hoping you can help me out with something I have to turn into a committee report by Friday. American English please, and make it usable: I want to be able to lift chunks of it straight into the writeup.

I teach a seminar that meets partly in a persistent virtual world a former student of mine built — its been running about three years. Here's the incident. One group (call them the Masons) spent roughly two years building a hall out of what the world calls attestations: server-enforced single-instance objects. There is exactly one of each, the full chain of custody is logged, and if one is destroyed it's gone — no restore, no duplicate, no reissue. People used that hall constantly: our class met there, two students had a wedding in it, a reading group kept its notes on the walls. Another player found a seige mechanic the code technically permits in that zone — it's meant for territory fights between large groups — and flattened the place in about half an hour, then sold a handful of the attestations he looted through the world's own sanctioned cash-exchange for a four-figure sum.

The committee is split, and both sides are leaning on an analogy instead of arguing the case.

The skeptics: this is Monopoly. Knocking over someone's hotel isn't theft and burning the cardboard isn't arson; you can't be robbed of something that was never really there, and what got damaged was a story people were telling themselves.

The other side: the cash-exchange is the tell. Money in a bank account is also just numbers in a database, and if someone drained a student's account we'd call it theft without blinking. The hall is like a building in a company town — scarce, expensive to make, socially recognized, impossible to replace.

What I need is an audit, not a verdict on the incident. Give me the correspondence table for each analogy. For the bank-account one I especially want to know what plays the role of the bank, the mint, the currency, the state, the exchange rate, insurance, and bankruptcy — and what the "account holder" actually has a claim against. Then the disanalogies, sorted: which ones matter for the question my committee has to answer (does this kind of thing fall under a conduct code written for theft and vandalism) and which are noise.

I also suspect the analogies are quietly answering three different questions at once — whether the objects are there at all, whether they can be owned, and whether losing them can harm someone — and I want those pulled apart, with a note about which analogy is doing real work on which question. And tell me whether it matters, and in which direction, that the code permitted the siege, that the value of the attestations depended on other players still wanting them, and that the student who built the world can switch it off whenever she feels like it.

Finish by saying whether either analogy survives the audit. If both break, hand me a better one I can actually put in the report.
