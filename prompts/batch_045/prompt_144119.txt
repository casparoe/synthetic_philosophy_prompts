I clerk at an intermediate appellate court, and I keep tripping over the line our court draws between questions of law and questions of fact. Findings of fact we review deferentially: a trial judge's account of what happened stands unless it is clearly erroneous, and a jury's verdict stands so long as some reasonable jury could have reached it. Questions of law we decide ourselves, from scratch, with no deference at all. A friend who took philosophy in college told me that this arrangement only makes sense if you believe in the analytic-synthetic distinction, and that Quine argued there is no such thing. I want to know what the arrangement really presupposes.

What puzzles me is that we announce the line as clean and then do not apply it cleanly. We defer on whether the officer saw what he says he saw, but decide for ourselves whether what he saw added up to reasonable suspicion. We defer on what the two parties wrote to each other, then decide for ourselves what the contract means. When a question resists classification we call it mixed and pick a standard case by case, which looks less like applying a test than like choosing an outcome and then a label. So I have three questions.

First, what would a legal system have to believe about meaning or about knowledge for the de novo/deferential split to be more than a division of labor between two tiers of judges? Is the analytic-synthetic distinction, or the a priori/a posteriori one, actually load-bearing here, or is it incidental vocabulary?

Second, suppose Quine is right that no statement is immune to revision and that any statement can be held true come what may, so that there is no principled line between truths of meaning and truths of fact. Would anything in our practice have to change? Or would we simply have to admit that we had been describing an allocation of decision-making authority in borrowed epistemological language, and that the allocation itself survives untouched?

Third, some judges here would like to scrap the distinction and replace it with a single question: which decision-maker is more likely to get this particular question right, once you subtract the cost of deciding it twice. That sounds like it presupposes nothing semantic at all. Is that right, or does it quietly presuppose something just as contested?

In answering, please separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with, and tell me where the current categories are philosophically underdetermined in the sense that several quite different theories of meaning would license exactly the arrangement we have.
