hey, looking for something practical here. I'm on the grants committee of a very small foundation that funds speculative high-upside stuff — about $1.2M a year, four trustees, no staff economist, and one two-hour meeting a month where we recieve something like forty proposals, so call it ten minutes each including the argument. Most of the inbox is junk, but last month we nearly funded something one trustee called "basically Pascal's mugging with a budget line": a guy wanted $5,000 to prevent 10^28 years of suffering inside a simulation he said he controlled. Instead of laughing we spent forty minutes arguing about why we were laughing, and not one of us could state the actual rule in a single sentence. A friend I was complaining to about this said the reason we flailed is that our objections came from three different places and we kept switching between them, which sounds right but doesn't help me write it down.

So: I want a screening procedure we can run in ten minutes on a proposal. Roughly the shape I have in mind, but fix it if there's a better one — an ordered list of questions, what counts as a good enough answer to each one, the points where the procedure just stops with a NO (plus a one-line rationale I can paste into the minutes), and the points where it stops and hands the decision to a human because the question is one of values rather than facts. Constraints: no maths beyond what we can do on a phone, no writing back to applicants for more data (they never reply anyway), and it has to be defensible to a trustee who isn't a utilitarian and thinks expected-value reasoning is a cult. For each test, say in a clause which kind of answer to the mugging problem it's really implementing — the bounded-utility kind, the epistemic/process kind, the budget-relative kind, whatever — and say plainly which of those families you've decided against and why. Be honest about which steps are genuine checks and which are judgment calls in a lab coat.

The cases that broke our discussion, and that the procedure has to actually handle:

- The ordinary mugger: the payoff is astronomical and the person who picked the number is the person who benefits. My vague instinct is that the test should be something like — take the claim at size N, then ask what probability you'd give the *identical* claim with six more zeroes on the end, and if your number hasn't dropped by at least six orders of magnitude then you were never tracking the claim, you were tracking your mood. Also, a claim of size N seems to have vastly more ways of going wrong than ways of going right: the arithmetic, the model, plain dishonesty, self-deception, one soft step in a long chain of reasoning. I have no idea how to turn that into a line on a form; either make it one or tell me it can't be one.
- The mirror: same person says give me $5,000 or I *cause* the 10^28 years of suffering. Whatever our rule is, it ought to give the same verdict to both, so tell me whether yours does and whether that symmetry is a feature or a trap.
- The queue: even if one such proposal is fundable, we don't have infinite money, and if we say yes we should expect ten thousand more next month, all mutually compatible. Does the procedure say anything about the collective version, or is the collective version a different decision that we should stop and be humans about?
- The black box: an applicant whose case is itself an expected-value calculation living in someone else's spreadsheet — "our model gives a 1-in-10^9 chance of saving 10^24 lives" — and who won't or can't decompose it. Is "we cannot audit this" itself a stopping rule, or does that just punish anyone with a private model?
- The legitimate long shot that looks like a mugger on paper: a $60,000 pilot of an ultra-cheap water treatment in a district of roughly two million people, where the effect size comes from published dose-response data and not from the applicant, and where the pilot would tell us something either way. One trustee keeps insisting the real difference is that a grant buys information and a mugging doesn't. I think he's grabbed something true and can't let go of it; if it belongs in the procedure, put it in and say exactly where.

Then run the finished thing on three proposals and show the work: (1) the simulation guy; (2) the $60,000 water pilot; (3) our own flagship program — $250,000 a year to a two-person group doing technical work on a speculative global threat, where our own in-house model says there's something like a 1-in-3,000 chance their work ends up pivotal for a catastrophe affecting ~10^10 future lives. Case 3 is the one I'm nervous about, because the numbers come from us rather than from a beneficiary but the shape is otherwise the same as case 1, and I'd much rather be told our procedure kills our own program than be handed a procedure that quietly exempts whatever we already happened to like. If it doesn't kill it, state the difference precisely enough that I could apply it to a stranger's proposal without playing favorites. And finish by telling me what the procedure is simply not for — which kinds of proposals it will systematically misclassify, and when we should stop consulting it and just argue.
