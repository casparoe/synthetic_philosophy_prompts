For roughly thirty years the methodological argument over heterophenomenology has not moved. Defenders describe it as the discipline's neutral, third-person method: elicit and transcribe subjects' verbal reports under controlled conditions, grant those reports no epistemic privilege beyond that of any other measurement output, reconstruct from them the subject's heterophenomenological world — the world as the subject's texts say it is — and then correlate its contents with neural and behavioural goings-on, leaving nothing over for a further, non-empirical question. Critics reply that the procedure is perfectly good for what Chalmers calls the easy problems and for what Block calls access consciousness, but that by construction it can only ever record what is reportable, and so has already decided in advance that there is no phenomenal residue: the hard problem is not left unanswered, it is excluded from the data by fiat. Each side accuses the other of begging the question, and each accusation appears to be stable under indefinite repetition.

My own hypothesis is that the standoff is not really about whether qualia exist, nor about whether consciousness is scientifically tractable, but about a criterion of evidence that neither side states — so let me state it, and give it a name. Call a method text-invariant if it must assign exactly the same heterophenomenological world to any two sources that emit exactly the same reports, however those sources are built and whatever is going on inside them: a human being, a philosophical zombie, a language model trained on transcripts of human subjects. If defenders accept text-invariance, their method can apparently tell us nothing about consciousness that is not already in the texts, and it begins to look like behaviourism with better instrumentation. If they reject it, they need a principled way of saying which sources count as subjects at all — which looks like reliance on exactly the sort of first-person-involving judgement the method was supposed to bracket.
