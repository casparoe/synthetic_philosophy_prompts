Hi there — I write about science and ideas for a magazine, and once or twice a year I guest-run a session for a friend's MA seminar on the history of science (about 14 people, mostly early-modern historians with two physicists auditing). She's asked me to set one essay question for the take-home final, and I want it on periodization: whether 'the Middle Ages', 'the Renaissance' and 'the Scientific Revolution' name real things in the past or are mostly retrospective inventions that later writers needed for their own reasons. The problem is that every version I sketch is answerable by a student who writes three paragraphs of 'periods are social constructs' and then lists continuities — medieval natural philosophers weren't flat-earthers, astrology and alchemy were still respectable in 1650, Copernicus read medieval commentators, and so on. All true, all worth saying, and none of it tells me whether the student can think. (My editor has now started killing any sentence of mine containting the phrase 'Scientific Revolution', which is its own kind of silliness, and I keep arguing with her about it, so I have some skin in this.)

So I need a question with a crux. Two things I've been playing with, which you should feel free to use, mangle, or replace: first, what I've been calling the swap test — take a competent textbook chapter on the Scientific Revolution and substitute every use of the period label with some other label covering roughly the same decades ('the long sixteenth century', 'the age of the printed book', whatever), and then ask which sentences in the chapter become false. If none do, is the period doing any work at all, or is it just a filing tab? If some do, then something real is being tracked, and the interesting question is what. Second, a three-variable trick: ask the student to track, over the same two centuries, (a) what practitioners cited as authorities, (b) who paid them and what they were paid to do, and (c) what they called themselves and what they thought they were members of. My hunch is that these three curves don't bend at the same place, and that the disagreement about when a period starts is partly a disagreement about which curve you're watching — but I want the question to force them to say what follows from that, not just notice it. I'd also like them to have to deal with the self-undermining bit: 'the Scientific Revolution was invented by later historians' is itself a claim about a period, and if periodizing is always invention, what is that claim doing? And maybe a cleaner test for the whole genus: imagine archivists in 2300 who have only our newspaper front pages and university course catalogues, and ask what eras they'd carve out of 1900-2100 and how we'd know whether they'd got it right.

What I need from you: the question itself, phrased so it works as a 1,200-1,500 word take-home (about 45 minutes if she ever turns it into a sit-down), plus a model answer of roughly 800 words and a marking scheme out of 20 built around five or six must-hit points, with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on how candidates typically get each one wrong. Don't pad the model answer with footnotes, citations, or a further-reading list — the students sit this without library access and there's no point asking for things they can't look up; names and works should appear only where the argument genuinely needs them. The scheme should actually separate an A from a B: I want to know the specific move an A answer makes that a B answer doesn't (and what a C looks like — I think it's the 'everything is a construct, therefore nothing is true' answer, but you tell me). Happy for the model answer to be opinionated and take a line rather than straddle every fence.
