Hey — I host a small philosophy podcast (roughly 40 min an episode, mostly applied ethics and decision theory), and next week's guest is a moral philosopher I'll call Dr. Nadia Ilvescu. She's argueing in a new book that moral trade is one of the most under-used tools we have. Brief version of the view, since I'll have to explain it to listeners: you and I disagree about what's valuable — I think insecticide-treated bednets are the best thing to fund, you think corporate animal-welfare campaigns are. Neither of us thinks the other's cause is the top one, but because of comparative advantage and diminishing returns, we can both end up buying more of what we ourselves care about if I fund your campaign and you fund mine, than if each of us just spent our own money directly.

She's very good at deflecting the objections I already know about. The one I keep hitting on the integrity / dirty-hands side is her stock line: "no one's hands get dirty when the only thing that changes hands is who does what." The thought being that trade merely reallocates who performs an action, it doesn't add to what gets done, so there's nothing to feel bad about.

Could you draft me eight to ten questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give (tell me what you expect her to say)? I want at least one each on:

- the core case for trade: why comparative advantage and diminishing returns generate real gains even when each trader is fully confident about which cause is best, and how that is a seperate argument from the moral-uncertainty / hedging one — she sometimes seems to slide between them and I'd like to pin down which is doing the work;
- whether trading commits her to granting positive weight to values she thinks are mistaken, and whether that quietly pushes toward relativism;
- enforcement: these aren't legal contracts, so what happens in one-shot trades or with strangers, and what stops defection;
- a problem I made up rather than read anywhere, so I'm not sure it's in the literature: if having an expensive or exotic value makes you a more attractive trading partner, doesn't a culture of moral trade reward people for cultivating inconvenient values, or for misrepresenting the ones they have? I'd like the question to land even if it's not a standard objection;
- the analogy with vote trading, which most people find corrupt even when both sides gain;
- trade across time — whether you can trade with future generations who can't reciprocate, or with your own future self when you expect your values to shift.

After that, give me a tightening sequence of three or four questions that make that "nothing gets dirty" line progressively harder to fall back on — starting with a partner whose aims you merely rank second, then one whose aims you take to be worthless, then one whose aims you think are positively harmful, then a case where your side of the trade is what makes their harmful activity possible.

Close with (a) one question about what empirical evidence could show that widespread moral trade makes things worse on net, and (b) one asking whether there's any trade she'd refuse on principle, and what distinguishes it. Please skip anything generic like how she got into the field, and no questions that require her to name papers or authors.
