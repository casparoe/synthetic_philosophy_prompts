i run discussion sections for a second-year social philosophy seminar — 18 students, one 75-minute slot, one room with movable chairs, index cards, no phones, no money for payoffs. Next week they're reading David Lewis on convention: the idea that a convention is one of several arbitrary solutions to a recurring coordination problem, picked out by salience or precedent and held up by everyone's expectations about what everyone else will do, so that each person conforms because the others conform. They've seen Nash equilibrium and Pareto efficiency but nothing on norms, and I don't want to name any of this up front or give a mini-lecture first.

Can you design an activity where they build a convention from scratch and then feel it break? What I have in mind: split the room into two halves that can't communicate, let each half silently converge on one of several arbitrary options (which colour card to hold up, which corner to stand in, whatever), then put them into contact and have them pair off repeatedly for points, so they run into the cost of mismatched conventions and have to work out whose convention wins and why. Two twists I'd like built into the rules rather than explained: (1) secretly hand three students in the smaller half a card instructing them never to change their choice no matter what — I want to see whether a small stubborn minority can drag everyone onto their option, and what the rest of the room does when it can't move them; (2) in a final round, change the payoffs so each person individually does a bit better by deviating, and give everyone some cheap way to penalise a partner who deviated — I want them to notice on their own that what's holding the pattern together has changed character.

Then a debrief that maps concrete moments onto the machinery: arbitrariness, the role of expectations about others' expectations, salience and precedent, the way a coordination equilibrium is self-policing, and the point where the thing stops being a convention in Lewis's sense and becomes something held up by sanctions. Tell me what will predictably go wrong (people talking in the silent phase, the stubborn three outing themselves, someone gaming the points, the smaller group just capitulating in the first round) and how to handle each. Be frank about which feature of the view the activity can't reproduce — my guess is timescale, anonymity, and that real conventions get frozen in by law, but say it properly.

Give me the design as prose, then a table with these columns: phase | minutes | what participants are told and doing | what they should run into | the debrief question that draws it out | what goes wrong and the fix. After that, a list of at most five things the activity can't stage and that I'll have to say out loud myself.
