I want a case study rather than a survey: take the reclassification of Pluto by the International Astronomical Union in 2006 and read it as an episode in the theory of verbal disputes and metaontology.

The facts, which I am stipulating so that nothing here turns on anything you would have to look up: Pluto was classified as a planet from its discovery until 2006; the discovery of Eris and other large trans-Neptunian objects in the early 2000s forced the question of where the category ends; the definition adopted at the Prague general assembly required orbiting the Sun, sufficient mass for a nearly round shape, and having "cleared the neighbourhood" around one's orbit; Pluto meets the first two conditions and not the third; it was assigned to a new intermediate category of dwarf planets alongside Eris and Ceres; the resolution was passed by a show of hands at the end of a fractious assembly, after a rival proposal had failed and much of the specialist membership had already left; a minority of planetary scientists, among them the principal investigator of the New Horizons mission, went on calling Pluto a planet; the public reaction was unusually heated and has never entirely subsided; textbooks, museum signage and school curricula had to be rewritten at real cost.

What I want from you is a mapping that runs in both directions. Specifically:

1. Set the episode up inside the standard apparatus for verbal disputes. Identify precisely which sentence is in dispute, produce the best candidate topic-neutral or subject-matter-level paraphrase on which the parties could be said to agree or disagree, and say whether the conditions the apparatus imposes are met: is there a reading on which the disagreement collapses into a disagreement about language, or does something survive the paraphrase?

2. Run the three familiar metaontological stances over the case and say what each one says the parties were doing: ontological realism (there is a fact of the matter about which kinds are there, and the dispute is about it); the Carnapian treatment (an internal question, easy once a framework is adopted, preceded by a merely practical external choice of framework); and quantifier variance (both answers true under some admissible interpretation, so the disagreement is pragmatic and verbal). For each, note what it predicts about the episode's phenomenology — the heat, the lasting resentment, the sense the public had of having been told something false about the world.

3. Apply a distinction I want tested rather than assumed: between concepts anchored to exemplars (planet was learned, for several generations, partly by ostension to a list of objects that included Pluto) and concepts anchored to a criterion (planet as a body that dominates its orbital zone). My suggestion is that disputes over the first kind of concept are experienced as discovery and disputes over the second as stipulation, and that the Pluto episode is inflammatory because the anchoring shifted mid-dispute. Say whether that distinction does any work the existing apparatus cannot do for it, or whether it is just metalinguistic negotiation redescribed.

4. Name what the theories are silent about. In particular: none of them, as far as I can see, has any account of institutional authority — the IAU had genuine but limited jurisdiction, and it settled by vote a question it was not obviously competent to settle. Say whether this is a gap the episode merely exposes or a counterexample of some kind, and whether the fact that stipulation was costly here (missions, curricula, public trust) is any evidence against the dispute being verbal.

5. Finally, report what the episode does to the theory. Does it vindicate the deflationist by showing an apparently factual disagreement resolving into framework choice? Or does the persistence of the disagreement after the vote — and the fact that the new framework's defenders argued for it on grounds of what the properties support, not convenience — show that even "external" questions are constrained by something more than pragmatics? If you think the episode forces a third category besides verbal and substantive, say what it is and what it would cost the theory to admit it.

Please be disciplined about the mapping and explicit where a framework has nothing to say; I would rather hear that the theory is silent than see it stretched to cover the case. Argumentative prose, no citations or reading list.
