I've been handed the "do we use the word" memo at my NGO for six months while my colleague is on leave — she's the one who's written every version of it for years, and whenever some new atrocity blows up, comms ask her whether we call it genocide or something blander. I've got her old memos and I'm trying to reverse-engineer what she's actually running, because a new one landed on my desk yesterday and I genuinely can't tell which way she'd go. Her record (legal verdict first, then whether she'd say it in our public comms):

- Herero and Nama in German South West Africa, 1904–08: yes, and yes publicly, even though there's nobody left to charge.
- Armenia 1915: yes.
- Holodomor: no — her line was that starving peasants as a class isn't destroying a protected group as such, and that the convention's exclusion of political and economic groups is a defect in the convention, not a reason to stretch it.
- Cambodia under the Khmer Rouge: yes for the Cham, no for everyone else, including the "new people" killed as suspected class enemies.
- Bosnia: yes at Srebrenica, no for Prijedor, no for the expulsion campaign generally — she wrote somewhere that if you let people survive somewhere else you haven't tried to destroy them.
- Forced removal of Indigenous children in Canada: cultural genocide, and no on genocide, with a foot-stamp that this is exactly where the legal and the moral verdict come apart.
- Biafra: no. Indonesia 1965–66: no (political group again).
- Darfur: yes, loudly, even though the UN commission of inquiry that looked into it stopped short of finding genocidal intent.
- Yazidis: yes. Rohingya: yes.

The one on my desk: the state of Kavaria has spent about seven years on what it calls a deradicalisation and integration programme in its southern province of Leth, aimed at the Surni, an ethnoreligious minority of roughly 1.8 million, about a quarter of whom live in Leth, which is also where most of their shrines, endowments and religious schools are. Every Surni religious institution has been closed or demolished; the language is banned in schools and courts; public ritual is criminalised; around 40,000 children have been removed to boarding schools in the north and placed with majority families, raised in the majority religion and language (which, as far as I can tell, is literally one of the acts enumerated in Article II, which is what makes me doubt she'd just say no). Roughly 3,000 people are dead, mostly in resettlement camps and in riots where the police stood by; no death lists have turned up, no extermination campaign, and the government warns before it bulldozes. There is a real Surni separatist insurgency in Leth that killed several hundred civilians before any of this started, and the state insists the Surni aren't a group at all but a heretical sect of the majority religion — so whether they're a protected group seems to depend on whether you take the perpetrator's classification or theirs. There's one leaked interior ministry document about the Surni question being solved within a generation.

So: would she call it genocide? I was arguing about this with my flatmate last night, who thinks the whole exercise is just about triggering the convention's prevention duty and nothing else — but that makes Darfur easy and doesn't explain why she said yes on the Herero or no on Indonesia. As I see it there are at least three rules in play: a strict convention one where "destroy" means physical or biological destruction and political groups are out; something closer to what Lemkin seems to have meant, where wrecking a group's institutions, language and children is the central case rather than a marginal one; and a straight political one where the label goes on when it does work and comes off when it doesn't. What I'd love from you: which of those (or what fourth thing) actually fits all of her old memos, since I don't think any of them fits cleanly; what each surviving rule predicts about Kavaria, separating the legal verdict from the moral one from the comms decision, and dealing properly with the "in part" question (a quarter of the group in one province, but the province that holds most of their institutions) and with the fact that the deaths are mostly from neglect rather than killing; whether the residential-schools memo is really the closest analogue or whether that's a trap, given she said no there; and if the rules do come apart on this case, what further case I'd need to see to know which one she's really running. I'll write the memo either way, I just want to know which principle I'd be commiting us to.
