I have no training in philosophy — I work in freight logistics — but I have been turning over a family crisis for a year now, and I would be grateful for a careful answer rather than a reassuring one.

My older brother was a violent teenager: fights, arrests, one serious assault. At about twenty-two he set about changing himself, and he did it methodically over roughly eight years — a court-ordered anger programme at first, then a therapist he kept seeing after the order expired, then a journal, then a deliberate change of the people he spent time with, then a job working with children because he thought it would force patience on him. It worked. For years he was the calmest person in our family. Last spring, under a combination of a job loss and a custody dispute, he hit a man in a car park, and the man is now permanently disabled. My brother says, of the man he was that night, that it was not him.

My father's view, which I half share, is that the years of work are exactly what condemns him. He reasons as follows: nobody made your brother do that work, he chose it, he has shown by doing it that he can steer his own character, and so he is precisely the person who cannot plead that his temper took over. A friend of mine says the opposite, and I find it colder but harder to answer. She says that every link in that chain was something that happened to him — the programme was ordered by a court, the therapist was recommended to him, the girlfriend who pushed him into it was someone he happened to meet, and the capacity to stick to a routine is a fact about his brain and his upbringing — so that to own any one step he would have to have owned the step before it, and the one before that, without end. On her account the eight years do not make him more responsible; they just add links to a chain that never begins anywhere.

Two things I have noticed while thinking about this, and I want you to deal with both directly.

First, I do not think the argument is really about determinism. It does not help my brother, or hurt him, if some part of what he did that night was uncaused in the way physics sometimes allows: a stray event in his nervous system would not be his achievement either. So please do not settle this by settling whether his action was determined.

Second, I have tried an answer of my own and cannot tell whether it is a real answer or a trick of arithmetic. Call it the compounding reply. Grant that he could not have made himself from nothing: still, perhaps he did not need to. Perhaps each step only had to be a small one — a nudge within the reach of the person he then was — and the accumulated effect of hundreds of such nudges over eight years is a self that is substantially his own work, the way interest compounds into a sum nobody ever deposited. Does this reply escape the regress, or does it merely repeat the same failure many times over? And if credit for a character can be a matter of degree, what follows about blame: is my brother, who spent eight years becoming gentle, more to blame for that one night than a man who never tried and did the same thing? That conclusion feels wrong to me, and I want to know whether the wrongness is a fault in my reply or just sentimentality getting in the way.

One more wrinkle, if it matters. I recently learned that the whole project began with something he did not choose and did not want: his girlfriend at the time told him she would leave if he did not get help, and he has said he experienced that as something that happened to him rather than something he decided. Does it make a difference whether the motive that set the chain going was itself his own? I cannot tell whether this is the deepest version of the problem or an irrelevance.

Finally: I am not asking what should happen to him legally, and please do not point me to books or articles, since I have no way to get them. What I want to know is whether the resentment the rest of us feel, and my brother's own guilt, are responses to something real — or whether we are all engaged in something we could not stop doing even if we understood that it is never justified.
