My policy workshop has two people who've been going at it for a year over refugees and asylum, and I'm the one who has to make them produce a joint document instead of another round of position papers. Camp A thinks a state's duties to outsiders are basically remedial and bounded: obligations kick in from that state's own causal role in the displacement, plus a fair-share duty of rescue that gets capped by what it can absorb without failing its own people. Camp B thinks the claims of someone fleeing serious harm don't get weaker because of where they were born, so membership is at best an administrative fact about who can be charged with helping, not a moral one.

Give me the document they should sign. First a numbered list of claims both can actually accept — substantive ones, not "everyone has dignity." I want them to say something real about whether the refugee/economic-migrant line tracks anything they both care about, and I want them to deal with a case neither of them has a clean answer to: people whose government or economy is slowly dismantling the conditions of a livable life — creeping land dispossession, aquifer collapse, wether or not you want to call it climate — so that nothing pinpointable has happened to them yet, but they obviously can't stay. Call them foreclosure claimants if you need a label. Then a table of the remaining disagreements, each row worded so the side holding it would sign it as stated. One row should be about the thing I think is actually the fight: whether you can settle who counts as a refugee first and then argue separately about who has to take them, or whether those are the same question asked twice. Then the cruxes, and for each one the case, argument, or pattern of evidence both would accept as bearing on it, with each camp's prediction written down. Make one crux about duty transfer — whether a state discharges its share by paying for resettlement somewhere else rather than admitting anyone. And if you think some slice of their dispute is verbal, say which slice and why.
