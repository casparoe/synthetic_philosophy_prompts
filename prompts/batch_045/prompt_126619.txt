I'm putting together the final for a small advanced seminar on anthropic reasoning and the epistemology of self-locating belief — MA and MPhil students, closed-book, three hours, no readings or notes permitted, so nothing in the exam should turn on having read or being able to cite the literature. The last third of the course has been on the self-sampling assumption versus the self-indication assumption and on the Presumptuous Philosopher case from Bostrom's *Anthropic Bias* (2002): two cosmological theories, T1 and T2, both compatible with everything we can observe locally, where we have been using 10^12 observers for T1 and 10^24 for T2, and where the SIA reasoner concludes in advance of any experiment that T2 is essentially certain, on the grounds that there were vastly more slots for her to have occupied. Every student can state that argument and every student can state the objection to it; almost none of them can say what turns on it, because they have learned to treat SSA and SIA as rival answers to a single question rather than as one component of a package whose other component is an aggregation rule. A colleague in the economics department told me over coffee that the whole dispute is just about whether you put the N in the prior or in the social welfare function, which I took for rhetoric at the time but have since come to think is nearly right — and I want the exam to force students to make that precise rather than sloganize it.

Here is the result from my own notes that I want the question built around, stated with the assumptions I think it needs: fix a reference class and a partition of hypotheses T_i with priors p_i, where T_i says that the reference class contains N_i observers; let the agent's only indexical evidence be that she exists, with no further self-locating specification. Then SSA leaves the priors alone (P(T_i) = p_i), while SIA weights them by observer number (P(T_i) = p_i·N_i / W, where W is the sum of p_j·N_j). Now let an available action A generate total welfare U_i under T_i. If the agent maximizes expected *total* welfare using SSA credences, she ranks actions by the sum of p_i·U_i. If instead she maximizes expected *average* welfare using SIA credences, she ranks them by that same sum divided by W. Since W does not depend on which action is under evaluation, the two packages — SIA plus average-welfare aggregation, and SSA plus total-welfare aggregation — induce exactly the same ranking of actions, and the disagreement between SSA and SIA looks, for purposes of action, like pure bookkeeping. I want students to derive this (it is three lines), and then to be pressed on what it does and does not establish.

I also want the second half of the question to break it, and here is the case I have been using in section, which I would like you to keep, numbers and all. T1 contains 10^6 observers each at welfare 100; T2 contains 10^12 observers each at welfare 100; priors are equal; and a proposed policy would, if and only if T1 is actual, bring into existence a further 10^12 observers each at welfare 1 — lives worth living, but barely, on the same scale. Because the policy changes how many observers there are under T1, the observer totals are now action-dependent, the constant no longer cancels across actions, and the two packages come apart: the SSA-plus-total package accepts the policy, which adds roughly 5×10^11 expected welfare units, while the SIA-plus-average package rejects it, since creating the extra observers both raises SIA's credence in T1 toward one half and drags T1's average welfare down from about 100 to about 1, so that expected average welfare falls from about 100 to about 50. The explanation I am looking for is that once observer numbers depend on what you do, SIA's credence in a hypothesis is no longer independent of your action, and the N that the two packages were quietly passing back and forth between the prior and the welfare function is now doing work in two places at once.

What I would like from you is one exam question — a single multi-part essay or problem question, not a set of short-answer items — that does all of the above, together with a model answer of roughly 1,000 to 1,200 words and a 25-mark marking scheme broken into named must-hit components, with the marks attached to each and a one-line note on the mistake that should cost marks at that point. Please state SSA and SIA operationally inside the question itself, since I want a student who has memorized the dialectic but cannot do the bookkeeping from the definitions to score worse than one who can do the bookkeeping and has read nothing: no part of a top answer should consist in recalling what someone said. The scheme should separate an A from a B by naming the specific moves each must contain, and should say which single omission caps a paper at a B regardless of how good the rest is. The question should also end somewhere genuinely hard: after the flip, ask whether the residual SSA/SIA disagreement is merely verbal or merely pragmatic given the equivalence in the fixed-N case, and what is left of it once action is held constant — what one should assert about the size of the universe, what happens when the agent is not an expected-utility maximizer over a welfare aggregate, whether the equivalence survives the move to observer-moments in the style of the strong self-sampling assumption or a reference class restricted to observers subjectively indistinguishable from the agent, and whether the flip case shows that SIA carries an objectionable incentive structure of its own, a dilution pressure against adding observers that the SSA-plus-total package does not have. Finally, if you think my equivalence needs a qualification I have not stated — about the priors, about actions that change which observers exist rather than how many, about risk-aversion or a nonlinear welfare aggregation, or anything else — please build that qualification into the model answer and, ideally, into the marking scheme as an extra-credit point: I would much rather reward a student who notices a caveat I missed than mark down one who reproduces my oversight.
