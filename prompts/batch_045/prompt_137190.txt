I'm drafting a novel set in a city built out over a tidal salt flat — the whole population lives on a lattice of raised walkways and low causeways, the tide comes in fast enough to kill you, and the place is fanatically rule-bound: you clear the low causeways twenty minutes before the turn, you never say a dead person's name out loud, nobody makes a sound during the midday quiet hour, and strangers are fined for standing on the wrong side of a stairwell. I want the strictness to be load-bearing rather than set dressing: my protagonist says a dead woman's name in a market in chapter four and the entire social order visibly wobbles, and I need to understand, in a non-handwavy way, what the rule-following is *for* and how much of it a society like this should actually demand. A friend of mine who works in comparative psychology walked me into an argument I can't climb out of, and it's ruining the book:

(1) A norm is worth having only if it changes what people do — a rule that made no difference to anyone's behaviour would be pure decoration.
(2) The difference a norm makes is the gap between how people behave with the norm in place and how they would behave without it.
(3) Tightness just is the degree to which people in a society actually conform, with deviance rare and punished; a perfectly tight society is one where conformity is total.
(4) But if conformity is total, the gap in (2) is zero — there is no deviant behaviour left for the norm to remove, so the norm makes no difference to anything.
(5) So the more successful tightness is, the less work its norms do; at the limit, the norms of a perfectly tight society are idle, and idle norms are exactly what a loose society has, since a loose society is one where people mostly do as they like and the rules don't bite.
(6) A society whose rules are idle has no reason to spend anything on enforcement, surveillance, shaming, or drumming the rules into children — all of that is pure cost with nothing buying it.
(7) Therefore tightness, taken seriously, is a self-defeating ideal, and the only coherent target for any society is maximum looseness, with enforcement kept at zero outside whatever narrow band where behaviour genuinely fails to coordinate on its own.

My friend says the argument is valid and I just have to pick a premise to drop. I think the rot is around (5) — that's the step that feels like sleight of hand — but I can't name the trick, and I'd rather keep (1) and (2) than give up (7), since those two are the bits I actually believe and the ones I've been using to justify the tide rule. Two things I've been using as intuition pumps, in case there useful: I invented a diagnostic for my city called the *overnight rate*, meaning the share of rule-following behaviour that survives the first week after every sanction is quietly suspended and everyone knows it has been — and I keep feeling that a society with near-total compliance but an overnight rate near zero is a very different animal from one with near-total compliance and an overnight rate near one hundred percent, but I can't work out which of the two my friend's argument is even describing. Second, my city has almost no scarcity, no external enemies and tons of space, which by the usual ecological story (density, scarcity, coordinated threat) ought to make it loose, and yet it obviously isn't — I don't think that's a contradiction, but I can't say why.

So: which step do you reject, why does that step look fine right up until you stare at it, and what does rejecting it cost me? Please price the alternatives too — if I drop something else instead, what do I end up committed to? And concretely: does whatever I give up force me to rewrite my city's strictness as arbitrary, or as mere crowd psychology, or can it stay as something a reasonable person might defend? If the answer turns on whether makes a difference means right now or over the whole history of how the norm got there, say so, because I suspect that's where I'm stuck.
