I run a small decision-theory reading group and we've now burned three sessions on the absent-minded driver with nobody moving an inch. Setup, so you don't have to dredge it up: two identical-looking intersections, the driver can't tell which one he's at, 0 for getting off at the first, 4 at the second, 1 for driving past both. Everyone agrees on all the arithmetic — that planning from the start you want to continue with probability 2/3 (payoff 4/3), and that once you're actually standing at an intersection with that plan in place you'd rather continue with a much lower probability (we get 1/3), and that with that plan you'd want to change again. Nobody disputes any of it. The fight is only about what it shows, and it's hardened into camps that talk past each other: one says the planning standpoint is the normative one and the intersection calculation is a prediction about what an uncommitted agent will do, not advice; another says the driver is there now, and 'what should I do' has to be answered from where he is, so the ex ante plan is just bad advice; a third says the whole thing is an artifact of representing forgetting as a single information set and would evaporate if imperfect recall were modelled properly; a fourth wants to treat his time-slices as players in a game and look for an equilibrium among them.

My hypothesis, offered as one, is that this is two different questions under one name — what plan to adopt versus what to do given that you're here — and that once they're separated there's nothing left to fight about. Two people say that's too quick, because he performs exactly one action at that intersection and both answers claim to tell him what it should be.

We tried to break the tie with a variant and it backfired: same driver, but his memory flickers, so at each intersection with probability ε he knows exactly where he is and with probability 1−ε he's in the dark as usual, unable to tell whether his memory has failed. ε=1 gives no paradox, ε=0 gives the original. What we couldn't settle is whether the gap between the planning answer and the intersection answer shrinks continuously as ε rises, or vanishes at any ε>0, or doesn't move at all at the dark node because the belief there is the same either way — every camp derived the result it liked and went home.

So diagnose the thing rather than survey it. Verbal, genuinely normative, a modelling dispute, several questions at once? Ground it in what the camps actually claim. And given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress — and say so plainly if the honest answer is that nothing will.
