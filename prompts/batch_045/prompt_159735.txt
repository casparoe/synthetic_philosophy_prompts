I write about science and ideas for a magazine, and I am drafting a long piece — with a sidebar explaining the puzzle for readers who have not read the book — on whether a system that only follows rules can ever get outside its own rules. Much of my argument leans on Hofstadter's MU puzzle from Gödel, Escher, Bach, which I want to use as a miniature of Gödel's first incompleteness theorem: you start from MI, grind away with the four rules (append a U to any string ending in I, double everything after the M, replace III by U, delete any UU), get nowhere, and then leap to a higher level, notice that the number of I is never divisible by three, and thereby reach a truth the system itself cannot reach. My thesis sentence is that this shows any machine confined to formal rule-following will be blind to truths its own rules entail, and that intelligence therefore requires the capacity to escape the frame.

Two things I would like checked before I file, since I am not a logician and would rather not print a howler. First, a philosopher friend says the move does not generalize in the way I need it to, and that the repair I had in mind — adjoining, as a fifth rule, the statement that MU is not a theorem, so that the enlarged system can see the truth about itself — rests on a category mistake she could not get me to see. Second, I want an empirical hook for the piece: I have been handing the four rules to chatbots and asking whether MU is derivable, and I am scoring a no accompanied by the divisibility argument as evidence that the system has jumped out of the system rather than merely searching longer inside it. Is that a fair reading of what the model is doing?

Where exactly does my framing go wrong, and what is the puzzle actually a case of? Please keep your reply under 400 words: tell me plainly which of my claims you would cut, and why.
