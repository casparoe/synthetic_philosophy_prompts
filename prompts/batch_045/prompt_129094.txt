I produce a scripted interview series for a university programme in the history of ideas: each episode puts a historical figure in the chair and questions them only from positions they actually published, without appeal to later developments. The next episode is set in the last years of Charles Darwin's life — after the Descent of Man and after the recollections he wrote for his family — and is confined to his views on the moral sense and on religion. My producer wants exactly five question-and-follow-up pairs: no biographical or scene-setting questions, each pair pressing on a real difficulty internal to Darwin's own account, and each follow-up naming the reply he is most likely to make and then closing that reply off.

Between them, the five should cover the following ground.

One: his claim that any animal endowed with well-marked social instincts would inevitably acquire a conscience once its intellectual powers approached ours, together with his own hedge that such an animal would not end up with exactly our moral sense — the case he imagines being humans reared under the conditions of hive-bees, who would count it a sacred duty to kill their brothers. Ask whether the account is meant to explain only why we have a conscience at all or also why it says the particular things it says, and what finding would count against it; then block his likely move of citing the hive-bee case as showing he never promised our exact code.

Two: his argument that tribes containing many members who are patriotic, faithful, obedient, courageous and sympathetic would prevail over other tribes, and whether selection of that kind can yield more than altruism within the group and deference to local opinion — hence whether the widening of sympathy to strangers, to distant future people and to animals is explained by his account or imported into it from somewhere else.

Three: a dilemma put to me last month by a moral philosopher visiting the department — either the pressures that shaped conscience were tracking reproductive fitness, in which case there is no reason to suppose its deliverances track any independent moral fact, or they were tracking independent moral facts, in which case some explanation is owed of why fitness and moral truth line up. Ask which horn he takes; then, when he answers that he never claimed conscience tracks independent moral facts and that sentiment is enough, press him on why his condemnations of slavery are written as claims about what human beings are owed.

Four: the asymmetry between his treatment of religion and his treatment of morality. In the Descent he derives belief in gods and spiritual agencies from ordinary mental tendencies, and in his recollections he reports that the design argument which once seemed to him conclusive fails once natural selection is in view, while also reporting that the impossibility of conceiving the universe as blind chance once compelled him toward a First Cause with a mind in some degree analogous to our own, and that he could then be called a theist even though elsewhere in the same recollections he says he must remain content as an agnostic. Ask why a genealogy should be deflating for religion but not for morality, or deflating for morality but not for religion; then block the reply that the origin of a belief is one thing and its truth another.

Five: his claim that we could not check our sympathy even at the urging of hard reason without deterioration in the noblest part of our nature, set against his view that aiding the weak and helpless is injurious to the race and must simply be borne. Ask what authority conscience retains on his account if, in the very case where it speaks most insistently, hard reason tells him its promptings are maladaptive; then block his likely comparison of us to a surgeon who must harden himself in order to perform an operation.

Deliver exactly five numbered items, each consisting of one question and one follow-up — two sentences in all — with no introduction and no conclusion.
