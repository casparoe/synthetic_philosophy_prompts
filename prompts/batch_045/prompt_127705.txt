Hi. I run a small book club — six of us, none of us academic, we've been going about four years — and we've just finished Ian McEwan's Atonement. Nobody liked liking it, if that makes sense, and we spent most of the evening arguing about why we go looking for books that make us miserable. Someone said philosophers have a name for this, the paradox of tragedy, and I've been reading around it on my own since, so I've got a rough grip on the main moves: that the sadness somehow gets converted into pleasure by the beauty of the writing, that maybe we don't really feel sadness towards things we know are made up so there's nothing to explain, and that what we actually enjoy is our own response rather than the events. I don't want references or a reading list, I just want to think it through properly, and I'd like the answer in British English please.

Two things I really want the reading to deal with.

First, my own hobby horse. It seems to me Atonement stacks two sadnesses on top of each other, and I think the paradox people only ever talk about one of them. There's the sadness of what happens to Robbie and Cecilia. Then there's the second one, when you reach the end and find out that the happy reunion you read earlier never happened and Briony invented it. That second one isn't sadness about the characters, it's sadness about having been told a lie by the narrator. And here's the bit I can't stop thinking about: I don't want the lie taken back. If you handed me a version where Robbie and Cecilia genuinely got the cottage, I'd think it was a worse book. So has the paradox got anything to say about sadness aimed at the storytelling rather than at the story? And is wanting to keep the lie a different puzzle from wanting to keep the tragedy, or the same one wearing a disguise?

Second, a fight in the group. Half of us say we didn't enjoy Atonement at all — we're just glad we read it. One member thinks the whole paradox is a word game: once you separate enjoying something from valuing it or being glad you went through it, the problem evaporates. The rest of us think that's dodging, because you still have to explain why we choose to spend our evenings with something that makes us feel wretched when we could watch something nice. Whose side should I be on? Is splitting enjoyment from appreciation a real answer or a change of subject — and if it is a real answer, what does it predict about the person who says they hated it and are glad they read it?

I'd also like you to be honest about where the book resists the framing. Our group mostly isn't moved to tears by Atonement; it's a cold, quiet book and the ending lands more like a chill than a catharsis. Does the philosophy have room for that, or is it all built around big aristotelian weeping at Greek plays and war films? And can you point me at two or three scenes to bring to the next meeting — I was going to go with the fountain, Dunkirk beach, and the last section, but tell me if I'm wrong — and say what each one actually shows, rather than just using it to illustrate the theory?

If any of the theories only work for Greek tragedy or for something like Schindler's List and fall apart on a literary novel with a guilty narrator, I'd definately rather know that upfront.
