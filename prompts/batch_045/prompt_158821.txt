For the satire page of my school newspaper I'm assembling a special issue on ideas that get defended seriously in philosophy and that would be horrifying if an institution actually implemented them, and my own piece is on illusionism about phenomenal consciousness. I mean the view that there are no phenomenal properties in the demanding sense — nothing intrinsic, ineffable, or privately revealed to introspection — that the apparent givenness of what-it's-like is a kind of introspective illusion produced by systems that misrepresent their own states, and that the right response is to drop the vocabulary or keep it only in a deliberately deflated, revised sense. Every time I bring it up in our philosophy club, the conversation ends with the same reassurance: nothing is lost, pain still gets treated, this is metaphysical tidying with no consequences at the bedside. I want to test that reassurance by having someone act on it. So please write me a mock internal memorandum of about 700 words: dated, with a header, issued by the Office of Clinical Standards of a hospital trust to its ward staff, announcing that the trust has adopted illusionism as its official ontology of consciousness and is rolling out a revised protocol for the assessment and management of pain and suffering.

Set it out as numbered clauses, with a short staff FAQ and a specimen line from the new surgical consent form appended. The joke should come from the real commitments rather than invented ones: a policy of dual bookkeeping in which phenomenal idiom is retained for bedside reassurance while the chart records only functional-neurocomputational states; a clause on quasi-phenomenal self-reports, filed like any other unreliable measurement rather than treated as authoritative; a Müller–Lyer clause explaining to nurses that the lines do not bend and the pain does not hurt; and a standing insistence that nothing of practical importance has changed. Have the memo rule, deadpan and without breaking character, on three cases: a post-operative patient who was accidentally under-anaesthetised, lay perfectly still throughout, and later reports having been in agony; a patient with congenital insensitivity to pain who reports terrible pain with no corresponding functional state anyone can find; and a comatose patient whose family insist that he looks like he is suffering and demand morphine. The horror should arrive through those rulings and through the vocabulary, not through any character winking at the reader or announcing that the hard problem is still unsolved — no one in the memo may admit that consciousness might be real, and no real philosopher's name should appear anywhere in it. It should be funny to someone who has read about illusionism and still legible to my classmates who haven't.

At the end, outside the memo, add a straight note of 120–150 words on which version of illusionism you chose to extend — the stronger eliminative-sounding one or the weaker revisionary one that keeps talking about quasi-phenomenal properties — and on where the memo exaggerates rather than follows: which ruling a real illusionist would disown, and which one they would have to accept if they are being consistent. Please commit to one reading and defend it rather than listing the options.
