I'm a backend engineer and a few of us at work run a very informal philosophy for engineers reading group — no philosophy background between us, so please don't assume any jargon sticks. We recently got onto accuracy-first epistemology: the view that the only distinctively epistemic good is having credences close to the truth, where closeness is measured by something like a Brier score (squared distance between your credence and the 0/1 truth value), and that you can then derive probabilism and updating-by-conditionalization from accuracy-dominance and expected-accuracy arguments instead of just assuming them. We got stuck on priors: the accuracy-first line seems to say any coherent prior is fine, so it can't say that one person's starting point is worse than another's, only that they'd better update consistently from wherever they started. So on the whiteboard I proposed a tweak, call it the guided Brier score: score = (1 − λ) × squared distance from your credence to the truth + λ × squared distance from your credence to the credence your total evidence supports, with λ somewhere between 0 and 1. In ML terms it's like a loss that's part true label, part teacher network, where the teacher can be wrong.

Maya liked it. She said it handles the case of someone whose evidence is thoroughly misleading: they end up inaccurate, but they did exactly what their evidence called for, and she wants to say they're epistemically fine, whereas the plain Brier score has to call them defective. Theo hated it, for a few reasons I only half followed: (1) the rule isn't truth-directed any more — at λ > 0 there are cases where the best-scoring credence is further from the truth than an available alternative, which he says perverts the whole point; (2) it makes the same credence function score differently in two worlds where the proposition has the same truth value, so accuracy isn't really doing the work; (3) under higher-order evidence — you learn your evidence-gathering was unreliable, or a trusted peer disagrees — the credence your evidence supports has no clean answer, so the λ term goes undefined exactly where epistemology gets interesting. Maya's replies, as best I can reconstruct them: to (2), that accuracy-first already concedes a lot by grading credences against worlds rather than against the agent's situation; to (3), that this is a hard case for everyone and she'd rather have a rule with a hard case than a rule that's silent about priors.
