Every popular discussion of commissurotomy I come across uses the same picture: the two hemispheres are two people, the corpus callosum is the telephone line between them, cutting it is cutting the line, and after surgery there are two subjects in one skull who can no longer talk to each other. The laboratory arrangement of flashing stimuli to one visual field at a time is then described as simply interrogating each of them separately. A philosophy podcast I like leaned on this to make a further claim: since the unity of a subject turns out to be a contingent fact about wiring, and since it is arguably arbitrary whether we count one subject or two, nothing much turns on the answer — nothing epistemic, nothing moral, nothing about who is responsible for what the body does.

I want an audit of that picture rather than a verdict on the host.

1. Set out the mapping explicitly, a small table is fine: hemisphere to person, corpus callosum to line, and then the harder ones — what plays the role of the shared body, of the experimenter's questions, of the left hemisphere's habit of inventing explanations for things the other hemisphere initiated, and of the fact that the two can still pass information to each other through the world by moving the hands and eyes they share.
2. Say which disanalogies matter for the conclusion the host wanted and which are harmless. The ones I can see: the hemispheres share a brainstem, one body, one set of effectors and one stream of sensory feedback from the world; they are not rivals for resources in the way two people are; and, unless I have this wrong, people born without a corpus callosum do not seem to go through life as doubled subjects.
3. The worry I actually care about: the telephone picture begins with two people and a line, so it may be unable even to represent the option some philosophers defend — one subject whose conscious life has come apart into two streams, as opposed to two subjects each with a stream. If so the analogy is not evidence for the two-subjects reading; it is that reading in costume. Do you agree, and how should someone who wants to keep the analogy respond?
4. My candidate replacement: one newspaper, two editors, no shared copy desk, one printing press and one readership, where the editor who writes the front page also writes the editorials explaining things the other editor commissioned. Is that better? Where does it distort?
