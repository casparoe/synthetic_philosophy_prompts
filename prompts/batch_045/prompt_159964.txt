Before I circulate chapter three to the reading group I would be grateful for an audit of the position I commit myself to in it. I have been living with this package long enough that I can no longer tell which parts are load-bearing, and I would rather find out now than in the seminar. I am not asking for references; I need the argumentative structure. Here is the view as I currently hold it.

(a) The thesis is semantic rather than first-order. Moral sentences express propositions whose truth is relative to a moral framework; relativism itself is not a moral claim. In ordinary first-order deliberation moral language keeps its unrelativized surface — speakers do not qualify their judgments with "relative to framework F," and I do not think they should have to.

(b) Whose framework is the relevant one: I have settled on the assessor's. The truth of an assessment of a moral judgment depends on the framework from which the assessment is made.

(c) Frameworks are individual but idealized. A framework is the set of normative commitments a person would retain under full information and unimpeded reflection, not the messy set they would avow on a Tuesday. I build in the idealization for two reasons: actual codes are frequently incoherent, and I want to leave room for a person to be mistaken about what their own framework commits them to, and for a framework to be faulted as internally incoherent on its own terms.

(d) Case verdict, the insider. The case I build the chapter around is a valley community that allocates irrigation water strictly by the seniority of the household head, so that in drought years the junior households lose their fields while senior households keep watering ornamental plots. A young household head within the community, arguing in the assembly, condemns this as unjust. I want to say her condemnation is true, and not merely true-for-her: it has critical force against the practice and against those who defend it.

(e) Case verdict, the outsider. An engineer from outside the valley, hired to redesign the channels, privately reaches the same condemnation. I want to say something weaker about him: his judgment is true relative to his framework, but it gives him no standing to press for reform and no reason to intervene in the allocation rule. The correct posture for him is toleration.

(f) The main argument for the view is the variation in moral codes across communities, and I want to add that the variation is not superficial: it is fundamental disagreement that survives ideal reflection, not the sort of thing that better information would dissolve.

(g) Two upshots I am attached to. First, cross-framework moral disagreement is genuine — there is something to argue about, not two parties talking past one another. Second, moral change can be genuine improvement: the abandonment of a practice can be progress rather than a change of fashion.

I have three candidate worries and cannot tell which are real. One: that (a) and the first half of (g) collide, since if each party's judgment is true relative to its own framework there seems to be nothing left to dispute. Two: that (c) quietly undermines (f), since idealizing frameworks may collapse the very variation the argument from variation needs. Three: that (e) is simply my own first-order morality wearing a relativist costume.

What I need is an audit rather than a summary. Tell me which of my candidate worries are genuine and which dissolve on inspection and why; identify the tensions I have not listed; rank all of them by severity; and for each, say exactly which commitment would have to give and what that costs me elsewhere in the set — in particular whether I can keep the asymmetry between (d) and (e) or whether the assessor-relativity in (b) forces me to drop it. Finally, if there is a single revision that restores consistency more cheaply than the alternatives, say what it is and what I would no longer be able to claim in the chapter. The one thing I am unwilling to do is retreat to a non-cognitivist version of the view; apart from that I have no fixed attachments and would rather hear which item is weakest than have my existing arrangement flattered.
