I work in insurance claims and have no formal background in any of this, but I came across Malthus in a popular book about the Industrial Revolution, got interested in why everyone treats him as obviously refuted and yet keeps dragging him back in, and joined a small reading group to talk it through. One member — a software engineer who reads a lot of economics blogs — built the following argument, which I have not been able to answer and whose conclusion I also cannot accept. His target is the familiar move of saying Malthus was right but early. (1) Malthus's substantive claim is that, because population can grow faster than the food supply, any sustained improvement in ordinary people's living standards is converted into more people rather than into higher incomes, so humanity is held near subsistence, with the holding done by hunger, disease and war, or else by restraint. (2) What actually happened over the two centuries after he wrote is the reverse in much of the world: food production outran population, incomes rose by multiples, and then families began choosing to have fewer children, first in rich countries, without famine or plague doing the checking. (3) The rescue says: right but early. The mechanism is real; what delayed it is a historical accident — coal and oil, which are stored sunlight, plus fertiliser and the Green Revolution, all of which are one-off inheritances rather than a permanent escape — and the voluntary fall in fertility is not a refutation of Malthus but his own preventive check finally working once children stopped being useful on farms. On a finite planet, anything that compounds meets its limit eventually; the reckoning is postponed, not cancelled. (4) But if that is now all the theory says — that a quantity growing at a positive rate inside finite bounds must eventually meet those bounds — then it is no longer a theory of population at all; it is arithmetic, and it is equally true of bacteria in a jar and of the number of books on my shelves. (5) And if that rescues Malthus, it rescues every long-run prediction with the same shape: that London would vanish under horse manure, that the world would run out of whale oil, that we would run out of oil by some date or other, that the sun will burn out. Each of those comes true given enough time, so none of them can fail. (6) A claim that is consistent with every finite run of contrary evidence and guaranteed to be true at some unspecified future date tells you nothing about what to expect next decade, next century, or under this policy rather than that one; and a claim that could not have been wrong is not the kind of thing that can be right, early or otherwise. (7) So right but early is either false or empty: it means either the substantive nineteenth-century claim in (1), which (2) refutes, or the triviality in (4), which nobody ever denied and no evidence could touch.

I am stuck because (7) seems to convict more than Malthus. There are claims of just that eventually shape that I think are perfectly respectable and worth acting on — a household that persistently spends more than it earns will eventually default, a population that overshoots what its habitat can support will eventually be knocked back, a structure loaded beyond what its material can bear will eventually give — and none of those specify a date, yet they seem to carry real content and to support real advice. My friend replies that these are informative precisely because they come with a mechanism and with things you can check along the way, and that Malthus-with-a-delay has none; but I am not sure that is true of the version in (3), which does name a mechanism and does seem to predict something about what happens when the fossil inheritance is drawn down. There are also honest cases, I think, of a theory being mocked for decades and vindicated long afterwards — the man who proposed that the continents move, for instance, whose evidence only arrived with the seafloor — and (6) seems to rule those out too. A third person in the group suggested that Malthus was simply describing the world before 1800 and was wrong about the world after it, which sounded to me like moving the goalposts, though I could not say why.

So: which step do you reject — (3), (4), (5) or (6) — and why did that step look sound enough to get past me? What does rejecting it commit me to, and what becomes of the argument once it is gone? Is there a version of right but early that survives your diagnosis, and if so what would distinguish it from the empty version — what testable implication, or what constraint on the transition path rather than the endpoint, would show the difference? Is the pre-1800 regime version my group mate offered an honest restriction or a rescue by redefinition? Please say plainly how much of Malthus is left at the end, if any.
