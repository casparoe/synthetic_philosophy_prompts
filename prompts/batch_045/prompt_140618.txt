It's for a 9-month master's thesis in one of those philosophy-and-economics programs: about 12,000 words, plus a small simulation/computation component that's supposed to be in service of the conceptual argument rather than the other way round.

Here's the half-formed idea. In the textbook sequential herding setup — binary state of the world, each person in a fixed order gets a private signal that's correct with probability q > 1/2, everyone sees the entire history of past choices before choosing — you get an information cascade: after a short run of identical choices each new person's own signal stops mattering and they copy. And the standard folklore is that cascades are at once very easy to start, quite likely to be wrong, and fragile: a little new public information is supposed to shatter one.

What I don't think anyone has really pressed on is how much the fragility result depends on everyone in the model knowing that they're in a herding situation. Real people often don't seem to reason that way: plenty of them read others' behaviour as roughly independent evidence, treating ten people doing X as ten data points rather than one data point plus nine echoes. So I want to introduce a mixed population: 'sophisticates' who update correctly given that predecessors may already be copying, and 'naives' (call them double-counters if you like) who read each observed action as if it carried a fresh private signal, and who therefore get more confident the longer the run goes. My guess — and this is the bit I want the proposal to set up as a question to be tested rather than assumed — is that even a modest fraction of naives makes wrong cascades much more stable then the canonical model predicts, and that the usual policy morals ('just release more information', 'seed the early movers with the truth') can backfire, because with double-counters around a public signal doesn't break the herd so much as get absorbed into the count and amplified.

There's a wrinkle I like but can't place: what if the sophisticates don't know the share of naives, only a distribution over it? My instinct is that this partly restores fragility, since a sophisticated late-mover has to ask whether the long run in front of them is ten independent signals or one signal plus nine copycats — and then a well-designed intervention might work by changing not what people know but what they believe about how naive everyone else is. But I dunno whether that's a section or a whole second project, and the proposal should say which.
