I am putting together the handout for the third session of a graduate reading group on Nyāya epistemology, and I want the handout itself to be a diagnostic instrument rather than a survey of views. The last session collapsed into a dispute about whether any classical theory of error is really saying something different from any other, because each participant could redescribe their preferred theory in the vocabulary of their neighbour's. So what I want from you is a decision tree — or, if it does the work better, a short quiz with a scoring key — that takes a participant from what she is willing to say about concrete cases of erroneous cognition to a named classical theory of error, without ever asking her which theory she favours.

The names I want available as leaves: anyathākhyāti, akhyāti, viparītakhyāti, asatkhyāti, ātmakhyāti, anirvacanīyakhyāti, and satkhyāti. If some cell of the logical space is occupied by a view I have not listed, add it and name the cell; I would rather have the space complete than have exactly seven leaves. Alongside each name give the school usually credited with it, but hedge wherever you are unsure — the attribution matters much less to me than the shape of the position, and I would rather have a hedge than a confident mislabelling.

Requirements:

1. Build the tree from the logical space, not from the list of names. Set out the branching questions first, then locate each named position in the cell its answers produce. The axes I expect to matter are roughly these: whether the episode has a single object or is really two episodes, a bare awareness plus a memory, that the subject fails to keep apart; whether what appears is existent, nonexistent, or neither; whether it is located where it appears to be, somewhere else, or nowhere; whether it is inside the mind or outside it; whether the failure lies in what is presented or in the subject's discrimination of what is presented; whether the episode is perceptual, memorial, or a hybrid; and how its later cancellation is to be explained. Add, drop, or regroup axes as you think fit. Six to eight branch questions in total, and a leaf for every occupied cell. For every cell no classical theory occupies, say whether that is because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

2. Phrase every branch node as a question a participant can answer without knowing where it leads. So not *is the object of error real?* but something like: in the nacre case, when the subject reaches for the silver, is there one thing she reaches for or two? The instrument should sort readers by what they say about cases, not by which label they like.

3. Distribute the following cases across the nodes, and add further cases of your own where one is needed:

- the nacre taken for silver on a beach in bright light;
- the rope taken for a snake at dusk;
- the white conch seen as yellow by a jaundiced subject;
- the two moons produced by pressing a finger against a closed eye;
- a subject who has never encountered silver at all confronting the nacre;
- mistaking a dust cloud on a distant hill for smoke, then inferring fire on the hill;
- a dream of a fire in one's own kitchen, taken for a real fire while the dream lasts;
- a user of a head-mounted display reaching for a virtual mug and afterwards saying that the mug she saw was right there;
- a room judged cold on the basis of a thermometer with a faulty sensor, where the room happens to be cold.

Then invent one further case that separates two leaves your tree otherwise cannot separate, or argue that no such case exists because the difference between those leaves is not one any case could expose.

4. Each leaf should carry the Sanskritic name, the school usually credited, one sentence on the position's most awkward commitment, and three further points: (a) whether the erroneous episode counts as a pramāṇa at all, or as an episode produced by a pramāṇa that fails to yield knowledge, or as no single episode in the first place; (b) how, on that position, the validity of an ordinary successful cognition is ascertained, intrinsically or extrinsically, and whether the theory of error forces that answer or merely sits naturally beside it; (c) what the position says about a mistaken cognition that nonetheless guides successful action.

5. Flag the pairs of leaves your tree cannot separate, and say for each whether the residue is a genuine metaphysical difference that no case can adjudicate or a merely verbal one. I especially want to know whether anyathākhyāti and anirvacanīyakhyāti can be prised apart at the level of cases rather than ontology, and whether the Bhāṭṭa position is separable from the Nyāya one without importing some extra commitment.

6. Practical constraints. No bibliography, no citations, no quoted passages: the group has no library access during the session and I want the distinctions rather than the sources. Gloss each Sanskrit term once on first use, since not everyone in the room reads it. The whole thing should fit on a two-sided handout — nested lists or an ASCII tree, plus a short closing memo on which cases turn out to do no discriminating work at all, and why.
