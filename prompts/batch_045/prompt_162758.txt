I am setting the final take-home paper for an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar, and I need one essay question on epiphenomenalism and the paradox of phenomenal judgment, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. Students will have 60 minutes and are expected to write roughly 850 words; the question is worth 20 marks. Please write (a) the question itself, (b) a model answer of about 850 words in the voice of a genuinely excellent student rather than a textbook, and (c) a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth and a one-or-two-line note on how candidates typically get each one wrong.

Three constraints on how the question is built.

First, it must be self-contained. Everything a student needs has to be stipulated in the question; do not presuppose any published paper, and do not give a bibliography or reading list. If a view is named, make clear in the wording that it is a stipulated position invented for the exam, so nobody goes hunting for a source. Alongside that: do not put any formulation from a real published text inside quotation marks; paraphrase standard formulations instead of quoting them.

Second, the question has to be unanswerable by reciting the paradox. It should require students to decompose the paradox into three separable sub-problems and then to notice that a solution to one need not be a solution to the others: the semantic or content question, of what makes a phenomenal judgment *about* the experience it seems to be about; the causal-genetic question, of what produces the judgment; and the epistemic question, of what would make it justified or knowledgeable. The crux of the question should be precisely whether a given reply fixes all three or only some.

Third, the reply they must evaluate should be one they have never seen. Present them with a stipulated position you should call Covariance Epiphenomenalism, built from three claims: phenomenal properties are causally inert with respect to the production of beliefs and behaviour; every phenomenal property is nonetheless nomologically co-instantiated with a physical signature state, and it is the signature rather than the experience that causes the introspective judgment; and phenomenal concepts are recognitional concepts whose reference is fixed by the co-instantiated signature, so that the judgment is counterfactually sensitive to which experience is present while being caused only by the signature. Have the position conclude that aboutness and knowledge are secured without any phenomenal-to-judgment causation.

Then build the difficulty out of three further demands. Ask students to evaluate Covariance Epiphenomenalism against a stipulated mismatch case in which the signature fires and the subject forms the judgment that she is now in pain while no phenomenal pain is present: is that judgment false, or does it fail to express a proposition at all, and either way what does the answer show about whether the theory of reference on offer was ever adequate? Ask whether nomological covariance is even available to the non-reductive epiphenomenalist, given that she wants to leave room for inverted- and absent-qualia possibilities, and whether covariance that is merely de facto rather than necessary still supports the counterfactual sensitivity, and hence any safety or tracking condition on knowledge, that the reply relies on. And ask whether the reply, if it works for first-person phenomenal judgments, also explains how the epiphenomenalist knows the theory itself, requiring students to say whether the theoretical case is the same problem in a different costume or a genuinely further one, and to say why.

For the marking scheme, be explicit about what separates an A from a B. A B answer should be characterised as one that states both horns of the paradox accurately, notes that the reply secures covariance but leaves causation out, and raises one objection; it should be capped around 13 or 14. An A answer must additionally do the following: say why the mismatch case forces a decision about reference rather than merely about truth value; distinguish the nomological from the merely de facto reading of covariance and connect that distinction to the modal neighbourhood knowledge requires; and treat the self-application problem on its own terms instead of re-running the original paradox. Name the specific errors that should cost marks, including at least these: conflating causal inertness with nomological idleness; offering the claim that the judgment is caused by the neural correlate as though that settled the reference question; treating causal production as a requirement on reference; assuming that covariance must be exceptionless to yield knowledge, or conversely ignoring nearby inverted-qualia possibilities; and collapsing the semantic and epistemic sub-problems into one. Close with a one-line statement of what an answer that lays out the paradox flawlessly but takes no position on the stipulated reply would score.
