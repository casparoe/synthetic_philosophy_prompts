I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've drifted into an argument about actual infinities that I can't settle on my own. I'd like you to tell me what would count against the position I've ended up holding.

How I got here: in code, an "infinite" thing is never a completed object. A stream, a generator, a lazy list — these are rules for producing the next item, not collections you can hand to `len()`. Meanwhile the mathematics I actually rely on treats infinite totalities as finished things. Reading the standard set-piece arguments (the hotel with infinitely many rooms that can always take one more guest by shifting everyone up a room; the diagonal argument showing no pairing between the naturals and the reals) I found myself unable to see the force of the set-piece. So here is my position, in three claims.

(1) The hotel-style arguments are non sequiturs as arguments against the coherence or possibility of actual infinities. They establish at most that our ordinary, finite-trained concepts of *full* and *just as many* — the ones built on counting to an end and on the thought that a proper part is smaller than the whole — have no infinite analogue.

(2) The correct successor notion is cardinality: two collections have the same number of members just when their members can be placed in one-to-one correspondence. Once that is internalized, the "paradox" is a report about our old concept, not about the world.

(3) The discomfort that survives is tracking something else, and here is my own reconstruction of what: our everyday concept of *full* is really two concepts that coincide only in finite cases — *exhaustive occupancy* (no room is unoccupied) and *inelastic fullness* (there is no way to accommodate anything further without displacing something already there). The hotel dramatizes their divergence. On this reading, the argument doesn't threaten infinite totalities; it shows that a distinction which was invisible in finite cases becomes visible once size is infinite.

Map my exposure. I want the considerations that should move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, including ones that arguably already obtain. Specifically:

**Rival notions of size.** In my own field I routinely say that almost all inputs have some property, using asymptotic density: the evens have density one-half against the naturals while being equinumerous with them, the squares have density zero, and the set of integers whose decimal representation begins with the digit 1 has no asymptotic density at all. Cardinality also files the naturals, the rationals, the finite binary strings, and the computable functions under one heading, which feels like it discards distinctions I care about. If *how many* has several precisifications in good standing and no principled way to choose among them, claim (2) looks overconfident — and claim (3) looks like it smuggles back a size notion (inelasticity) that claim (2) declares obsolete. Can cardinality's privilege be defended, or is it merely the notion that happens to serve the metamathematics?

**Does (3) do any work?** My worry: inelastic fullness may just amount to Dedekind-finiteness, in which case my reconstruction reduces to "infinite collections are Dedekind-infinite" — a set-theoretic fact restated as a discovery about concepts. Worse, my understanding is that finiteness and Dedekind-finiteness can come apart when you drop the axiom of choice, which would make the reduction messier than I think. Correct me if I have that wrong, and tell me whether the collapse is fatal or merely deflationary.

**What does my view predict?** I think it predicts that the felt absurdity of the hotel should be sensitive to presentation: it should fade under a relabel-the-doors description in which nobody moves, and persist under a description involving a physical supertask with guests shuffling down an endless corridor and the manager's instruction propagating at finite speed. If the pull survives symmetric presentation, claims (1) and (3) are in trouble. Is that a genuine test, and what else should I look for?

**Two variants I constructed to isolate this.** (a) The unbuilt hotel: no rearrangement, no construction, an always-existing infinite hotel with every room occupied from eternity. (b) The finite-speed hotel: the shift is broadcast, but the signal travels at finite speed along an endless corridor, so at every later finite time only finitely many guests have moved and the task never completes. Does my position have to say (b) is irrelevant? If it does, has it been qualified into something unfalsifiable — every report of absurdity absorbed as finite-trained intuition misfiring? Is that a problem for a view of this kind, or the appropriate fate of a view that is really about concept revision? Note also whether the lamp toggled at one-half, one-quarter, one-eighth of a minute and so on before noon — on or off at noon? — is the same problem in miniature or a different one.

**Size outrunning the axioms.** If cardinality is the correct account of *how many*, then the question of how many reals there are should have a determinate answer; my understanding is that the standard axioms do not settle whether there is a size strictly between that of the naturals and that of the reals. Does that count against cardinality as an account of size, or only against the axioms?

**Operations a completed totality won't support.** You cannot draw a uniformly random natural number — no countably additive uniform probability measure exists over the naturals. Is the unavailability of certain operations evidence against the totality, or only a fact about measures? What is the general form of this move, and should I take it seriously?

For each of these, tell me which a committed holder of my view would concede as genuinely counting against it, and which they would dismiss as a confusion — the honest version, not a debater's list. Then give me one or two defeaters I haven't listed. You can assume that "but it still feels strange," and appeals to what could physically be built, will not move me; I'd be most moved by a case where the mathematics itself, and not the story wrapped around it, forces a distinction my reconstruction cannot express.

Please define any term of art you use (Dedekind-infinite, countable additivity, asymptotic density) rather than assuming I know it. Don't give me citations or reading lists — I have no way to chase them. If any piece of standard mathematics in my description above is garbled, say so plainly instead of building on it. Finally, state how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the one you are least sure of, along with any place where your answer leans on a contested reading of Cantor, of the part–whole principle, or of the density notions.
