Im messing around with the problem of verisimilitude (truthlikeness) and I want you to argue for a position I've been kicking around, as hard as you can. Not a balanced overview — an actual argument. Steelman it, and if you think it collapses, say so in a line or two at the end.

Quick background so we're on the same page. Popper's thing was that most of the great theories in the history of science are strictly speaking false, but science still seems to make progress toward the truth, so it has to be possible for one false theory to be closer to the truth than another. His own defintion basically scored a theory by its true consequences minus its false ones. In 1974 Tichý, Harris and Miller (three papers in the same issue of the BJPS, I believe) showed this has the consequence that no false theory can be closer to the truth than another, which is the exact opposite of what it was supposed to deliver. Popper accepted the criticism almost immediately. People have been proposing fixes ever since — I don't want a survey of them, I want the case for mine.

The position: verisimilitude is not a two-place relation between a theory and the world. Its at least three-place — T is closer to the truth than T′ relative to a partition P of the space of possibilities, where P is the set of distinctions the inquirers are actually trying to draw: the live rival hypotheses, the contrasts on the table, whatever question the research programme is currently trying to settle. On this view there is no such thing as closeness to the truth full stop; asking which theory is 'closer to the truth' with no contrast class specified is like asking whether something is further left without saying left of what.

Heres the toy model I want you to work with. Take a world specifiable by three independent yes/no questions q1, q2, q3, and suppose the actual answers are (yes, no, yes). Consider:

- A: q1 is yes, q2 is no, q3 is no. (false — it gets q3 wrong)
- B: q1 is no, q2 is no, q3 is yes. (false — it gets q1 wrong)
- C: either q1 is yes or q1 is no. (true, and completely worthless)

Scored over the whole space, A and B come out equally good (one mistake each) and C comes out perfect, which is the wrong result. Now suppose the live question is only whether q1 is yes — the inquiry is trying to settle q1, and q2 and q3 just arent on the table. Then A picks out the true cell of that partition, B picks out the wrong one, and C picks out nothing at all. So the ranking should be A above C above B, and that's the ranking that actually matters for whether this inquiry is getting somewhere.

Please argue that this is the right way to think about the whole problem. In particular:

1. Make the case that the contrastive view is what Popper was really after, and that the 1974-style results only get their bite from letting in every consequence and every distinction in the space, including ones nobody is asking about. Bonus: say what happens if P is taken to be the finest-grained partition of all eight cells, and whether that's a problem or a feature.
2. Push back on the obvious objection: that this has quietly stopped being about truth and started being about usefulness, or about what we happen to care about. Truthlikeness was supposed to be objective. Argue either that the contrastive notion is still perfectly objective, or that objectivity was overrated here.
3. Push back on the gerrymandering objection: if the partition is up to us, I can pick whatever contrast class makes my favourite theory come out on top, so 'closer to the truth relative to P' threatens to mean whatever I like.
4. Say something about whether the view can still make sense of progress across theory change — say from Newton to Einstein — given that the later theory arguably changes what the live contrasts even are.

Feel free to make the toy model more precise if it helps: propose an actual scoring rule over cells of a partition if you want, but keep it readable. And please don't just tell me this is basically somebody's published view and leave it there — argue for it.
