I am finishing a paper on material constitution — the debate that starts from a statue and the quantity of clay that currently makes it up — and I have reached the point where I am less interested in which view is true than in why the argument has stopped moving. I would like a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement rather than a verdict on it, and I would like the answer written in American English. Please don't simply survey the positions again in different words; I know them.

The dialectic, as I would reconstruct it: the statue and the clay occupy the same region at the same time and share all their matter, yet the clay would survive being rolled into a ball while the statue would not, and the statue would survive the replacement of a small part while the clay would not. Given the indiscernibility of identicals, they are distinct, and so we appear to have two coinciding objects related by something other than identity. Four sorts of response have been on the table for as long as I have been reading this literature. The constitutionalist treats constitution as a genuine, asymmetric, non-identity relation that does explanatory work: the clay constitutes the statue and not conversely. The four-dimensionalist dissolves the coincidence into overlap: the statue and the clay are different spatio-temporal parts, or perhaps different sums of parts of a single worm, and nothing is ever wholly present twice over in one place. The eliminativist denies that the quantifier of serious ontology ranges over statues or lumps at all — there is clay arranged statuewise, and ordinary counting is a loose, interest-relative gloss on that. And the identity theorist accepts one thing here, described once under an artefact sortal and once under a matter sortal, and treats the apparent modal asymmetry as a feature of how sortals trace things across modal space rather than a feature of the thing. A second wave of argument then asked a further question: what makes it the case that this clay constitutes a statue at all, and why is there exactly one statue here rather than a plenitude of coinciding candidates? That question has produced the familiar complaint that constitution, if it is not identity, cannot do any grounding work that identity could not do, and so either collapses into identity or leaves the arrangement of the clay brute.

The device I have been pushing in my own chapter is meant to break the tie, and it has conspicuously failed to. Consider a statue produced by layer-by-layer deposition — clay extruded directly into statue form — so that at no time is there a shapeless quantity of clay that subsequently comes to constitute a statue, and at no time is there a statue that is not made of exactly the clay that then constitutes it. The modal argument still runs: the finished object can be squashed into a ball, and in principle a section of it could be excised and replaced. But the temporal and explanatory asymmetry that seems to carry so much of the intuitive weight in the ordinary case — the lump was there first, and it is the lump that explains why there is a statue — has been removed. What I did not anticipate is that this discriminates between no one at all. The constitutionalist says constitution can be synchronous; the four-dimensionalist says it was never relying on priority; the identity theorist says the artefact sortal and the matter sortal have been co-applied from the first moment; the eliminativist says it never granted either object in the first place. Each camp absorbs the case with a one-line adjustment, at no theoretical cost, and no one changes sides. Every further case I construct behaves the same way, which is beginning to look like the interesting fact rather than an accident of my examples.

My hypothesis, which I would like you to test rather than confirm, is that this is not a first-order disagreement about what is in the room. It is a disagreement about which features of a case are permitted to count as data — the modal intuitions, the temporal and causal priority of the matter, and the explanatory demands of grounding look like three separate sources of evidence, and each position may really be a standing decision about how to weight them when they pull apart, together with a rule for discounting whatever the others lean on. On that reading, no new case could settle anything, because each side's weighting determines in advance how it will describe any case you hand it, and the only genuine progress would be argument about the weighting from outside the cases. A colleague of mine goes further and thinks the impasse is itself evidence that the question is ill-posed: that there is no answer to how many things are in this room apart from a choice of conceptual scheme, and that several decades of argument with almost no conversions is just what we should expect if that were so.

So: is the competing-data-sources diagnosis right, and if it is, which of those weighting disputes is actually load-bearing? Is part of the disagreement verbal — not about the word 'object', but about what a relation has to do in order to count as explanatory, or about whether sortals are in the business of tracking at all? Is there a hidden formal question doing the work, for instance whether grounding is fine-grained enough to distinguish constitution from identity, or whether a modal framework that permits contingent identity can even formulate the identity theorist's claim? And given whatever diagnosis you give, what would count as progress here — a distinction, a formal result, an argument about method, a change of question — or should we expect none, and what would justify that expectation? I would like the diagnosis supported by what each view is actually committed to, and an honest assessment of whether my own deposition case tells us anything beyond the fact that the views are more elastic than I had assumed.
