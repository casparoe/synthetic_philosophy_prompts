I work on inference-time compute and recursive self-distillation in language models, and I have absorbed the alignment literature only at second hand, through summaries and through arguments about it rather than through the sources themselves. So please treat me as fluent in my own engineering vocabulary and as close to uninformed about the philosophical framing around it.

The claim I want translated is one I keep circling. Set up a procedure as follows. An answer to a question is produced by a bounded human who may at any point split the question into subquestions, hand each subquestion to a fresh copy of the very same procedure, and then combine the answers that come back; the subquestions are supposed to be small enough that a bounded human can answer them, and check them, without help. Call the limit of that recursion, if it has a clean one, H. The claim I keep meeting is that H is not merely a description of what an extraordinarily well-resourced committee would say, but an idealization of deliberation itself: that what you should believe, or what you would believe if you thought as well as you possibly could, is what H outputs, and that the distance between your actual verdict and H's verdict measures how far you fell short of thinking properly.

I have been carrying around a translation of this into my own vocabulary, and I want you to improve it or tear it down. On my reading, H is the fixed point of a self-distillation operator. A policy samples a decomposition of the question from a learned decomposition policy, routes the pieces to copies of itself under a per-message token budget, aggregates the returned messages, and emits a final answer; the whole recursive procedure is then distilled back into the policy as a single pass; repeat until the policy stops moving. On that translation, deliberation is amortized inference, and thinking is what you do when you pay the full test-time compute bill instead of guessing from the amortized prior. Recursion depth is a compute budget. The per-message token budget is a communication channel, and its bandwidth is the real content of the demand that subquestions be individually checkable, since small enough for a bounded human to verify becomes small enough that a weak verifier's signal is not swamped. The demand that sub-agents not mislead the aggregator becomes a constraint on the channel, a requirement that the messages be trainable against rather than a moral requirement. And the normativity of H becomes a claim about the existence and uniqueness of the fixed point, so that what you should believe comes out as what the operator converges to.

Do that translation as faithfully as you can in that vocabulary — policies, sampling, test-time compute, context windows as bounded channels, distillation and amortization, verifiers and reward models, multi-agent training — correcting me wherever my version quietly changes the subject. Then give me the residue report, in three parts and as honestly as you can: which parts of the original claim survive the translation unchanged, which survive only by being bent, and which cannot be said in my vocabulary at all. My own suspicion is that at least four things fall into the last category: the difference between the operator's fixed point and the correct answer, since my vocabulary admits many self-consistent fixed points and gives me no way to choose among them; the identity of the deliberator, since my scheme has a population of copies that share weights but not a subject, and I cannot say whether H is one mind thinking or many minds voting; the difference between a bounded human's inability to check a subquestion and a learned verifier's inability to check it, which my vocabulary collapses into one number; and the should itself, since my framework minimizes expected loss and has no primitive for a verdict being the one you owe.
