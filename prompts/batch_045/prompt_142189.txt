I had a long argument at dinner last night with a friend who works on higher-order thought (HOT) theories of consciousness — the Rosenthal-style view that what makes a mental state conscious is that it is the target of a suitable higher-order representation, rather than any intrinsic property of the first-order state itself. I claimed this is just an observer hierarchy wearing metaphysical costume. On the vehicles I build (long-dwell autonomous underwater vehicles, almost no bandwidth to the surface) we run a bank of first-order estimators over the sensor suite, and above them a supervisory layer doing fault detection and isolation: residual generation, hypothesis tests over fault modes, confidence-weighted state estimates, a monitor that estimates the health and outputs of the estimators themselves, and a planner that reads those estimates. My friend said the analogy flatters me and that the interesting part is exactly what my vocabulary cannot say. I want to settle it properly, so: do the translation seriously, not the verdict first.

Assume I am fluent in state estimation and observer design, Kalman and particle filtering, residual-based fault detection and isolation, detectability and observability, hierarchical and decentralized estimation, calibration and ROC-style tradeoffs between missed detection and false alarm, and supervisory control architectures — and that my philosophy is at the level of a graduate seminar I sat in on years ago. Take the HOT story and restate it claim by claim in that vocabulary: the first-order state, the higher-order representation that takes it as its target, the requirement that the higher-order state itself normally not be conscious (so no tower of watchers), the transitive/intransitive and creature/state distinctions, the difference between a higher-order thought and a higher-order perceptual or inner-sense state, and the claim that the higher-order state is what makes the subject conscious of the first-order one. Then apply the translation to four architectures I can actually specify, and give me a verdict on each rather than a shrug: (A) a monitor that reads only a 32-dimensional compressed latent of a 4096-dimensional first-order state and reports a coarse category label, so the fine-grained detail is in the system but not in the report; (B) a monitor running at 1 Hz over a 200 Hz process, reporting a hysteretic, time-averaged summary; (C) a monitor whose report, after sensor loss, is generated by a learned prior over typical missions rather than by any measurement of the current state, including the case where it reports a state that is not instantiated at all and the case where the channel it reports on does not exist; (D) a monitor whose reports are accurate but written to a log that no downstream controller ever reads.

Then the part I actually care about: the residue report. Tell me which claims translated cleanly, which translated only after you smuggled in an assumption that belongs to my framework or to yours rather than to the mapping itself, and which cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all — I expect trouble around what-it-is-likeness, around the difference between a state's being conscious and its being merely available to a downstream consumer, and around whatever epistemic or normative constraint keeps a higher-order representation from being a lucky guess. And then tell me which way the residue cuts: is it that an engineering vocabulary built for a designer's third-person stance has no slot for phenomenality, or is it that the HOT claim is thinner than it sounds — if being made conscious cashes out functionally as availability to a downstream system, is HOT still distinguishable from a global-workspace or access view, or from a same-order self-representational one? Don't flatter my side of the dinner argument; if the mapping is worse than I think, say exactly where it breaks. No citations or suggested reading, please — I want the argument, not the literature.
