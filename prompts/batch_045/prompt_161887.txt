I have been working through Jeffrey's rule for learning from uncertain evidence, and I have talked myself into an argument whose conclusion I do not believe. Set the machinery up as follows. An agent has credences over a finite algebra of propositions; call the atoms of that algebra A₁ through Aₙ, and assume every atom has positive credence both before and after the episode, so that none of the conditional probabilities below goes undefined. An experience bears directly on some partition E = {E₁, …, Eₖ}: it moves the probabilities of the cells and moves nothing else. The rule then has two parts — rigidity, that the new credence in any proposition B conditional on a cell equals the old one, P_new(B | Eᵢ) = P_old(B | Eᵢ); and the update itself, P_new(B) = Σᵢ P_old(B | Eᵢ) · P_new(Eᵢ).

The argument:

(1) This rule is offered as the generalization of conditioning to the case of uncertain evidence: what an agent is required to do, if she is to count as having responded rationally to an experience that does not hand her any proposition outright.

(2) Take the partition to be the finest one available, the partition into atoms {A₁, …, Aₙ}. Rigidity is then satisfied automatically, whatever P_new happens to be: since every proposition in the algebra is a union of atoms, both P_old(B | Aⱼ) and P_new(B | Aⱼ) equal 1 if Aⱼ is contained in B and 0 otherwise.

(3) Substituting, P_new(B) = Σⱼ P_old(B | Aⱼ) · P_new(Aⱼ) = Σ_{ⱼ : Aⱼ ⊆ B} P_new(Aⱼ) = P_new(B). The rule's output is the identity on credence functions. So for any pair of credence functions at all, however wild the transition between them, there is a partition on which the rule issues exactly that transition.

(4) Hence the rule declares every possible change of opinion admissible.

(5) A norm that declares every possible change of opinion admissible forbids nothing. It has no more content than the instruction to revise as you please.

(6) So the rule is empty. Its entire appearance of content comes from the choice of partition, and nothing inside the formalism fixes that choice; the agent appears to make it.

I would rather keep (2) and (3) than accept (6). The arithmetic looks unassailable to me, and I would sooner accuse myself of a slide somewhere than conclude that the central Bayesian norm for uncertain evidence says nothing. But which slide, where, and what does giving up that step commit me to? Say why the step looked fine, and price the alternatives I am not taking.

Three things beyond the diagnosis.

First, a control. Run the same construction against ordinary conditioning on certain evidence, where the experience delivers a proposition outright and the rule says to set P_new(·) = P_old(· | E). My impression is that the construction fails there: the output is fixed by E together with the old credences, and even letting E range over every proposition of positive probability leaves most credence functions unreachable. Confirm that or correct it, and then say what the contrast establishes. If ordinary conditioning survives and the uncertain-evidence rule does not, where exactly must the missing content be located — and can it be located there without circularity, i.e. can the partition be supplied by the experience rather than read backwards off the revision I wanted to make?

Second, a repair I have been toying with and cannot evaluate. Call it Pre-partitioned Updating: before any experience arrives, the agent fixes a distinguished partition, the observation partition, and may thereafter revise only by the rule applied to that partition. This seems to escape (6), but I suspect it pays for the escape, and I want the bill itemized. Two payments worry me. (i) It cannot handle evidence that cuts across the pre-drawn distinctions, or that introduces a distinction the algebra did not already contain — you learn a new word for something and now have to redraw the cells. Is that the central case of learning or a marginal one, and does the repair's inability to handle it count against the repair, or merely against the finite-algebra setting I started with? (ii) It relocates the normativity into a choice made before the evidence, a choice that is itself unconstrained. Is that a vicious regress, or is it benign — a structural commitment more like adopting a language, undertaken once and then held fixed?

Third, a variant. Suppose the experience delivers less: not the new probabilities of the cells but only their new ratios, the odds between cells, with the absolute values recovered by normalization. Does (6) still follow? My guess is that it does not, but for a reason that leaves me more suspicious rather than less: the input now contains just enough numbers to pin the output down, so the rule is non-vacuous only because the input has quietly become a full description of where the agent ended up. Tell me whether that diagnosis is right, and whether the ratio variant is therefore genuinely better off than the original or merely better disguised.
