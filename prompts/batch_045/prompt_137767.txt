For a paper I'm writing in my senior humanities seminar, I'm comparing two attitudes toward the successful: the American Dream outlook, on which an individual who rises is to be admired and held up as evidence that effort and ambition pay off, and the Scandinavian Jantelagen / tall-poppy instinct, on which the person who sticks up above the rest is to be cut back down to size. Every example I can find runs in the expected direction, so I want one that runs the other way.

Build me a single-paragraph, realistic scenario set in an ordinary high-school world — a school, a team, a part-time job, a club; no celebrities, no billionaires, no surprise inheritances — in which someone who takes the American Dream seriously would refuse to celebrate a success and would, in some genuine sense, think less of the person who achieved it, while someone raised on Jantelagen would sincerely celebrate that same person. Constraints: the achievement has to be real and genuinely earned; the achiever must not be arrogant, corrupt, or secretly unhappy; and neither reaction may be ironic, grudging, or backhanded. The two verdicts need to fall out of what each outlook is actually for, not from my having written the reactions into the setup.

After the scenario: state what each framework says and why; identify the one feature of the case doing the work and show that removing it collapses the case back to the ordinary direction; give me the strongest objection available to whichever framework comes off worse; and tell me honestly whether the reversal is substantive or merely verbal — whether celebrate and cut down have quietly shifted senses between the two columns. I want to use the case to argue that the two outlooks are not really reacting to the same property of a success at all, so if my argument and my constraints can't both be satisfied, say which one has to give.
