Two philosophers have spent three years arguing past each other about moral particularism and generalism, and have agreed to settle it as an adversarial collaboration. Write the joint statement they could both sign.

Party A is a particularist. She accepts holism about reasons: whether a natural feature is a reason at all, and if so in which direction and how strongly, can depend on what else is true in the case, including on features that are not themselves morally significant. She denies that a moral judgment is correct in virtue of falling under any principle, and she denies that citing a reason explains anything by subsuming the case under a generalization.

Party B is a generalist. He holds that whenever a feature is a reason in a case, there is some principle — possibly unknown to us, possibly too long to be written down, possibly useless in deliberation — under which the case falls and which makes the feature a reason there; and that a moral explanation works only if it implicitly appeals to such a principle.

Both accept that moral properties supervene on natural ones; neither is a skeptic, an expressivist, or a relativist.

Structure the document as follows.

1. Grades. Open with a short section where each party says which grade of her own view she holds and which she explicitly disclaims. For particularism: (i) there are no true informative moral principles; (ii) there may be such principles, but no correct moral judgment is correct in virtue of instantiating one; (iii) there may be such principles, but no finite exceptionless action-guiding set is available to creatures like us. Give the analogous three grades for generalism: supervenience-only; the claim that some principle makes every reason a reason; the claim that competent judgment genuinely deploys principles. Then state plainly which grades the live disagreement does not touch, and say whether any well-known quarrel in this neighborhood dissolves into a verbal one once the grades are separated.

2. Agreed claims. Eight to twelve numbered claims both can sign. Each must be substantive enough that a thoughtful outsider could deny it. If a candidate claim is uncontested by anyone, or looks agreed only because the word *principle* is doing two different jobs, put it in a footnote saying why instead of listing it.

3. Disagreements. A table with one row per dispute and these columns: a neutral description of what is at issue; the particularist's own formulation, worded as she would defend it and not as the generalist would caricature it; the generalist's own formulation, likewise; and a verdict on whether the dispute is genuine, verbal, or partly each.

4. Cruxes. For each crux, give the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one party; a concrete case, argument, or test both accept as diagnostic of it; and each side's prediction, recorded in advance, together with what each would concede if her prediction failed.

Candidate cruxes, which you may replace if you have stronger ones: (a) whether the generalist's habit of patching a principle after a hostile case — adding a defeater clause, or redescribing the antecedent — is ad hoc, and what test would distinguish principled refinement from ad hoc rescue; (b) whether reliable, generative moral performance in genuinely novel domains requires a tacit principle, and what the analogy with a trained classifier that is highly reliable but not rule-articulable shows; (c) whether the distinction between a silencer (a condition that makes a feature no reason at all) and a defeater (a condition that opposes a reason that is there anyway) is metaphysical or merely epistemic, and connectedly whether default reasons are a thesis about what reasons there are or about burden of proof; (d) whether singular moral explanation can work without subsumption under a generalization; (e) whether a principle consisting of the infinite disjunction of all actual cases would count as a principle, and whether anything beyond words hangs on the answer.

5. Two constructed cases. Invent one valence-flip pair — two cases sharing every candidate reason-feature, differing only in a background condition that is not itself morally salient, with the shared feature's direction reversed in the second — and one silent-background pair — the same feature is a reason in the first case and simply no reason at all in the second. For each, say what each party should say, and whether the pair, if genuine, would settle anything or only relocate the dispute.

For crux (a), design the protocol in detail: a pre-registered adversarial case-construction game in which the generalist states a principle for a domain, the particularist then produces a case, and the generalist may amend once per round under rules both accept. State the stopping rule and what each party predicts about the state of the principle after ten rounds.

Constraints: define every technical term in the text rather than assuming it; never name or cite any author, paper, or book; address a reader who knows nothing of the debate. Aim for 1,200 to 1,600 words. Write in American English.
