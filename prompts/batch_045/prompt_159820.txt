I write about law and its history for a magazine covering science and ideas, and I am finishing a piece on two live proposals. The first is a country emerging from a long dictatorship that wants to prosecute former ministers under a statute enacted after the transition, for conduct that was lawful — in some cases legally required — under the old regime's own law. The second is an international court's plan to exercise jurisdiction over the crime of aggression against leaders of states that never accepted that jurisdiction. In almost every interview the same three words close the discussion: victors' justice. The label is plainly doing argumentative work, and I do not think my sources all mean the same thing by it.

What I need from you is a disambiguation rather than a verdict on Nuremberg and Tokyo. Please set out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims the phrase can be used to make, and for each say what would have to be true for it to hold, how one could tell, whether it is intended as a criticism of the trials themselves or of something else, and whether it transfers to either of my two present-day cases. The candidates I can hear in my interviews include: a claim about the composition and impartiality of the bench; a claim about selective prosecution — that the winning side did the same things and was never charged; a claim that the tribunal had no jurisdiction over these defendants at all; a claim about retroactivity, in the form of the old maxim nullum crimen, nulla poena sine lege; a claim that the outcomes were settled before the evidence was heard; and a much stronger claim to the effect that there is no international criminal law, only the capacity of the strong to punish the weak. Say which of these are near-tautological, which are substantive and arguable, which are false as stated, and which are the ones a speaker usually has in mind when the phrase is deployed to end a discussion.

The retroactivity family is the one I most need made precise, because it is what my sources retreat to when I press them. Please split it further and give verdicts on the variants: a complaint that no written enactment defined the offence; a complaint that no penalty had been attached to it; a complaint that no court stood ready to try it; a complaint that the accused could not reasonably have foreseen that it would be treated as criminal; and a complaint that the rule existed but had never once been enforced against anyone, so that on the ground it was a dead letter. I would also like you to consider three complications I have not seen handled cleanly. First, whether the maxim is best understood as protecting a person's ability to plan their conduct, or as constraining which sources a court may draw on — and, as a test of that, whether it has anything to say about retroactive leniency, as when a successor government retroactively decriminalises the acts of the old regime's collaborators and thereby shields them. Second, whether the accused's own good-faith belief that his conduct was legal is a separate objection from retroactivity or only a version of it. Third, whether the objection has to be assessed count by count, on the assumption that charging someone with murder and ill-treatment of civilians in occupied territory stands on a different footing from charging them with launching a war, given that the one had detailed conventions behind it while the other had at most an interwar treaty in which dozens of states renounced war as an instrument of national policy.

Two closing questions. On which of these readings is victors' justice a genuine argument against a prosecution rather than a sneer, and can it be stated in a form that does not also immunise everyone, including the critics' own preferred alternative of amnesty? And if a court convicts in violation of the legality principle, is the trial itself best described as an injustice of the same kind it was convened to punish, or is that a confusion?
