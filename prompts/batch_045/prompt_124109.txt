I run concept development at a small industrial-design studio. Every other Monday I get ninety minutes with four designers to turn a client brief into three directions; the creative director then picks one, and I have to defend the choice. I want to run those sessions on Hofstadter's model of creativity as the search for variations on a theme: fix the theme, find its knobs, explore the space. As a slogan it is useless to me, so I need it as a procedure the team can actually run in the ninety minutes, with a stop rule.

The steps I care about are the ones the idea turns on: how we settle on the theme instead of just listing the product's features; how we search for knobs rather than taking the first three anyone names; how we tell a genuine variation from a cosmetic restyling; and how we catch the case where turning a knob quietly breaks an invariant that was doing the real work in the theme, so that the result is no longer a variation of anything — I'd call that theme collapse. For each step, say what counts as a sufficient answer, who makes the call, and where the procedure can only surface a judgment it cannot settle. Say too which distinctions have to survive the translation into something designers with no theory will use, and which can be dropped.

Apply it to three briefs: a hospital waiting-room chair, a pour-over coffee dripper whose maker wants "the next thing," and a museum audio guide. At least one must come out with a verdict I won't like — I suspect the dripper team's favorite, the same object in new materials and colors, is not a variation at all. Close by telling me what the procedure cannot do, and above all whether it can ever deliver the direction that changes the theme itself, which is the one clients actually pay for.
