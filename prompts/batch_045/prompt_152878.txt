I have no formal philosophy training (I'm a medical statistician; the rest of the group is mostly philosophers plus one physicist) and I've been asked to introduce chapter 5 at next week's meeting, which means I need to know how much of it to trust. My flatmate, who did a philosophy degree, read it and said something like "competent popularisation, with two or three slides in it that a specialist would wince at," but she's away this week and wouldn't tell me which claims were the slides. The chapter presents the old exchange between W. K. Clifford and William James roughly as follows:

Two Victorians set the terms of the debate. Clifford's principle is blunt: no one, anywhere, may hold a belief on evidence that does not measure up. His parable is of a shipowner about to send an emigrant ship to sea. Doubts about her seaworthiness prey on him; he talks himself out of them by recalling how many voyages she has already survived and by trusting Providence to care for the poor families aboard; he arrives at a sincere and comfortable conviction that she is sound; he watches her sail; he collects the insurance when she goes down with all hands. Clifford's point is that the sincerity is irrelevant: the belief was not earned by inquiry but stolen by stifling doubt, and the owner would have been no less guilty had the ship arrived safely, because what matters is how he came by the belief, not what it was about or how it turned out. On this principle a person who inherits a creed and never examines it is guilty of a kind of theft from the community, and so is anyone who accepts a scientific result on the word of experts rather than checking it; Clifford's duty of inquiry, taken seriously, requires each of us to verify everything for himself, which of course no one can do.

James's reply, as the chapter has it, is that the principle is both unliveable and self-destructive. Where the evidence runs out — and it runs out exactly on the questions that matter most — a person is entitled to let his hopes and needs decide, since refusing to believe is also a way of losing the truth. His examples are the climber who must leap, the lover who must trust, the citizen who must assume that others will do their part: in such cases the belief partly brings about what it asserts, so intellectual scruple is self-defeating. The point is general, though what James mainly wanted was to vindicate religious faith, and he concedes that one can always suspend judgment — agnosticism is always a third possibility — but holds that on the important questions suspending judgment comes to the same thing as choosing against. Two things then kill Clifford's position. First, his own rule cannot be certified by its own standard, so by his own lights we are not permitted to believe it. Second, it forbids reliance on testimony, and no one can live that way. One thing kills James's: you cannot simply decide to believe. Belief is not the sort of thing the will can produce on command — I cannot now choose to believe the sky is green — so a defence of a "right" to believe at will defends nothing. The chapter concludes that James loses on the details but wins overall: Clifford's principle eats itself, and the sensible position is the pragmatist one, that what should concern us about a belief is not whether it is supported but whether holding it works.

The chapter then offers what it calls the most interesting contemporary development of James, "the leverage clause." The idea is that when adopting a belief would itself raise the probability that the belief is true, the shortfall in evidence may be made up by the size of the expected boost: the more a belief would help bring about its own truth, the weaker the evidence you need for it. So a founder who believes her company will succeed, a patient who believes she will recover, and a graduate student who believes her adviser is on her side may each permissibly believe against mildly adverse evidence, and the worse the evidence, the more the boost compensates, provided the boost is real. On this reading the only question that matters is whether believing would be better than not, and the further conditions — that the option be forced, momentous, live — drop out as surplus machinery.

I have three hunches and no confidence in any of them. The "you can't choose to believe" objection struck me as decisive when I read it, and then my flatmate's remark made me wonder whether it is a cheap trick: if belief really isn't under our control, doesn't that damage Clifford's blame of the shipowner at least as much as it damages James's permission, since Clifford accuses him of working himself into a frame of mind? The bit about testimony being forbidden sounds like a straw man to me — surely taking an expert's word is evidence, not the absence of evidence — but I can't say exactly where the chapter goes wrong. And the leverage clause reads to me like a base-rate fallacy with extra steps: a clinician who believes his treatment works, when the trial data are mildly negative, may indeed get better outcomes through his patients' trust, but that improves outcomes, not the truth of the belief, and I suspect the clause is quietly trading on that ambiguity.
