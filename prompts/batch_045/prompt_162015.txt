I am a novelist, and in three weeks I appear before the ethics committee of my national writers' association, which is deciding whether to censure me over a complaint filed by another member. I would like you to prepare me for the questioning.

The complaint concerns my most recent novel. Its architecture — the sequence of scenes, the disposition of chapters, the shape of the reveal, the way the subplot closes against the main plot — is taken, more or less whole, from a novel published in 1931 by a writer who died in 1968 and left no heirs and no estate worth tracing. Every sentence is mine. The period, the setting, the characters' trades, their names, and the ending are changed. The source is named in an afterword and again in the acknowledgments. A senior member of the association has written an open letter calling this plagiarism dressed up as homage, and the committee must now say something.

My defence, which I have about ten minutes to state, runs in three parts.

First, the familiar line between idea and expression is drawn in roughly the right place but justified by the wrong argument. The usual argument is that ideas are scarce in some sense, or that protecting them would chill later writers. My argument is different: architecture is not the kind of thing a writer can own because it is not the kind of thing a writer finishes. A plot skeleton is a solution to a set of local problems, and the problems recur; the same skeleton gets rebuilt independently, which is why independent-creation defences keep working in practice.

Second, what a writer actually makes — and the only thing that can genuinely be taken — is what I call a register of attention: the pattern of what this writer notices, dwells on, returns to, and conspicuously leaves out. That is a habit rather than an object. It can be imitated, learned from, and eventually echoed, but it cannot be stolen in the way a sentence can, and it is not the sort of item property rules are about. So the injury in plagiarism is not the loss of an asset. It is a falsification of the record of who attended to what, and that injury is repaired by naming, not by damages. Naming is not a courtesy added to the moral accounting; on my account it is most of the moral accounting.

Third, therefore, a three-tier proposal, which I will be asked to defend as policy and not merely as self-justification: sentences and their immediate surrounds get strong exclusive rights; architecture is free to reuse but subject to mandatory naming inside the text itself rather than buried in acknowledgments; and register of attention is protected, if at all, only by an inalienable right of attribution, never by an action for damages.

The committee has six members, and I can predict roughly who they are. Two are prize-winning literary novelists of my parents' generation, formed in the workshop tradition, who believe a writer's job is to find a voice that is unmistakably her own and who regard borrowing as a failure of that search. One is a fantasy novelist who was badly burned twenty years ago when a better-selling writer reused his magic system, and who thinks worldbuilding is plainly the product of labour and plainly property. One is a literary translator, who will hear my talk of sentences as property through the lens of her own derivative status. One is an academic literary scholar who takes the Romantic figure of the solitary originator to be an ideological construction and will suspect my register-of-attention talk of reinstating exactly that figure under a new name. The sixth is the association's honorary legal adviser, a retired copyright barrister who will speak in terms of what a court would actually do and who will not be moved by any argument that cannot be turned into a rule.

What I want from you is a prep sheet, not a defence. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get, in the order they are likely to arrive, each attributed to the member most likely to make it and phrased in that member's idiom rather than in mine — the barrister's objections should sound like a barrister, the fantasy novelist's like a working writer who has been robbed, the scholar's like a scholar. For each: how much damage it does to my three-part case if I mishandle it, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, and one line on where that reply is weakest. Tell me what I should concede before anyone raises it. And tell me the question I am hoping nobody asks.

One thing you should know, because it is the worst fact in the room: six years ago, in a published interview, I criticised a younger writer for structure-lifting from a novel of mine, and I used the word theft.
