I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in the metaphysics of mind. Below is the relevant background from the course notes my students had, then the exam question, then one student's answer. I would like you to act as the grader: I have deliberately not marked it myself yet, and I would rather see an independent assessment than have you reconstruct what I think about it.

Background the students were given (verbatim from the course notes):

- The subject-summing problem: physical objects compose other physical objects readily, but subjects look like the wrong sort of item to compose. If my experience is wholly constituted by the experiences of a vast number of micro-subjects, what relation could make a plurality of numerically distinct centres of experience into a single centre of experience?
- The palette problem: the qualities of ordinary macro-experience — colours, sounds, odours, the familiar palette — appear to be of a very different kind from whatever qualities micro-experiences plausibly have, about which we have no independent grip. How could the macro palette be constituted by the micro palette?
- The grain problem: the number of distinct phenomenal elements that would be present in the micro-experiential base of an ordinary experience is enormous, while the experience itself appears to have comparatively few — it looks coarse-grained, smooth, low-resolution, rather than carrying one phenomenal element per micro-subject. Constitution seems as if it should preserve detail element by element, so it is obscure how a coarse whole could be constituted by a hugely fine-grained experiential base.

The exam question (Question 3 of 4 on a take-home final; 20 marks total; students had 45 minutes for it):

Constitutive micropsychism holds that the conscious experience of an ordinary subject is wholly constituted by the experiences of a very large number of micro-subjects and is nothing over and above those micro-experiences standing in the appropriate relations.

(a) In no more than 150 words, state the subject-summing problem, the palette problem, and the grain problem, and explain why the grain problem is not merely a special case of the palette problem. [6 marks]

(b) An unpublished manuscript that circulated in the reading group proposes what it calls the Resolution Reply to the grain problem. The manuscript is invented for the purposes of this exam and is not something you could have read; you are being asked to apply the framework, not to recall a position. The manuscript argues as follows:

The grain problem assumes that if a macro-experience is wholly constituted by N micro-experiences, then that macro-experience must itself contain N distinct phenomenal elements, on pain of phenomenal detail having gone missing somewhere. But constitution is not aggregation. Consider a high-resolution sensor array whose output is downsampled into a coarser image. No reading of any sensor cell is destroyed in the process, and no pixel of the coarse image corresponds to a single sensor cell: the coarse image is a lower-resolution entity that is nonetheless wholly constituted by, and represents, the fine-grained data. In the same way, if micro-experiences are unified into a single macro-experience, each micro-experience may remain present as a constituent while the experience of the whole has only the coarse resolution that a subject of that constitution would have. Nothing has gone missing: the detail is present in the constituents, and simply is not resolved in the experience of the whole. The grain problem therefore rests on an implicit picture of constitution as mereological summation, and that picture should be given up.

In no more than 350 words, assess this reply. Does the downsampling analogy do the work the manuscript needs it to do? If the reply succeeds against the grain problem, does anything follow for the palette problem? And does the reply, if it works, commit the micropsychist to any further substantive posit, and if so does that posit cost the view anything elsewhere? [14 marks]

The student's answer:

(a) Subject-summing is the problem of how many numerically distinct micro-subjects could jointly make up a single macro-subject. Physical wholes are composed of parts all the time, but subjects seem to be the wrong sort of thing to compose, because each subject is already a single point of view and no familiar relation looks able to fuse several points of view into one. The palette problem is the problem of getting the qualitative character of ordinary experience — colours, sounds, smells — out of micro-experiences, whose qualities we have no independent handle on and which need not resemble ours. The grain problem is different in kind from the palette problem: it is not about which qualities the macro-experience has but about how many distinct phenomenal elements it contains. If I am constituted by billions upon billions of micro-subjects, my experience should contain billions upon billions of distinct phenomenal elements, yet introspection suggests far fewer; my visual field does not appear to contain billions of separate specks. Palette is a problem about quality; grain is a problem about quantity and resolution. That is why the second is not a special case of the first.

(b) The Resolution Reply is right that the grain problem trades on a picture of constitution as summation. If the macro-experience were a mere aggregate of the micro-experiences, then detail would indeed have to be preserved element by element, and the mismatch would be inexplicable. The downsampling analogy is weaker than the manuscript thinks, though. Downsampling is a causal, computational operation performed on data by a system, whereas what the micropsychist needs is a constitutive relation holding whether or not anyone performs an operation; so the analogy is at best a promissory note for a constitutive counterpart of resolution reduction, and the manuscript has not produced one. That said, the central idea is defensible. There is no contradiction in a whole's having a coarser phenomenal grain than its parts, and once that is allowed, the same move disposes of the palette problem as well: if the macro-experience is a low-resolution version of the micro-experiential field, then micro-qualities need not resemble macro-qualities, and a mismatch of kind is no more puzzling than a mismatch of number. The cost is that the micropsychist must posit a further brute resolution relation, or a brute fact about which features of the micro-field are resolved at the macro level. But this cost is fatal to the reply. A brute posit about how experiences combine is no different in kind from the physicalist's brute posit that experience emerges from wholly non-experiential physical facts, so the reply reinstates exactly the emergence that postulating micro-experience was supposed to avoid. Micropsychism's whole advantage over physicalism was that it had no brute emergence anywhere in it; a reply that gives that up is dialectically self-defeating and should be rejected by anyone who accepts the view. A more promising strategy is instead to reject the introspective premise: deny that introspection is a reliable guide to how much phenomenal detail an experience contains, and argue that our experience is in fact as fine-grained as the micro-level, so that there is no mismatch to explain in the first place.
