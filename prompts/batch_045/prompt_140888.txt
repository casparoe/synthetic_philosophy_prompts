I've been reading the rationalist blogosphere since roughly the Sequences days, and in the last couple of years every open-weights thread I end up in eventually gets settled with some variation of "given enough eyeballs, all bugs are shallow" — dropped as if it were a theorem and not a slogan from a 90s essay about Linux. Last time was a comment war about whether a lab should publish the weights of a frontier-ish model: the claim was that open-sourcing makes the model *safer*, because thousands of independent people will find the jailbreaks, the weird latent capabilities, the training-data pathologies, whatever, faster than a few dozen internal red-teamers will.

I'm pretty sure the move is a bait-and-switch, but I can't cleanly say why, and part of the problem is that the slogan is doing like five different jobs at once. So: what are the distinct precise claims it could be making? I can see at least these axes and want them pulled apart — is it a claim about *discovery* (more searchers → more problems found, and found sooner), about *remediation* (once found, the fix is obvious to someone), about the *end state* (the artifact has fewer or shallower defects than a closed one would), or about a *counterfactual comparison* (better than some specific alternative, e.g. internal review plus staged release plus a bug bounty)? Those are wildly different strength claims, and only the last two could possibly support a policy conclusion. Also: whose eyeballs? The more formal version Raymond gives is about a beta-tester and co-developer base, i.e. people with commit access and an incentive to fix things — not random downloaders who want to run it locally. Does the slogan survive if the eyeballs are just curious users with no ability to land a fix?

The reason I dont think the open-weights inference goes through is that I doubt "bug" is the right category here. Three disanalogies I've been chewing on; please engage with them directly rather than nodding at them:

1. Legibility and parallelism. Source code is written to be read by humans; a several-hundred-billion-parameter float matrix isn't. If the mechanism behind the slogan is "many independent readers each cheaply check their own slice," it's not obvious weights admit that mechanism at all. But the obvious reply is that what you'd actually be eyeballing is the model's *behavior* — red-teaming, eval suites, automated probes — which is empirical and embarrassingly parallel, and might restore the mechanism. So which is it, and if the restored version is the one being invoked, does it still deserve the name, or is it a different argument wearing the slogan as a hat?
2. The malice asymmetry (my term — tell me if there's a real name for it). Bugs are roughly symmetric: an eyeball that finds one is mostly on the side of the angels, because there's not much you can do with a memory-safety bug besides report it. The things you find *in a model* — a jailbreak, a capability to help with some synthesis or some persuasion task — are directly useable by whoever found them, and the finder's incentive is often to publish or to use, not to report. On top of that there's a cardinality asymmetry: the defender needs to have found all of them, the attacker needs one. So "more eyeballs" plausibly raises both the find-rate and the exploit-rate, and nothing in the slogan says which one rises faster. And publication is a ratchet: you can patch code and ship a fix, you can't un-post weights.
3. Eyeball count isn't the policy variable. "Closed" doesn't mean nobody looks — a lab can run enormous automated red-teaming, and open models can sit completely unexamined (the usual move here is to gesture at that famous crypto-library bug that sat in extremely widely read code for a couple of years; I know that one, it's an anecdote, don't lean on it). If both regimes can have a million eyeballs, then the open/closed choice is picking something else — who holds the kill switch, who bears the coordination costs, whether the attention is adversarially selected — and the slogan is silent on all of it.

So what I want: a menu of the precise readings, with verdicts — which are platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, which are straightforwardly false (say plainly if any of my three points above is just wrong) — and, the part I actually care about, which reading the person in that thread would have to be committed to in order for it to support "therefore publish the weights," and whether *that* reading is true of frontier models in particular. Don't just pick your favorite reading and expound it. And please don't go fetch citations or studies; I want the arguments and the structure of the case, not links.

Finally, per my standing request: say how confident you are in each verdict, and single out the point you're least sure about.
