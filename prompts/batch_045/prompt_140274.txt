I've got a slightly odd one and I'm not even sure it's philosophy, so tell me to sod off if it isn't.

I maintain the knowledge model for an incident-review tool at work. It ingests a pile of maintenance records and then has to answer questions like "did anyone on the ops team know, on 4 March, that the pressure valve was out of spec?" So it keeps track of what various people are on record as knowing, and then answers queries about that. Two things keep happening, and they feel like the same sort of thing pointing in opposite directions, and I need a word for whatever it is.

Undershooting. The store will happily chain a long run of conditionals — A brings about B, B brings about C, all the way out to K — so long as every step uses the same expression for the same thing. But it comes apart the moment a step requires noticing that two descriptions pick out the same thing. It has that the badge issued at 06:40 on the 4th was used to open the pump room. It has that the badge issued at 06:40 on the 4th was Okonjo's. Ask it "did Okonjo enter the pump room?" and you get *unknown*, not no — as if the question had never been put. There are only three links. It isn't the length. It's the identity in the middle. And it's not a synonyms thing: "the badge issued at 06:40" isn't slang for Okonjo, and the model has both. Nor is it generality — give it "every valve in bay 3 was re-certified on 12 March" plus "this is a valve in bay 3" and it's fine. Strangely, ask "was the pump room opened by anybody?" and it says yes; it's only once Okonjo's name is in the question that it dries up.

Overshooting, meanwhile, goes the other way. Ask whether any component was run outside spec that week and it comes back confident, because for each component it has no row saying there was a problem, and the absence of a row is being treated as a fact and then generalised over. Nobody told it the records were complete. One of our data people says that's just the closed-world assumption and can be configured off, but that's an answer about how the query engine behaves, and what I want to know about is what the person in the system can be said to know.

So: is there an established concept for this pair, or for whatever the single thing underneath both is? And are they really one thing? My guess is the interesting bit is that the failures don't line up where you'd expect — it isn't that the model is too slow or too small to derive hard things (it does long derivations fine), it's that it's shut on one axis and wide open on another, and the axis is about re-description rather than effort. But I've not been able to work out whether that's a real distinction or whether I'm inventing one.

Please don't just hand back a label. I want to know what the thing is, what it isn't, what the nearest few established notions are and precisely how my cases sit against each — including the ones that sound right but aren't — and, for whichever fits best, a case that falls just on the wrong side of the line so I can see the edge. If there genuinely isn't an established concept, say so and coin one, with a definition and a boundary case. A few paragraphs of discussion first, and then the table.

Table, one row per candidate you canvass (established or invented), columns in this order:

- `candidate` — the name
- `home_field` — the area it normally belongs to, one phrase
- `covers_undershoot` — fits / partly / no, against the identity-link failure above
- `covers_overshoot` — fits / partly / no, against the no-row-means-fine failure
- `where_it_misfits` — one sentence: the respect in which it breaks against my cases
- `verdict` — keep / reshape / drop

If you end up coining something, give it its own row and use `where_it_misfits` for the nearest thing it could be confused with.
