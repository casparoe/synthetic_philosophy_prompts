I work as the policy analyst to the Community Safety and Licensing Committee of our city council. The committee has to send a recommendation on a new adult-services licensing bylaw to full council in three weeks, and I need the draft memo I will put into the committee package under my own name.

The immediate trigger is a licence application from a nine-person worker-owned collective that currently works out of separate apartments and wants to operate one licensed premises: a shared reception and booking service, a panic-button system in each room, a maintained list of clients they will not see, and a house rule against accepting bookings from intoxicated clients. They have also told us, in a consultation submission, that they do not want their names entered in any municipal registration database, on the grounds that registration creates risks of outing, of consequences for immigration status, and of harm to future employment outside the trade.

Three positions have formed on the committee. Two members support an end-demand approach of the kind associated with the Nordic model: penalise or deter the buyer, treat the seller as someone to be protected rather than punished, and put real pressure on advertising and on third parties. Two support full decriminalisation along broadly New Zealand lines, removing municipal and provincial regulation specific to the trade and letting ordinary business, tenancy and employment law do the work. One supports a legalisation-and-registration model of the German or Dutch kind: licensed and inspected premises, mandatory registration, health and safety standards. The police chief's stated priority is only that the trade not spill into residential streets, which cuts against the licensing of concentrated premises and in favour of dispersal.

Our municipal lawyer has advised that the city cannot create criminal offences; what we control is business licensing, licence conditions, zoning, and bylaw-enforcement priorities. Anything that would criminalise purchase, advertising or third-party involvement has to be requested from the provincial Attorney General and the police service as a complementary enforcement policy, and the memo has to be explicit about what we are asking them for and why.

Please draft the memo as follows.

Give the bottom line up front: a recommendation, with the reasoning, and then the strongest consideration against it stated honestly rather than dismissed.

Rather than recommending a country model wholesale, unbundle the decision. Specify separately what the city should do about buyers, about sellers, about third parties and platforms and landlords, and about advertising; and for each, say whether the recommended tool is a criminal-law request to the province, a municipal licensing or zoning condition, or deliberate municipal inaction. Justify each cell, because the committee has been treating these as a package deal and I don't think they are.

Two things in particular need to be worked through rather than asserted. First, the asymmetry: if we ask the province to penalise purchase while insisting that sellers be shielded, what exactly is the rationale, and what does that rationale imply about the competence and the standing of the sellers' own testimony — including the collective's submission, which we solicited and now have to take seriously or explain away? Second, the purpose of the bylaw: state in a purpose clause whether the city's aim is to reduce the incidence of the practice or to govern its conditions, since the committee is split on whether the practice's incidence is itself the harm, and the two readings will support very different bylaw designs.

Apply whatever rationale you settle on to adjacent cases as a consistency check — paid surrogacy, licensed strip-club work, adult film production, paid care work involving intimate touching — and say plainly whether the rationale condemns those too, permits them, or requires a distinction, and whether that result is acceptable.

I don't want the memo leaning on particular studies; we can't resolve the empirical literature here and the committee won't read it. Where a claim depends on an empirical assumption, name the assumption rather than the source, and say how the recommendation would change if the assumption failed.

Finally, include a sunset and review clause with metrics and a review trigger. The three camps disagree about what success even looks like, so propose metrics that a supporter of each model would at least accept as relevant evidence even while disputing that it is decisive — including whether a measured decline in the number of people selling sex should count as success, failure, or neither.

Format it as a committee memo of roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words, with headings, and close with a short paragraph of motion language that council could actually pass.
