hey — I need a piece of satire for a packet I'm putting together. It should make someone who's actually read the philosophical/policy literature on the precautionary principle laugh, and still be readable for someone who hasn't.

Write a mock op-ed, headline plus 650–800 words, in the voice of the founding chair of a small town's Office of Precautionary Review — call her Marlene Duff, call the town Coldspring, and set it on the office's thirtieth anniversary in the local paper. The concrete occasion: back in the mid-nineties her office blocked the water department from relining the town's old wooden mains with a new polymer, on the strength of one small study in rats. Three decades later the polymer is standard everywhere, the wooden mains are failing, and the office still won't lift the ban. Marlene is writing to explain why, and to announce that review will now be extended to ongoing activities as well as new ones.

The jokes have to come from getting the position right, not from inventing a silly version of it. Work in:

- the strong, cost-insensitive form: she must never once say the benefits outweigh the risks. Every time she's tempted toward a cost argument — the mains bursting, the repair bills, the boil-water notices — she recasts it as a safety argument instead.
- the reversal of the burden of proof: the person proposing the new thing has to disprove the harm, not the alarmed neighbour prove it.
- the point that absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence, and specifically that thirty years without an incident is exactly what you'd expect from a slow, irreversible harm.
- the symmetry problem: doing nothing is also risky, so the principle as stated has no verdict. Give her a deadpan rule for breaking the tie — something like: where both courses carry a threat, we don't weigh, we just continue whatever was already happening before anyone filed anything — and let her own the consequence (the one-way ratchet, the fact that a precaution can never be repealed because repeal is a fresh action requiring proof of safety) as a feature rather than a bug.
- the fact that the ban blocks the very trials that would settle whether the fear was ever real, so the ban can't be empirically dislodged. She should find this reassuring.
- self-application: applying the principle to the adopting of precautions produces a regress, and her office quietly exempts itself by grandfathering. She might mention, without embarrassment, that a precautionary moratorium on precautions could never be adopted either, since nobody could prove the moratorium safe.

Include at least one quoted critic — a retired statistician or the high-school chemistry teacher — who raises the real objection (that a principle forbidding every option, inaction included, ends up prescribing nothing in particular), and have Marlene grant the premise and draw her own consequence from it rather than dodging it.

Constraints: total deadpan, no winking, and the narration should never use words like "absurd", "ironic", or "of course" — a reader should be able to tell what's being ironized without Marlene or the prose ever signalling it. Don't let her slip into cost-benefit reasoning halfway through, and don't turn her into a cartoon: everything she says should be something a real defender of the strongest version of the principle could say with a straight face. Someone who doesn't know the literature should just read it as a funny small-town column; someone who does should finish it feeling the paralysis objection, the self-refutation worry and the ratchet problem without any of them ever being named.
