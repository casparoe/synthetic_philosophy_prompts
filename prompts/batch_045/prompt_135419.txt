I'm writing the final for my upper-level seminar on evolution, computation, and mind — 15 students, in-class, 75 minutes, and they get the question sheet a week ahead so they can mull it over but write it cold. The spine of the course has been the two strange inversions of reasoning: Darwin's, where you stop explaining the fit between a creature and its world by a foresightful designer's reasons and instead get the fit from a foresight-free filter, so the reasons show up last, as an effect rather than a cause; and Turing's, where you stop explaining calculation by a mind that grasps what it's doing and instead get it from a mindless mechanism following rules, so comprehension comes last there too. Every single year I get the same essay back: Darwin showed design without a designer, Turing showed you don't need understanding to compute, so a mind is just a machine that evolution built, the end. My TA thinks I should be happy with that. I'm not.

So here's the mess I've been circling and can't get right. I want to hand them a fake candidate's thesis — I've been calling it the Ratchet Principle — which runs roughly like this: the two inversions are literally the same argument, since in both cases you swap a reason-explanation for a mechanism-explanation; and once you've made both of them you get comprehension for free, because comprehensions are just what you get by strapping a lot of comprehension-free competences together and letting them ratchet, and understanding was never a glow, it was a competence that further competences can be built on. Mabye I should also give them a second half where the same candidate says that this is exactly why the Chinese Room misses the point. Then ask: what's right here, and where exactly does it break.

The joint I actually want them to find is that the two inversions invert different things. Darwin's is an etiological point about where the fit comes from — how a competence can be reliably about something and serve an end without anybody entertaining that end — whereas Turing's is a point about execution, about how a mechanism can be reason-respecting step by step without being reason-responsive, without knowing why. And neither of those, nor the two together, obviously gives you comprehension out of competences, because stacking comprehension-free layers doesn't automatically make a comprehending whole. So they should have to say what the extra ingredient would even have to be (self-monitoring? being able to reuse or cite the reasons you're moved by? a history that fixes what your states are about?) and whether that ingredient is a legitimate crane or a skyhook with the serial numbers filed off. And they should have to face the switcheroo worry head on — that the Ratchet Principle only looks convincing because understanding has been quietly redefined as competence — so that the right answer isn't Dennett wins or Searle wins, it's locating which move is carrying the weight. Could you write me one essay question along those lines, doable in 75 minutes and 1200 to 1500 words, worth 20 marks total, plus a model answer of about 700 words and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points with the marks for each. One line per point on how people usually blow it, please — last year half the class just restated design-without-a-designer, a few answered by summarizing the Chinese Room as if that settled something, and one person wrote two pages saying it's all emergent. Keep it at the level of nameable moves rather than a reading list: a student who only has the course's own version of the two inversions should be able to score full marks without citing anybody, and the marks should seperate the ones who found the joint from the ones who memorized the slogans.
