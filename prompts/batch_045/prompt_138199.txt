I'm a software engineer with no philosophy background, and my team of about five is about to ship an LLM agent that takes real actions on customers' behalf: opens PRs, emails customers, moves money inside a budget cap. We've got roughly six months of one person's time plus occasional lawyer hours to spend on the "who's on the hook when this thing screws up" question. Reading around, I keep hitting the responsibility gap literature, and it mostly seems to argue about whether anyone can be blamed at all — which might be interesting but doesn't obviously tell me what to build or write in the runbook.

So here's what I actually want: among the open questions in this area, which deserve our limited time, and which of the alleged practical upshots are real versus overrated or just don't follow? The three candidate claims I keep running into:

1. The "gap" is really three different things people lump together — whether anyone can be blamed, whether anyone owes anyone an account or explanation, and whether anyone is in a position to fix things going forward — and it matters a lot which one we're trying to close.
2. "Keep a human in the loop" does nothing if the human can only rubber-stamp, so the real design target is meaningful control, and we should be measuring whether our operators actually have it instead of whether a human exists somewhere in the chain.
3. Since blame may genuinely be unavailable, we should just accept strict liability for whatever the agent does and price it in.

Rank those by how much they'd change what we build, and defend the ranking: which is upstream of which, which we could settle ourselves in six months using our own logs and incident data versus which needs actual philosophers, and what we'd have to observe to move something up or down the list. Also be honest if one of these is a fake problem that dissolves once you stop using one word for three different things — I'd rather drop it than write a design doc about it.
