Hey. I help run a small peer support group for people with chronic neuropathic pain — mostly trigeminal neuralgia and CRPS, about eight of us on Zoom. One of our volunteer facilitators does a secular meditation thing on the side and keeps closing discussions with "pain is inevitable, suffering is optional." Last week he aimed it straight at me: I'd been arguing that the group should put some energy into a letter-writing campaign to our health authority about access to nerve blocks and to pain meds that are getting harder to get prescribed, and he said the line as if it settled it — as if it showed the campaign was aiming at the wrong target, that the actual work is internal and nobody can do it for you. I said something snarky and now I want to get clear instead.

I've also been reading a bit about tranquilism — the view that what matters, welfare-wise, is basically just the absence of craving and suffering rather than the presence of positive states — and it makes the question sharper rather than easier.

Here's my own stake in it. For a few months I've kept a nightly three number log: S = how intense the unpleasant sensation was that day, 0 to 10; G = "grip", how much of the day my attention was caught up in wanting it to stop, bracing against it, checking on it; T = "sting", my end-of-day verdict on how bad the whole thing was. What got me is that these numbers come apart in ways I can't predict: some days G is high and T is low, and on two of the objectively worst days S was 8 and T was 3, which feels like it definately shouldn't happen. I no longer trust that I know what any of these words mean.

So: can you take that one sentence apart for me? I want the menu, not a verdict on the slogan as a whole — the distinct precise claims it could be making, from the ones that are just a stipulation about how to use the word "suffering" up to the ones that are genuinely load-bearing. For each reading I want to know: what "pain" and "suffering" cash out as under it; whether it's a conceptual point, an empirical claim about how minds work, a normative claim, or just practical advice wearing a philosophical costume; whether it's trivial, substantive and defensible, contested, false, or quietly blaming the person in pain; and — the bit I actually care about — whether it would really support the move he made, i.e. that campaigning for better access to pain treatment is aiming at the wrong thing. Include at least one reading on which the campaign obviously still matters and one on which it definitely doesn't.

Two more things while you're in there. First, for each reading say what it predicts about my log: which of S, G and T should track which, and which column a tranquilist should stop filling in. Second, there's a case I keep turning over that feels like either a reductio or the whole point, and I can't tell which: a person with the same nerve damage as me but zero craving, zero resistance, no "get it away from me" at all — on the strong readings, does such a person lose nothing that matters? Say plainly whether you think that's the intended upshot of the slogan or a sign the reading has gone wrong.

Give it to me as a markdown table, one row per reading, with exactly these columns: Reading (the precise thesis in one sentence), PainMeans, SufferingMeans, ClaimType, Verdict, SupportsFacilitator (yes / no / only if), LogPrediction. Order the rows from most trivial to most substantive. Then, under the table, two lines: one giving your overall confidence in the whole thing out of 10, and one naming the single claim in your answer you're least sure about and why.

Please don't give me a reading list or citations — I can't chase references and I don't want them. Just argue for the distinctions.
