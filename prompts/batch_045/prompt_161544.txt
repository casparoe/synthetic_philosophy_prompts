I'm a PhD student in political theory, working at the boundary with comparative historical sociology, and I'm revising a chapter on whether historical atrocities can be ranked without obscenity. Before I commit to a position in print I'd like an audit of the package I've been defending in supervision, because I can't locate where it strains. Here is the set, as I actually hold it:

(1) Anti-exceptionalism. Comparisons are unavoidable: any decision about commemoration, reparations, humanitarian priority, or what gets taught already ranks cases, and a refusal to compare is itself a ranking — a default allocation of attention and resources. So the only question is how to compare, never whether. In particular I reject uniqueness and incomparability claims, including the claim that any one case is beyond comparison; as far as I can tell, such claims function to insulate a case from scrutiny, and they are usually advanced by people with an interest in that case's standing.

(2) One currency, additively aggregated. The badness of an atrocity is a function of the suffering and death it causes; deaths count the same whoever dies; and the marginal badness of a death is roughly constant, so two million is close to twice as bad as one million, other things equal. Rankings by magnitude are therefore, other things equal, the correct rankings.

(3) Intent is not merely epistemic. Deliberate extermination is worse, other things equal, than the same number of deaths produced as a foreseen but unwanted side effect of a policy adopted for other ends; a smaller intentional killing can thus be the greater evil. I want this to be a claim about the evil of the event, not only about the blameworthiness of the agents who brought it about.

(4) Some losses exceed the deaths that compose them. When what is destroyed is a group with a distinctive form of life, the loss includes the non-existence of that form of life and of the future it would have had, and this is not captured by counting the dead. This is why I find myself wanting to say that the destruction of a small community by the people who had lived alongside it can be worse than a much larger, administratively organised killing that did not target a form of life.

(5) Counterfactual framing. I want cases compared by how much worse they left the world, measured against the closest baseline in which the atrocity did not occur.

(6) Evidentiary dualism. I think comparisons must be anchored in the testimony and the moral phenomenology of survivors, since aggregative demography cannot tell you what the loss was like for those who suffered it; and I also think demographic estimates built largely from perpetrator records are indispensable, because the most efficient killing leaves no one to testify, so a testimony-anchored method systematically undercounts precisely the cases I am most likely to rank at the top.

(7) The obscenity caveat. A comparative judgment can be true and still be obscene to utter: when it is performed as competitive victimhood, when it is performed by an outsider with no stake in the cases, or when it is performed in a register of connoisseurship. There are contexts in which a correct ranking should be withheld.

(8) Individuation. I have been individuating atrocities by perpetrator aim, so that a cluster of killing programmes sharing an aim counts as a single atrocity while occupation famines or battlefield deaths count as separate ones. But I worry that "the worst atrocity of the century" then depends on how finely we carve, which makes the ranking look arbitrary in a way I can't afford given (1).

What I want is an audit, not a survey of the literature. Please tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which only look like it, ordered by severity rather than listed as equally bad; for each genuine tension, say what would have to give and what giving it would cost me elsewhere in the set; and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. If I am collapsing a distinction — between the badness of an outcome, the evil of an event, and the culpability of an agent, say — name it, and say which of my claims survive once it is drawn. Be specific about which numbered commitment has to go: I would rather drop one thesis than discover that I have been relying on all eight while quietly switching between them.
