hey — I write a column on science and ideas and I'm finally doing the long piece I've been putting off about polarization, specifically about that moment in every argument where someone says the other lot isn't arguing in good faith. Everyone has a theory about it, and I don't want to read another one until I know what I should be demanding of one. So: write me the spec.

What I need it for: I'm going to have to make calls in print. Given a real dispute — a school board fight, a faculty row, a comment section — I want to be able to say whether the bad-faith accusation is actually warranted, or whether it's just the move people make when they've run out of answers. So I need something diagnostic, for real cases, usable by someone who is inside the mess and can't step outside it. Not a formal model. And please no bibliography, footnotes, or reading list — I want the arguments; sources I can chase myself later.

The list, then: what an account of echo chambers / good-faith disagreement has to deliver, with the case or consideration behind each requirement. Here are the things I keep tripping over in interviews and reporting — use them, replace them, or tell me one of them is a bad demand:

- The distinction I keep having to make between people who literally never encounter the other side's arguments (they just don't reach them) and people who encounter them constantly but have been supplied with a standing reason to distrust anyone who says them. Those feel like different failures with different fixes, but from the outside I cant always tell them apart, and I'm not sure a theory owes me a clean line here.
- I trust my mechanic about my brakes and my doctor about my scan, and in a structural sense I'm in exactly the thing I'm supposed to be worried about: I take their word and can't check it. Any test for "echo chamber" that also condemns that is broken, I think — but I don't know how to state the exception without making it swallow the rule.
- The difference between being wrong and sincere, being wrong and indifferent to whether you're wrong, and straight-up lying. I've read somewhere that indifference to truth is its own thing, distinct from lying and distinct from honest error, and it seems to me the accusation "bad faith" collapses all three. Does an adequate theory have to keep them apart?
- An asymmetry problem: sometimes the out-group really is worse off epistemically. If the theory can never return that verdict it's useless to me; if it returns it too easily it's a weapon. Is that a contradiction or just a calibration problem?
- A hypothetical I want it to handle. Suppose you take people on each side and show them the other side's actual arguments, verbatim and unlabelled, and they rate them as noticeably more reasonable than the labelled versions — yet nobody's views move at all. Assume that's what happens. Does an adequate account have to explain that gap, or is it social psych's problem and not the theory's?
- A case I keep coming back to: a small band of researchers in some field, dismissed as cranks for twenty years, who turn out to have been right. Structurally that looks a lot like the groups I'm supposed to diagnose. Does the account owe me a way to separate the two, and if not, why not?
- Scope: does one theory have to cover both "she is arguing in bad faith" (about a person) and "this is an echo chamber" (about an information environment), or is it fine to do one and punt the other? I suspect conflating them is where a lot of bad commentary comes from, but I'd rather you tell me.

Also: mark which requirements are non-negotiable for my purposes and which are preferences of one camp — I suspect some people are smuggling in a requirement that the theory be even-handed between left and right, and I genuinely don't know if that's a real desideratum or a political taste. Flag any pair of requirements on your list that probably can't be satisfied together, because if the list turns out to be impossible, that's the story. And tell me which single requirement does the most work in ruling out the standard stories people recieve from pundits — "it's their media diet", "motivated reasoning", "they're signaling tribal loyalty, not stating beliefs".

At the very end, three lines max on how those standard stories fare against your list. The list is the deliverable; I'll do the scoring in the piece.
