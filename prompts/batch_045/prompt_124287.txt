I'm revising a chapter on the original/derived intentionality distinction and I've built an argument that I think overreaches — but I can't locate the overreach, and I'd be grateful for a diagnosis.

The device I use is one I'll call an orphaned network. Take a deep-space probe whose control system is a large network of Dennett-style two-bitsers — adders whose states we are happy to treat as representing numbers and sums — fed by transducers at the input end and driving the probe's actuators at the output end. Launch it; let it run autonomously for a century; suppose no human ever reads any of its internal states or outputs, and that everyone who designed it is long dead. It steers, corrects and records, and nothing it does makes a difference to any mind.

The argument:

(1) Representational content is derived when it is fixed by the attitudes — design intentions, interpretive practices, or use — of some agent that already has content.
(2) Original intentionality, in Searle's sense, is intrinsic: a state's content is not fixed by anything outside the subject, and only conscious subjects have it.
(3) Derived content is inherited: any chain of derived representations must terminate in a bearer of original intentionality, on pain of nothing in the chain having content at all.
(4) The orphaned network has no such terminus: no living interpreter reads it, and its designers are dead. So if (3) is right, none of its states represent anything — including right now, while it is steering the probe.
(5) But they plainly do represent: its success and failure conditions — mis-tracking a star position, mis-summing a course correction — are as determinate as anyone's, and the whole point of the design was that it would keep track of things after no one was left to interpret it.
(6) So (4) is false, and one of two things follows: either the network's content is fixed once and for all by the dead designers' past intentions, so that 'fixed by the attitudes of an agent' in (1) means only historically caused by them rather than currently interpreted by them; or content need not bottom out in an original source at all.
(7) On the first horn, (1) no longer marks a contrast with anything. An evolved organism's internal states, or a beaver's dam-building responses, are historically caused by selection, which is no agent with attitudes. If that counts as fixing content, then content is naturalized and is not second-class in Searle's sense; if it does not count, then dead designers cannot fix the network's content either, and we are back to (4). On the second horn, derived intentionality does not need original intentionality, and again there is no contrast class.
(8) So either no state of anything — including my own beliefs — has content in the original sense, or the original/derived distinction is empty, and with it the claim that the Chinese Room system does not understand Chinese.

I am committed to (5) and would be sorry to give up (2). My suspicion is that something shifts between (1), (3) and (6): perhaps an equivocation over 'fixed by', perhaps the assumption that inheritance must be well-founded rather than distributed or looping, perhaps an assumption about whether content requires even the possibility of an interpreter. I cannot say which, or what each move costs.

Please tell me which step you reject, why that step looked compulsory, and what each of the main available positions — Searlean intrinsicality, Dennett-style derivedness all the way down, and teleosemantic or causal-informational accounts of content — has to pay in order to reject it. Do not just stipulate that the distinction survives; show me where the chain breaks and what the alternatives cost. After the diagnosis, give the assessment as a table with the columns: Step; Verdict (keep / reject / reinterpret); Why the step looked fine; What rejecting it costs; Which position can best afford that cost.
