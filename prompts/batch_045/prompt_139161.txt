I've been going back and forth on Garrett Hardin's lifeboat essay and the Mignonette case and I want someone to actually put them together properly, because I think both come out looking different than either does alone.

The case, so you don't have to dredge it up: the yacht Mignonette sank on 5 July 1884 about 1600 miles off the coast, northwest of the Cape. Four men got away in a 13-foot open boat with two tins of turnips and no fresh water: captain Tom Dudley, mate Edwin Stephens, seaman Edmund Brooks, and Richard Parker, the 17-year-old cabin boy and the least experienced hand aboard. They caught a turtle around 9 July, which with the turnips kept them till roughly the middle of the month; from about 13 July they were drinking their own urine. Parker fell ill around 20 July from drinking seawater and by 23 or 24 July was probably in a coma. Drawing lots was talked about a couple of times and never resolved; Brooks refused. Dudley and Stephens then decided to kill Parker, and Dudley's stated reason for doing it then rather than waiting was that killing him before he died naturally meant there would be blood to drink. Brooks neither agreed nor objected and later ate. Dudley opened Parker's jugular with a penknife. All three were picked up a few days later alive, tried, convicted, sentenced to death, and had it commuted to six months.

Here's roughly where I want you to go with it, though don't feel bound to my list.

First, do the mapping honestly. Who is the player in a lifeboat-ethics analysis of this? Is it the four in the boat, or is it the court deciding afterwards, or the public? What is the boat's capacity even supposed to be — a holed 13-foot dinghy with no water arguably has a capacity of zero, in which case the central question the theory is built to answer never arises. And what are the options: kill now, kill later, wait for him to die, draw lots, nobody eats.

Second, the thing I keep tripping on, which I'll call the capacity/selection split. Hardin's whole apparatus is about a boat that can hold some fixed number and whether to pull more people in. Everyone on the Mignonette was already aboard. So one reading is that lifeboat ethics is simply silent here: capacity is settled, and all that remains is selection, which the theory has no rules for — it just quietly assumes whoever happens to be in the boat is the right set of people. The other reading is that the theory does have a hidden selection rule and the case exposes it. Argue for one and tell me what it costs.

Third, my own pet worry, the timing thing. Dudley killed early for the blood. But that means the earlier you act, the bigger the survival benefit and the weaker the claim that you only took someone who was already lost; the later you act, the stronger that claim and the smaller the benefit. The justification seems to eat itself: the very feature that makes the killing useful (doing it while he's still alive) is the one that makes it look like murder rather than harvesting. Formalize that trade-off and say what a lifeboat ethicist is supposed to say about the optimal killing time, if there is one.

Fourth, Brooks. He refused the procedure and took the benefit. Is that coherent? And all three lived, so the killing worked on the terms the theory cares about — can the theory condemn a necessity killing that succeeded, or does it have to move to a rule about rules rather than outcomes?

Then the contrast I'd like: the William Brown in 1841, where an overcrowded longboat was lightened by forcing a dozen or so male passengers overboard while the whole crew stayed aboard, and the one sailor who could be found, Alexander Holmes, got manslaughter and six months plus a fine. Roughly the same punishment as Dudley and Stephens for a lot more deaths. On your account, is that consistent or is it an accident? I don't know whether lots were actually drawn in that case — I have a vague memory the judge said something about selecting by lot, but if that turns out to be false I'd rather you said so than built on it.

Finally, does the episode help Hardin or hurt him? My suspicion is that the real lifeboat cases are all about who gets thrown out and almost never about who gets pulled in, which would make the metaphor do the opposite of what he wanted it to do, but I'm not confident and I want the argument both ways.

And be explicit about your own fragility: which link in your mapping would you drop first if pushed, where are you filling in the facts from something less than certainty, and what single fact or argument — lots actually drawn, Parker consenting, Parker being the strongest rather than the weakest, a real chance of rescue in sight — would make you change the verdict? I'd rather have the conditional version of your answer than a flat one.
