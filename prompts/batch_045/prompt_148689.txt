I work in insurance claims, have no academic background in philosophy, and about a year ago got into an argument with my brother-in-law about whether it is ever fine to stay quiet when someone has made a mistake in your favour. He lent me Kant's short book on the foundations of ethics — the one with the four examples about a promise you never intend to keep, ending your own life to escape misery, letting your talents sit unused, and never helping anyone — and since then three of us have been meeting monthly to run ordinary decisions through his test. What I want is not a summary of Kant but a diagnosis of my own verdicts: I have been keeping a list of them, they feel consistent to me, and I cannot state the principle they answer to. My working rule is that a maxim fails if, once everyone acted on it, the thing the maxim is a way of doing could no longer be done — promising dies if everyone breaks promises, and so on. Some of my verdicts fit that rule and some do not, and I would like to know which ones have to go, and whether the rule can be repaired rather than replaced.

Here are the verdicts, given as I wrote them down. Promising to repay a friend with no intention of repaying: fails, cleanly. Inflating my income on a loan application: fails, same shape. Telling a woman at a car-boot sale that the painting she is pricing at twenty pounds is a worthless print, when I am fairly sure it is the real thing, so I can buy it cheap: fails. Standing silent while that same seller, entirely unprompted, prices it at twenty pounds, with the same knowledge and the same outcome: fine, or at least very much less bad — and I cannot say why, since in both cases she acts on a false belief and I get a bargain. Leaving an enthusiastic review for a friend's café when my meal was only mediocre: fine, even kind; yet if everyone did it, reviews would stop doing their job, which by my rule should sink it. Telling my sister her poem is good when I think it is mediocre: fine — structurally the same as the café case, a false statement made for the hearer's benefit, except that it does not lean on any institution. Undercutting a rival's quote to win a contract: fine, even though if everyone undercut, nobody would win by undercutting, which makes me suspect my rule conflates "everyone doing this destroys the practice" with "everyone doing this destroys the point of my doing it." Never helping anyone while being perfectly willing to be helped if things go badly: fails, but not in the way the promise case fails — the world it describes is perfectly possible, I simply cannot will being in it while expecting help. Never developing any of my abilities and coasting through a job I could do well: I want to say fails, but shakily, and I cannot locate the collapse. Going through my partner's phone while she is in the shower: clearly wrong — and yet universalising snooping does not seem to break anything, so my rule is silent exactly where I am most confident. Leaving my phone on during a film: universalising makes the cinema miserable, so my rule says it fails, but that is bad manners and not immorality, and if the rule cannot tell the difference I do not trust it. The case I keep going back and forth on: booking two tables at a restaurant on a busy Saturday and not cancelling the one I do not use. It feels wrong, but the practice of booking does not vanish — restaurants would add deposits or penalties — so everything depends on whether "booking without a penalty" is the thing I am supposed to check for. I have also noticed that I can sink almost any maxim by describing it broadly and rescue almost any maxim by describing it narrowly, which makes me wonder whether the test is really a test of how honestly I describe what I am doing.

So: is there one principle, or a small set of candidates, that delivers these verdicts — stated precisely enough that I can see which clause is doing the work in which case, and including whatever filter distinguishes matters of morality from matters of etiquette, and whatever fixes the level of description at which a maxim is to be stated? Tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which you would overturn, and whether the cost of the principle is that I have to give up the intuition I am most attached to. Then tell me what it predicts about two cases I have not decided: claiming a tax deduction for a home office I use twice a year, and using an automated bot to buy four tickets to a concert and reselling two of them above face value. And one last worry I would like addressed directly: when I ask "what if everybody did that?", am I really doing something Kantian at all, or have I quietly become a rule-utilitarian estimating consequences while using his vocabulary?
