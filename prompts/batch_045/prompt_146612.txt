I sit on the standards committee of a national association of structural engineers, and we are halfway through a revision of our code of professional conduct. The sticking point is a proposed clause encouraging members to volunteer their expertise for unpaid post-disaster safety assessments — entering damaged buildings, working without indemnity, on their own time. One senior member keeps killing the proposal with the same line: that sort of thing is "beyond the call of duty," and, as he also puts it, you can't legislate heroism. We do have a live third option, which is to move the clause into an annex labeled aspirational. The complication is that the annex would still be citable in promotion files and in the mitigating-factors stage of our disciplinary hearings, so an aspirational clause may convert the optional into the expected through informal pressure without any formal requirement ever being created. I want to know what work the phrase is actually doing in his objection.

Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims "beyond the call of duty" could be making in this setting:

- the near-trivial reading on which it means only not-required, so that of course a code should not require it — and from which, I take it, nothing follows about whether a code may so much as mention it;
- readings about the gap between an act's deontic status and the legitimacy of coercing, penalizing, or blaming someone who omits it, which would let something be genuinely required while remaining unenforceable, or vice versa;
- readings about what kind of body has standing or epistemic competence to demand this sort of thing, which would make the claim jurisdictional rather than about the act at all;
- readings on which the value of the act depends on its being done for reasons other than a rule, so that codifying it destroys what made it worth encouraging in the first place;
- readings on which the cost to the agent, or alternatively the good left on the table, is doing the real work, and on which the phrase therefore marks a threshold in a scalar quantity rather than a boundary in kind;
- readings on which the phrase registers ignorance or indeterminacy about where the line falls, rather than a fact about the act;
- readings on which the category collapses — nothing is beyond duty, or the claim that the act must not be required is itself a requirement and so cannot coherently be written into a code.

For each, tell me whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, false, or question-begging in our setting, and whether it would support deleting the clause, demoting it to the annex, or neither. Note where two readings can be held together and where accepting one excludes another. Say which reading you take my colleague to be relying on, and say explicitly where the annex option falls between readings rather than cleanly inside one of them — that is the case I most need help with. Where a verdict turns on a contingent fact about how our association actually behaves rather than on a conceptual point, flag that rather than assuming it. Please argue from the distinctions themselves: I do not want the literature on supererogation, attributions, citations, or reading suggestions, since I cannot follow them up from here.
