Next month I am giving a job talk at a department whose value-theory side consists of two Kantian constructivists, one rule-consequentialist whose own work sits close to the effective-altruism literature, a feminist care ethicist who has written on the moral structure of care labor, and a historian of early modern philosophy who will chair the session and who has read almost no Chinese philosophy. Nobody in the room works on Chinese thought; the line is in value theory, open as to tradition. The talk is forty minutes with twenty minutes of questions, so assume roughly two minutes per answer and that I can hold at most two threads at a time.

The paper defends three claims. First, that Confucian role ethics is best read neither as a variant of virtue ethics nor as a species of moral particularism, but as what I call a third-personal account of moral reasons: a consideration counts as a reason when it can be acknowledged from the standpoint of the relationship itself — by anyone occupying either relatum of the role — rather than from the first-personal standpoint of either party or from some impersonal standpoint outside the relationship altogether. Second, that the standard charge of structural conservatism — that role ethics can only articulate duties the inherited roles already contain, and so has no resources for criticizing the roles themselves — can be answered from inside the view, because early Confucian role practice contains remonstrance, a role-bound obligation of the subordinate to correct the superior, discharged in the vocabulary of the relationship rather than in the vocabulary of rights, impartial morality, or personal authenticity. Third, that remonstrance yields a test for what I call role failure: a role is defective and not merely badly occupied when its structure systematically disables correction from below, when the people subject to it have no role-governed way of registering that it is being misused. I apply that test to two contemporary cases, mandatory arbitration clauses that bar employees from collective complaint, and the norm of filial deference as it bears on adult children caring for a parent with dementia.

I already handle the simple version of the moral-progress objection in the final section of the paper, so do not spend one of your objections there unless you think that section is actually wrong.
