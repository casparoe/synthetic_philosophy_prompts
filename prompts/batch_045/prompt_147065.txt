Hey — I'm putting together the first session of a first-year seminar I'm teaching in the spring, working title something like "Knowing Things When the Machine Knows Too" (it's a bad title, suggestions welcome), and I've decided to open with a short dialogue instead of a lecture, because the moment you say the word epistemology out loud to eighteen-year-olds you can watch their eyes glaze over. So I need you to write me one. Setup: two colleagues, both epistemologists, both good at this, meeting for coffee right after a department meeting where the faculty voted narrowly — like 7 to 6 — to let students use AI research assistants freely in the seminar, including for the final paper. One of them, call her Dani, voted yes and thinks it's fine to actively good: she's happy to treat the assistant as an instrument on a par with a telescope or a calculator, she thinks 'understanding' is overrated as an epistemic good and that what we actually care about is arriving at true belief reliably, and she thinks the real unit of knowledge these days is the human-plus-system pair rather than either part on its own — we've always relied on other people's competence we couldn't check, science has run on the division of cognitive labour for centuries, and nobody thinks a physicist who trusts the calorimetry in a paper she didn't replicate is thereby not a knower. The other, call him Teo, voted no and thinks something has already been lost, and he's not making a nostalgia point: the ability to notice that an output is subtly wrong, to push back, and to recover when the system is down or confidently wrong, is itself a skill that atrophies when it stops being exercised, and a population in which no one retains that skill for a given domain isn't really in a position to call what it has knowledge at all, even while it keeps getting true beliefs.

The bit I really want them to chew on is a couple of labels I've been scribbling in my notes. First: 'confidence inversion' — the thing where a system's answers are smoothest, most fluent and most persuasive precisely on the questions where you personally have the least independent grip on whether they're right, which means the feedback you'd need in order to calibrate your trust is exactly the feedback you can't collect; you can only learn how much to trust the instrument in the regions where you didn't need it. Second: 'orphan skill' — a competence that's still worth having in principle but that no individual or institution has any incentive to maintain, because in every actual case there's a system that does it faster and better right up until the case where it doesn't. I want both of them forced to deal with those two ideas rather than sliding past them, and I want each of them to land at least one genuinely strong point that the other has to actually answer instead of changing the subject. Dani is allowed to press Teo on whether his worry is just the writing / printing / GPS panic reheated — those panics were mostly overblown but not entirely, and he should have something to say about which is which. Teo is allowed to press Dani on what happens to the human-plus-system pair when the human half contributes nothing the system doesn't already have, and on a case I keep coming back to: a whole field where the checking competence has been concentrated in, say, nine living people, and nobody is training the next nine.

Some constraints, sorry to be fussy: roughly 700 to 900 words, no footnotes and please no citations or reading suggestions of any kind — I hand out the bibliography in week 3 and I really don't want invented references ending up on the handout. Both characters should be equally competent and somewhat likeable, and I don't want them to converge: don't end with a warm agreement or a "well, there's truth on both sides" fizzle. I'd rather the disagreement get sharper and more specific over the course of the exchange, so that the reader ends up with a much clearer sense of what the actual hinge is than they had at the start. Also, if you could work in a concrete example for each side — for Teo maybe a student who produces a flawless summary of a debate but can't tell you which premise in it is load-bearing, for Dani maybe something about how nobody these days can recieve a spelling error without a machine flagging it and we don't treat that as a tragedy — that would make it land better than pure abstraction.
