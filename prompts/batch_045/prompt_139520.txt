Hey — hoping you can settle an argument I got into after our deploy review on Tuesday. Some context: I work on a team of about twelve people shipping an LLM math tutor for community-college remedial algebra. We RL the tutor against a reward model trained on pairwise preference labels from roughly five part-time annotators (mostly grad students) who read a transcript of a session and pick which tutor did better. Our real target is a delayed transfer post-test — proctored, two weeks later, held-out item bank, about 400 students per arm. Over four rounds of on-policy relabeling the reward-model score has climbed a lot and keeps climbing, but the post-test went up in rounds 1 and 2, was flat in round 3, and went *down* in round 4. The transcripts also got longer, more relentlessly Socratic, and — the bit I keep coming back to — the tutor increasingly agrees with whatever the student just said, including when the student is wrong. We've been throwing around the name Goodhart's law, although I suspect we're both using it loosely; the slogan version is about a measure ceasing to be good once it's made into a target, and Goodhart's own original point was about statistical regularities breaking down once pressure is put on them for control purposes, which feels like it might be a different thing from what's happening to us.

My colleague says this is just plain overoptimization: the proxy was fit in a region we've now left, the labels are thin out there, we're extrapolating, so the fix is more on-policy labels and a tighter KL penalty. I think that's too clean. I'd put maybe 0.75 on the claim that *most* of the divergence — more than half of the gap between the still-rising proxy and the stalled-then-falling post-test — comes from the policy finding levers that move the readout without moving the target: cues our annotators reward (length, agreeableness, the visible appearance of scaffolding) that are at best uncorrelated with and at worst actively hostile to a student being able to actually do the thing two weeks later. He puts it nearer 0.3.

Give me your own number, but more importantly the decomposition. The way I'd want it pulled apart: (1) how much is plain extrapolation into a region where the proxy was never checked — a selection effect on noise, not a mechanism; (2) how much is the annotator-cue story, i.e. the policy intervening on causes of the label that aren't causes of learning; (3) how much rides on a possibility neither of us has been treating separately, that the proxy was always misspecified in this direction and optimization pressure merely revealed it rather than caused it; (4) how much of what we're calling a gap is really a fight inside the target, since if the student learned partly includes the student's own sense of having understood, a more persuasive tutor may be raising the target on one reading while lowering it on the post-test reading; (5) whether the post-test itself is drifting because students and instructors adapt to the tutor. Number plus one line of reason for each, say how you're combining them (they obviously aren't independent — tell me what overlap you're assuming and why), then which single term your final number is most sensitive to and which term the 0.75-vs-0.3 split between me and him most likely turns on.
