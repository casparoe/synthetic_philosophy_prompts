Im in a philosophy/econ joint course this term — its listed under something like "Rational Choice and Social Theory" — and we just spent a week on the common prior assumption: Aumann-style agreement results, no-trade results, that whole cluster. I want to write my short paper on something real instead of another made-up example, so I started reading about how governments actually sell off oil drilling leases and radio spectrum, and now I cant tell what the design is assuming. Please write in American English.

Heres the practice as I understand it, so you dont have to guess what Im pointing at. In the big spectrum sales, the auctioneer runs an open ascending auction: everyone can see the standing bids and who is still active at each price level, round after round. At the same time there is an explicit rule banning bidders from talking to each other about their valuations or their bidding plans. So you are allowed to watch each other bid but not to discuss why. In the oil lease setting the sales are often sealed-bid, and the textbook advice there is the opposite of bid-what-you-think-its-worth: you shade your bid down, because winning is itself evidence that your estimate was the highest of the group and therefore probably too high. Regulators on both sides also act as if getting more bidders into the room, and getting them better informed before the sale, is good for revenue.

What I want is the implicit theory of disagreement underneath all that.

First, reconstruct it. Does this package strictly presuppose a common prior in the strong sense — one shared probability distribution over the relevant states of the world, from which every bidder's private information is a draw via a shared likelihood — or does it only need something weaker, like symmetric information, conditional independence of signals, or just common value plus a shared error distribution? Be precise about which part of the practice each piece is actually load-bearing for. I especially want to know what the winner's curse reasoning needs as opposed to what the open-outcry design needs: my sense is they are not the same commitment, but I cant separate them.

Second, is the package internally consistent? It looks like it does two opposite things. The ascending format is built so that other people's continued bidding is informative to me — disagreement and drop-out are treated as evidence about the value. But the communication ban forbids exactly the exchange that, under a common prior, is supposed to make disagreement evaporate and beliefs converge. Is that a real tension, or are the two rules doing unrelated jobs — one aggregating information through prices, one suppressing collusion — that just happen to look inconsistent?

Third, where is the practice underdetermined? I suspect several different views of rational disagreement would license the same design, and I want to know which parts of the design dont discriminate between them, so I dont overclaim in the paper.

Fourth, tell me what would actually have to change if we dropped the common prior and let bidders disagree because they have genuinely different models of the world — different geological theories, different forecasts of demand for wireless data, different priors in the literal sense. Does drop-out stop being informative? Does the winner's curse shrink, or turn into something like the winner is probably the person with the best model? Does the case for subsidizing pre-auction information survive?

And here is the bit Ive been turning over on my own, which nobody in the class has an answer to: suppose the auctioneer required each bidder to file, before bidding opens, a short plain-language statement of the model or assumptions they are using to value the license, and then published all those statements to every bidder while keeping the bids themselves secret. Under a common prior that disclosure should do nothing at all, since everyone already knows what everyone believes. Under heterogeneous priors it should do something, but I cant tell whether it would help or hurt the seller — whether it reduces the winner's curse in a way that raises bids, or just makes it easier for bidders to figure out who is strongest and coordinate around that. What does your answer to that tell us about whether the original practice really needed the common prior in the first place?
