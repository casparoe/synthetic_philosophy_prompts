I'm a graduate TA for a first-year seminar that pairs metaphysics readings with films, and in two weeks we're doing persistence over time — endurantism versus perdurantism, the argument from temporary intrinsics, the standard treatment of fission as a matter of two continuers sharing earlier stages — and then screening Christopher Nolan's The Prestige. I'd like a genuine reading of the film against that material rather than a decorative one, and I'd rather you not spend your answer re-explaining the endurance/perdurance dispute or recapping the plot: assume I know both. What I care about is what the film does to the theories. Two rival magicians in turn-of-the-century London each perform a trick in which a man vanishes and instantly reappears across the theatre. Borden's version is done with an identical twin: the brothers live as one man, alternating between the public identity of the magician Alfred Borden and the private identity of his engineer under an assumed name, even mutilating a hand so the two bodies match. Angier's version uses a machine built for him by Nikola Tesla which, as he discovers from a duplicated top hat and two identical cats, does not transport but duplicates: each night one Angier appears at the back of the hall while the one in the machine drops through a trapdoor into a locked water tank and drowns, and the film ends among a warehouse of those drowned bodies — with Angier saying, in effect, that he never knew whether he would be the man in the box or the man who takes the bow.

The two tricks look like very different cases, and I want that asymmetry pressed. Borden's involves no branching and no shared stages at all: there are simply two organisms taking turns in one role, with no shared stream of consciousness between them. So if there is a single persisting thing here, its unity cannot be the mental continuity that perdurantists about persons standardly appeal to. Is Borden then best described as one scattered spacetime worm whose temporal parts are drawn alternately from two bodies, or as two people with the unity of the name being a legal and social fiction — and if the latter, does the case tell against psychological-continuity versions of perdurantism specifically, by showing that a creature can be one thing over time without any continuity of experience? Angier's trick is the opposite: genuine fission with a created duplicate and a shared earlier stage. How should each theory count what happens in that warehouse — one person with dozens of terminal branches, or many people each of whom was once Angier? And here is what puzzles me: Angier's willingness to perform the trick every night, and his remark about not knowing which one he would end up being, seem to presuppose that there is a further fact about which continuer is him, which is exactly what the standard perdurantist dissolution of fission denies. Does the film's emotional logic therefore smuggle in an endurantist or at least non-branching conception of the self, or can the perdurantist recover that horror through the what-will-happen-to-me question? One more wrinkle: Tesla's machine is nomologically impossible, though perhaps not metaphysically so — say whether that difference matters here, given how the endurance/perdurance dispute is standardly conducted.

A student in the seminar proposed a view we have been calling role-first persistence, and I want it taken seriously and then tested rather than patted on the head. On this view a thing persists across an interval just in case a single role, office, or ongoing public identity is continuously occupied from time to time within it, and whatever occupies the role at a given time supplies that thing's temporal parts then. This seems to give the intuitive verdict in Borden's case, where the continuity-based view fails, and the counterintuitive verdict in Angier's — since the role is occupied by exactly one survivor each night, the drowned men all come out as stages of one and the same Angier, which is precisely what Angier seems to believe and precisely what makes the finale horrifying. So: is this a coherent alternative persistence condition, or is it vacuous — a relabeling of the fact that the show must go on, which guarantees the persistence of anything with a public name and so fails to be a theory of persistence at all? And notice what the film itself keeps insisting, that the audience does not want to know, that both men ruin themselves for a trick, that a career is built on an agreed-upon deception: does that amount to an argument that a persistence condition hostage to a socially recognized role is exactly the sort of thing this film distrusts? Please tell me which two or three scenes you would put in front of the seminar, be explicit about how confident you are in each part of the reading and where the reading is most likely to fail, and say what would change your mind — which scene, read differently, would break it, and whether an honest critic should conclude that the film is not making a claim about persistence at all and that the whole line of interpretation is projection.
