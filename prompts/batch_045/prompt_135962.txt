ok so a friend of mine — statistician, not a philosopher, and anoyingly good at this kind of thing — has an argument that I'm pretty sure proves way too much, and I cant find the place to grab hold of it. He says it's just Goodman's grue problem grown up, but it ends somewhere I refuse to follow.

His setup: take any predicate P we happily project (green, brittle, conductive, decays within 4.5 billion years, whatever) and let Q be a contrary of it (blue, ductile, insulating, ...). Now form the twin — he calls it "schm-P", after the Yiddish shm-reduplication — as follows:

x is schm-P iff (x is examined before t and x is P) or (x is first examined at or after t and x is Q)

Note the definition uses "examined", not a fixed calendar date like Goodman's grue; he says that's the whole improvement, because you can always slide t to just past whatever evidence you happen to have. The argument:

(1) For any projectible P and any finite body of evidence E, there is a twin schm-P that fits E exactly as well as P does — every positive instance of P in E is a positive instance of schm-P and vice versa — and which, if t is placed just after E, entails the opposite prediction about the very next unexamined case.

(2) P and schm-P are built from exactly the same vocabulary and are interdefinable with each other using only that vocabulary plus "examined before t". So no test that looks at the predicate's form, at whether it's qualitative rather than positional, or at the words it's couched in, can mark P as projectible and schm-P as not.

(3) The only criterion left is Goodman's own: entrenchment. P wins because P and its parent predicates have actually been projected more often. But that's a fact about which noises our community happened to make, not a fact about the emeralds. A tribe who grew up speaking schm-ese — their basic colour words being grue and bleen — would call green the gerrymandered predicate and, by that test, would be right. So entrenchment can explain which prediction we make; it can't be what makes it the reasonable one.

(4) If nothing distinguishes P from schm-P except historical and linguistic facts about us, then E does not support the P-prediction over the schm-P-prediction. Evidence supports one hypothesis over another only if something other than our habits of speech is doing the work.

(5) Nor can priors rescue you: before seeing E you had no more reason to expect P than schm-P, and by (2) and (3) any prior you assign to P you owe equally to schm-P, since the two are interdefinable over the same vocabulary and differ only in projection history.

(6) And it's not one rival, it's endlessly many: vary t, vary which contrary Q you pair P with, stack the construction on itself (schm-schm-P), each one fits E and each contradicts the others about the next case. Spread credence across them and "the next one is P" goes to ~0 however much E you pile up.

(7) So no finite body of evidence ever supports any prediction about an unexamined case — not merely for grueish contrivances, but for green, for brittleness, for half-lives.

(8) His favourite corollary, which he aims at me personally: my job is built on nothing. I do reliability modelling for aircraft maintenance intervals; every interval I set is, on (7), a speech habit with better PR.

What I want to keep: that a million examined green emeralds really do support "the next emerald is green" more than "the next is grue", that this isn't true merely because of which words English happens to contain, and that (7) is false. I'd sooner drop (6) than the rest, but I suspect the rot is earlier — something about building the twin out of "examined" rather than a fixed date smells like it's doing work it shouldn't — though maybe that's a red herring and the real slide is somewhere in (3) to (5).

Which step do you reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost me? If there are two or three places it could go, price each. And: does a version of this survive if I delete the step you'd drop, or is he left with nothing?
