I convene a fortnightly graduate reading group in political philosophy, and for our session on whether the world has become less violent I want a handout that works as a sorting instrument rather than as a survey. The participants — final-year undergraduates and postgraduates from philosophy, politics and history — will all have encountered the headline claim that violence has declined, and most will arrive with a stock response to it. I want them to leave knowing which position their own case-by-case answers actually commit them to, including where those answers commit them to something they would not have claimed for themselves.

What I am asking for is a taxonomy of the positions on "is the world becoming less violent?", built as a decision tree, and I want it built from the logical space rather than from a list of names. More precisely, the handout should contain the following five parts.

1. The dimensions, before the tree. Set out the four or five independent distinctions along which views on this question genuinely diverge — what kinds of event count as instances of violence, what the numerator is (victims, events, expected harm, tail risk), what the denominator or exposure unit is (person-years, whole lives, the sub-population actually at risk, the set of moral patients), whether the trend is assessed by expectation or by the shape of the tail, and whether "violence" picks out a single measurable quantity at all. One or two sentences each, with a note on which pairs of distinctions are genuinely independent and which collapse into one another.

2. The tree itself: ten to twelve branch questions yielding eight to ten leaves. Every node must be phrased so that someone can answer it without knowing which leaf it leads to, and ideally each should be anchored to a concrete case rather than to a slogan. No node may take the form "do you think structural violence counts as violence?" — that asks for the label. Give the case (the prison, the famine, the abattoir, the threat that was never carried out) and ask what the reader says about it.

3. The following cases must supply at least six of the nodes; add four to six more of your own. (a) One state credibly threatens to destroy another's city and the threatened invasion never happens: has violence been prevented, or merely held in reserve? (b) The annual risk of being killed stays constant while life expectancy doubles, so the chance of dying violently at some point in a life goes up. (c) Interpersonal violence falls steeply over the period while imprisonment rises sharply and prison violence is common. (d) The number of animals killed for food per head of population multiplies several times over the same period. (e) Expected annual battle deaths fall while the probability of a conflict killing a large fraction of the population rises. (f) One airstrike kills five hundred people; in the comparison period the same five hundred deaths occurred in five hundred separate fights between individuals. (g) A famine follows from trade and aid policy, and no one strikes a blow. (h) A rate computed over the whole population falls while the rate among young men in contested borderlands does not.

4. Labelled leaves. Each leaf should carry a short name, one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and the minimal set of answers that fixes it. Among the leaves I want at least these: straightforward declinism under a bodily, agent-caused, per-person-year measure; declinism about expected harm combined with reversal under tail risk; a substitution or displacement scepticism on which the decline is an artefact of violence being outsourced to the state or held in reserve by deterrence; a structural expansionism on which coercion and remediable deprivation count and the trend is flat or worsening; a moral-patient expansionism on which the trend is sharply upward because of animals; an absolutism about absolute counts on which a falling rate with a rising number of victims is not a decline at all; and a "no fact of the matter" position on which violence is a cluster or essentially contested concept with no single trend. Two further requirements: where a label would require you to guess who has defended the view, write "unnamed" rather than inventing an attribution; and the "no fact of the matter" leaf must be distinguished from the position that there is a fact of the matter but our evidence — skeletal, documentary, resting on contested population estimates — cannot reach it. If your tree cannot separate those two, say so explicitly and explain why.

5. Diagnostics of the space. List the combinations of answers that the dimensions allow but that nobody occupies, with a one-clause verdict on each: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves the instrument cannot separate, and any node whose answer depends on the order in which the questions are put. Finally, flag the single node most likely to be mis-answered by participants who already know the headline debate — the one where they will pick the option that sounds like their side rather than the one their other answers commit them to — and give a rephrasing that removes the cue.

Three constraints. First, no citations, no bibliography, no suggested reading: I want positions, not sources. Second, do not adjudicate the empirical record. Treat every empirical premise as a supposition granted for the sake of argument ("suppose the homicide rate has fallen substantially over the relevant period"), so that the instrument sorts readers by the measure they are implicitly using rather than by what they believe the data show. Third, the whole handout should be self-contained: a reader with no library access should be able to work through it.

Please write in British English throughout. Use markdown, with the tree as an indented, numbered structure with node IDs, and add a compact routing table at the end giving, for each leaf, the shortest path of answers that reaches it.
