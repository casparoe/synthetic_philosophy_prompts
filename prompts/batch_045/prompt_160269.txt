I'm taking a graduate seminar on the philosophy of emotion, and the central question of what emotions are has stalled in a way I'd like to understand rather than settle. The three standard camps — emotions as evaluative judgments, as perceptions or other evaluative attitudes, and as patterned bodily or interoceptive feelings — all grant the same stock of phenomena: directedness and formal objects, recalcitrance to judgment, action readiness, bodily disturbance. Each has a rehearsed reply to the standard objections drawn from those very phenomena, so we trade intuitions and nobody moves.

My hypothesis is that this is not one disagreement but three run together, and that the impasse partly consists in nobody saying which one is being asked: first, what makes a state an emotion at all; second, what makes an emotion the one it is rather than another; third, what it is to undergo an emotion. I suspect the camps are officially competing answers to the third question while actually fighting over the first, because they disagree about which states are the data: judgment theorists seem to build their account on adult, articulate, deliberative episodes and treat moods, startle responses, and infant or animal distress as peripheral, while feeling theorists treat precisely those as central. If that is right, most of the arguments are missing each other, and the residue is a fight about what a theory of emotion is for: underwriting the rationality of emotional reasons, explaining phenomenology, or predicting and taxonomizing affective behaviour across species.

I also want your verdict on a thought experiment our seminar tried. Suppose future neuroscience gave us independent two-way control over the two components, so that we could reliably produce the appraisal and action-readiness profile without the bodily signature, and the bodily signature without the appraisal. My sense is that even that would not end the debate, because the sides would simply disagree about which manipulation produces an emotion — and if so, I want to know what that reveals about the kind of disagreement this is.

So: is my three-questions hypothesis right? Is the standoff verbal, a clash over which cases count as data, a conflict about the aims of a theory, or bedrock intuition that no argument can touch? Ground your diagnosis in what each camp actually argues, and tell me what would count as progress here — a distinction, an empirical result, a change of question — or why we should expect none.
