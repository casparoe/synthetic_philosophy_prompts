I teach a mixed graduate and advanced-undergraduate seminar called "Reasoning Without Proof," and in three weeks I have one seventy-five-minute session on probabilistic heuristic arguments in number theory — specifically, on what the presumption of independence inside such an argument is, and what it would even mean to make it more than a presumption. The students have had a first course in probability (up to conditional expectation, no measure theory) and no number theory beyond the infinitude of the primes. Roughly half of them are philosophy students who will arrive wanting to know whether an argument of this kind gives anyone a reason to believe the conjecture it produces.

Please do not write the explanation. Plan how I should give it: an ordered sequence of moves with rough timings, and an account of what depends on what.

The particular things I need settled:

1. Which comes first — making them watch a heuristic actually produce a number, or making them confront that the target is deterministic? There are no chances in the primes; every event the model assigns probability 1/log n to either happens or does not. My instinct is to start with the computation and let the numbers do the seducing. But I can also see that if I do not deal with determinism early, the philosophers will spend the rest of the hour stuck on it and the mathematicians will think the worry is pointless. Tell me which opening the rest of the session wants.

2. The one example that carries the whole thing, and what it must have and lack. I have been using the heuristic for the density of twin primes: treat the primality of n and of n + 2 as independent events each of probability about 1/log n, get the right order of growth, then note that the constant is wrong and is repaired by an explicit correction factor — a product over small odd primes — that compensates for local congruence obstructions, the crude one being that both n and n + 2 are odd. Its virtues are that I can derive the correction in front of them and that it is visibly a local repair of a failed independence assumption rather than a fudge. Its vice is that it drowns the non-majors in logarithms. Keep it or replace it, and say why.

3. Where to place a local notion of mine that I call the calibration rule: do not trust an independence-based heuristic unless the same presumption, applied at a scale small enough to check by brute force, reproduces the answer already known at that scale. I want to know whether this is a real constraint or false comfort. My suspicion is the latter in one precise sense: a presumption can be exactly right at every computable scale and still break asymptotically, as in Maier's theorem on the numbers of primes in short intervals, where Cramér's random model of the primes gives the wrong answer. If that is right, the rule filters out nonsense without defending the extrapolation, and I should present it as a filter and not as a vindication. A related habit of mine is making them list explicitly which correlations the argument is presuming away; tell me whether that disclosure habit earns its minutes.

4. Which formalization of the presumption to organize the session around, given that I cannot do three in seventy-five minutes: flatness, i.e. indifference over the correlation parameters; the maximum-entropy characterization, on which independence is what you get by maximizing entropy subject to the marginals; or a Reichenbach-style default on which a correlation stands in need of a common cause or a direct causal link, so that independence is the resting position. Pick one, or argue that all three belong outside a session at this level.

5. The misconceptions, in the order they will actually arise, with the point in the sequence where each should be headed off. Those I anticipate: that the output of the argument is a probability that the conjecture is true; that independence is simply an empirical hypothesis about the primes which one can go out and test, so that nothing here is philosophical; that once the local correction factor is in place the model has been confirmed; that independence is the same as zero correlation, so that checking pairwise correlations would suffice; and — the one I care most about — that the maximum-entropy characterization turns the presumption into a derivation and thereby dissolves the problem. For that last one I do not want a refutation delivered after the fact. What has to be in place earlier so that they can see for themselves that which variables you take, and how finely you carve the space, is a choice made before the machinery runs?

6. One closing question that would tell me they have understood the structure of these arguments rather than merely assented to the conjectures, and two or three things I should openly decline to explain at this level, with the refusal itself stated.
