hey — I'm doing a PhD on imprecise probabilities and I keep coming back to how the IPCC actually writes up uncertainty, since it looks like the only big real-world institution that reports set-valued probabilities on purpose. You get likelihood words (likely, very likely, and so on) with a legend mapping each one to a probability band, then a completely seperate confidence scale that tracks how much evidence there is and how far the authors agree, and then — the bit that bugs me — in the very same sentence they still give a single best estimate with a likely range around it, like the headline number for climate sensitivity.

Help me reconstruct what that package of habits realy presupposes. Does it strictly need genuinely imprecise credences, or would a precise Bayesian with one wide posterior over models license every bit of it, best estimate included? Does running two scales commit it to two different sources of imprecision — disagreement between experts, i.e. a set of probability functions, versus thin evidence inside a single head — or could one story cover both? And is that single best estimate a genuine commitment, to a privileged member of the credal set or to some decision rule like maximality, or just pragmatics for policymakers?

One more thing I basically never see discussed: the bands have sharp endpoints. If your probability for a claim isn't precise, what does it mean to say it clears 66 per cent? To me the imprecision just seems to get pushed up a level — is that stable, or does it regress? And if you swapped the whole practice for straight single-number reporting, what would actually be lost?
