Hey, I need Q&A prep for a talk I'm giving in three weeks. It's a philosophy department's Rational Choice seminar — the regulars are two causal decision theory types, one game theorist who works on equilibrium selection and correlated equilibrium, one person who writes on team reasoning / collective intentionality, a handful of grad students, and a visiting psychologist who runs one-shot cooperation experiments. Nobody in the room reads the blogs; the game theorist has vaguely heard of functional decision theory and I'm pretty sure he thinks it's evidential decision theory with extra steps. I've been reading this stuff since the Sequences were still being posted and I've never given a talk to people who don't already share the frame, so I don't know what lands.

The talk: I want to argue that Hofstadter's superrationality material — the twenty-person Platonia setup where exactly one claimant wins the fortune, and the lottery he actually ran on his friends where the payoff to each entrant shrank as more people entered — isn't a rival decision theory, and also isn't the data point everyone quotes it as. My thesis is that everything turns on what kind of correlation holds between my decision and the other players' decisions, and that two very different kinds keep getting conflated:

- Predictive correlation: the others are relevantly like me, so what they do is evidence about what I'll do and vice versa. This is the twin / copy / everyone-read-the-same-argument case.
- Constitutive correlation: there's one thing — one algorithm, one physical roll of the dice — that settles all of our acts at once, so there aren't really N decisions, there's one decision showing up in N places.

I claim only the second kind licenses the famous move where you reason "if I claim the prize then every superrational player claims it." Under merely predictive correlation the reasoning inverts: my claiming is evidence that they claim, which makes exactly-one-claimant less likely, not more. Then I have a small model — a parameter rho for how strongly each other player's action tracks mine, plus a baseline rate of claiming — and I derive a threshold above which the deterministic "just claim it" strategy is worth essentially nothing and the only play with real expected value is the dither everyone randomizes at 1/N. On my numbers the threshold is low, under 0.2 even for twenty players, and it falls as N rises, which I think is the actual punchline: the superrational answer is only the right answer in a regime so demanding that no real experiment, including his, has ever put anybody in it. Then the last part of the talk says the lottery data is uninterpretable as evidence about superrationality, because the setup never established that the subjects accepted the symmetry premise and beliefs were never elicited — you observe one marginal from one shot, so nothing about the correlation structure is recoverable from it.

What I want from you: the seven or so objections I'll actually get from this room, ordered by likelihood times damage rather than by how interesting they are, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it (the game theorist talks about joint distributions, the CDT people about causal structure, the team-reasoning person about frames and who counts as a "we", the psychologist about identifiability and power). For each: a reply I can give in about ninety seconds, and an honest note on where that reply is soft, because I'd rather know now than at minute fifty. Also tell me what I should concede in the first five minutes so the room doesn't turn on me before I get to the model, and which single reply has to land for the talk to be worth giving.

And please include the objection I'm hoping nobody asks. My own guess at it is something like: if constitutive correlation means there's really only one decision in N places, then N shouldn't appear in your recommendation at all — the number of bodies is irrelevant to a single agent's choice — and yet your whole answer, dither at 1/N, is a function of N, so which is it? I don't have a good answer to that and I'd like one, or at least a way of not visibly panicking when it comes.
