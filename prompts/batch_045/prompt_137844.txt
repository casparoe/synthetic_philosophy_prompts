I'm a novelist and I've managed to write myself straight into a metaphysics problem, so bear with me while I set it up.

My manuscript has two characters — let's call them A and B — who are, as written, indiscernible: same history, same body, same memories, same everything you could state without pointing. The book is in first person and the narrator is one of them, but nothing in the text says which. At the end a line lands: "There was never two of us." My writing group is split on whether that line is a revelation or just a cute way of saying nothing.

Everyone keeps telling me this is Black's two spheres with wigs on, so I finally went and read about the spheres, and now I'm stuck on the actual claim. So here's the ask. Give me a number — your credence — on this proposition:

PII-q: necessarily, for any x and y, if x and y share every qualitative property (relational and modal ones included; haecceities and being-identical-to-a style properties excluded), then x = y.

and separately, a number on:

SYM: a universe containing exactly two indiscernible spheres and nothing else is a genuine possible world, i.e. not just one sphere described twice, or an inconsistent description dressed up as a scenario.

But mostly I want the decomposition, not the headline number. Break it into the sub-questions you think it actually rides on — I'd guess at least these, add your own:

- how much rides on where the qualitative / non-qualitative line falls (is being the one the narrator is a property? being the one who dies in chapter nine?)
- whether a perfectly symmetric description can even be said to describe two things rather than one thing twice, and whether that's a real question or just a stalemate about counting
- how much rides on whether conceivability/imaginability of the symmetric setup is evidence of possibility at all
- whether anything in physics does work here — I half-remember something about indistinguishable particles and permutation, and something about two mathematically different descriptions of a spacetime counting as the same physical situation, but I may be mangling both

Give each sub-question a rough probability and one line of why, say how they combine, then tell me which term your final number is most sensitive to and what would move it most. And please flag how much of your number is "this is where the armchair argument lands" versus "this is where I'd expect the field to end up" — I care about the difference.

Two things I tried that I want you to rule on rather than be polite about. First, my fix for the symmetry problem was to add a third, asymmetric object — a lighthouse — exactly midway between the two characters. A beta reader said this does nothing, because both characters stand in exactly the same relations to the lighthouse, so the swap is still a symmetry. Is that right, and does it generalize to any symmetric arrangement, or does adding stuff eventually break the symmetry in a way that matters? Does it matter whether space itself is supposed to supply the positions?

Second, the trick I'm proudest of: I wrote two chapters that are word-for-word identical except the names are swapped, and I want to know whether I have written two chapters or one chapter twice. Is that the same question as the spheres question, a different question wearing its clothes, or a confusion? If it's a confusion, say what it's a confusion between.

For calibration: I'm at about 0.35 on PII-q, and my writing group friend who does analytic stuff is at about 0.8. I'd like your number, but more than that I want to know which sub-question the two of us are most likely disagreeing about — I have a feeling we're not actually arguing about the same thing, and I'd like to find out before the next draft.

Oh, and one last thing: is there any non-armchair development that could move this number, or is it sealed inside the armchair forever? If it's sealed, say so plainly. That's actually good news for the book.
