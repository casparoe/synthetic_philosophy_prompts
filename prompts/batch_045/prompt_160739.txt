I teach a seminar on moral progress, and there's one picture that keeps doing a lot of quiet work in it, so I want an audit of the picture rather than another rehearsal of the arguments around it.

Here it is: moral concern as a circle with the self (or the family, or the tribe) at the centre, expanding outward over history. Singer gave it its canonical form in The Expanding Circle (1981), Pinker runs with the same image in Better Angels, and a colleague in political theory used it last week in a department talk on AI welfare. In her hands it does three jobs: (a) it makes moral progress look like a real, measurable, directional thing rather than a change in intellectual fashion; (b) it names the engine — reason universalizing our parochial altruistic impulses; and (c) it licenses an extrapolation: the circle has gone family, tribe, nation, all humans, animals, so it is now moving, and we should help it move, toward digital minds, with sentience or being-affected as the natural stopping point.

I want the mapping made explicit before I decide whether its worth using myself. What is the centre, what is the radius — what distance metric, kinship, similarity, causal or social proximity — what is the circumference, what does inside mean, full equal consideration or something weaker, and what plays the role of whatever pushes the boundary out? A table of correspondences would be ideal.

Then the places it breaks, and — the part I actually care about — which of those breaks matter for jobs (a), (b) and (c) and which are harmless. The ones I already have notes on:

1. One metric. A circle is defined by a single distance from a single centre, so everything sits on one ordered line of nearness. But the candidate entrants — a chimp, a stranger on the other side of the world, a person in 2300, a river, a digital mind — don't look like they are ranked on one dimension at all. Is the single metric something the geometry smuggles in, and does the extrapolation in (c) depend on there being one?

2. Dilution. A student raised this and I didn't have an answer ready. Nothing in the geometry says the interior gains concern rather than keeping the same stock and spreading it over more beings. Area grows with the square of the radius, but so does the number of things inside, so per-capita concern could be falling while the circle looks bigger — the widening-beam or butter-over-more-bread reading. If both readings are compatible with the picture, the picture can't do job (a) on its own: the circle got bigger would settle only that concern was distributed differently, not that anything improved. Relatedly, circles invite a gravitational reading, concern fading with distance, which seems to cut against the equal-consideration conclusion the image is usually wheeled out to support.

3. Convexity and binary membership. Circles have no holes and no partial membership. Actual concern is patchy — pets and cetaceans yes, insects mostly no; future people in principle yes, in practice barely — and graded rather than on/off. Fatal, or does the picture only ever need the frontier?

4. The centre. In the image the self sits still while the boundary moves out. But the thing at the centre has itself changed over the relevant history — thinner selves, collective and corporate agents, standing granted to non-persons — and with digital minds there may be no stable centre, and they can be copied, so how many beings are inside can explode overnight. Does (c) survive that?

So: does the conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table? I'd like separate verdicts for (a), (b) and (c), since I suspect the analogy can carry one or two of them and is doing all the work on a third. And if it can't carry them, is there a better image — I've been toying with moral standing as expanding jurisdiction, standing and personhood accreted case by case by courts and legislatures rather than radiating from a centre, which gets the patchiness and the institutional mechanism right, but I can't tell what it loses. Say what any better analogy would have to preserve.
