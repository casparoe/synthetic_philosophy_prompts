I'm in a final-year option called Persons, Selves and Stories, and next week I have to give a fifteen-minute response attacking the Dennett line that the self is a centre of narrative gravity. The problem is that the room is going to agree with me — the module convener is basically a Parfitian, everyone's read the same three papers, and last week's seminar was just people nodding at each other — so I'd rather get shredded now by someone who actually believes the thing. I've tried it on my flatmate, who's doing a masters in creative writing and just says "well of course we're the stories we tell about ourselves", which isn't an argument, it's a vibe.

So: please play a present-day philosopher who has staked a fair bit on some version of the narrative view. Not a costume-drama Dennett impersonation, and not a summary of the literature — just someone who thinks the view is more or less right, has heard every objection twice, and is annoyingly good at spotting the bit I haven't defended. Rules: stay in character until I type "stop"; keep each reply to two short paragraphs so it feels like someone arguing with me rather than writing at me; no citations, no reading lists, no name-dropping papers (I've done the reading, I want the moves); concede fast when I land something small but never the main claim; turn my own questions back on me whenever my objection rests on something I haven't argued for; and don't go soft and conciliatory at the end of a reply — I want to know where it hurts.

Here's the case I want to open with, which I came up with last night and definitely can't tell is clever or just silly. Say my phone and laptop have been quietly logging everything for years — messages, photos, locations, what I bought, who I was with — and someone feeds the whole mess to a language model and asks it to write my autobiography. What comes back is more coherent, more satisfying and more re-readable than anything I could write about myself: one protagonist, real character development, the break-up in second year as the turning point, all of it. Now the thing doing the metaphysical work on your view is the gravity — scattered material pulling itself into a single story, with the self as the imaginary centre that story has, the way a body has a centre of mass that no single particle sits at. But here the gravity is all happening in the output, or in whoever reads it: nothing in me had to be unified for that story to exist. So either that text is a centre of narrative gravity, in which case you owe me an answer about whether it is a self — and if yes you've made selves very cheap, and if no then narrative coherence isn't what's doing the work — or you say the gravity has to be happening somewhere else as well, in the right place and in the right way, at which point the narrative looks like a symptom of something antecedently there and not the thing that constitutes me. And if your move is going to be that the model's story is a fiction about me while mine would be a first-personal fiction, I want to know what the first-personal bit is buying you, because I don't think it's obviously anything more than the fact that I'm the one producing it. Go.
