I'm in a small reading group that has been working through the problem of induction, and one member — call him Marcus — keeps an informal ledger of predicates. For each one he rules on whether it is projectible: whether a run of positive instances gives you any reason at all to expect the next instance to fall under it, or whether the predicate merely summarizes the cases already in hand. His verdicts so far, more or less as he wrote them down: green, blue, emerald, raven, and "is a cephalopod" (he noted that most of us had never used that word before the group began, and it did not trouble him) — projectible. A Goodman-style grue pair built on a future cutoff — a thing is grue if it is examined before 2030 and green, or not examined before 2030 and blue, with bleen the same thing with green and blue swapped — not projectible. The same pair rebuilt on a past cutoff, 1990: still not projectible, which he took to show that the trouble is not that the date lies in the future. "Grell" — green if the thing is examined indoors, blue if it is examined outdoors — not projectible, which he took to show that the trouble is not specifically about times. "Grue or bleen," which on the usual assumption that emeralds are either green or blue is true of every emerald there is: projectible in name only, since it excludes nothing and no instance could ever disconfirm it. And "is a 20th-century novel": projectible, the date in its definition notwithstanding.

Two new cases came up last week and he refused to rule on either until the group meets again, and another member and I have a small bet riding on which way he will go. First: a mineralogist in the group works on vespeline, a crystal structure first described two years ago; the word appears in three papers, all of them hers and one of them her own dissertation chapter. She has nine samples, all of them vespeline, and all nine fluoresce under ultraviolet light, and she wants to say that vespeline fluoresces. Second: "is a vegetable" — thoroughly entrenched, in no way tied to when or how anything is examined, and a motley class if there ever was one: tomatoes and carrots and celery, lentils in some uses and not in others, but not apples, not wheat, not mushrooms. Does the run of vegetables in the pot give you any reason to expect the next one?

I am less interested in which way he goes than in what rule he has been running all along. I can think of three that fit his ledger equally well — something about how entrenched a predicate is, something about definitions that make whether the predicate applies depend on the circumstances in which the instance is collected, and something about predicates that cut across kinds rather than tracking them — and as far as I can tell they come apart on these two cases in opposite directions. So: what rules fit his verdicts, what does each of them predict about vespeline and about vegetables, which one do you think Marcus is actually running and why, and — the part I care most about — what single further predicate would you put to him to pry the surviving rules apart from each other? If the three rules I sketched are too crude as I have stated them, say so and state the repair, but keep the repaired versions distinct enough that they still disagree about some case you can describe.
