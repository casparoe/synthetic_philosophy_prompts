i'm reading a fairly popular history of early modern science and there's a passage on Hobbes and geometry that I can't tell is fair. It goes roughly: Hobbes thought geometry was the one genuinely certain science, and the reason is that we produce the objects ourselves — you move a point to make a line, rotate a line to make a circle — so we know their causes, whereas in physics the causes are mostly hidden and we only reason backwards from effects. That's why he wanted definitions that say how a figure is generated rather than what it is, and why he rewrote Euclid's definitions of point and line. So far so plausible. But then it says: because the definitions are stipulations we ourselves lay down, geometric theorems are true by convention and tell us nothing about nature — geometry is a game played with our own rules — and that is what made Hobbes think politics could be made just as certain, since you only need to fix your definitions and derive. It also mentions that Hobbes later claimed to have squared the circle and got ripped apart by the Oxford mathematicians, and concludes that this episode shows his whole "geometry as the model for knowledge" project was a castle in the air.

The last bit feels like an overreach to me, and I'm suspicious of the "true by convention, tells us nothing about nature" part too — I'd heard Hobbes was some kind of materialist who thought geometry was about body and motion, which sounds like the opposite. But maybe those actually fit together somehow? Which parts would a Hobbes specialist accept, where does the summary quietly slide into something he wouldn't have said, and is the failed quadrature really evidence against the rest?
