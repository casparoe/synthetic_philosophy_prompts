I'm a high school student, and I recently came across something called updateless decision theory. My rough understanding is that instead of waiting to see which situation you end up in and then choosing, you decide in advance what your whole policy should be and then simply follow it, on the grounds that how you would choose inside the situation is already part of what determines what happens to you.

It reminded me of a practice I can't make sense of. In many places, someone with a recurring mental illness can write a document while well, describing how they want to be treated during a future episode; clinicians may then go ahead and treat them, sometimes over the person's protests, even while the same person is now clearly saying no. That seems to run against how things work everywhere else: a competent patient can normally withdraw consent to treatment at any time, and an agreement made while someone was drunk, panicked, or not themselves isn't binding on them. Here the earlier decision, made with less information, wins over the later one.

What view of persons, or of which choice is authoritative, does that practice presuppose? Is it genuinely an updateless-style idea — the policy chosen from the earlier standpoint is what decides, and the later self isn't supposed to reopen the question — or can an ordinary take-in-the-new-evidence-then-choose picture explain it just as well, for instance by arguing that the episode itself makes the later refusal untrustworthy, or makes it a poor guide to what the person really wants? Which parts of the practice strictly require the first story, and which would several different stories license equally well? If we dropped that story and said only the present, best-informed self ever gets to decide, what would the practice have to look like?

Please also tell me where your reasoning is most likely to break down, and what would change your mind.
