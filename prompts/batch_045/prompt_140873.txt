I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy, currently in a seminar on formal epistemology, and we have just spent two weeks on Aumann's agreement theorem and on the epistemology of disagreement that it tends to get wheeled into. My verdicts on concrete cases don't seem to line up with any of the positions we have discussed, and I would like help working out what principle, if any, they add up to. I may be misremembering the theorem, so tell me if I am: as I understand it, if two agents start from a common prior over a shared space of possibilities, and it is common knowledge between them what each of their posterior probabilities for some event is, then those posteriors are equal — ideally rational agents with a shared prior cannot agree to disagree. I would rather not be told what to read next; the cases are what I want help with.

Two forecasters at the same weather service. Same model, same training, each gets the readings from half the stations in the region; they exchange their probability of heavy rain tomorrow, then exchange again, and could go on indefinitely. I think each round should shrink the gap, that any residual gap after one round is entirely fine, and that persisting in a gap after ten rounds would be irrational.

Same setup, except that each forecaster's evidence is a radar image whose noise level the viewer cannot tell from the image itself: you see what you see, and you have no independent handle on how much of it is artefact. They share the images and their probabilities. Here I do not think convergence is required even in the limit — neither has any way of telling whose image was cleaner, and sharing does not manufacture one. I feel there is something structurally different about this case, but I cannot say what it is.

Two climatologists who disagree about the probability that some threshold will be crossed by 2050. They have read everything, they know each other's posteriors, and the disagreement traces to how much weight each gives to a class of historical evidence. My verdict is that the disagreement is permissible, because the common-prior assumption is exactly what fails here. But I know the obvious reply — that evidential weightings are themselves rather like probabilities, so why should the theorem not apply one level up and force agreement about the weighting? My gut resists that, and I cannot say why.

Two analysts with a common prior, full common knowledge of each other's posteriors, disagreeing about whether there will be a run on a particular bank next week, where whether the run happens depends partly on what depositors believe will happen. I think standing firm here is fine, and I think the theorem does not apply at all, but I would like to know what the principled statement of the exemption is.

Two of us in the seminar, similar training and similar track records, read the same forty-page paper over the weekend; on Monday one says the central argument is sound and the other says it is a howler. Neither has done anything extra. I think both should reduce confidence substantially, though not all the way to agnosticism. But now vary it: same pair, same paper, except that one of them spent the weekend reconstructing the central lemma step by step and checking it against a textbook treatment, while the other read it once on a train. I think the careful reader may keep nearly all her confidence and need give almost no ground. I hold both verdicts, and I notice that the surface situation is the same in each — two peers, one text.

Two pairs of agents both end the day with posteriors of roughly 0.7 and 0.4 for the same event after three rounds of exchanging posteriors. In one pair the exchange stopped because the market closed at five. In the other it stopped because they lost interest and went to the pub. I think the first pair's residual disagreement is not irrational and the second pair's is. I know this commits me to permissibility being a property of something other than the posterior reached, and I would like to see a principle say what.

Two agents with a common prior face a posterior that would require integrating over an enormous space; neither can compute it and each knows the other cannot. I do not want to call their persistence irrational, and I do not want to call it permitted either — I oscillate between thinking them excused and thinking them fine, and I suspect the distinction matters for everything above.

Finally, I am told the theorem entails that purely informational trade cannot occur between rational agents with a common prior. I do not believe that of actual markets, and this makes me suspect the common prior is doing all the work, so I distrust any conclusion that leans on it.

The case I keep going back and forth on is peer disagreement about a normative question — say whether a particular gene-editing policy is permissible — where the peers share a background, share all the empirical evidence, and have no experts to defer to. I cannot settle whether full conciliation is required there or whether something about normative subject matter licenses steadfastness.

Is there one principle that captures these verdicts, or nearly all of them, and what is it? Please state it precisely, say which of my cases motivates which clause, and then give me its verdicts on three cases I have not listed: the normative case above; a case where one peer is demonstrably better calibrated in the domain but the other has the more specific evidence; and a case where the disagreement is discovered only after the decision has been made and cannot be revisited. Say plainly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and would force me to give up, and give me the simpler alternative principle that overturns one of them, so I can see what the trade really is. Where you can, separate what is settled — the status of the theorem itself as mathematics, about which I want to be corrected if I am wrong — from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this: whether a common prior is a requirement of rationality, whether results about ideal agents constrain agents like us, whether higher-order evidence about one's own reliability changes the answer, and anything else you think specialists would divide over. Please do not present a contested view as the consensus, and flag the points where you would expect a seminar reading list to have pushed me in one direction. And please write the whole thing as continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — the way you would write a long email to a student you know.
