I am a third-year undergraduate taking Minds and Machines, and I've been asked to lead one thirty-minute discussion section on the symbol grounding problem. The section is twelve students: roughly half are CS majors who have trained small neural networks and use language models daily, four are philosophy majors, and there is one linguist. Almost all of them arrive believing either that the problem was solved once systems started ingesting images and acting in the world, or that it is a philosopher's parlour trick that evaporates the moment you point at a working system. I have a whiteboard and no projector.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher of mind and cognitive scientist who has taught this material many times and who knows how it is standardly misunderstood. Before you give me the plan, spend a few paragraphs brainstorming: sketch two or three orderings of the material you considered and rejected, with what each of them gets wrong, and identify what you take to be the single hardest thing about the idea — the thing that makes it worth thirty minutes rather than three. Then give me the plan itself.

Do not explain the symbol grounding problem to me; I have done the reading. Plan how I should explain it, and be concrete about the sequence:

1. **The dependency structure.** What has to be in place before what, and why. Specifically: do I open with the regress of symbols defined by further symbols (the dictionary whose entries are all made of words), or with the contrast between the syntactic and the semantic, or with a case of a system that behaves correctly but is supposedly not in contact with anything? Does Searle's Chinese room argument belong at the start, as a bridge from what the students already think they know, or at the end, as a contrast that stops them from collapsing grounding into consciousness? Justify each ordering choice by what a later step needs from an earlier one.

2. **The carrying example.** One example the whole session can be built around, with an account of what it must have and what it must lack. It needs to be a case where the grounded and the ungrounded systems produce indistinguishable verbal behaviour, where it is not obvious that bolting on a camera or a robot arm settles anything, and where the question does not reduce to whether the system is conscious. Tell me why the usual candidates fail as the carrying example — the Chinese room, the ostensive case of being shown a red thing, the dictionary regress — and whether any of them can be salvaged as a secondary illustration later in the session.

3. **The misconceptions, in the order they will arise**, with the exact point in the sequence at which I should head each off. The ones I am predicting: that grounding is a causal or informational link to an object, so multimodal training or a robot body obviously supplies it; that this is just the Chinese room again, or is really about qualia; that if a system says true things about cheese, or catches some of its own mistakes, the question is settled; that grounding is all-or-nothing; and that this is an empirical question that scaling will decide, leaving philosophy nothing to say. Correct any of these that I have misdescribed, and supply the ones I have missed.

4. **Two moves I want the session to absorb rather than be derailed by.** First, the borrowed-grounding reply: the symbols in a text-trained system were written by people who were in perceptual contact with the world, and a map copied from a surveyor's map inherits its accuracy, so text-only training inherits grounding rather than lacking it. Where should I let this in, and how do I stop the discussion sliding from inherited to therefore not a problem? Second, a distinction someone sketched in my seminar last week between *contact-grounding* and *answerability-grounding*: contact being the requirement that a symbol stand in the right causal or covariational relation to what it is about, answerability being the requirement that the system's use of the symbol be the sort of thing the world can correct, in a way that makes a difference to the system. I do not know whether this tracks anything in the literature or is something my classmate invented on the spot. Tell me whether a specialist would recognise it, whether it is better framed as one requirement, two, or a fake contrast — and whichever you conclude, how I should use it to handle (a) a system whose symbols covary with the world through a sensor but which has no stake in being right, and (b) a system trained by success and failure in the world but with no perceptual categories. Relatedly: must grounding bottom out in non-symbolic representations at all, or could it bottom out in action?

5. **The check.** One question for the end, built around a case they have not seen, whose correct answer cannot be produced by repeating a slogan. Give me the question, what would count as a real grasp of the idea, and what a convincing-sounding miss would look like.

6. **What I should openly refuse to explain at this level**, with a one-line way of saying so. I am inclined to leave out the hard problem of consciousness, the frame problem, whether any named current system understands anything, and externalist theories of reference — but tell me if one of those is load-bearing for the distinction I am trying to teach and deserves thirty seconds and a hand-wave instead.

Format the plan as numbered steps with a rough time budget, then a misconceptions table with a column for where in the sequence each should be defused, then the final check with its grading notes.
