I'm in a formal epistemology / phil of machine learning seminar this term and I've gotten myself into an argument I can't find my way out of, mostly with my flatmate who does stats and thinks the whole philosophy of simplicity thing is a waste of time. We did Solomonoff induction last week and now I have this chain that seems to show something I definitely don't want to accept, and I can't locate the bad step.

(1) Solomonoff's setup: you fix a universal machine, weight each candidate hypothesis by 2 to the minus the length of its shortest description on that machine, then conditionalize on the data. The usual selling point is that this is a rigorous vindication of Occam's razor — simpler hypotheses get more prior weight, and the scheme converges on the truth for any computable data stream, so preferring the simpler hypothesis is officially rational.

(2) But "length of the shortest description" is only defined relative to a choice of machine / description language, and that choice is arbitrary. For any two hypotheses A and B you like, there is a universal language in which A is the shorter one and another in which B is.

(3) So which hypothesis the prior favors is settled by an arbitrary choice we make, not by anything about A or B.

(4) If a preference is fixed entirely by an arbitrary choice of ours, it isn't tracking an epistemic reason. Nobody thinks you acquire evidence by switching units.

(5) So simplicity, as Solomonoff measures it, carries no evidential weight. The convergence results, if they hold, hold for any language you pick and therefore have nothing to do with simplicity being truth-conducive.

(6) And it's not special to Solomonoff. Every formal simplicity measure has to be defined over some representation — program length, number of parameters, number of edges in a causal graph, formula length in some logic — and the same move works on each: for any pair of candidate hypotheses there's a representational scheme that flips the ordering.

(7) So Occam's razor is not a principle of evidence at all. When scientists prefer the simpler theory they're either expressing a taste, or making a pragmatic bet about what's tractable to compute and fit, or reporting a local convention of their field.

(8) The conclusion is nuts, so something upstream is wrong.

Which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost me? One thing: people keep telling me the constant factor doesn't matter because it washes out as the data comes in, but I don't see how that's an answer and not a promissory note — the constant can be as huge as you like and nobody has infinite data, so for any real inference it looks like the language choice can dominate. Even if that reply works though, I can't tell which step it's supposed to break. My flatmate says just accept (7) and treat simplicity as a computational constraint plus a convention, and I want to know what I'd be signing up for if I did that. If it helps: I'd rather keep (1) and (4) than (5), and I really don't want to have to say that the history of science's preference for simple theories has zero epistemic backing, which is what (7) seems to force.
