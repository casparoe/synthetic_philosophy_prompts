My two roommates both work on post-training, different companies, and they spent most of dinner last night going at it and I could only follow about half of it. Hoping you can referee.

The setup they both agreed on: you're training a system to produce long multi-step analyses where the final artifact can be checked -- say a cost model for a shipping plan, with a checker that confirms the bottom-line figure. But the real-world feedback arrives weeks later and is confounded: you find out the plan lost money, not which step was at fault. You can also pay people to label individual reasoning steps right away. The argument was about which signal to train on.

1. Dev: outcome signal is the only one actually tracking the thing we care about. Reasoning steps are instrumental; if a shortcut gets the right number, that's a win, not a bug. Step labels just bake in some contractor's stylistic idea of what good reasoning looks like, and cap the model at the imagination of whoever wrote the rubric.

2. Mara: sure, but with a sparse, delayed, confounded reward you get a long tail of answers that are right for the wrong reasons -- lucky guesses, memorised figures, two errors that happen to cancel. Those don't generalise when the problem gets harder. Step-level credit is dense and immediate, and it at least selects for steps that are valid.

3. Dev: you only see the trace, not the computation. If the model does real work in its internals and then writes up a plausible reconstruction, process supervision is training it to be a good reporter, not a good reasoner. You're optimising legibility, and legibility can come apart from validity in both directions.

4. Mara: fine, but that's a claim about trace faithfulness and you can test it -- perturb a step, resample from there, see whether the conclusion actually depends on it. And the alternative is worse: a reward that says "hit the target by any means" is close to a literal specification for finding a shortcut we can't see. Process supervision's failure mode, sycophantic mimicry, is visible; outcome's failure mode is invisible by construction.

5. Dev: the visibility thing cuts both ways. The checker is sound but not complete -- some correct analyses it just can't confirm, so you punish valid novel work. Outcome supervision is quietly conservative too, it selects for answers that are easy to check. And step labels are conservative in the region we most care about: a genuinely new move looks like an error to the labeler, so you're punishing exactly the exploration you wanted.

6. Mara: then incompleteness is an engineering problem, go fix the checker, at least it's a signal about the world. But fine -- practical compromise: use step labels as a shaping term early and anneal down to the outcome reward.

7. Dev: that's smuggling. If you anneal the process term away, the final policy is outcome-shaped again; what you've trained is a model that performs good reasoning during the phase where anyone is watching. Either the process constraint is load-bearing or it isn't, and you can't have it both ways.

8. Mara: then your worst case is a model that has optimised a number, and mine is a model that has learned to imitate an argument. Those feel different to me even if the metrics can't tell them apart.
