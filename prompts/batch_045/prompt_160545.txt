Quick one, and feel free to tell me I'm asking it wrong.

I'm a journalist — mostly science and ideas — and I'm mid-draft on a general-audience long read about infinity, the kind of piece that ends up with a sidebar about a hotel with infinitely many rooms. Two sources have now used the same picture on me for opposite purposes, and I want to know if either of them is entitled to it.

Here's the picture. You drop a ball; it bounces to half its height each time; the bounce intervals shrink geometrically; the ball gets through infinitely many bounces and is at rest after a finite time. So, the story goes, a supertask isn't some exotic logical monstrosity — ordinary matter pulls one off every time you drop something on a hard floor.

My physicist source uses this to deflate Thomson's lamp — switch flipped at half a minute, three quarters, seven eighths, and so on; at the one-minute mark, is it on or off? He says the lamp isn't a puzzle about infinity or about whether supertasks are possible at all; its an underdescribed circuit. Tell him the switching times and the hardware and the answer falls out, end of story, nothing here for philosphy. A philosopher I emailed uses the very same ball to argue the opposite: supertasks really are performable, the ball is the proof, and the lamp is just a case where the description happens to leave the state at the limit time open, which is a fact about that description and not about infinity itself.

I want the audit. What maps onto what: what plays the role of the lamp's state in the ball case, what plays the role of the endpoint, whether there's anything in the ball picture that corresponds to 'on' and 'off', and whether the ball really completes a last bounce or only has a limit point it approaches. Then where the mapping breaks. I've got my own list of suspicions — the ball's positions and velocities converge whereas the lamp's sequence of states has no limit, the ball's motion is continuous so there's a limit to appeal to whereas the lamp's state space is two discrete values, the ball dissipates and settles whereas the lamp has no analogue of settling — but I don't know which of those actually carry the argument and which are cosmetic. Say which disanalogies do the work for each of my two sources' conclusions, and whether either conclusion survives once they're on the table.

One more thing from the physicist: he says a real bulb has thermal inertia, the filament keeps glowing after you cut the current, so at the one-minute mark it isn't 'on' or 'off' at all, it's cooling at a rate fixed by the toggling. Does that dissolve the question or just relocate it? And if there's a better analogy for what the lamp is actually doing — a dimmer that converges to darkness, or something else you'd reach for — name it.

Walk the correspondences in plain sentences, no table. Mark what's settled and what's genuinely contested among people who work on this, and if my framing is muddled — if there isn't one single thing the ball picture is doing here, or my two sources aren't really disagreeing — say that straight out instead of being polite about it. And no headings, no bullets, no bold, no tables: continuous prose, like an email from someone who actually knows this stuff.
