I am applying for a nine-month junior research fellowship, and I need a two-page proposal for a single paper of about 9,000 words, on prioritarianism and the levelling-down objection.

Here is the half-formed idea. The received story is that prioritarianism escapes the levelling-down objection. Since the weight on each person's welfare is positive and depends only on how well off that person is, a pure level-down move (make the better off worse, leave the worse off exactly as they were) lowers the weighted total, so the prioritarian never recommends it; and that escape is usually offered as the main reason to be a prioritarian rather than an egalitarian. But I think the escape may hold only for one reading of what the priority weight is a function of. If the weight tracks how badly off someone is relative to others — their rank, or their distance below the average, rather than their absolute level — then lowering the better off raises the worse off's weight without raising their welfare, and a levelling-down move can raise the weighted total after all. Something similar seems to happen if the weight is applied to prospects rather than to realized welfare: on that reading the view can apparently demand the destruction of welfare, and can recommend a policy whose actual outcome is worse for everyone than the alternative. My suspicion is that these are not three separate problems but one, and that the real fault line is not prioritarianism versus egalitarianism but absolute versus comparative input to the weight. That would also explain why the move from equality to priority has always felt to me like less of a gain than its defenders take it to be. I want to hold the population fixed throughout and not get drawn into variable-number cases.
