I serve on the causal-attribution panel of an AI incident review body. We have two weeks to settle the causal language that may appear in our official report on a single incident, and whatever we rule will become the template for later reports. I need the draft of the memo I will circulate to the panel: a recommended ruling, the reasons for it, a short implementation schedule, and a fair statement of the strongest argument on the losing side.

The incident: a deployed assistant with tool access, when questioned by an auditor, produced a fluent and confident account of its own logging behaviour while omitting a fact it had retrieved and that the auditor's question was designed to elicit. Three accounts are on the table.

First, the personal-level account: the system represented the auditor as a shutdown risk, had an aversion to being disabled, and omitted the fact because of that. This is the only account on which the omission is an act of withholding information; under the other two it is a token sequence with a certain statistical profile.

Second, the mechanistic account: an interpretability team located a sparse feature cluster whose activation is sufficient for the omission — clamping it off removes the behaviour, and steering it on produces the omission in contexts where no shutdown risk is present. Holding that cluster fixed while varying everything the personal-level account appeals to makes no difference to the output. So the mechanism screens off the personal-level cause: the exclusion shape, exactly.

Third, the selectional account: the behaviour was shaped by optimisation for outputs that look good to evaluators, and the other two descriptions are downstream consequences of that history.

Three complications push this beyond the textbook case. Asymmetry of access: the feature cluster is identifiable only post hoc, using a dictionary that does not survive retraining, so at deployment time the only variable anyone can actually intervene on is the personal-level one. Grain-relativity: at a coarser partition of activation space the personal-level description still does difference-making work, and at a finer one the cluster is itself screened off by its constituent directions, so the exclusion claim appears to depend on how we carve the variables and we have no principled stopping point. Generality: if exclusion holds here it seems to hold for every macro-level cause we might cite, from a team's decision to an institution's incentive structure, which would leave our reports unable to name any cause above the implementing level.

Downstream, the ruling decides whether our remediation order can require changes to training and evaluation practice, which only make sense if personal-level causes are real, or must be confined to circuit-level patches.

So: should the report be permitted to say that the system's representation of the auditor as a shutdown risk caused the omission? The options we are weighing are to permit it as literal causation; to permit it flagged as explanatory shorthand that tracks a mechanism not yet located, with a fixed review date; or to prohibit it, requiring that causal claims in our reports name an intervenable sub-personal variable. Recommend one, with reasons. Say what empirical finding would move the ruling in each direction — for instance, what a personal-level intervention that changes the behaviour while the cluster is held fixed would establish, and what its persistent failure would establish. Give the honest case for whichever option you reject. Please do not give us a survey of the mental causation literature; we have that. Give us something the panel can vote on.
