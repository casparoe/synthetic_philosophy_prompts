In three weeks I defend a chapter of my dissertation in a departmental seminar, and I want a Q&A prep sheet written for the specific room I will be standing in rather than for an ideal reader.

The thesis I am defending is that the question of whether you could have come from a different sperm and egg is not a well-formed question about the modal profile of a human organism, because the items that gamete talk purports to pick out are not determinate enough to be the relata of an essentialist dependence claim. The argument has three moves and a corollary. First: a single meiosis yields a large population of genetically distinct sperm, so even granting the sperm, a great many numerically different cells were available, and there is no non-arbitrary way to say which of them is the one I "must" have come from. Second: recombination means the zygote's nuclear genome is a novel sequence present in no gamete, and post-zygotic mutation and early cell-lineage variation mean even the zygote's own genome is not a fixed object across the first divisions, so "the genome I essentially have" fails to pin down a particular either. Third, from these: the essentialist's necessity claim is not false but indeterminate, and the indeterminacy is metaphysical rather than merely epistemic — there is no fact of the matter to be discovered, not a fact we are failing to access. The corollary, which is the part I actually care about and the part I expect to be attacked: the same indeterminacy infects the move in population ethics where an essentialist blocks the claim that a child conceived later or to different parents could have been that very child, so that debate inherits a vague verdict structure it does not currently recognize.

The room: a Kripkean essentialist who thinks origin essentialism is close to a datum and will want to know what intuition I am trading on; a hylomorphic or animalist metaphysician who is friendly to biological essences but hostile to the idea that essences attach to genetic or cellular material; a Lewisian counterpart theorist who will say the whole thing turns on which counterpart relation is in play and that my argument silently assumes the wrong one; a Parfitian who is sympathetic to the corollary and impatient with the metaphysics; a modal logician who will press on whether my indeterminacy claim is even expressible — what the semantics of a vague necessity operator is supposed to be, and whether I am committed to a third truth-value or to supervaluation over precisifications of "sperm" and "genome"; and one philosopher of biology who knows gametogenesis properly and will press the empirical details.

What I want from you: the eight or so objections I will actually get, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, each attributed to the person who will raise it and phrased the way that person would phrase it in their own vocabulary, each with a ninety-second answer, a note on where that answer is soft, and a line on what I should concede in it rather than fight. Then: the two things I should concede in my opening two minutes to buy goodwill; the single reply that has to land if the chapter is to survive; the question I am hoping nobody asks; and the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend instead of hoping they miss it.

Two constraints and one epistemic request. Do not give me citations, reading lists, or anything else that needs a library or a web search — I know the literature and I am not asking for it; argue the objections on their merits. Where one of my replies turns on an empirical claim about meiosis, recombination, or early development, mark it explicitly as a premise I should confirm with a biologist rather than assert from the podium. Finally, tell me how confident you are in each of your substantive diagnoses of the disagreement, and single out the claim you are least sure of.
