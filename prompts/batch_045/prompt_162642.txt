At the reading group I run on cooperation among autonomous AI systems, a colleague argued last week for an identification I now have to answer in writing. Her claim: superrationality — the proposal associated with Hofstadter, that an agent facing a symmetric problem should include in its own calculation the fact that every other superrational agent facing that problem will arrive at the same answer, and should choose by maximizing payoff subject to that sameness constraint — is nothing other than Kant's categorical imperative in game-theoretic costume. She drew two conclusions from the identification. First, that if we build agentic systems that deliberate in this way, we get moral constraints for free in the multi-agent case, without having to solve value alignment as a separate problem. Second, that defection in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma is not merely unfortunate but irrational, and that this is the same fact as the wrongness of the false promise. The reply will be read by decision and game theorists who have never opened the Groundwork and by Kantians who have never worked through a payoff matrix, and it has to be legible to both while granting the resemblance enough credit not to be dismissible as a refusal to see it.

What I want from you is an audit, not a verdict. Give me the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the maxim, of the universal law, of the test for contradiction in conception and of the distinct test for contradiction in the will, of the agent as legislating member of a kingdom of ends, of rationality, of the other agents, and of non-compliance — and then take it apart. In particular, I want the following stress points addressed in the order that makes them bite, not as a list of unrelated objections:

The verdict of superrationality is fixed by a payoff matrix and flips when the payoffs are reordered; the verdict of the formula of universal law does not. If the cooperative answer is coming from the numbers, the moral content was never in the decision rule, and the for-free claim looks like it has relocated Kantian concern into the payoffs where no one has to argue for it.

Superrationality is defined against a population of superrational agents; against a merely game-theoretically rational opponent it recommends defecting, and the standard extension to partial take-up makes cooperation conditional on a payoff-dependent threshold share. Kantian universalization has no such parameter: one wills the universal law while knowing full well that others will not comply. Say where the threshold sits on the Kantian side of the mapping, or say plainly that the mapping has nowhere to put it and therefore holds only under full take-up.

The case I actually care about sits between the two. Take two agents fine-tuned from a shared base model but deployed with different inputs and objectives, so that their decision procedures return the same output with probability lambda strictly between zero and one — partial correlation rather than perfect sameness or independence. Tell me what the analogy recommends as lambda falls, whether lambda corresponds to anything at all on the Kantian side, and whether the fact that superrationality's recommendation degrades continuously while the moral verdict does not is evidence that the identification was always about the correlation rather than about the constraint.

Superrationality is at home in symmetric games, and there is no settled extension of it to asymmetric ones; the categorical imperative is meant to test maxims quite generally, including maxims about oneself and maxims with no strategic structure at all. Say how much of morality the analogy can even reach.

Then name what the analogy quietly imports: that the relevant all in universal law means all agents who reason as I do, which in the AI case is a moral community of structurally similar systems rather than of persons; and that universalization is a side-constraint on permissibility, whereas superrationality is a recommendation from maximization, so that the two have opposite directions of fit and one of them cannot generate what the other one is.

Close by separating the disanalogies that are load-bearing for her two conclusions from the ones that are merely awkward, telling me whether either conclusion survives and whether the weaker version of the second one can be rescued, naming the single case that flatters the analogy most and the single case that kills it so that I can put one of each in front of the room, stating what further premise she would need for the identification to actually earn what she derives from it, and proposing a better analogy if there is one — a different comparison that carries whatever true part of her claim survives. Do not give me readings.
