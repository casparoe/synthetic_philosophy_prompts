I have argued for a deflationary line about truth for some years, and a colleague's reconstruction of the case against it has left me unable to put my finger on the defect. The reconstruction runs as follows.

(1) Truth is the correctness condition on belief: necessarily, a belief that p is correct as a belief if and only if it is true that p; and it is truth, rather than anything weaker that might proxy for it, at which inquiry aims. Call this the normative platitude.

(2) Deflationism, in the version I hold: to be true is to have no nature beyond what the equivalence schema fixes. There is no substantive, natural, or causally efficacious property for the truth predicate to stand for, and no informative theory of what truth consists in is to be had.

(3) A norm must be a standard there is something to it. If a predicate stands for no substantive property, then a claim of the form "X is correct if and only if X is F" specifies no standard of assessment: the right-hand side supplies nothing for the correctness of X to consist in, and nothing by reference to which X could be guided, corrected, or criticized.

(4) So, given (2) and (3), the normative platitude is not a substantive normative thesis. To say that a belief that p is correct if and only if it is true that p is, on the deflationary account, to say no more than that a belief that p is correct if and only if p — and the latter, taken instance by instance, states no standard of assessment at all. Each instance is, relative to its own instance, close to vacuous.

(5) Hence the dilemma: either deflationism is false, or truth is not genuinely normative. On the second horn, "believe only what is true" is not a norm we can so much as state, but at best a façon de parler, or a norm only relative to ends we happen to have.

(6) Nor can the argument be read as an argument for the first horn. The demand in (3), if it disqualifies thin truth, disqualifies a substantive truth-property as well: a property's being substantive is precisely what makes it obscure how it could serve as a guide to inquiry, and the complaint that a correspondence-style property is epistemically inaccessible is the familiar complaint against it. So the argument, if it works, shows that no account of truth can underwrite truth's normativity. That I cannot accept.

I want to keep (1) and (2). I have tried three repairs and each seems to cost me something: I have tried saying that the fault is a slide from the triviality of each instance of the schema to the triviality of the universal generalization; I have tried saying that it is an illicit substitution into a normative or otherwise non-extensional context that the schema does not license; and I have tried saying that it trades on an equivocation between a predicate's expressing no substantive property and its doing no work. None of these, as I formulate them, quite convinces me, and I cannot tell which is the right one or whether all three are the same point in different dress.

So: which step do you reject, and why did it look sound? Please be explicit about how one would tell the diagnoses apart — what each predicts about cases the others do not — and then price the alternatives. I would like the following in particular.

- For each of the main families of view that rejects (2) — substantive-property accounts, primitivism, alethic pluralism, quietism, expressivism about normative discourse — what exactly must be given up, and which of them can consistently hold both (1) and (2)?
- If I keep (2), what must I say about the expressive role of the truth predicate — semantic ascent, blind endorsement, the statement of infinite conjunctions and of generalizations I cannot otherwise formulate — in order to block (4)? And if I say it, have I not drifted into a role-functional or pluralist position, and if so, is that a cost or a discovery?
- If I instead keep (1) and give up (2), what must a substantive truth-property do — guide inquiry, explain the success of true belief, be available to inquirers in a way thin truth purportedly is not — and is it credible that any property does it?
- Finally, rank the escape routes by what they cost, and tell me whether any is unattractive enough that I should simply take the second horn of (5) and deny that truth is normative. If you think I should, say what the norm of belief is instead, and what becomes of the aim of inquiry.
