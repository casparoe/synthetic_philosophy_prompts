I'm finishing the position paper for my normative ethics seminar — we read Taurek's paper on whether the numbers count a couple of weeks ago, and I came away more sympathetic to it than I expected — and I would like my commitments audited before I have to stand up and defend them. This is the set as I currently hold it.

(1) The numbers do not count. There is no reason to save the greater number that consists simply in the greater number. What there is, in the standard case, is five people each of whom loses his life and one person who loses his life; nothing over and above those six losses occurs. My usual way of putting it is that there is no one for whom the summed loss is a loss.

(2) Within a single life, losses do add up. Someone facing two migraines next month is worse off than someone facing one, and — what I take to be the same point carried over to risk — a person's prospect can be better or worse: a five-in-six chance of survival is a better prospect for the person who has it than a one-in-six chance.

(3) In the one-off case — six strangers, no special ties, I can save either the one or the five — it is permissible to save the one. Permissible, not required. The five have no complaint that the one lacks.

(4) It is also permissible to run a lottery that gives each of the six an equal chance, so that the five are rescued with probability five-sixths. I would go further and call this the fairest procedure available, though I do not think it is required of me.

(5) Methodologically I am working inside the contractualist framework we used earlier in the term: an act is wrong if it is forbidden by a principle that some individual could reasonably reject, and complaints are weighed against one another one at a time — several people's smaller grievances never combine to outweigh one person's larger one. This is the machinery I use to get from the picture in (1) to the verdicts in (3) and (4).

(6) At the level of a standing policy, though — if I knew I would face a long run of such choices, or had to settle on a rule before learning whether I would be among the one or among the five — I choose to save the many, on the ground that each person's ex ante prospect is better under that policy than under its rival.

(7) And the same in a small-stakes case I invented to check myself: all six will be pulled out of the water in ten minutes whatever I do, and the only thing I can affect is who treads water until then. I can spare one person ten minutes of terror or spare five people ten minutes of terror, and I think the numbers give me no reason here either.

What I want is an audit, not an overview of the debate. Tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look as though they are — I would bet that at least one of the apparent conflicts dissolves under inspection, and I want to know which. Rank the real tensions by severity rather than setting them side by side as equals, and for each one say exactly what I would have to give up and what that would cost me elsewhere in the set. Then, if I were willing to make only one revision, tell me which one removes the most strain and what it would leave me holding. Please be specific about the mechanism in each case: I want to see what commits me to what, not just be told that two of my views pull in different directions.
