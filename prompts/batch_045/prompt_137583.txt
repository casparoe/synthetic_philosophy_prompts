Someone in a policy seminar defended a proposal that you'd have to sit through a short financial-literacy course before you're allowed to take out certain kinds of high-interest credit, and when I said this is obviously a restriction on freedom he pulled out the old forced-to-be-free line: making people do it doesn't reduce their freedom, it literally increases it, so there's no liberty objection left to weigh against the benefits. (Extra wrinkle: whether you've done the course "sincerely" is decided case by case by a private board with wide discretion, which feels like it should matter somehow but I can't say why.) I'm sure something slippery is being done with the word freedom here, I just can't pin down what.

Can you lay out as a menu the different precise theses that remark could be expressing — that the compulsion is more than paid back in future options, that real freedom is effective ability rather than mere absence of interference, that the law makes you do what your own considered will already wants, that you're a co-author of the law so obeying it is obeying yourself, something about not being under another's arbitrary power, something about freedom being a status you hold, or maybe just a straight redefinition of the term — and for each say whether it's a platitude, a substantive claim worth arguing about, or verbal sleight of hand, and crucially whether it would actually deliver what he needs, i.e. no freedom lost at all, rather than only a freedom cost that's worth paying.
