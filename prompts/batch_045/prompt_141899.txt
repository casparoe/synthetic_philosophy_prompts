So this came up at a barbeque on saturday and I've been chewing on it since and I genuinely can't tell who's got the better of it, so I want you to referee.

My buddy Dan bought a small abstract painting at an estate sale a couple of years back, forty bucks, purely because he liked the colours. Turns out — long story involving his uncle knowing somebody — it's a forgery. One of those mid-century names whose stuff goes for six figures. It was painted around 1962 by the painter's sister, who had worked in her studio for years, could copy the signature in her sleep, and used leftover canvas off the same roll and the same brand of paint. She did it after the painter died, broke and angry about it. Nobody could tell. The confession only surfaced decades later. Some of the details are fuzzy but that's the shape.

Dan says he doesn't care: he liked it before, he likes it now, it's staying on the wall. That set Jen and Omar off for about an hour. Roughly in order:

(1) Jen: you can't just decide not to care. Half of what you were enjoying was the thought that this was made by that person, that year, in that studio. That thought is gone now. If the colours alone were doing all the work you'd be equally happy with a thirty-dollar print, and you're not, so the history was doing something.

(2) Omar: sure, but he had two years of actual enjoyment with it on the wall, and nothing about the object changed the day he found out — same canvas, same brushstrokes, same sixty years of age. Finding out later can't reach backwards and spoil the two years. Whatever the history adds, it isn't everything, because the pleasure was real before anyone knew.

(3) Jen: I'm not saying it's everything. I'm saying the painting isn't only the physical object. Dan has been enjoying a story and the story was false. It's like finding out the letter that got you through a bad month was written by someone pretending to be your father. Same words on the page.

(4) Omar: bad analogy, letters carry information. Turn it around instead. Suppose the painter planned a series of ten, sketched all ten, and died after finishing four — and the sister painted the other six from the sketches, in the same studio, with the same paint. Forgeries, or just the series finished? If you say forgeries and worthless, that's crazy. If you say they're the real thing, then it was never about whose hand held the brush; it's more like whether the thing belongs to her project. Either way "not made by her hand, therefore nothing" is too crude.

(5) Jen: I never said worthless and I never said it's about the hand. It's about the object telling you a false story about where it came from. A print doesn't pretend to be a 1962 painting. The sister's six would be labelled as the sister's six. A forgery is a thing that lies. Label it honestly and I'll look at it all day — knowing the whole messed-up situation I might like it more.

(6) Omar: then take a case with no lie in it. Say some curator in 1970 misattributed the painting by honest mistake, no forger anywhere, and everyone's believed it ever since. Dan finds out it's by somebody nobody's heard of. I'd bet his enjoyment drops just as far. So it isn't the lying, is it. You just wanted the famous one.

(7) Jen: your kid brings home a drawing, you stick it on the fridge, ten years later you find out it was traced by the kid at the next table and swapped. Same lines, same everything, ten years of real pride behind you. Tell me you don't care.

(8) Omar: I'd care. But that's because the drawing is a trace of *her* — evidence of what was going on in her head. That's your letter again. Not every object works like that. And Dan never met the painter. He isn't treasuring a trace of someone he loved, he's treasuring a trace of someone he read about. So what does he actually have left once the name comes off the label, and is that leftover enough? That's the real question, and I think it's a decent amount.

(9) Jen: fine, then here's my last one. If what you admire is the achievement, the sister's is bigger. She had to solve the same problems cold, in reverse, without the twenty years of winding up to it.

(10) Omar: — wait, that's my point, not yours, but go on.

(11) Jen: I'm steelmanning you and it still fails. Admiring that is like admiring an impersonator more than the person. And it's an achievement you can only get by deceiving people: she took the hard route only because the easy one, painting under her own name, didn't pay. I don't think we owe that admiration.

(12) Omar: okay but it's had its own sixty years. Period canvas, period paint, it survived a flood in somebody's basement, Dan's kid drew a rocket on the back of it. Old things partly matter because they made it through, and none of that is forged.

Then someone else at the table said she'd read that famous forgeries sometimes end up valuable *as forgeries* — worth real money precisely because of the story about the faking — and that broke my brain a little and they moved on to something else.

Can you go through it move by move and tell me who's right about what, not just who wins overall? Like, which of those points actually land and which are dodges. And I have a feeling they're both missing something — maybe that there are two or three different kinds of value getting mixed together in there, or that they're arguing about different objects without noticing. If that's the case, say so and lay out what the actual disagreement is underneath. Don't worry about being diplomatic, and please don't tell me what Dan should do, I don't care about that part.
