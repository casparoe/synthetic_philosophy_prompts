I write backend services for a living and have zero philosophy training, but I've been poking at philosophy of mind on weekends and scribbled the notes below for a short, blog-post-length essay. I'd like comments before I bother turning it into prose.

THE NOTES

Setup. Higher-order theories say roughly: a mental state of mine is conscious not because of what it is, but because some other state in me is *about* it — I'm in some sense aware of being in it. Unconscious states are the ones nobody at home is looking at. The appeal is a clean line between a state that's merely there and one that's there *for* you, with reportability, introspection, and the gap between blindsight-ish cases and ordinary seeing all falling out of one mechanism.

Worry 1: the higher-order state does all the work. Once you let the "looking" state be wrong — which defenders do, to handle dreams and hallucinations, where there's no matching first-order state — the first-order state drops out of the story entirely. The theory becomes: consciousness = having a higher-order state with a certain content. At which point why call it higher-order at all?

Worry 2: two made-up cases. I haven't seen either discussed, though I probably just havent read enough.

- Cross-wiring. A monitoring module that normally reads my own first-order states is surgically re-pointed at someone else's. It then issues perfectly ordinary "I am in pain" states, sourced from her pain and not mine. Am I in conscious pain? I want to say obviously not, and that if the theory says yes it has fabricated a feeling out of nothing.
- Log replay. Same module, but fed a delayed tape of my own states from yesterday. Right now it reports an itch I had yesterday, and nothing itchy is going on now. Felt now, or merely believed?

The point of both: if the honest answer is that something has to line up correctly between the two states — same system, same time, the right causal chain — then the theory has quietly bolted a tracking condition onto the higher-order one, and that condition is doing the real work. Which starts to look less like a higher-order theory and more like its rivals in a costume.

Where I think it lands (the part I'm least sure about). Day-job analogy: adding telemetry to a service doesn't change what the service does. Structured logs, metrics, a sidecar scraping internal state — none of that makes the computation feel like anything to itself, and nobody expects it to. What observability buys you is availability: the state becomes readable by other subsystems, reportable, something you can act on later. So maybe the honest reading of these theories is that they're theories of access, or of being mine-and-sayable, not of what it's like. But then the door swings the other way: if the first-order states are already felt and the higher-order machinery only publishes them, you're most of the way to saying a lot of unpublished states are felt too, just dimly — which is the view the theory existed to avoid. So it looks stable only in a narrow band, and I cant tell why that band is the right place to stand.
