I'm rebuilding the first session of our first-year philosophy of biology seminar — the students are mostly on the pre-med biochemistry stream, so they arrive with a head full of mechanism and no metaphysics at all — and instead of opening with the usual slides about definitions I want to put a tier list on the corridor wall and let them argue about it before anyone in the room says the word 'definition'. I need you to help me build it. Here's a deliberately unsorted pile of cases: a prion; a giant virus with a genome larger than those of some bacteria and a sizeable share of genes nobody can put a job to; a mule; an immortalised human cell line that's been passaged in labs for seventy-odd years and has drifted a long way from anything recognisably human; a bacterial spore pulled out of permafrost and revived after tens of thousands of years; a tardigrade in its dried-out tun state and then the same animal a day after the water comes back; a fire front moving through a pine plantation; a slime mould that has just solved a maze; a computer worm that copies itself, its variants selected by how well they dodge antivirus; a synthetic bacterium whose genome has been cut down to a few hundred genes, a good chunk of them with no known function; and, because I want to know whether your criterion is doing any work, our departmental mailing list, which has outlived most of the people on it and reproduces by being forwarded.

Score each one out of a hundred for how alive it is, sort them into four or five tiers with names I could actually put on a poster, and give me one sentence per case saying what did the work in landing it where it did. I don't want a survey of positions on the question and I don't want a bibliography — just your numbers and your reasons. I also have a hobby-horse and I want to see whether it survives contact with a real ordering: I think the quantity worth tracking is how much of a thing's continued persistence is owed to its own ongoing activity rather than to the plain inertness of the stuff it's made of — call it the upkeep ratio. Score the pile on that, then tell me how the list reshuffles if you score it instead against the working definition that gets tossed around in astrobiology, more or less self-sustaining chemistry able to undergo Darwinian evolution, and then against a third test I half believe: that a thing is alive to the extent that it can be starved, poisoned, infected or in some other way harmed in its own right, i.e. to the extent that it has a good of its own even if it doesn't know it.

Then tell me which single case you'd put right on the cutoff between the living and the not, and say why that one rather than its neighbours; and flag any case where your number is really a confession that you're unsure about the biology rather than a judgement about life. British English throughout please, and keep the whole thing to a side of A4 or so — its a poster, not an essay.
