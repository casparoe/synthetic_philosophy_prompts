I am presenting a paper next week at a small interdisciplinary workshop on culture and political economy, and I would like your help preparing for the question period.

The thesis: the Protestant ethic thesis has almost always been read as a causal claim of the ordinary variable kind — did religion, or something correlated with it, produce the economic transformation of Northern Europe? — and on that reading it looks both empirically shaky, since better data and sharper identification strategies keep shrinking the effect, and philosophically puzzling, since it asks us to treat a value as a cause. I want to defend a different reconstruction: that what the new religious vocabulary changed was neither agents' preferences over outcomes nor their beliefs about how wealth is acquired, but the set of courses of conduct available to them as things to do at all — which actions could be chosen under some description. On this reading the spirit is not a taste for accumulation but a change in the space of options, and Weber's notion of elective affinity is not a correlation to be estimated but the relation between a vocabulary and the actions it makes describable. His image of ideas as switchmen setting the tracks along which interests push action should be taken more literally than it usually is.

The empirical half follows from this. I concede in advance that the reconstruction cannot be tested with a design that treats confession as the treatment and a growth or occupational outcome as the dependent variable, because here the causal variable is partly constitutive of the outcome: the outcome in the counterfactual case is not the same kind of thing, so there is no well-defined difference to estimate. What I offer instead as an observable signature is a lag — the new conduct should appear before the vocabulary that makes it comfortable, and transitional agents should display new forms of scruple, guilt about idleness and about expenditure that yields nothing, in diaries and disciplinary records, before these show up in aggregate behavior.

The room: about thirty people, none of whom will have read the paper. Two thirds are economists and political scientists doing historical work with modern causal-inference tooling, who have learned to hear an appeal to culture as a sign of an unobserved confounder; several have published on religion and economic outcomes and are invested in that literature. Two are comparative-historical sociologists in the Weberian line, sympathetic to the claim that meanings matter but likely to think I have emptied it of content. One is a philosopher of social science who takes an interventionist account of causation as the default. The chair is a historian of early modern Europe who regards the empirical basis of the thesis as having been dismantled decades ago.

What I want is a prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room — not the ones I would get from a philosophy of action seminar — phrased the way each subgroup would phrase them, in the order the room's etiquette dictates (the economists first, the sociologists once they are done, the historian when she can no longer keep quiet, the philosopher last), ranked by how much damage they would do to whether the paper is taken seriously afterward, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is softest. Also tell me what to concede in the first minute of the talk rather than being forced to concede during questions; which single reply has to land for the version of the paper I actually want to write; and the one question I am hoping nobody asks, with what I should say if they do.
