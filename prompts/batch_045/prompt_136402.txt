I spent a career in experimental physics and came to the philosophy of science only after retiring, so do forgive me if I use a technical term loosely. What I want is a case analysis of an episode I have watched from the outside for decades, read through a distinction I have been trying to work out for myself.

The episode is the long argument over the stability of the solar system: the perturbation results of the late eighteenth century that made the planetary motions look quasi-periodic and permanently stable; Poincaré's work on the three-body problem for King Oscar's prize, which broke the hope that a series solution could carry us to a proof; and then the numerical integrations of the last thirty-five years, above all Laskar's 1989 paper in Nature, which reported a maximum Lyapunov exponent corresponding to a Lyapunov time of roughly five million years for the inner planets (later work quotes a spread of values, somewhere in the range of two to two hundred and thirty million years depending on which bodies and which model), and the 2009 integrations by Laskar and Gastineau, which ran some twenty-five hundred slightly varied futures forward five billion years and found about twenty-five of them ending in a severely disrupted system, usually via Mercury. I am supplying these figures so that nothing in your answer has to rest on looking them up.

The distinction I want used is one I have not seen drawn cleanly, and I would like you to criticize it as much as apply it. It seems to me that three claims get run together under the word determinism, and that a fourth may be needed:

First, what I will call trajectory determinism: the state at one time fixes, uniquely, the state at all times. Second, Laplacean predictability: from data of finite precision, and by a finite computation, one can obtain predictions of finite precision to an arbitrarily distant time. This is the claim the famous intelligence in Laplace's essay on probabilities has been read as making, and it is the one chaos is usually said to kill. Third, distributional predictability: trajectories are unpredictable, but the statistics over an invariant measure are stable, so that one can forecast the climate even when one cannot forecast the weather. Fourth, what I will venture to call categorical predictability: not which trajectory, and not a distribution over trajectories, but a stable probability for a coarse partition of outcome types — the system stays put; one planet is ejected; two planets collide — where the probabilities are taken over an ensemble of initial conditions consistent with our measurement uncertainty, and where there need be no invariant measure underneath them at all.

The case I want made in both directions, and I would rather you be disciplined about the mapping than comprehensive:

Say precisely which of the four theses the episode tests, and which it leaves untouched. My suspicion is that the solar system has nothing whatever to say about the first, and that the usual claim that chaos refuted determinism is a slide from the second to the first. Tell me if that suspicion is right, and whether the first thesis is doing any work anyone cares about once the second is gone.

Give me the actors and their options as the theory sees them. Laplace was not merely being optimistic: within the model he had, at the order he had, the result was correct, and I want credit given where it is due. Poincaré's obstruction was not an empirical discovery about the planets. Laskar's numbers are properties of a system of averaged secular equations, not of a full integration of the world, and one of his successors' findings was that including general relativity changes the instability rate for Mercury by something like a factor of sixty. So: is 'the solar system is chaotic' a claim about the planets or a claim about a model class? What kind of evidence could in principle settle that, and does the machinery of finite-time Lyapunov exponents and shadowing help, or does it merely move the question one level up?

Do the arithmetic honestly and then say what follows from it. With a Lyapunov time of about five million years, forecasting to the Sun's remaining lifetime demands an initial precision on the order of one part in ten to the four hundred and thirty, which is not merely beyond measurement but beyond any precision at which the model is physically defined at all, given asteroid perturbations, solar mass loss, and post-Newtonian terms. I take this to show that what defeats us here is chaos plus the truncation of the model, not chaos alone, and that a demon working inside the true equations was never threatened. Is that the right moral, or does the truncation point cut deeper than I think?

Then the part I care most about. In weather forecasting, the third thesis is the rescue: give up the trajectory, keep the statistics. In this episode there is no attractor and no invariant measure to fall back on, because the interesting outcomes are escapes and collisions, and the system may simply come apart. So the third rescue is unavailable precisely where it is needed. I want to know whether my fourth thesis can be made to bear weight without the third, or whether a probability over an ensemble of numerically perturbed initial conditions is really just a fact about how widely we chose to smear them, dressed up as a forecast. What are the twenty-five disrupted runs out of twenty-five hundred supposed to be a probability of?

Finally, does the episode confirm the standard story, or is it an anomaly, or is it a case the standard story never anticipated? Say where the theory is silent rather than making it speak, and do not give me references or further reading — I can find those myself.
