Last night I had a long argument with a friend — a working mathematician with no patience for philosophy — about whether intuitionistic logic is a genuine rival to classical logic or just classical logic with the excluded middle removed and a guilt trip attached. Two of his jabs stuck with me. First: if your only complaint about an instance of excluded middle over an undecidable predicate is that nobody has a decision procedure for it, that sounds like epistemic modesty, not a different logic — a claim about what we can currently compute, dressed up as a claim about what is true. Second: if the advertised payoff of constructive proof is that it hands you an algorithm, people now extract computational content from classical proofs too, so the payoff no longer distinguishes the logics. I had replies to neither.

Next month I am recording an episode of a podcast I host. The audience is people who took one logic course years ago and remember little of it: they know what a proof by contradiction looks like and have heard that constructivists refuse some of them, and not much else. My guest is a philosopher of mathematics who defends constructivism and who calls her position local constructivism: classical reasoning is in good standing over domains we can survey or decide — finite structures, decidable predicates, bounded quantification — whereas intuitionistic logic is what is correct once quantification ranges over unsurveyable infinite domains. She insists this is not logical pluralism, because there is a single account of validity; it is just that validity is indexed to the epistemic profile of the domain being quantified over.

Draft me ten interview questions, none longer than two sentences and none that can be discharged by restating local constructivism. I need at least one question on each of the following, and I want the questions distributed so the episode moves rather than sitting in one spot:

1. The collapse worry: intuitionistic logic already yields every instance of excluded middle for decidable predicates, and adding those instances for the atomic cases recovers full classical logic, so what does her permission to reason classically over decidable domains actually add? I expect her to say the point is not extra theorems but a story about why the classical fragment is safe; press on what that story is doing if it licenses nothing new.
2. The Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov reading of the connectives, and in particular the clause for implication, which explains a proof of a conditional as a transformation carrying any proof of the antecedent to a proof of the consequent. Ask whose proofs these are, proofs in which system, and whether explaining the connectives in terms of constructions and proofs is an explanation or a restatement in slightly larger vocabulary.
3. The disjunction and existence properties: intuitionistic systems prove a disjunction only if they prove a disjunct, and an existential only with a witness. Ask whether this is a virtue of intuitionistic logic or a symptom of proving less, and what it is worth to a mathematician who wants to know whether a solution exists.
4. The double-negation translation, which embeds classical logic and classical arithmetic into their intuitionistic counterparts. Ask whether this shows classical logic to be a special case of intuitionistic logic, a fragment of it, or something whose apparent rivalry is an artifact of translation, and have her say what she thinks the translation does to meaning.
5. The thesis that every total function from the reals to the reals is continuous, which holds in Brouwer-style intuitionistic mathematics but not in the constructivism of the Bishop school, which is deliberately compatible with classical mathematics. Ask whether that thesis follows from the logic or from a substantive theory of the continuum, and which kind of constructivist she takes herself to be.
6. Markov's principle, the step from the absurdity of there being no example to there being an example, for a decidable predicate. Ask whether accepting it is compatible with the proof-conditions reading of negation and existence, whether rejecting it creates trouble for ordinary constructive analysis such as showing that a real number that is not equal to zero is apart from zero, and why this principle rather than excluded middle is the real fault line.
7. My friend's first jab, put to her directly and without softening: is local constructivism a metaphysical thesis about indeterminate truth values in unsurveyable domains, or only a recommendation about which inferences to trust given our epistemic situation?
8. My friend's second jab: if the payoff is algorithmic content, why should anyone change logics rather than keep classical proofs and extract what they need from them, and separately, if classical mathematics is as locally illegitimate as she thinks over infinite domains, why does it work so well?
9. The metalanguage problem she will have to face: the results she relies on, such as the disjunction property itself or the soundness of Kripke semantics, are standardly proved using classical reasoning about an infinite set of proofs. Ask whether that is inconsistency on her part, and whether the move of treating metamathematics as surveyable can survive the fact that these proofs range over infinitely many derivations.

For each question, give me the answer she is most likely to give, in two or three sentences, and then one follow-up question written specifically against that answer, so that I am not scrambling in the studio. End the list with a tenth question about what would count as evidence against local constructivism — what result, decision procedure, or methodological failure would make her give it up — and with a closing question in which I relay the argument I had with my friend and let her answer him. Keep the questions in spoken register, gloss any technical term in the same sentence you use it, and keep the whole thing under nine hundred words.
