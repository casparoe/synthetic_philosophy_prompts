I've been reading rationalist blogs since the SSC days and one thing I feel like that corner of the internet has never really cashed out is how much we should trust anything we think we know about the distant past. Everyone's got calibration practice and base rates and Bayes for *contemporary* stuff, but then someone says "the Persians lost 20,000 men at Plataea" or "this king reigned for 40 years" and we either shrug or defer to whoever sounds most like a historian, with no numbers attached. So I've been toying with building what I half-jokingly call a reliability ledger: rows are types of claim about the ancient world (battle casualties, eclipse and omen reports, foundation dates, succession lists, population figures, "X said Y" quotations from speeches), columns are checks — does the claim have independent attestation, does it have physical corroboration, is it internally consistent, what was the text *for* (its genre, audience, and the incentive of the person who wrote it), and how many copying-steps it is from the event. The idea is to end up with rough base rates by claim-type rather than vibes. My friend thinks this is hopeless and that anything without physical corroboration is basically fan fiction; I think that's too strong, but I can't say exactly why in a way that cashes out in numbers.

So pretend you're advising me on where to put the effort. The budget is one person, maybe ten to fifteen hours a week for a year, no institutional affiliation, no new excavations, no paywalled databases — just published translations, whatever's online, and a decent used bookshop. I'm not asking for a reading list or citations. What I want is a defended agenda: which four or five open questions in this area are most worth that year, and in what order? Some candidates I've been kicking around, so you can see the shape of what I mean and tell me which of them are worth anything: (1) corroboration inflation — two surviving sources that look independent very often aren't, because both descend from the same lost source, and I have no idea how to estimate how often that happens or by how much it should discount an apparent two-source report; (2) the selection problem — the stuff that survived is a filtered sample, and the filter correlates with what later copyists and monasteries found useful or edifying, so "no source mentions it" is much weaker evidence than it looks, but I also don't know how to make that quantitative; (3) an "expected remains" test — for a given claim, ask what other traces we'd expect if it were true and what fraction of those traces would plausibly still exist, i.e. turning corroboration into something like a likelihood ratio instead of a checkbox; (4) treating transmission like a chain of custody, where each link is a copyist with their own error rate *and* their own incentive to smooth or improve the text, and asking what that does to confidence as a function of chain length; (5) what to do with acknowledged reconstructions — I'm thinking of Thucydides more or less admitting he put plausible sentiments into his speakers' mouths rather than reporting what was said, which strikes me as a much bigger deal for how we read all ancient "quotation" than people usually treat it as.

Tell me which of these are real questions and which are dead ends or already-solved-in-spirit, and which important one I'm not listing. But spend most of your answer defending the ordering: for each, what makes it upstream of the others (so that getting it wrong contaminates everything downstream), whether it's actually tractable for one person working from existing material versus needing a field's worth of data collection, how much it would change the numbers that actually go in the ledger versus just changing how I talk about them, and what I could notice over the next year — a digitization project, a new radiocarbon calibration, a big corpus release, an argument someone makes — that would move it up or down the list. I'd also like to know where you'd deliberately deprioritize something interesting because its answer wouldn't change much else. And if you think the whole ledger idea is misconceived — that base rates by claim-type can't be meaningfully aggregated, or that the real bottleneck is something else entirely — say so and tell me what the year should go into instead.
