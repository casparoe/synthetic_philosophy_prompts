My housemate is a psychiatry resident and we got into this at breakfast on Sunday and I've now wasted most of a day on it, so I'm writing it out in steps because that's the only way I can see what's bothering me.

I don't care whether any actual diagnostic manual is good or bad. The question is whether a psychiatric category could so much as be a genuine kind of thing — something a classifier could be right or wrong about — given that the people being classified can learn the classification and change because of it. That looping-effects point, the one about human kinds feeding back into themselves.

Here's the argument. I think it proves too much and I can't locate where.

(1) A kind is genuine rather than merely conventional or useful only if its members share a causal structure that is what it is independently of anyone's classifying them as members. Call this the independence condition.

(2) Human kinds loop. Get classified, and you reinterpret your own history, other people treat you differently, institutions route you into different paths, you get handed a vocabulary for yourself, and the category itself gets revised in response to how the classified population turns out. Classification and classified co-evolve.

(3) For psychiatric categories the loop is unusually strong, and it comes in two layers.
(3a) First-order: someone encounters a category, recognises themselves in it, and presents differently at intake as a result — the criteria get met partly because the person now has the description.
(3b) Second-order: people respond not just to being classified but to the fact that the classification loops. Someone arrives already fluent in the vocabulary because thirty-second symptom checklists went viral on a video platform; clinicians pick a label partly for its downstream effects, because that label is the one that unlocks services or is the one a school will act on. The circulation of the category is now one of the causes of its own instances.

(4) In the (3b) case there is no classification-independent structure left for the category to track. What the criteria latch onto is in part manufactured by the criteria being in circulation.

(5) So no psychiatric category is a genuine kind. It's a going concern, a practical tool, a way of moving people through institutions — not something anyone gets right or wrong.

(6) But nothing in (2) is specific to psychiatry. Any kind whose members can learn that they are members loops: gifted, delinquent, alcoholic, traumatised, diabetic, neurodivergent, employed. Some of these we think of as perfectly respectable.

(7) And the same structure shows up entirely outside medicine: credit scores, at-risk flags, employee of the month. All of them do the (3b) thing to some degree.

(8) So if looping defeats kindhood, no kind of person is a genuine kind. But then we lose a complaint clinicians actually make all the time and which seems to be a real complaint — that some category is a mistake because it lumps together two different underlying conditions, or that a prevalence rise is an artefact rather than a finding. On the (8) view there's nothing for that complaint to be about. It can only be a preference.

(9) So I have to either weaken (1), block the move from (3) to (4), or swallow (8). All three feel like they cost something I don't want to pay.

What I'd keep, if forced: I want to keep (2) and (3) — I think the phenomenon is real and I think the second-order layer is doing important work that people usually skip. I'm much more suspicious of (5) than of (3). And I'd rather keep the clinicians' complaint in (8) than keep the independence condition in its current form.

There's a distinction I've been using that might be doing real work or might be a red herring, so please tell me which. I've been sorting loops into three: loops that *amplify* (classifying makes the criteria get met more reliably, because the person consolidates around the description), loops that *erase* (classify, treat, and the person no longer meets the criteria), and loops that *run away* (no fixed point; the criteria and the population keep sliding). Then a test: a kind survives looping if the loop has an attractor, and only runaway loops defeat it. But I notice two things that make me distrust my own test. If an amplifying loop has a stable fixed point, that looks like causal structure coming back — looping would then be evidence *for* the kind being real, not against, which kills (4). And an erasing loop is unstable but obviously not unreal, so instability can't be the test either. Which leaves only runaway loops doing any work, at which point the whole issue looks empirical — how many categories actually run away — and I don't want an empirical answer, I want to know whether that is even the right place to put the burden, or whether I've moved the argument somewhere it doesn't belong.

So: which step do you reject, why does it look fine right up until it doesn't, and what does each of the three ways out have to pay? Be concrete about the bill — what do you give up about diagnosis, about disagreement between clinicians, about patients' own self-descriptions, if you take that route.

And the bit I'll actually judge the answer on: say what would change your mind. Which consideration would make you drop the step you dropped and pick a different one, and where is your diagnosis most likely to be wrong? I'd rather have a verdict I can audit than a confident one.
