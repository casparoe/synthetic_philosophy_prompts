I sit on the allocation committee of a small philanthropic fund that will spend roughly $5M over the next three years, mostly on two-year postdoctoral fellowships and small pilot grants, and we have committed our next cycle to a single question: how much of the world's expected moral cost is being carried by animals whose nervous systems comprise on the order of a million neurons or fewer. We are badly stuck on where the money should go. Our working heuristic has been what one of us calls the one-in-a-million rule: the small invertebrates we are unsure about outnumber the animals we already take seriously by something like six orders of magnitude in headcount, so any credence in their having valenced states above roughly one in a million makes them dominate the expected-total arithmetic regardless of how confident we are about chickens or fish. Another committee member replies that this is not an argument but a way of laundering uncalibrated guesses through multiplication — that at credences that small the honest formalism is a set of priors rather than a point estimate, or a merely comparative ranking of confidence — and that if the formalism is doing that much of the work, the same money might accomplish more on animals whose sentience nobody seriously doubts. A third says the arithmetic is idle until someone closes what he calls the candidate-marker gap: we have no agreed account of what would even count as evidence of valenced experience in a nervous system with no neocortex, no recognizable homologue of vertebrate pain pathways, and perhaps a hundred thousand neurons, so we cannot yet say whether the plausible markers are the kind of thing such a system could have.

Our current shortlist, which I do not trust, runs roughly as follows: comparative neurobiology aimed at sharpening the credence itself; taxon-level triage meant to identify which orders and which life stages are the leading candidates, so that effort is not spread evenly across groups that are probably very unalike; empirical work on what I have started calling the rebound problem, the worry that suppressing a population of fast-breeding insects may not reduce the total amount of insect life lived at all, because reproduction and ecological replacement refill the niche within a season, so that the expected effect of an intervention depends mostly on population dynamics rather than on the headcount you removed; decision-theoretic and meta-normative work on how to reason at all when both the empirical credence and the moral weight per unit are uncertain by orders of magnitude; welfare standards and intervention research for the rapidly growing insect-protein industry, including the substitution worry that farmed species such as black soldier fly and mealworm may differ in candidate-sentience from the wild insects whose ecological and economic role they take over, and that the industry may increase the total headcount even while shifting it; and a rather different lever that a pest-management consultant put to me last month, namely that the largest near-term numbers may be sitting in agricultural and municipal pest control, where trillions of animals die, where a marginal change in what counts as an acceptable method is often cheap to implement, and where essentially nobody is currently asking questions.

What I want from you is a defended agenda, not a survey and not a reading list. Given that budget, that time horizon, and the people we can actually hire — mostly philosophers, a few computational and empirical collaborators, no one able to run a large wet lab — rank these lines of work, or replace them with better ones I have not listed, and defend the ordering explicitly on the criteria you take to matter: how much other work depends on each item, tractability for the kind of people we can hire rather than for someone else, neglectedness given who else is funding what, and the cost of being wrong in each direction. Then tell me what would move things around: for each of your top few items, what result over the next two or three years would push it up or down the list, and what a genuine negative or null result would look like as opposed to merely an inconclusive one. Finally, part of our committee wants the fund to draw practical conclusions right now — to avoid or discourage insect-protein products, to treat the widely reported decline of wild insect populations as something to be welcomed rather than lamented, or to push for humane-killing standards in pest control. Say which of those implications actually follow from a serious version of the scale argument, which are overrated or are quietly free-riding on other commitments, and which would be embarrassing to have funded on the strength of an argument this unsettled.
