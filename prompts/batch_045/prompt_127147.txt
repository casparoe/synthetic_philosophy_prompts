My flatmate is a hardcore utilitarian and we got into this at like 1am after a reading group on triage ethics, and I've been going back and foward on it ever since. The topic was those rescue cases where you can save one person or five. Years ago in a seminar I read a paper arguing that the numbers shouldn't count at all — Taurek, I think it was, something along the lines of "Should the Numbers Count?" — and I remember being irritated by it without being able to say what was wrong with it. So I've started writing down my own gut verdicts on cases to see whether they add up to anything, and honestly they kind of don't. Here they are. Unless I say otherwise everyone is a stranger, the stakes are death vs death, and "save" means save for sure:

(1) One vs five, everyone dies otherwise: save the five. But it bugs me to say the five's claim is five times as strong, and I feel something about the one that I can't cash out.
(2) Five vs four: almost no pull at all, a coin flip feels fine. A hundred vs ninety-six: a coin flip still feels fine, which is weird, because that's four actual lives.
(3) The one is my kid and the five are strangers: I think it's permissible, maybe even required of me, to save my kid.
(4) Five people each lose a hand vs one person dies: save the one. A million people each get a pinprick vs one person dies: save the one too (my flatmate thinks the second is obvious; I lean that way but I'm not confident).
(5) A million pinpricks all delivered to one person vs one pinprick each to a million people: I'd prevent the million pinpricks to the one person, because a million pinpricks in one body sounds like torture, even though the total is the same and even though in (4) I let the spread-out version lose.
(6) Same idea inside a single life: I can prevent five separate injuries to one person, or one injury each to five people. This feels like a genuine wash to me, maybe even leaning toward the five people — but it feels nowhere near as clear as (1), and I can't say why it isn't.
(7) I can save one person for sure, or go to another island where there's a 51% chance of six people: I take the one. At 99% I'd take the island. I have no idea where my threshold is or whether having one makes me incoherent.
(8) Instead of saving a group for sure, I can throw a switch that picks exactly one of the six at random and saves them. One vs one: coin flip, obviously fine. Five vs one: not fine, I want the five saved, and the equal-chance lottery feels like a cop-out.
(9) The one is the guy who started the fire, the five are innocent: I feel zero guilt about saving the five, whereas in (1) I feel some. The verdict itself doesn't change.

The closest I've got to a principle is: compare the worst thing that happens to any single person under each option, and if one option is worse for someone in a way the other isn't, that settles it; only when the worst individual outcomes are on a par do you count heads at all, and then you count them proportionally rather than by difference — which is why five vs one moves me and a hundred vs ninety-six doesn't. But I don't think it survives contact with my own list: it says nothing useful about (7), it doesn't have room for (3), I suspect it gives an answer on (6) that I don't actually believe, and I can't work out what it does with (5).

So: is there a cleaner principle, or a short list of candidate principles, that gets most of these verdicts? Which of my verdicts does each candidate force me to give up? And what does the best one predict about cases I haven't settled — the version of (1) where I can't save the one but can give him a 20% chance instead of none; the version where the stakes aren't death but a scarce drug, and the five would each get six months while the one would get ten years; and the version where I'm the one? Please don't just tell me to multiply and add up expected lives, I've heard that bit and it's exactly what I'm trying to get behind or refute.
