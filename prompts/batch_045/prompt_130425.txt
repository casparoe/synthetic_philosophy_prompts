THE NOTES

1. The stone thing is a red herring (one page max)

Standard setup: can an all-powerful being make a stone so heavy it can't lift it. The usual answer is that this is like asking whether it can make a square circle — not a real task, so not being able to do it doesn't count against omnipotence. I think there's a better answer that I never see, which is that "can" is ambiguous between doing something and bringing it about that something is the case. It can't DO the task make a stone I am unable to lift, because the task has its own failure built in. But it can BRING ABOUT the state of affairs there exists a stone that this being cannot lift — by creating some lesser creator with the power to make such a stone, and letting that creator make one. So in one sense the answer is yes, and the paradox only bites because you slide between the two senses of can. The cost of saying this, which I want to own: omnipotence stops meaning can do anything and starts meaning can bring about any state of affairs, possibly through a chain of other agents. That's a weaker and weirder claim than people think they're signing up for.

2. The actual problem: omnipotence eats itself

(a) It can't be shared. If there were two all-powerful beings, each could try to stop the other. Either both succeed, which is a contradiction, or one fails, in which case that one was never all-powerful. So there can be at most one. Everybody grants that. But look at what follows: whether you count as omnipotent depends on what else exists. Omnipotence is supposed to be this intrinsic property, the top of some scale of greatness, and instead it turns out to be relational — it depends on there being no rivals. That's already a weird result and I think it's more interesting than the stone.

(b) This is the part I actually care about. An all-powerful being can presumably do at least these: create something it can't control, bind itself with a promise it can't break, hand its power over to someone, or stop existing. Call these the abdication moves. Every one of them ends its omnipotence. So a being that CAN do them has more power than a being that can't, since it has everything the second one has plus these. But a being that is omnipotent necessarily — which is what the religious version usually says, it couldn't stop being God — can't do any abdication move at all. So necessary omnipotence is strictly less powerful than contingent omnipotence. Insisting that God couldn't possibly fail to be all-powerful therefore makes him less powerful, not more. And you can't patch it by saying he's able to abdicate but always chooses not to, because that just is contingent omnipotence again.

3. Why there's no top

Section 2(b) generalizes. Take any collection of powers you want to call maximal, and I can describe a bigger one by adding the power to give up that collection. So how much power ordering has no maximum, in roughly the way there's no set of all sets: the thing you'd get by taking everything isn't itself in the collection. Which means omnipotent can't mean has the most power, because there is no most. The best available claim is unsurpassed — nothing else actually has more — rather than unsurpassable — nothing could have more. And unsurpassed is a much thinner property than people want: a being with three powers in a universe where everything else has two is unsurpassed too.

4. Where I think this leaves the original paradox

The stone question isn't interesting because of the stone. It's interesting because it's the first place you notice that maximal power might not name a coherent maximum at all, and the stone version hides that behind a puzzle about lifting. My sort-of conclusion is that if you want to keep the doctrine you have three options: take unsurpassed and accept that it's thin, take can bring about anything and accept that it's cheap and maybe hollow, or drop the doctrine. I don't have a favorite yet, which might be the real problem with the paper.
