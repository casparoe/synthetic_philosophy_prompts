A cluster of institutions has lately converged on the same design requirement. Automated systems that deny a benefit, rank an applicant, flag a tumour, remove a post, or select a target are increasingly not permitted to decide on their own: a person must be positioned so as to be able to review the machine's recommendation and override it, and in the stricter versions now written into professional guidance and procurement rules the person must record a reason whenever they depart from it. The requirement is justified, in one breath, by appeal to what machines supposedly lack and people supposedly have. Yet the same rules discipline the reviewer's discretion from the other side: they work from mandatory guidelines, under time limits, with their overrides logged, their disagreement rates audited, and their departures from the manual treated as deviations to be explained. In commercial content moderation, where review is done by contractors following a script against a stopwatch, the arrangement is at its most explicit. And the uncomfortable empirical companion to all of this is that reviewers mostly agree with the machine: override rates are tiny, and reviewers drift toward whatever the system puts in front of them.

I want the implicit theory of agency dug out of that practice, and I want it dug out in the vocabulary of sphexishness. The philosophical anecdote concerns a digger wasp that, by the standard telling, drags a paralysed cricket to its burrow, leaves it on the threshold, goes inside to check, emerges, and drags it in; an experimenter who moves the cricket a few inches while the wasp is inside can make it repeat the whole sequence, apparently without end. The empirical basis of the story has been questioned, but I am interested in the conceptual work it is made to do: it is supposed to show that apparently purposive behaviour can be rigid, and that the rigidity is revealed only under an intervention the creature's normal environment does not supply.

To keep the discussion from sliding, I have been using four distinctions of my own, and I would like them used, refined, or replaced: first, mere perturbation sensitivity, the causal responsiveness an organism shows when its circumstances are disturbed; second, resumability, the capacity to re-enter an interrupted routine partway through rather than restarting it at step one; third, policy revisability, the capacity to revise the routine itself in light of how things are going; and fourth, responsiveness to the point of an intervention, as opposed to responsiveness to the intervention as a mere event. The wasp, on the story, has the first but not the second, and neither of the last two.

So: what view of agency, of flexibility, and of freedom does the meaningful-human-review requirement actually presuppose? Which of my four properties does it strictly need, which does it merely gesture at, and where can they be pulled apart in practice — a reviewer who is sensitive and resumable but never revises anything, a machine that resumes and adapts its path while never revising its own criteria? Is the machine's alleged defect rigidity at all, or something else, such as having no one who can be held answerable? Does the practice presuppose that the human reviewer could have done otherwise, and if so does it matter that in the actual installations the counterfactual is almost never exercised? Is sphexishness a property of an agent, or only of an agent in an environment that contains experimenters — and if the latter, is the requirement really testing for a disposition, or allocating blame?

Then the standard diagnostics. Reconstruct the implicit theory in the form in which it is strongest, and say what it rules out: does it exclude the view that human judgment is another mechanism differing from the machine only in complexity, or the view that nothing but the reliability of outcomes matters? Separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits well with — an error-catching rationale, a dignitary rationale about what a citizen is owed, an accountability rationale, a rationale about meaningful work. Say where the practice is underdetermined and could be licensed by theories that disagree about free will, including hard determinist and compatibilist ones. And finally, tell me what would concretely have to change in the design of these systems if the presupposed view were replaced: if reviewers were assumed to be as mechanical as the systems they review, or if what mattered were not flexibility but the giving of an account, what would the review requirement have to become in order to earn its keep rather than certify a click?
