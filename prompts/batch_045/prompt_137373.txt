I work for an international aid organisation and was recently sent, along with everyone else in my unit, to a mandatory cultural-sensitivity training. The facilitator opened with a line he attributed to Montaigne — that we call barbarism whatever is not our own custom — and used it to argue that our discomfort with local practices is usually just parochialism. What struck me was how selectively the principle got applied. Nobody in the room thought it applied to our own safeguarding policies, which we treat as entirely non-negotiable; relativism was a tool for deflecting criticism from one direction only. That asymmetry has been nagging at me, and I started reading Of Cannibals (Book I, chapter 31 of the Essays) on my own and then writing about it. I am currently at a field posting with no library and effectively no internet, so I am Working from memory of the essay and cannot check anything for a few weeks. I have promised a small reading group of non-specialists about two thousand words for the autumn. Below is the draft-in-progress — closer to structured notes in places than to prose. I would be grateful for critical comments rather than encouragement. Please do not add citations or suggest reading; I will supply those myself later, and in any case you should work only from what you already know and tell me plainly where your memory is uncertain.

The draft (working title: Borrowed Standards — Montaigne's Cannibals and the Uses of Relativism):

1. Start from a small irony. The essay most often enlisted as a charter for cultural relativism opens with Pyrrhus inspecting the Roman army and remarking that, whatever barbarians may be, the disposition of the troops in front of him has nothing barbarous about it — and Montaigne draws from this that one should not take things on trust from vulgar opinion but judge by the eye of reason and not by common report. A text that begins by demanding that we judge by reason is an odd headquarters for relativism. My essay proposes to take that opening seriously and see how far it destabilises the standard reading.

2. Distinguish two things the standard reading runs together, which I have been calling costume relativism and reason relativism. Costume relativism: judgments about dress, food, beards, greetings, housing, poetry, marriage arrangements, whether one obeys a magistrate, whether property is held in common — all of these track local usage, and calling another people's version defective is mere parochialism. Reason relativism: the claim about whether the considerations that count in favour of or against a practice are themselves only local. My first claim is that almost all the descriptive material in the essay supports costume relativism only, while the polemic against Europe needs something much stronger, and that the move between them is never argued for.

3. The second claim is that relativism turns out to be idle in the argument's actual work. Montaigne does not say of the eating of captured enemies that it is permissible because it is their custom. He concedes the horror and then makes a comparative claim: that European practice in the religious wars — killing men slowly, mutilating and torturing the living — is worse. That comparison requires a ranking on which cruelty inflicted on the living is worse than killing, and the ranking is doing all the work and is not presented as local. I call this comparative vice-minimisation, and my suggestion is that the essay's engine is a shared moral ranking, not a thesis about the locality of reasons.

4. A smaller point I am fond of and slightly distrust: the testimony structure. Montaigne's main source is a man who lived ten or twelve years in the New World near where Villegagnon landed, and Montaigne says explicitly that the man was simple and coarse, which is the condition proper to truthful testimony, because clever observers notice more but gloss it, slanting the story to make their interpretation persuasive. Set that beside the separate scene of three men brought to Rouen while Charles IX was there, whose reported remarks about inequality and about who gets obeyed are made to carry the indictment of France. The tension I want to draw out is that the epistemic warrant for the whole portrait rests on a condescending, graded judgment about a low-status European's reliability, while the moral credit is transferred to the people described. Authority flows downwards within Europe and outwards from it. I am not sure whether that is a real inconsistency or just what any sixteenth-century ethnography must look like.

5. The main charge, which gives the essay its title. When the essay indicts Europe it must help itself to a standard — that gratuitous cruelty is bad, that decent societies do not tear each other apart over doctrine — and it does not relativise that standard at all. So what we actually have is a one-way ratchet: relativism for the excuse, universalism for the verdict. I call it asymmetric relativism, and I want to claim that this structure is not a defect peculiar to Montaigne but is the normal social function of relativising talk, which is why it showed up in my training session: the principle is never used to weaken one's own commitments, only to disarm other people's criticism.

6. And here is where I think the draft may be in trouble. A natural reply is that of-course-it-is-inconsistent is the wrong charge, because the essay is a trial or attempt rather than a doctrine: the genre licenses trying on a stance, and the appearance of relativism is an artifact of a form in which a position is tested rather than asserted. But if I take that line seriously it eats section 5, which presupposes that Montaigne is asserting something that can be inconsistent. I cannot have both, and I do not currently see which sacrifice is cheaper.

What I would like from you:

(a) Is the costume-relativism / reason-relativism distinction stable, or does it collapse under pressure — for instance because the conventions in question encode reasons, or because the ranking of cruelties in section 3 is itself partly a local sensibility? If this distinction already circulates under a different name, tell me that name without pointing me to a source; I would rather know I have reinvented a wheel than publish it as a discovery.

(b) Is the borrowed-standard charge in section 5 fair, or does it hold a sixteenth-century essay to a standard of doctrinal consistency it never claimed? Is there a version of it that survives that objection?

(c) Given (b), which of sections 5 and 6 should I cut, and what exactly do I lose by cutting it? I would rather be told that one of my two central ideas undermines the other than be helped to paper over it.

(d) Anything I have got wrong about the essay itself. I am working from memory: the Pyrrhus anecdote and its moral, the defence of the simple witness, the comparative claim about eating the dead versus torturing the living, the Rouen episode, the reported remarks about inequality. Flag where you are guessing as well as where I may be.

(e) Whether this is worth two thousand words for intelligent non-specialists at all, or whether it reduces to a seminar exercise once the jargon is stripped out. My own suspicion is that section 2 drags. If you think the honest answer is that the whole thing is a lot of scaffolding around the observation in section 5, say so, and if there is a more interesting claim available on the same material — a reading on which Montaigne escapes both of my charges, or on which the real subject is self-knowledge and the difficulty of seeing one's own vices rather than relativism at all — tell me what it is and what would have to be cut to make room for it.
