I am a graduate TA for an undergraduate decision-theory course, and I have been asked to run next week's discussion section on ambiguity using a chapter from a popular book on irrationality that the lecturer is fond of. Before I stand up in front of the students I want to know which sentences of it I would be embarrassed to repeat. The chapter's core passage reads as follows: The Ellsberg setup is simple. An urn holds 90 balls: 30 are red, and the remaining 60 are black and yellow in a proportion you are not told. Offer people a straight choice between a bet that pays $100 on red and one that pays $100 on black, and they take the red. Then offer them a choice between a bet that pays $100 on red-or-yellow and one that pays $100 on black-or-yellow, and they take black-or-yellow. Now notice that no assignment of probabilities to the possible compositions fits both of those choices: the first requires you to treat black as unlikely, the second requires you to treat it as likely. So people are not maximizing expected utility. The reason is that they are ambiguity averse: they dislike acting on probabilities they do not know, and they behave as if the composition of the urn were the worst case consistent with what they have been told. That is irrational in a strict sense, and not merely a quirk of taste — it makes your preferences turn on how the options are carved up rather than on anything about the world, and a bookie who knows the composition can charge you a small fee to shuffle your bets and leave you poorer whatever happens. Nor is any of this risk aversion: the prize is the same in every bet, so someone's distaste for variability applies equally to both bets in each pair and cannot produce the reversal. And the moral travels well beyond urns — whenever one option's probabilities are known and another's are not, the known one is the rational choice, and no adjustment in the size of the known risk changes that.

I have worked through enough of the algebra not to need it re-derived: if K is the number of black balls, preferring red to black forces the expected value of K below 30, while preferring black-or-yellow to red-or-yellow forces it above 30, so no single prior over K is consistent with both. What I cannot tell is how much of the surrounding commentary a decision theorist would stand behind. Three things make me suspicious. First, the sentence about behaving as if the composition were the worst case: I had the impression that there is a whole family of formal models of ambiguity-sensitive choice and that the maximally pessimistic one is merely the best-known member of it, so that this gloss overstates what the data support. Second, the money-pump claim: I have also half-heard the opposite argument, namely that it is the person who ignores ambiguity who is exposed, because a counterparty who knows the composition can always offer them bets priced on information they lack — so I do not know which way that consideration actually cuts, or whether a genuine sure-loss argument against ambiguity aversion exists at all as opposed to an argument that it violates a particular axiom. Third, the closing sentence appears to sit badly with the earlier one: if ambiguity aversion is irrational in a strict sense, the author cannot also recommend it, and anyway the claim that the magnitude of the known risk never matters looks like it must be false in any model that lets stakes matter. A related worry: our lecturer's slides use the two-urn version instead, with a known 50/50 urn beside an unknown one, and the striking result there is that the same people prefer the known urn when betting on red and also when betting on black. My guess is that this version demonstrates something slightly different from the one-urn version — a failure of additivity rather than a failure of the sure-thing principle — but I am not confident, and I do not know whether the book's worst-case gloss explains that pattern better or worse than it explains the one-urn pattern. Finally, my study group tried a variant on each other: we replaced the ambiguous urn with a two-stage device, drawing one ticket from a bag of 61 tickets numbered 0 to 60 to fix K, with everything about the bag公开 known, so that the process generating the composition carries no ambiguity and the only remaining ignorance is about which ticket came up. Two people in the group said the book's author would predict exactly the same preferences here, on the grounds that what people react to is not knowing the actual proportion. I suspect that depends on whether the leading models collapse the two-stage gamble into a single-stage one or treat the stages separately, but I cannot tell which models do which, nor whether the matter is settled theoretically or is really an empirical question about whether people reduce compound gambles.

So please audit the passage claim by claim: mark what a decision theorist would accept as written, what is a simplification that would pass in a popular book but would not survive a seminar, and what is straightforwardly wrong or backwards. Tell me explicitly whether the closing lesson survives after the errors are corrected, or whether the defensible version of it is narrower than the author suggests, and whether the author's own claims are in tension with one another. Then take my three side-questions — the relation between the one-urn and two-urn presentations, the status of the money-pump or sure-loss argument, and what should be expected in the ticket-bag variant — and for each separate what is implied by the theory from what remains an open empirical question. Where a claim is genuinely contested rather than settled, say so plainly: for the purposes of a discussion section I would much rather be able to tell students that experts disagree about a point than assert something false with confidence.
