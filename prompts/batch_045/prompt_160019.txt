A blog post I was reading last night (one of those "probability for the perplexed" things) summed up Lewis's triviality result like this:

"The Ramsey test says your confidence in 'if A then C' should just be your confidence in C on the supposition that A. Lewis showed this can't be right. If there were a conditional proposition whose probability always equalled the conditional probability P(C given A), your credence function would have to be trivial: every proposition you consider gets probability 0 or 1, so you're never genuinely uncertain about anything. Since that's absurd, Lewis concluded that indicitive conditionals have no truth values at all — there's nothing there to be true or false, only degrees of assertability — and that's why there's no real logic of conditionals: you can't even infer C from A and 'if A then C', and you can't put a conditional inside a negation or a disjunction either. The result is called 'trivial' because the equation is trivially false once you think about it for a second."

I've also heard it said that the Ramsey/Adams thesis is alive and well, and that Lewis's own conclusion was more complicated than "conditionals aren't truth-apt", so I suspect at least one step here is off — I just can't tell which. Could you go through it claim by claim: what's right, what's a simplification, what's just wrong, and what some one who defends the suppositional reading and some one who wants truth conditions would each say about the bits they reject? Do the conclusions about truth values and about the logic of conditionals actually follow from Lewis's theorem, or do they need extra premises I'm not seeing? And is "trivial" here just a fancy way of saying the result is unimportant?
