Hi — I'm a PhD student in philosophy (mind and cognition, working mostly on temporal experience) and I need to turn a half-baked idea into a two-page proposal for a three-month summer fellowship. The output would be one standalone paper, call it 8000 words, spun out of a chapter of my thesis. British English throughout please, and don't bother with citations or a bibliography — I'll slot the references in myself, I want the argument and the framing, not the reading list.

The idea. Take the classic colour-phi setup: two differently coloured flashes at slightly different positions, short gap between them, and subjects report a single thing moving across that changes colour partway along — apparently changing colour before the second flash has even been presented. Everyone who writes about the Orwellian versus Stalinesque options here seems to me to be sharing one assumption: that a conscious experience is a kind of finished item that gets edited, and the only live question is whether the edit happens before or after the item reaches consciousness. Stalinesque: the record is doctored first, then shown. Orwellian: it's shown, then the record is rewritten. The standard move is that the question is empty, since nothing the subject can say or do separates the two; the standard counter-move is that it's a genuine question we just have no handle on. I want to argue that both are wrong in the same way — the dilemma presupposes that experiences are punctate enough to have a single time of occurrence, when the interesting thing about phi is that the experience is extended and that its content (where along the trajectory the colour shifts) seems to get fixed only as the process plays out. So my question isn't before-or-after but this: can the content of an extended conscious experience be determinate only after that experience is over — retrospective determination — or is it rather that the experience is genuinely indeterminate in its temporal content while it lasts, call it prospective vagueness? And the bit I actually care about: is there any difference between those two that isn't just the old verificationist dispute turning up in a new costume?

What I want from the proposal: state the question so it has a determinate answer (I keep geting told my question is three questions in a trenchcoat, so split it and say which one I'm answering and why that one); explain why the standard positions and the standard replies leave it open; give the method — what I'd actually do across three months, why this is a philosophical project rather than a job for the psych department, and why the obvious alternatives won't settle it, whether that's going and measuring the neural timing or doing a pure conceptual analysis of when an experience occurs in the abstract; then set out the three or four possible conclusions and what considerations would decide between them. I also want a genuinely honest failure condition, not a token one. My two worries are that retrospective determination collapses into the already-familiar no-fact-of-the-matter view, in which case I've written an elaborate footnote, or that it collapses into a bare empirical claim about the time-course of visual processing that I'm not in a position to establish. Tell me which is the real risk, and what I'd need to have in hand by the end of month two to rule it out.

One more half-formed bit, use it or bin it: I keep coming back to a way of getting leverage that doesn't go through the subject's report at all — find a response channel that is continuous, fine-grained in time, and leaves a trace outside the head, so that the record was rewritten definately doesn't come cheap. Oculomotor tracking of the apparent motion is my candidate, because where the eyes are changes what hits the retina next, so the revision couldn't stay intracranial. If that's a dead end, or if it just invites the reply that the eye movement is another draft like any other, say so in the proposal and give me either the better version of it or the fallback.
