Teaching a 300-level seminar on luck, blame, and liability this spring, and I want to close the unit with a film instead of another paper. I'm thinking No Country for Old Men, which is a big swing but the coin stuff is almost too on the nose, and that might be useful rather than fatal. Scenes I have in mind: the gas station early on, where Chigurh makes the proprietor call a coin toss for his life and then lets him walk when he calls it right; Carla Jean at the end, refusing to call and telling him the coin had nothing to do with it, it was him; and then, right after, Chigurh leaving her house and getting hit by a car that had nothing to do with anyone's choices.

What I want is a reading of it through the moral luck literature, not a paraphrase of the literature. I've been working with Nagel's four-way split (constitutive, circumstantial, causal, resultant) and the usual Gauguin case about retrospective justification, and the problem I keep hitting while prepping is that the taxonomy assumes luck is something that befalls an agent, whereas Chigurh installs it: he puts a chance device between his will and the outcome and then honors what it says. So:

1. Is luck you deliberately manufacture for yourself and then submit to a fifth kind, a degenerate case of resultant luck, or not luck at all in the sense the debate cares about? I don't mean the boring point that you're responsible for setting up the lottery. I mean whether the structure is doing something the standard categories can't describe — is he trying to reduce his exposure to luck, or to increase it?

2. Does the coin make him less blameworthy or more? Half my students say it obviously makes it worse, since he'd have killed her anyway and the coin is theater; the other half think the opposite is at least arguable, and I cant decide whether they've got something.

3. The victim's participation. When the gas station man calls the toss and wins, is that relevantly like assuming a risk? Is coerced participation in your own lottery consent, and if it isn't, does that shift the blame owed to Chigurh, or only the wrongness of the act, or neither?

4. The crash. The film looks like it's doing cosmic bookkeeping — the man who ran everything through a coin gets taken out by pure chance — but Carla Jean's refusal reads like the opposite claim: there's no chance here, only agency. Are those two moves in tension, or the same claim from different sides? And should we hear Bell's fatalism as a view about luck, or as a failure of nerve that the moral luck lens would diagnose rather than endorse?

Where I expect the reading to push back: Chigurh may be a figure of fate rather than a person with a decision procedure, the coin may be a prop rather than an argument, and Moss dies offscreen so the film declines the whole revenge economy. I want the honest version of that too — where the lens distorts the film and where the film distorts the lens.

Deliverable: two or three moments to pause on, the claim each one is making in luck terms, and a line on what the film adds that the cases in the papers don't. If the answer is nothing, that it's just an illustration, say that and I'll swap it out. One last thing I keep circling and can't get into focus: is there a reading on which the coin is about the agent's relation to his own will rather than about the fairness of assessment?
