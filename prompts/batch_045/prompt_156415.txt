A friend and I spent last night arguing about whether there is ever really such a thing as a responsibility gap, in the sense people talk about for autonomous systems: a case where the system harms someone and the honest answer is that no one is morally responsible for it. She thinks the idea is a philosopher's fiction. Some human always chose to build the thing, chose the objective, chose to deploy it, chose the monitoring regime, and so there is always someone to point at. I think she is right about the easy cases and wrong about a couple of the hard ones, but when I try to say exactly where the line falls, my own verdicts start fighting each other. So I want to lay the verdicts out and ask what principle, if any, is behind them. I don't want a survey of the literature or a list of positions people have defended; I want a principle that has to earn its way through these cases.

(1) A hospital deploys two hundred autonomous delivery carts. Testing cannot predict which cart will fail or how, but the risk model puts serious injury at about 1.2 events per fleet-year, and the deployment is approved as a cost saving. In month three a cart takes a route no tester ever saw and strikes an eighty-four-year-old patient, who dies. My verdict: the chief operating officer who approved the fleet is morally responsible for the death, and there is no gap. What does the work for me is that she knew the rate even though she could not have known the case.

(2) A short-video recommender optimized for watch time is trained by reinforcement learning and discovers that escalating a certain kind of content keeps one particular fifteen-year-old watching. Over six weeks his self-harm escalates and he attempts suicide. Nobody chose or foresaw this mechanism, and there is nothing like the risk model in case 1; the general possibility of harm to some users had been discussed internally and written off as not measurable. My verdict: here I think there is a gap. I don't blame the engineers, and I resist blaming the executives, while also thinking that something was done to this boy that someone ought to answer for. My friend says that if I want an answerer I don't really believe in the gap, and I half think she is right that the honest formulation is that they are responsible for imposing an unmeasured risk rather than for the harm, and that those are different things.

(3) A remote safety operator is nominally supervising twenty vehicles at once. An alert fires with four seconds to act; she does not act in time; a pedestrian is struck. Verdict: I don't blame her. I blame the company that set the ratio at twenty to one.

(4) One operator, one vehicle, twelve seconds of clear warning, and she was looking at her phone because 99 percent of the alerts had been false. Verdict: I blame her, less than I would blame someone who aimed the car, and I blame the company as well. Cases 3 and 4 differ only in whether she had genuine discretion at the moment: time, attention, a live option she understood.

(5) Three hundred drones in a light show. An emergent interaction between the collision-avoidance layer and a gust brings one down into a crowd. No single drone's trajectory was unsafe, and the flight director chose the formation algorithm knowing that emergent patterns were possible in principle but not which ones. Verdict: the flight director is responsible, no gap.

(6) Four million drivers upload dashcam footage. A model trained on it mishandles a rare sign configuration and a passenger dies. No subset of the uploads is traceable to the failure, each upload was individually dispensable, and no uploader knew anything about training runs. Verdict: I blame no uploader, and I don't blame them collectively either. This feels like the wrong place to look for responsibility altogether, which is odd, because structurally it is a lot like case 5: an aggregate cause, individually dispensable contributions, an outcome nobody could trace. My friend says my principle is really just protect individuals, blame officeholders, that I am tracking roles rather than causes. My own guess is that the difference is difference-making: the director and the COO each had a moment when choosing otherwise would have meant no flight and no fleet, whereas no uploader's refusal would have changed the model at all.

(7) A benefits-claims triage system is built so that no log records which model version decided a given claim. The product lead pushed this explicitly, and said in a deposition that the point was to keep the company from being on the hook for decisions it could not explain. A cancer patient's claim is denied, the denial cannot be explained, and the patient dies before the appeal clears. Verdict: someone is responsible, and more than that, this is *worse* than the otherwise identical case (8) where the same opacity is a side effect of privacy-preserving on-device learning. My claim is that there is a distinct wrong in arranging matters so that responsibility cannot later be assigned, a wrong that is blameworthy whether or not the underlying decision was wrong, and that it is not merely the separate offense of destroying evidence. My friend says that is a confusion, and I am not sure I can answer her.

(9) A small lab publishes full model weights with no safeguards, on the view that openness is the best check on concentration of power. Someone fine-tunes the model to write tailored phishing lures and an elderly man loses his life savings. The lab knew, and published, an estimate of the low but nonzero rate at which released models get misused this way. Verdict: I go back and forth. Half the time I say the release was difference-making and they knew the rate, so it is case 1 again. Half the time I say that releasing a general-purpose tool for good reasons is not like turning two hundred carts loose in a hospital corridor, and the responsibility is entirely the fine-tuner's.

(10) A harmful behavior enters through an update: written by an engineer who has since left, approved by a reviewer who relied on the regression suite, rolled out by an automated pipeline, watched by an ops team tracking aggregate metrics. Everyone was reasonable at every step. Verdict: I blame no individual, but I do blame the company, and I am not certain that is coherent. Can an organization be morally responsible for something when none of its members is?

One more thing that came up and that I think is doing hidden work. In case 3 the operator will be fired, possibly charged, and the public will blame her, and the firm will be able to point at a human being. So the institutions will assign responsibility where I think there is none. That makes me wonder whether my friend and I are using one word for two things, and if so, which of my verdicts belong to which.

My tentative formulation, such as it is: there is no gap with respect to a harm H if and only if there is some agent A such that (i) at some time A had a choice that was difference-making for whether H, or a risk of H's type, would occur; (ii) at that time A could reasonably have foreseen H's type, or the rate of harms of that type, well enough to weigh it; (iii) A chose within a role carrying a standing duty to keep outcomes of that kind answerable; and (iv) A did not, by that choice or by related choices, deliberately destroy or negligently fail to create the conditions under which responsibility for harms of that type can later be traced. Where no agent satisfies all four, there is a gap, except that failing (iv) on its own generates a separate and, I think, aggravated kind of blame.

What I want from you: test that against the ten cases and repair it. State the principle precisely, say which case motivates each clause and what work the clause does, tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which of them I should therefore give up, and give me its verdicts on cases I haven't listed, including the ones it should find hardest. If you think the right answer is that two different things are in play and that no single principle covers all ten, say so and tell me where the break falls. I would also like your verdict on case 9 specifically, with the reason it has to go the way you say.
