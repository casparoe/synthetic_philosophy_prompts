I recently read a long-form piece in a popular science magazine on the question of whether the wavefunction is real, built around the 2012 result of Pusey, Barrett, and Rudolph. It made the debate sound more settled than I remember it being, and I would like to know how much of it I should actually trust. The central paragraph runs like this:

"In the 1920s it was still possible to hope that the wavefunction was only a bookkeeping device — a summary of what an experimenter knows about a system, the way a probability distribution summarizes what a gambler knows about a coin. In 2012 three physicists closed that question for good. Building on Bell's theorem, which had already shown that no locally causal hidden-variable theory can reproduce the quantum predictions, they proved that no hidden-variable theory of any kind can either: any model in which a quantum state represents mere information about an underlying physical state of the system, and in which systems prepared independently have independent physical states, must make predictions that contradict those of quantum theory. The wavefunction is therefore not a state of knowledge but a state of reality — a physical field, albeit one defined not on ordinary three-dimensional space but on a configuration space whose dimension grows with the number of particles. A system's physical state is nothing over and above its wavefunction. The Copenhagen interpretation is finished, and with it recent attempts such as QBism that treat quantum states as degrees of belief. What survives of the measurement problem is a well-defined dynamical question: by what physical mechanism does a real wavefunction collapse?"

It closes with the aside that the result has since been confirmed in the laboratory.

Please audit this sentence by sentence: what a philosopher of physics working on foundations would accept outright, what they would wince at as a simplification, and what is straightforwardly wrong or doesn't follow. I am especially unsure about three things, which I would like treated separately from the line-by-line pass.

First: my seminar handout distinguishes three claims I can't keep straight — state-supervenience (the quantum state is fixed by, i.e. a function of, the complete physical state of the system); state-constitution (the quantum state is one real component of that physical state, alongside any hidden variables); and state-exclusivity (the physical state is nothing but the quantum state). Which, if any, of these does the result actually establish? Is the three-way distinction coherent as stated? And does what the argument delivers amount to full supervenience, or only to something weaker, given that the conclusion is about the overlap of probability distributions over physical states? I also want to know whether "real" in the magazine's sense ends up meaning anything more than "distinct quantum states must correspond to distinct physical states."

Second: the independence assumption. The article states it in passing but never says what happens if you drop it, and it implies that experimental confirmation settles the matter. Is the assumption doing more work than the article admits? Could an experiment in principle corroborate a result whose extra premise concerns the physical states of independently prepared systems?

Third: which live research programs survive the corrected version of the claim? I am thinking of Bohmian mechanics, Everettian quantum mechanics, QBism, and any view on which the wavefunction is nomological or the theory is treated instrumentally. For each, say whether it is touched by the result, and what its defenders would object to in the article's phrasing. One more technical point: does the argument essentially require several independently prepared systems, or does an analogous no-go hold for a single system?

I don't want citations, bibliographies, or reading suggestions — just the conceptual audit, in American English. Where the article is wrong, tell me what the corrected sentence would be; where it is right, tell me what follows and what doesn't.
