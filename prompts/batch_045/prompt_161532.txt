My friend and I got into a three-hour argument last night about whether what you know can change what you see, and I ended up making a bet I now have to make good on: I told her I could get a room of undergrads to feel, in one session, exactly why the obvious classroom demos prove nothing, and why the skeptical position is a lot harder to shake than it sounds at 11pm. Session is Thursday and I have nothing planned.

The setup: upper-level philosophy of mind seminar, 16 students, 90 minutes, one projector, everyone has a phone or a laptop, no lab gear, no money (participation points are my only currency, maybe a bag of candy). They've done some reading already and they know the phrase cognitive penetration, so there's no point pretending otherwise — I just don't want to stand at the front and explain it.

What I want them walking out having seen, not having been told:

1. That a change in what people say they saw is close to worthless as evidence that what they saw changed. I want them to catch themselves doing this, not to hear me say it.
2. That the real question is what a measure would have to look like to separate the two — that it's a design problem, not a gut-feel problem.
3. That the skeptical view is a demand about evidence, not the claim that experience is sealed off from everything you believe. My friend kept hearing it as the second thing and I couldn't get her off it.

My one idea, which is probably half-baked: I have a dim memory of signal detection theory from an undergrad methods class — sensitivity versus criterion, d-prime and all that — and I think the lever is that a manipulation can move how people answer without touching how well they discriminate. Use it or throw it out, but if you use it I need it turned into something 16 humanities students feel in their bones rather than a stats lecture. I also had a vague thought about letting them bet on their own answers, or pass, to make the guessing visible, but I can't get the mechanics to work and I don't know whether a pass option actually helps or just adds noise.

A couple of things I care about structurally:
- Give them a real demo first, something that genuinely works and makes the room go "obviously yes" — I want them seduced before they're skeptical. Tell me specifically what stimulus will survive a projector in a room with the lights half up, and what I do if the effect doesn't land.
- Then something that makes them catch the confound themselves using their own behavior, ideally with zero change to the stimuli. I like the idea of moving their reports just by messing with the incentives.
- Then they should try to build, in small groups, the manipulation that would definately settle it — and I want their attempts to fail in the specific way that's instructive, so I need to know what to put on the board and what the failure modes of their proposals will be.
- I'd rather the last stretch be them doing something real and live than me describing an experiment, even if the data is messy.
