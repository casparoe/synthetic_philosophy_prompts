Hey — I've been messing around with a toy model of a bounded reasoner (inspired by some stuff I've been reading about logical uncertainty and logical induction) and I ended up with an argument whose conclusion I definitely don't believe. I want to know where to get off.

Setup: the reasoner keeps a credence function at each time step t. Call a proposition "settled" if there's some computation the reasoner could in principle run that would output its truth value — whether program P halts within N steps, whether the 10^9th digit of pi is 7, that kind of thing. Settled questions are exactly the ones where the reasoner's uncertainty is purely logical: there's no evidence to wait for, only thinking to do. I'm also assuming the reasoner faces an unending supply of them — it never runs out of settled questions to answer.

(1) Being uncertain about settled questions is fine and unavoidable. A bounded reasoner can't be certain of everything decidable, and that's the whole phenomenon we're trying to model rather than explain away.

(2) Even so, its uncertainty is criticizable: if there's a computable trading strategy that extracts unbounded value by betting against the reasoner's credences, the reasoner is doing something wrong. Call this no-computable-exploitability. It's the only grip I have on what "better" and "worse" even mean for a bounded reasoner, so it's the premise I most want to keep.

(3) No-computable-exploitability forces convergence on each settled question individually. Fix a settled p: if the reasoner's credence in p were off by more than epsilon infinitely often, a trader that simply bets against it on p on exactly those occasions would make unbounded money. So for each settled p, the credence eventually gets within epsilon of the truth and stays there.

(4) If the reasoner converges on each settled question, then its credence function converges on the truth: as t grows it's right about more and more of them, and in the limit it's right about all of them at once.

(5) A reasoner whose credences are right about every settled question has no logical uncertainty left worth the name. Asked what a given computation will output, it already knows the answer, so there isn't any computation that could move it.

(6) But a reasoner that can't be moved by a computation has no reason to run one. Running a computation is spending resources in order to learn an outcome; if you're already certain of the outcome the information gain is zero and the expected value is negative. So the converged reasoner stops thinking. It also can't deliberate about which conjecture to test next, since for it everything is already settled.

(7) So the standard in (2) and (3) is self-defeating: the only reasoner that satisfies it is one that has stopped reasoning, and reasoning is exactly what was supposed to produce the convergence in the first place. Which makes the whole framework look pointless.

I keep going back and forth on this. My first guess was that the rot is in (3) — that exploitability doesnt really force per-question convergence — but I've more or less talked myself out of that, and (6) is the one I least want to give up: the thought that you only compute because you're unsure of the output seems obviously right to me. That leaves (4) and (5), and I can't tell which one is doing the damage.

Which step do you reject? Why does it look fine? And what does rejecting it cost — if I drop (4), do I lose any grip on what makes one bounded reasoner better than another at a given time, or is there a weaker claim there that still does the work I wanted? Does it help anything to say the reasoner only ever faces finitely many questions, or does that make the conclusion worse rather than better? I'd rather get a diagnosis than a reading list.
