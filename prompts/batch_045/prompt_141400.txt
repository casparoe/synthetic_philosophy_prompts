I organize a fortnightly ninety-minute session at work called Craft Hour — 14 to 18 software engineers, mixed levels, one or two of them openly hostile to anything that smells like a soft-skills workshop, several not writing in their first language, and nobody with any philosophy training (including me: I have a CS degree and got maybe sixty pages into the Hofstadter-and-Sander book on analogy as the core of cognition before a release ate my evenings). The claim that lodged in my head is not the tame one — that analogy is a nice creative tool — but the strong one: that ordinary, boring categorization is itself analogy to a remembered case; that seeing a thing as a chair, or deciding that this new internal service is the same kind of thing as the one we deprecated, works by retrieving a prior instance and sliding it onto the new one; that the rule we think we are applying is rebuilt each time from whatever example got retrieved; and that concepts quietly slip from one frame to another without us noticing. I mentioned this to a friend who does appellate law and she said it sounded like her whole job, which made me think there is something real here. I want the room to feel that claim in their own behavior rather than hear it from me, and I want to avoid saying words like slippage, framing, or essence until after they have already done the thing those words name.

The mechanism I have been toying with — and I would like you either to use it or to beat it, and to tell me plainly why you chose what you chose — is this: split the room in half, give both halves the identical written rule for a made-up category, give each half a different set of three or four worked examples that the rule is supposedly illustrated by, then have both halves classify the same list of eight or nine deliberately engineered probe items, several of which sit exactly where the rule text and the examples part company. My hope is that the two halves draw the boundary in different places while being equally convinced they merely applied the rule. If you build on that, I want the probe items engineered so that at least two are genuinely underdetermined by the rule text alone, at least one is a trap where a vivid example pulls against the rule, and at least one is a late item that reframes the whole category so earlier verdicts look wrong in retrospect. I also want one round built on a single example: show them exactly one instance of an invented kind (a glimwacket, say), ask them what else counts and make them stake something on it, then reveal a second instance that breaks the essence they just invented, and only then name what happened. And if it fits, I would like a short ladder of letter-string analogies where the answer that jumps out and the answer that is right under a different framing come apart, because that is where I first felt the book's point myself. Constraints: ninety minutes including the debrief, no real money (points or chips are fine, and I would like a scoring rule that rewards calibrated confidence rather than volume), no phones-in-a-basket drama (assume people will look things up), instructions simple enough to be read aloud once, a structure that does not collapse if the most senior person in the room anchors everyone in the first ninety seconds, and nothing that requires me to explain a philosophical theory before the activity has done its work.

Please answer as someone who has actually run sessions like this — part cognitive-science-literate facilitator, part working engineer — and commit to your choices rather than offering me a menu. Before the final version, spend up to four hundred words brainstorming at least three candidate mechanisms (mine included or replaced), pick one, and say in a single sentence what you are giving up by picking it. Then give me the full design as one valid JSON object, no comments and no trailing commas, with exactly these top-level fields: design_brief (working_title, thesis_participants_should_feel, mechanism_chosen, alternatives_rejected_and_why, what_a_hostile_participant_would_say_this_proves_instead); setup (duration_minutes, group_size_range, room_layout, materials_list, norms_announced_before_anyone_starts, what_facilitator_must_memorize); timeline (array of phases, each with start_min, end_min, phase_name, what_participants_do, what_facilitator_does, planted_surprise, contamination_risk); materials (rule_text_verbatim, training_examples_group_A, training_examples_group_B, probe_items as an array of objects each with item, rule_verdict, predicted_group_A_verdict, predicted_group_B_verdict, why_this_item_is_here, one_shot_first_instance, one_shot_second_instance, analogy_ladder_items); scoring (currency, payout_rule, what_it_rewards, what_it_punishes); debrief_map (array where each object maps a moment in the activity to an idea, with minute, observed_moment, concept_it_maps_to, facilitator_line, jargon_introduced_here_or_none); failure_modes (array of objects with failure, likelihood, early_warning_sign, in_the_moment_fix, prevention_next_time); what_the_activity_cannot_reproduce (array of objects with feature_of_the_thesis, why_the_activity_cannot_show_it, how_i_supply_it_in_two_minutes_of_talking); transfer_to_engineering (array of habits, each with the_habit, when_to_use_it, what_it_replaces, failure_sign); and committed_calls (array of objects with decision_point, the_call, reason).
