Two friends of mine got into it at a barbeque on Saturday and I've been chewing on it since, because I really couldnt tell who was winning. It started when Deniz said that open borders and a generous welfare state are basically incompatible -- you can have one or the other, so anyone whose priority is protecting the welfare state ought to be nervous about open borders. Rosa said that was wrong. Here's roughly how it went.

Deniz: the fiscal stuff is a distraction either way, because the real mechanism is political. Generous redistribution runs on a felt sense of shared membership and reciprocity -- I pay in now, I'm covered later, and the people I'm covered alongside are people I recognise as part of the same project. Big, fast inflows of people with no prior stake in that project weaken the feeling, and weakened feeling means a smaller, meaner welfare state at the next election. So the trade-off holds even if immigrants turn out to be net contributors -- the cost isn't on the budget line, it's in the coalition that keeps the budget generous.

Rosa: then your claim isn't conceptual, it's contingent, and the thing doing the work isn't diversity or newcomers as such. It's whether newcomers get folded into the same unions, parties, schools and workplaces, and get treated as insiders by the institutions that organise people politically. Where that happens, support for redistribution doesn't collapse. So it's not "open borders versus the welfare state," it's "open borders with no serious incorporation policy versus the welfare state," which is a much narrower and much less interesting claim.

Deniz: that doesn't rescue it, because of timing. Incorporation is a generational project. The backlash shows up at the next election. So even granting you the happy steady state, the transition reliably eats the thing you wanted to protect. Reforms have to happen in some order, and the only order available in the real world is the bad one -- you open first, and then you try to build the solidarities that would have made opening safe. That's a sequencing problem, not an argument you can answer by describing a better world.

Rosa: okay, but notice you've quietly downgraded the thesis. It's no longer "these two things can't coexist," it's "opening quickly is risky for the welfare state," which is a speed claim, and as a speed claim it proves far too much. Any rapid disruption of the solidarities a place runs on would be objectionable on the same reasoning -- shutting a company town overnight, say -- so your principle isn't about immigration at all. And if the problem really is the order, then fix the order: broaden and universalise provision first, then open, so that newcomers are inside the institutions from day one rather than outside them for a generation.

Deniz: you can't, because generosity isn't a setting you dial in and walk away from. The legitimacy of the institution depends on exactly the sentiment you're betting against: people back transfers to people they can see as contributors and fellow members, and if that erodes, the institution hollows out from the inside no matter how it was engineered. And while we're at it, the fiscal side isn't as settled as you implied -- open borders don't select for high earners, and on the usual way of scoring it, low-wage arrivals and their children cost the exchequer more than they pay in.

Rosa: on the money, that scoring is the problem. It only counts the receiving country's books. Somebody who arrives at twenty-five was fed, housed and schooled somewhere else, so the destination books two or three productive decades without ever booking the cost of producing a worker. Score it globally and a lot of the "drain" is just an accounting artifact.

Deniz: possibly -- but that's my argument wearing a hat. If the benefit only appears on global books, then the case for open borders is a global-welfare case, and a global-welfare case has no particular reason to care about preserving a national welfare state. You're invoking the welfare state as your motive and then defending a policy whose returns are denominated in a currency the welfare state doesn't spend.

Rosa: I don't think reciprocity is the fixed constraint you're treating it as. Welfare states have always covered people who don't contribute -- children, disabled people, the long-term unemployed, and plenty of others -- and the line has moved repeatedly without anyone concluding that the institution had lost its legitimacy. So "people only support transfers to contributors" is really a description of whatever the current coalition happens to tolerate. Also, the places with the largest welfare states already admit a lot of people who've never paid in, refugees and family members among them, and nobody thinks those systems fell over because of it.

Deniz: we can sharpen this. Suppose we treat the welfare state like a pension or a club with vesting -- entitlements accrue with contribution. Open borders plus full immediate access is what breaks it. Open borders plus vesting isn't open borders, it's a large guest-worker programme with a path, which neither of us is arguing for. So maybe the real question is whether "open borders" names one package at all, and my claim is just that the version worth wanting is the incompatible one.
