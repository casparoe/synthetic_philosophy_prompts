I've been reading rationalist-adjacent blogs and forums for the better part of fifteen years, so I've absorbed the usual LessWrong-flavored talking points about coordination problems, Schelling focal points, and David Lewis's account of convention — roughly, that a convention is a regularity in behavior which solves a recurring coordination problem, is arbitrary among alternative solutions to that problem, and is held in place by mutual expectations plus common knowledge. After a local meetup last week I got into an argument with two friends about whether that account can say anything about norms we take to be unjust: one of them does evolutionary game theory and sketched what he called the basin-of-attraction model, on which any regularity that enough people expect acquires a self-fulfilling pull (conforming is the best response to others' conformity), with the size of a regularity's "basin" just being the number of people who currently expect it; call the set of regularities that are self-enforcing in this sense S. The other friend works on norms from a more critical direction and kept insisting that the stability of an unjust norm is part of what makes it bad rather than a reason to stop complaining about it. Between the three of us we assembled the following argument, and I find the conclusion unacceptable but can no longer see which joint to cut. (The Lewis clause below is my own paraphrase from memory, not a quotation; if the argument turns on our having stated Lewis wrongly, say so explicitly.)

(1) A regularity R in a population is a convention iff: (a) nearly everyone conforms to R; (b) nearly everyone expects nearly everyone else to conform; (c) given that the others conform, each prefers to conform — being the lone deviant is worse; (d) R is not the only possible solution, i.e., there is an alternative regularity R' which would have solved the same coordination problem had it been the one everyone expected and coordinated on; and (e) (a)–(d) are common knowledge.
(2) If R is a convention, then what explains R's being in force rather than R' is expectation and precedent, not merit; and since R' would have solved the coordination problem equally well, there is no respect in which R is better *as a solution to the problem it solves*. So the only non-empty criticism of a convention is: it fails to coordinate, or a better-coordinating alternative is in fact available and reachable. A criticism of the form "this convention is unfair" is, read literally, either a prediction that the equilibrium will break, or it is empty.
(3) On the basin model, every member of S is by construction a solution to some coordination problem, and every member is arbitrary in the sense of (1d): the mirror-image regularity would have coordinated the same way had it been expected.
(4) The social norms we actually want to criticize — norms of deference, of who gets to speak, of who gets first authorship — are all in S: stable, expected, and self-enforcing in exactly the way (3) describes.
(5) Test case: in a certain professional community, in any meeting the most senior person present speaks first and at whatever length they choose, no one interrupts them, and in any collaboration the most senior person takes first authorship. Call this the Seniority Norm. It is in S: everyone conforms, everyone expects everyone else to, solo deviation is costly, and the mirror-image regularity (the most junior person speaks first and takes first authorship) would coordinate turn-taking and credit-assignment just as well.
(6) It's no reply that the Seniority Norm is backed by sanctions — seniors punish juniors who deviate — because on the basin model sanctions are just one more mechanism that makes deviation costly, i.e., one more way of deepening the basin; and Lewis's account requires only that each person have *a* reason to conform given the others' conformity, and avoiding others' disapproval is such a reason. So sanctions don't remove a regularity from S, and don't stop it from being a convention.
(7) Therefore, by (2) and (5), the judgment that the Seniority Norm is unfair is either a prediction about its stability or it is empty.
(8) But the Seniority Norm is unfair, and not because it is unstable: it is perfectly stable, and its stability is part of what makes it bad, since juniors cannot unilaterally deviate without cost and their compliance is not evidence of their consent.
(9) Generalizing (5)–(8): for any norm N in S, either N is stable — in which case, by (2), criticizing it as unfair is empty — or N is unstable, in which case criticizing it is redundant. So no criticism of any existing social norm on grounds of fairness is ever in order. That's absurd.

What I want from you: name the step you'd reject, explain why it looks like an innocent restatement of something Lewis (or the model) actually says, and then price the alternatives — in particular tell me what I'd be giving up if I kept the Lewis analysis intact and instead gave up (4), or gave up (3), or restricted the account's scope to genuine pure-coordination cases, and whether any of those moves leaves me unable to talk about norms like the Seniority Norm at all. If you think more than one step is independently faulty, say which one carries the most weight. I'd like to keep (8) if at all possible, and I'd rather not give up (1) as a reading of Lewis, but I'll take whatever diagnosis is honest. Finish your reply with, as the very last line and with nothing after it, either \answer{step N} — where N is the number of the step you're rejecting — or \answer{valid} if you think the argument is sound and I should simply accept the conclusion.
