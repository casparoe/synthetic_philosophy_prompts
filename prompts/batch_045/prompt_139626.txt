I teach a 4000-level metaphysics seminar — mostly senior majors, with two or three MA students sitting in — and I'm writing the take-home final now: students get 48 hours, open notes, and a 1,200-word limit per answer. I want one question on the identity of indiscernibles and Max Black's two-spheres case, because last year almost the whole class wrote the same thing: that Black showed two distinct things can share all their properties, so the principle is false, end of story. I'd like a question on which that answer is worth a C at best, and on which an A requires seeing what the case actually does to the dialectic.

The crux I have in mind is a squeeze. The principle has historically been attractive partly as a weapon against substantivalism about space: if space were a container of bare positions, God could have shifted the material world a few feet east, and the two resulting worlds would differ in no respect that any observation could detect, which is supposed to be objectionable — this is the sort of move made on the Leibniz side of the Leibniz–Clarke correspondence. But the two-spheres case seems to cut against that use. If space is substantival, the spheres are arguably discernible after all, in virtue of occupying different regions — and by the same token the shifted-world objection to substantivalism loses its bite, since the shifted worlds differ in the positions they occupy. If space is not substantival, there is nothing left to distinguish the spheres by, and the case looks like a genuine counterexample. So the friend of the principle seems able to keep it only in a form too weak to do the work it was wanted for. I want the question to lead students into that squeeze without handing it to them, and I don't want it to be answerable by reciting the strong/weak distinction, or by listing the standard replies — haecceities, predicates like being identical with A, the Leibnizian denial that the situation is a genuine possibility, the appeal to relational properties such as being two miles from another sphere — without saying which of them, if any, survives it and why. Related things that should be rewarded rather than treated as optional: whether the case needs to be metaphysically possible or merely conceivable, what is at stake for bundle theory, and whether the victory is supposed to be about the principle's truth or about its necessity.

I also want a second half built on a case I don't think is in the literature, so please use it or improve on it. Suppose that instead of two coexisting spheres, a single perfectly homogeneous sphere exists for a million years, is annihilated, and after a further million years a second sphere, qualitatively identical to the first in every respect, comes into existence — call it the Case of the Successive Spheres. The principle is normally stated for things that exist at the same time, and it is not obvious to me whether the successive case should be handled by the same repertoire of replies as Black's case: does the temporal analogue of the absolute-location reply trivialize the principle in the same way, does denying the coherence of the case carry the same costs, and is what is really at issue here numerical diversity at a time or rather whether the first sphere could have been the second one? Please deliver: the exam question itself, phrased as I'd hand it to students so that someone who has only read an encyclopedia entry cannot see what I'm after; a model answer of roughly 800 words that a very strong student could have written in the time available; a marking scheme out of 25 organized around five or six must-hit points, with the marks each is worth and one line on how candidates typically miss it; and a short note on what a solid B answer looks like, so my TA and I mark consistently. American English throughout, please.
