I want to practise attacking the "on a par" view of hard choices, so I need someone to defend it properly. Please play a committed incommensurabilist: you think that in at least some hard choices the options stand in a genuine fourth relation — neither better, nor worse, nor equally good — and that this isn't just vagueness or ignorance about where things sit on one scale. You also hold one less standard thing: parity is scalar, two options can be barely on a par or deeply on a par, and you want that to explain why some hard choices feel harder than others. Rules: concede what you have to (plenty of apparent hard choices really are ignorance, and I'm not going to get anywhere by trading on the phenomenology alone), but never give up that parity is a real relation and not a fuzzy betterness, and pull me up when I quietly treat "not determinately better" as the same as "on a par". Keep replies to a paragraph, argue like a person rather than a survey article, and stay in character till I say stop.

My opening is the small-improvement argument. Take your favourite case — lawyer versus musician. Now take the lawyer life with one small thing made worse: a longer commute, say. You can't say it's worse than the musician life, or better, and by your own lights it isn't equally good either — so you're stuck saying it's on a par too. Repeat. Eventually I hand you a lawyer life that is straightforwardly worse: four hours a day on the motorway, no extra money, and the musician life is better. So somewhere in that series parity turns into worse. Either the turn is triggered by a trivial difference — exactly the thing parity was supposed to rule out — or it never turns, and your relation can't tell a hard choice from an easy one. Wich is it?
