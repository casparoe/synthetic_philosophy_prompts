I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I've got a practical problem I keep failing to solve by reading, so I'm hoping you'll do the mapping for me.

I'm about two hundred pages into a WWII novel. My protagonist is a young woman — devout, scrupulous, recruited into one of the British deception outfits. Her job in the book is a small-scale version of what Operation Fortitude did: stitching together wireless traffic for a unit that doesn't exist, moving inflatable tanks around at dusk, feeding a double agent just enough true material that his German handler goes on believing him. She has decided, very seriously, that she will never tell a lie. What she is entirely willing to do is everything else.

I've done the obvious reading on the murderer at the door and on Kant's essay answering Constant, and I've definately got the standard package — the absolute ban, Constant's line that truth is only owed to those who have a right to it, the argument about whether Kant really means it, the business about being answerable for whatever your misdirection causes. I don't need any of that again. What I want is the analysis run on an actual case, in both directions, because my character is doing in miniature the thing that whole apparatus was built for.

Facts as I understand them, put in so you dont have to look anything up (only stop me if I've got something badly wrong): in the run-up to D-Day the Allies constructed a phantom army in southeast England — a fictional army group under Patton, dummy landing craft, inflatable armour, fake radio traffic, double agents reporting to their Abwehr handlers — aimed at convincing the German command that the real landing would come at the Pas de Calais rather than Normandy, and the German Fifteenth Army was still held there for weeks after June 6th. Nobody thinks the deception was wrong. Plenty of people who think lying to a murderer is wrong think this was fine.

So:

1. Who is the murderer at the door here, if anybody? The person actually deceived is a case officer; the one who acts on it is a high command; the one who dies is a conscript somewhere else entirely. Does the ban reach a deception that travels through an intermediary, and does it matter that the immediate audience isn't the killer and may not be murderous at all? Does it matter that he's an enemy rather than a private person inside your own legal order? I have a suspicion that this last thing changes everything and that the murderer at the door is a bad test case for exactly that reason, but I want you to say it properly rather than me.

2. My actual problem, which is the part I can't find anything on: most of what my character does involves no false statement. Dummy tanks. Radio traffic that isn't, as far as I can see, an assertion to anybody. True sentences arranged so that a false conclusion gets drawn. Silence. And — the one that interests me most — telling the truth scrupulously for weeks precisely in order to build a reputation for reliability that can be spent later. Take those one at a time and tell me what the doctrine does with each. My guess is that the ban is defined over declarations, so three or four of them just fall outside it and she never lies at all, which makes the whole thing feel morally arbitrary in the wrong way. But check whether anything else in the framework — truthfulness as a duty of right, the bit about the formal condition of right, the stuff about a lie destroying the dignity of humanity in one's own person — drags them back in.

3. Where the framework goes quiet. It's built, as near as I can tell, for one person speaking to another face to face; this is an organisation deceiving another organisation over eight months, with dozens of people each contributing a piece. Who is the subject of the duty — my character, her section head, the state? If no individual is doing the lying and no individual's maxim is the lie, does the doctrine have anything to say, or is it simply silent? Say which. (And while you're there: what happens to the move about being answerable for the consequences of your misdirection when nobody can trace what this deception caused?)

4. The other direction. What does the episode do to the doctrine? I keep coming back to the distinction the laws of war draw between ruses — camouflage, decoys, dummy traffic, generally permitted — and perfidy, abusing a flag of truce or a protected emblem or a surrender, forbidden. That distinction doesn't track falsity at all; it tracks abuse of a convention that exists to make communication possible in circumstances where otherwise there'd be none. It seems to me to be doing all the work Kant's ban can't do, and it seems to me to be the distinction my character is actually feeling her way along. Am I right that Kant has no room for it? And if the case shows the ban is really scoped to a particular kind of relationship, tell me plainly what that scope is and whether anything is left of the absolutism.

5. What does she think she's doing at the moment she sends the message? If you were writing her: what would a scrupulous person in 1944 actually have said to herself — and be honest if the answer is that she probably wouldn't have framed any of it in these terms at all.

Two housekeeping things. First, no further reading please: I'm in a cottage with no library and I can't look anything up, so work from what you already know and don't send me after sources. Second, mark as you go which claims are you reporting what Kant says and which are you doing philosophy on his behalf, say how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the one you're least sure about. If you think my whole framing is wrong, say that first — then answer anyway.
