Hi — I need something practical, and I keep bouncing off the usual writeups of this stuff.

I'm the treasurer of a small members' betting fund: about 40 of us, pool is currently ~$180k, three years old. Every Sunday night I sit down with a spreadsheet of the next week's slate and decide what fraction of the pool goes on what. Two other members have to sign off on a ~20 minute call, and neither of them has read anything about any of this — they're spreadsheet-comfortable and that's it. So whatever you give me has to be runnable in that window, by me, with a spreadsheet.

The politics matter here: our members get a monthly note, and several of them think that if the model says a bet has positive expected value we should bet as much as the book will take. One guy has started sending us his own spreadsheets showing how much we "left on the table" by sizing small. I need an answer to him that I can paste into the note.

Here's where I get stuck with the standard presentations of Kelly / geometric rationality:

- Most weeks I have 5–15 bets that all resolve on the same Sunday (football, some politics, some weather markets). I can't rebalance between them. And several are on the same underlying — a team to win, that team to cover, their striker to score. The basic "bet edge-over-odds of your bankroll" thing seems to assume one bet at a time and sequential resolution, and I don't know what it becomes when the bets are simultaneous and correlated.
- My edge estimates come from a model with maybe 300 resolved bets behind it, so they're noisy. Everyone says "bet a fraction of Kelly because of that," but I've never seen a rule that says which fraction, tied to something I can actually compute from my own track record.
- Some bets are genuinely one-off. There's a special election in March with no precedent and we have a view. There's no repeated sequence there at all. Does the geometric-growth argument give me anything for a single non-repeating event, or is it just expected-value maximization with extra steps?
- We have a hard term. The fund returns capital in four years, we've told members we're aiming to at least double the pool, and if we're below 60% of where we started at the two-year mark the committee is supposed to wind things up voluntarily. So my horizon isn't infinite and there's a cliff in the middle of it.
- And the weird one, which is why I'm writing to you instead of reading another blog post. One of our original members — call him the backer — has a side letter. If the pool ever closes a quarter below half its high-water mark, he restores it to that half. Once, and only once, over the life of the fund. Legally binding. Never used. My two co-signers disagree wildly about what it means for sizing: one says ignore it and size as if it isn't there; one says it means we can effectively bet twice as big because our real ruin point isn't zero. I think it does something weirder — its value depends on how near we are to the trigger, and it's gone forever once we've used it.

What I want:

1. A sizing procedure I can run on a Sunday. Ordered steps, what counts as a good enough answer to each, and what to do when I can't get a number — which is often; for maybe a third of the bets I'd be straight-up inventing the correlation numbers. Mark the points where the procedure just says no (don't bet this), and the points where it hands the question to a person or to the committee because it's a values call and not a fact.
2. A rule for shrinking the raw fraction given how noisy my edge estimate is, computed from my own record, and be honest about whether it's a real derivation or a rule of thumb wearing a derivation's clothes.
3. One paragraph on the one-off/non-repeating case: whether to size those differently from the repeating ones, and why.
4. Something about the backer clause. What it does to the sizing rule, including whether the right fraction should depend on where we are relative to the trigger, and whether we should adopt a self-denying policy about it — e.g. size as if it doesn't exist, or disclose to members when it's affecting our sizing. My guess is this is where the procedure has to admit it can't settle things.
5. Worked applications to three slates, one of which I expect I won't like the answer to.

The slates, roughly as they'd come to me:

(a) Twelve football bets from the model, all resolving inside six hours, four of them on the same two matches, model says our edge on each is 3–6%. Best slate we've had in months and I definately want to bet big. I expect the procedure to tell me something I don't want to hear here, and I want to see how it gets there.

(b) The March special election: one bet, no precedent, model has no data, but our politics member is very confident and puts a 20% chance on the market being badly wrong.

(c) A week where the only action available is a bundle — the book will only take us if we take all six legs as a single accumulator — and we like four legs and are roughly indifferent on two.

Constraints again: 20 minutes, spreadsheet only, three signers, one of whom will ask "but what if we just bet more" every single time. Please be explicit about which parts of the procedure are the actual principle and which are compromises forced by my situation, and say where the procedure will misfire — I'd rather learn its failure modes now than in month nine.
