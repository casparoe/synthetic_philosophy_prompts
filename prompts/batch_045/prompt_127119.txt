Hi — I need help drafting something for a committee I sit on and I keep going in circles, so sorry in advance if this is a wall of text.

I'm the token non-clinician on our regional hospital's Clinical Ethics and Therapeutics subcommittee (I teach intro philosophy at the community college up the street, which I think is the only reason I was asked), and we owe the full medical board a written recommendation by the 14th. What we're deciding on is a trial compound we all just call "the splitter." Here's the short version: patients on it still rate their pain the same on the 0–10 scale, still say it's burning and not stabbing, still point to exactly where it is and whether it's sharp or dull — but they report that it doesn't bother them. A couple of our post-op patients described it in their chart interviews as something like: the pain is still completely there, it just isn't about me anymore. So the sensory side seems untouched and the suffering side seems more or less gone. We have maybe sixty patients through it so far, adults, post-surgical and a few burns, and three separate fights have broken out that we can't resolve. First: our quality office says the drug is a flop, because the only pain number our audits track is intensity and intensity doesn't move at all, while the palliative team and one of the burn nurses say it's the biggest thing since morphine. Half the argument is really about what we should even be counting. Second: there are three requests in front of us to expand it — to intubated ICU patients who can't self-report anything at all, to an outpatient arm for chronic non-cancer pain, and a loose "on request" request for any adult inpatient outside the trial — plus a vocal group that wants the whole thing shut down. Third: there's a real safety signal. Three patients on it turned up with things nobody heard about — a pressure injury that got to stage III, a compartment syndrome picked up late, an ischemic toe — and as far as anyone can tell they felt all of it and simply didn't care enough to shift, ask, or ring the bell. There's also a weird consent wrinkle I can't stop thinking about: two patients who came off the drug said they were glad to have the suffering back, that it had been their body telling them something was wrong, and one of them had agreed while on it to an aggressive rehab transfer his wife says he'd refused twice in actual pain.

What makes it worse for me personally is two comparisons people keep raising. One of our neurologists points out that the stroke service sees patients who have this exact thing naturally — they'll grin when you pinch them, they can tell you it hurts, they just aren't troubled by it — and nobody proposes treating that as an emergency; and our neurosurgery service still does cingulotomies for intractable pain, which is arguably producing the same state on purpose with a lesion, and the board never blinks at that. If the state itself isn't a bad state to be in, why is it bad to hand someone a pill for it — or is the pill version somehow different because it's reversible and chosen and wide-scale? Meanwhile one of our anesthesiologists says this is just pre-op benzodiazepine anxiolysis with better PR, and that we've been dimming the misery of pain without touching the sensation for decades, so there's nothing new here to decide.

So what I need is the memo itself, not advice about my situation: something around two pages that I could put in front of the board with my name on it. It should (1) give a concrete yes/no/yes-with-conditions on each of the three expansion requests, with a time frame and a stated point at which we review actual data; (2) take a clear position on what our outcome measure should be and why, since that seems to be where the real disagreement is buried — and I do think I want one honest paragraph on the question of whether the badness of pain is just its unpleasantness or whether the sensation and the "pay attention to me, something is wrong" part carry some of the badness themselves, and maybe on whether the patients who were glad to get the suffering back are telling us something true; and (3) not hand-wave the strongest thing on the other side. For me the strongest thing is the missed-injury signal combined with the fact that the people who got hurt are exactly the ones who'd say afterward that not minding was the problem — that's an argument I find genuinely hard to answer and I don't want the memo to pretend it's easy. Tone-wise: it has to land as something clinicians can act on Monday morning, because about half the board checks out the second anything starts sounding like a philosophy seminar, so please keep the conceptual stuff doing real work in the recommendations rather than sitting on top of them as a preface. Name the drug and the committee whatever you like, I'll edit to fit.
