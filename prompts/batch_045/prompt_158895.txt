For the past year, since my father died, I have been helping to run a fortnightly film group at the hospice where he spent his last weeks. Before each session we circulate a short note of the kind compiled by websites that will tell you whether the dog dies, whether a child dies, whether there is sexual violence — and that deliberately tell you nothing else: not who, not when, not how. Lately one member has started adding what she calls "affect notes": "this one is about fathers," "you will cry at this one," "two hours of slow dread." They contain no plot at all, and yet some members treat them as worse than spoilers while others say they are the only warning that has ever actually helped them. A friend tells me philosophers have a name for the underlying puzzle — something about how we can be moved by people we know do not exist — but I do not know what they say about it, and I would rather have the question than the label.

Three things about our own practice strike me as hard to reconstruct.

First, the notes are drawn from a list that reads like a list of real harms — animal death, suicide, sexual violence, illness in a child — rather than a list of plot elements, and it is very much the list one would use for a news bulletin. Yet in the same room nobody thinks to warn anyone before we read an obituary aloud or watch documentary footage of a real death, and we issue no warnings at all about the films that reliably make people in the room weep hardest. The wider culture seems to share the reflex: films are certified as unsuitable for children on the ground that they are frightening.

Second, the spoiler etiquette we observe for fiction does not extend to nonfiction. In a history seminar nobody objects to being told the outcome on the first day, and no one thinks a biography can be ruined by knowing how it ends. But our members also re-watch the films that devastate them, and several report that the second viewing is worse.

Third, the notes sometimes backfire. Warned that a dog dies, one member spent the entire film waiting for the dog and afterwards said the warning had spoiled her more than a plot summary would have — which suggests to me that the difference between a warning and a spoiler is not the quantity of plot revealed.

What does this bundle of practices presuppose about what an emotion is and about what fiction does to us? Please reconstruct the implicit theory, and then tell me: which rival accounts of our emotional responses to fiction it strictly rules out and which it merely fits comfortably with; which features of the practice any of those accounts would license, so that our doing them is evidence of nothing; whether the different parts of the practice presuppose consistent things; and what our notes and our etiquette would have to look like if we replaced the implicit theory with, respectively, the view that emotions about fiction are ordinary fear, pity and grief directed at imagined or merely possible things, the view that they are a distinct state produced by a game of make-believe that only resembles those emotions, and the view that what we respond to is not the invented particular at all but what the story shows about real life.
