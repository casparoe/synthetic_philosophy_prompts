My housemate (also a philosophy grad student, also insufferable) has been chewing on this for about a week, and I'm now fairly sure it proves too much, but I cant locate the broken step. Some context: I want satisficing to be a genuinely normative view for bounded agents, not just the thing you retreat to when optimizing gets expensive. The strong version I want is that sometimes it is right to stop searching even where another look was affordable and would in expectation have turned up something better. Call the time, attention and forgone options that running a procedure eats up its deliberation tax. My housemate's argument introduces that idea and then, I suspect, quietly stops paying it.

Her argument, more or less as it stands on the whiteboard:

(1) A choice is rational for an agent only if there is some procedure she could have run in the time she actually had which would have arrived at that choice and which is the best procedure available to her.

(2) Procedures have to be assessed net of their own deliberation tax.

(3) For any procedure P there is a P-plus: do everything P does, and then do one more thing, where the extra thing is either looking at one more option or spending one more moment deciding whether to look at one more option. Since that second kind of move is always available, the series P, P-plus, P-plus-plus and so on has no last member.

(4) P-plus never recommends anything worse than P does, and sometimes recommends something better, so P-plus dominates P.

(5) If every procedure is dominated by some other procedure, then no procedure is the best available one.

(6) By (1), it follows that no choice is rational.

(7) That is absurd: some choices really are irrational and others are not, and we need to be able to say which.

(8) And notice the argument does not care what you plug in for P. Put satisficing with a fixed aspiration level in and you get the same result. So it dosn't show that satisficing is rational; it shows that nothing is, which is exactly why I think it breaks somewhere.

I definately want to keep (7) and (3): (3) looks like plain truth, since you can always look at one more listing or think for one more second, and I would sooner give up (1) than give up (7).
