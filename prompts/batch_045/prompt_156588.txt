Quick context so you know what I'm optimizing for: I'm the in-house philosopher at a small foundation that makes maybe eight to twelve big bets a year — the hits-based giving style, where most grants go to zero and one or two are suppose to carry the whole thing — plus a boring hedge portfolio that's meant to keep the lights on. Decisions get made by a committee of five, and for a while now we've been using risk-weighted expected utility as our working framework, the Buchak-style one where you list outcomes worst-to-best and weight each increment by a risk function applied to the cumulative probability of doing at least that well: REU(a) = x1 + sum over i>1 of r(P(X >= xi)) * (xi - x_{i-1}), with convex r buying you risk aversion without bending the utility function. That last feature is most of why we liked it — we can be cautious about gambles without signing up for the weird large-stakes implications you get out of a concave u.

I've got about eighteen months of protected time coming up: a fellowship, basically me and a whiteboard, no RA, no data collection, and no appetite in the org for anything that smells like an empirical project run on staff. I want to spend it on the one or two foundational questions in this area where progress would actually change what we recommend, not on the question that would be most fun to write a paper about. So give me a ranked agenda.

The things I keep circling, roughly in the order they worry me, though I don't trust my own ordering:

1. r is global. One function over the whole cumulative-probability scale, so if we tune it to be sensibly cautious about a 60/40 grant call, we've also committed ourselves to damping long shots with tiny probabilities — which is exactly the case the whole strategy is built around. Is there a coherent stake-sensitive or local version, something with more than one parameter, or does letting r vary by stake size or outcome region cost you whatever made the framework attractive in the first place?

2. Level of application. Should the committee apply r once, at the level of the portfolio, and be straight expected-value maximizers underneath? Or at each individual decision? Applying it at both levels looks like double-counting caution to me, but I can't say why in a way that survives pushback from the others.

3. We're five people with genuinely different risk functions. I'm not sure the framework has anything to say when the decision-maker is a committee, and I can't tell whether that's a tractable gap or a sign we picked up the wrong tool. Is there an aggregation story worth trying to reconstruct, or does the whole approach only make sense for a single agent?

4. Our probabilities are contested. Most of our arguments are about the distribution, not about the risk attitude, and the rank-dependent machinery needs a precise distribution to even get going. Does risk-weighting do any work when the input probabilities themselves are up for grabs, or is that a separate problem I should stop trying to fold in?

5. A sanity check that might sink the rest: for numbers in the range we actually see, how often does plugging in something like r(p) = p^2 flip a decision relative to plain EU? If the honest answer is almost never, then the right use of eighteen months might be something else entirely, and I'd rather find that out now.

What I want is the ordering and the reasoning behind it: what you're counting as importance here (my own candidate: would the answer change a check we write), what's genuinely tractable for one philosopher with no data, which of these is upstream of which, which ones are philosophers' playgrounds that wouldn't move practice at all, and what kind of result or argument over the next couple of years would move something up or down the list. Please don't just describe the state of the literature at me — I can look that up myself. Tell me what to work on and what to drop, and say which of my five I've mis-ranked.
