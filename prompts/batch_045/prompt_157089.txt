**Student answer:**

Nietzsche's question in this essay isn't an epistemological one about how we can know the past. It's a therapeutic one: what does carrying a past around do to a person or a people, and when does it let them act and when does it make them sick. His answer is that we need history, but in the way a living thing needs food — a certain amount nourishes, too much chokes. The measure is life, by which he means roughly a culture's or an individual's capacity to go on willing and shaping itself, and the faculty that matters is what he calls plastic power: the ability to take what is past and foreign into yourself, transform it, and rebuild yourself out of it rather than being buried under it.

I want to argue that the three stances are really three stages. A culture begins in the monumental stance, matures into the antiquarian one, and then, once the weight of what it has inherited has become intolerable, has to pass through the critical stance in order to be free.

Monumental history treats the past as a store of examples. The need it answers is the need of someone who wants to do something great and has to be shown that greatness was possible — the monumental historian points to the high moments of the human struggle and says: this really happened, so it can happen again. The disadvantage is that this works by analogy, and analogy lies. Differences get smoothed away, the circumstances that made the great deed possible are forgotten, and the past is rewritten into a gallery of heroes. The living are then measured against an impossible standard and come to look small; or worse, success gets worshipped for its own sake and whatever won gets treated as having deserved to win.

Antiquarian history treats the past as an inheritance. The need it answers is the need to belong. A person or a people that reveres where it comes from, and preserves the conditions under which it became what it is, gets a rootedness and a gratitude for existing that there's no building without. But the antiquarian stance can't discriminate. It wants to keep everything, so the trivial sits beside the important with the same piety, and things that are dead or even hateful get preserved just because they're old. It knows how to preserve but not how to create, and it ends up judging whatever is new by how faithfully it repeats what already exists.

Critical history treats the past as something to settle accounts with. The need it answers is the need of the sufferer. We are, as Nietzsche insists, the results of earlier generations and of their errors, passions and crimes, and someone has to be able to drag some of that before a tribunal and condemn it. This is where his own remedy lies. The first two stances pile up the past until it crushes the present; the critical stance is the one that lets us cut the chain, freeing us to give ourselves the ancestry we would have chosen rather than the one we happen to have, and so it's the one of the three he really endorses. He's clear, of course, that critical history is always to some degree unjust, that it condemns what we are ourselves made of, and that the attempt can fail. But the failure is a failure of nerve, not a structural problem with the stance itself, and it's the only one of the three that aims at liberation rather than at preservation or edification. Hence the prescription: more critical history, less reverence, and a willingness to forget whatever can't be used.

Two smaller points. The suprahistorical standpoint he mentions at the beginning is basically the monumental stance under a grander name — the view that treats the past as a quarry of great examples. And the unhistorical is just the critical stance's capacity to set some of the past aside. Neither of them adds a fourth item to the scheme.
