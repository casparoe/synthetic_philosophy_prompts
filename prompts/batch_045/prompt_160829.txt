so I've been going back and forth on when I'm actually willing to say a system believes something, and I figure the way to find out what I think is to just write down my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases and see whether any principle covers them. Sorry if this is long, the cases matter more than the setup.

1. The thermostat in my hallway. It's 19 degrees, it switches the boiler on. Verdict: no belief. Saying it thinks the house is cold is a shortcut and nothing more.

2. A chess program that gives up a rook because it has a knight fork coming in three moves. Verdict: yes. I'm happy saying it believes the fork wins the rook. Maybe a thin or reduced kind of belief, but belief.

3. A honeybee doing the waggle dance that points to a nectar patch four hundred metres southwest. Verdict: no. Feels like the thermostat with extra steps, even though it's evolved and alive.

4. My dog when the garage door goes: up, tail going, at the door before it finishes. Verdict: obviously yes. Full-blooded belief, this is the benchmark everything else gets measured against.

5. A single stateless LLM call: 'what's the capital of france' goes in, 'Paris' comes out. Verdict: no. My gut says that's shape-matching against the training distribution, not belief. I know this one is shaky.

6. The same model in a long text-adventure session: picks up a brass key in room four, carries it through forty turns, and then unlocks the green door with it, never having been told where the door was. Verdict: yes. And it bugs me that this is a yes while 5 is a no, given it's literally the same kind of thing.

7. A chatbot that tells a flat earther that yes the earth is flat, tells a physicist it's an oblate spheroid, and whose answers flip with how the question is framed. Verdict: no belief about the shape of the earth. Or at least: not one belief.

8. The probe case a friend showed me. She trains a linear classifier on the internal activations of a model playing chess through text; it reads out the true board state with high accuracy on positions the model never saw, and when she flips that direction in the activations the model's next moves change in the way you'd predict if it now took the board to be different. Verdict: strongest yes of the lot, stronger than 6, even though the model is stateless and is only ever emitting tokens.

9. Take a model and fine-tune it on a corpus where every document says the capital of France is Lyon. Afterwards it says Lyon every time, it books trains to Lyon when asked to plan a trip, and the readout probe for capital-of-france now says Lyon. Verdict: I keep flipping. Part of me says it has a false belief, part of me says belief needs some real hookup to the world and this thing's hookup is broken, so it's a broken gauge rather than a mistaken believer.

10. Two chain-of-thought models. Model A writes out reasoning that causally drives its final answer: mess with the reasoning and the answer changes. Model B emits an explanation after its answer is already fixed: delete the explanation, answer unchanged. Verdict: I want to say A is a better candidate for belief than B, but I can't say why and I suspect I'm being superstitious.

11. 'The firm believes demand will rise next quarter.' Verdict: yes, but derivatively, because there are people inside it who believe it and its actions track that. If the firm were an empty shell running a trading algorithm with nobody in it who believed anything, I'd say... maybe still yes? Honestly not sure.

My tentative principle: X believes that p iff (a) X has some state that carries information about whether p; (b) that state gets used downstream in more than one way, or across more than one context, rather than being read off once and discarded; (c) it's counterfactually efficacious — had the state been different, the behaviour would have been different in the way that state says; (d) it can be wrong, and when it is wrong the behaviour gets worse in a way traceable to that.

I think (a) through (d) gets 2, 4, 6 and 8 as yes and 1 and 3 as no, but it looks like it gives the wrong answer on 5 and 7, since both seem to satisfy it, and it doesn't settle 9, 10 or 11 at all. So: test my principle against the cases, repair it or replace it, and tell me which of my verdicts has to go. Please don't cite anything or tell me who said what first — I don't care about the literature, I want the principle. I'd also rather you tell me honestly that one of my verdicts is just inconsistent than build something baroque to save it.

Give me the answer as a single JSON object with these fields:

- "principle": string, one precise statement of your best candidate, 70 words max
- "diagnosis_of_my_version": string, what specifically is wrong with my (a)-(d), one paragraph
- "clauses": array of objects, each with "clause_id", "clause_text", "motivating_case" (which of my cases forces this clause), and "what_it_excludes"
- "case_verdicts": array of objects, one per case 1-11, each with "case_id", "my_verdict", "principle_verdict", "agreement" (one of accommodated / partially accommodated / overturned), "clause_doing_the_work", "note"
- "overturned": array of objects, each with "case_id", "verdict_that_must_go", "why", for any verdict your principle can't keep
- "open_cases": array of objects for cases 9, 10 and 11, each with "case_id", "your_verdict", "confidence" (0 to 1), "what_would_settle_it"
- "new_cases": array of four objects, each with "case_description", "predicted_verdict", "confidence", and "why_this_is_a_good_test" — these should be cases I haven't listed, picked because my gut and your principle are likely to come apart on them
- "alternatives": array of two or three objects, each with "principle", "verdicts_it_gets_right", "verdicts_it_overturns", "complexity_cost", so I can see the tradeoff between saving every verdict with a complicated principle and keeping it simple and dropping one
- "bottom_line": string, two sentences on what you think I'm actually tracking when I say belief
