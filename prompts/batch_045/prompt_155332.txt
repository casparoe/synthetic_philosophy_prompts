My mate Dan and I have the same argument every time we're picking something to watch. He says "trust me, it's beautiful," I say you can't know that till you've seen it — except half the time I do just take his word. I gather philosophers call this aesthetic testimony. I'm trying to work out what I actually think, going by gut, so here's my list.

Dan (same taste, twenty years, we agree maybe four times in five) says a film is gorgeous: I say I know it's good, and I'd assert it to someone else. Fine. The exact same sentence from a stranger on a review site: I'd only say I've heard it's good. Not fine. A critic whose calls I've learned to trust says a novel is beautifully written: I say I know, without reading it. Fine. The same critic says the same novel is very funny: I won't say I know that until I've read it and laughed. Not fine. Dan says a film is boring: I take it, skip it, and say I know it's boring. Fine — which sits really badly with the funny one, and I can't see why. The streaming algorithm, which has been freakishly accurate about what I'll enjoy for three years, says I'll love a track: I'd say I know I'll like it, but I would not say I know it's good, even though it's basically never been wrong about that either. A mastering engineer friend who can hear things I physically can't says a record's low end is muddy: I take her word immediately. Fine. Dan's girlfriend, whose taste he says is identical to his — I've never checked her against my own reactions directly — says a film is gorgeous: I don't think I know. Not really fine. Someone tells me a poem in a language I don't read is exquisite: I feel OK saying it, but I couldn't defend it for a second. An album I was told was a masterpiece before I ever heard it; I'm fairly sure I only love it because I was primed to listen to it that way; I still think calling it a masterpiece is fine.

The one I go back and forth on: my fourteen-year-old tells me a song is amazing, I play it, I think it's dreadful. I definately don't want to say I know it's good, but I don't want to tell her it's bad either, and I can't say why those two are different.

The thing I half-believe is: you can inherit a verdict second-hand when the property is the kind a competent teller could check off — craft, technique, pacing, even boringness — but not when it's a property that partly consists in the response of the person being told; and even the first kind only works if that teller is calibrated to you in particular.

Can you give me a principle that does better than that, or show me where mine breaks? I want it stated precisely enough that I can run a fresh case through it, with the case that motivates each clause. Then run it on two I haven't decided: (a) a friend whose judgement I trust completely on novels tells me a gamelan recording — a tradition I know nothing about at all — is the finest ever made; (b) Dan adored an album when we were nineteen and now says it's rubbish, and I can't tell which of those is the real verdict. Throw in one case of your own that the principle has to bite on too. And be honest about which of my verdicts above it can't save, and why saving them isn't worth the mess. British English please, I'm in Manchester and American spellings do my head in.
