I'm a PhD student in public administration, and for the past year I've been doing paid fieldwork with the homelessness services division of a mid-sized city government, sitting in on the redesign of how it contracts with nonprofit providers. The practical setting: beginning next fiscal year, renewal of the division's roughly twenty service contracts will be partly tied to measured performance, and the city charter requires that any contract specify "objectively verifiable" performance measures, which is being read internally as ruling out a purely narrative or site-visit-based review. I've been asked to draft the stress-test that each candidate measure must pass before it is written into a contract, and then to run it. What I need from you is a reusable decision procedure, not a general discussion: an ordered set of questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points at which the procedure stops with a no, and the points at which it hands the question to a person because it is a values question rather than a factual one. The people who will actually run it are two policy analysts with MPAs and no statistics beyond basic regression; they have about half a day per measure and six measures to get through in eight weeks before the contracts go out, with the deputy director signing off. The conceptual difficulty I keep running into is that the standard warning — that a measure stops being a good measure once control pressure is placed on it, the phenomenon usually called Goodhart's law, and its cousin in machine learning where an agent exploits the gap between the objective as specified and the goal as intended — is easy to assert and hard to build into a checklist, because most of the work a measure does is fine and only some channels of degradation are live for any given measure. So the procedure should force the analyst to name, in advance, the specific channels through which this measure in this setting could be improved without the thing we care about improving: reallocation of effort toward the measured case, selection of whom to serve, reclassification of what counts as a case, and any others you think belong on that list. I also want each incentivized measure paired with something that is not itself incentivized but would move if the target were being hit in the wrong way — a sentinel or tripwire indicator — and I want the procedure to say what to do when no good sentinel exists, since I suspect that is often. One more phenomenon I'd like handled: the tendency for an indicator to stop standing in for the messy thing it was chosen to track and to become, in staff reasoning, the thing itself, so that a drop in the number is not noticed as a drop in the quality of anyone's life. Where that happens, the procedure should say so explicitly rather than leaving it to the analyst's discretion.

Please then run the procedure on three measures that are actually on the table. First: the share of enrolled households that exit to permanent housing within a defined period, which is the leading candidate for the largest contract weight. Second: the average number of days from program entry to housing placement, which the division likes because it is cheap to compute monthly. Third: the year-over-year change in the unsheltered count from the single-night January field enumeration, which the director is fond of because it is the number the city reports publicly. Be aware, and please do not soften this on my account, that I have argued in internal meetings for the first of these as the headline accountability measure and have gone on record supporting it; if the procedure's honest verdict is that it should carry no contract weight, or only a reduced weight under conditions, I would rather find that out from you than from a provider's lawyer. I'd also like one worked example in which the procedure returns an answer I will not like, and I want you to be explicit about what the procedure cannot do: where it will misfire, which forms of gaming it is structurally blind to (for instance gaming by providers who know more about their own data than the city does, or gaming that moves the sentinels along with the target), and where the residual judgment is irreducible rather than merely uneliminated.

Finally, on your epistemic manner: please commit. Give a definite verdict on each of the three measures — tie it to contract weight, demote it to monitoring only, or drop it — with your reasons, and give a definite recommendation about the shape of the overall procedure rather than a menu of options for me to choose between. Where you are genuinely uncertain, say so and say what evidence would settle it, but still tell me what I should do on Monday.
