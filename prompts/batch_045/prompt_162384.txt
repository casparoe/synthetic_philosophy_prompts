Last night I argued at length with a friend about epiphenomenalism, and I want one move of his audited properly. I raised the standard paradox of phenomenal judgment: if my phenomenal states do no causal work, then my judgment that I am in pain is neither caused by nor about the pain, and I have no reason to trust it. He answered with the simile people always reach for: consciousness is to the brain as the steam whistle is to the locomotive, the noise the machine makes while getting on with its work, and nobody thinks the whistle steers the engine. When I said that only relocates the puzzle, since a locomotive never issues reports about its whistle, he offered a patched version: the whistle is audible to a monitoring subsystem that keeps a logbook, so my reports about consciousness are causally explained after all, without consciousness itself pushing anything around.

Please give me the audit. An explicit table of correspondences for both versions — what plays the engine, the steam, the whistle, the sound, the monitor, the logbook — then the disanalogies: which features of the locomotive case get quietly imported into the mind case that the mind lacks (the noise is itself a physical event with downstream effects, the whistle was fitted to signal, engine and whistle are both in space and can interact), and which of those actually bear on the paradox rather than being cosmetic. Then say whether the patched version is even available to an epiphenomenalist, or whether making the logbook depend on the whistle smuggles phenomenal states back in as causes.

I tried to test the whole thing on a case where the machinery can be inspected: suppose a language model's chain-of-thought turned out to be a whistle that does not drive its answers. Would that license distrust of its self-reports, and does whatever we should conclude there carry back to our own case? If neither version of the analogy can bear the conclusion he wants, suggest what would.
