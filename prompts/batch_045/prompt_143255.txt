I convene a philosophy-of-physics reading group that meets for 100 minutes a week. About sixteen people: half philosophy graduate students whose quantum mechanics stops at a survey course, half physics undergraduates who can compute an amplitude but have never thought about what probability is. Next week is the session where an Everettian picture has to say something about probability, and I do not want to open by writing the Born rule on the board. I want them to run into the difficulty themselves and only then be told its name.

Design me a branching betting game for that room. Constraints and desiderata:

First-personal structure. Each participant's stream of experience continues in exactly one branch of a split, and they never observe the others. Implement that physically and cheaply — separate corners of the room, sealed envelopes, cards dealt face down, a phone-free reveal ritual — so that the branching is something they undergo rather than a diagram they look at. Every decision must be committed to before the participant learns which branch they are in.

Real but tiny stakes. A prize budget of about thirty dollars, or chocolate, or a small amount of course credit. I am willing to have the facilitator honour payoffs for real; I am not willing to run anything that needs a bankroll per student.

The elicitation. The core measuring instrument should be a certainty-equivalent: before a split, a student is offered a coupon that pays the full prize to their future self in one designated branch and nothing in the other, and they write down the largest fraction of the prize they would pay for it. Their number reveals an implicit weighting over branches. I want this used repeatedly, not once, and recorded on a whiteboard as a distribution across the room.

A round sequence that first flatters the wrong answer. Round one should be an unequal-amplitude split that can be represented as several equal-amplitude outcome slots, so that counting branches and squaring amplitudes give the same answer and the room feels comfortable. Round two should break the agreement: two outcomes, equal branch count, unequal weight. Round three is the fine-graining reveal — midway through, after commitments are locked, the facilitator announces that what everyone was calling one branch is in fact a very large number of finer branches, so that any care-policy sensitive to branch count is wrecked while one sensitive to weight is untouched. Round four is the irrelevant-device round: a machine in the next room branches into a huge number of copies, changing nothing about the local payoffs, and the room has to say whether their coupon price should move. Round five, if it fits, is the near-null branch: an outcome of positive but absurdly tiny weight that nonetheless gets a branch.

The care-function. At some point each participant should write down, explicitly, a function: how much they care about a future descendant, given that descendant's branch weight, the number of branches of that kind, and the population of descendants. Then the facilitator runs the rounds above as diagnostics on those functions rather than as illustrations of a theory. I want the constraints the functions get tested against stated as moves in the game — insensitivity to fine-graining, insensitivity to causally isolated branching elsewhere, continuity under small changes in weight, sane behaviour at the zero limit — and I want a description of which natural care-functions survive which constraints and where each one breaks.

The debrief. Map specific moments onto specific pieces of the problem: why the coupon price is a measure of caring rather than of credence, why the question is not the same as ordinary uncertainty about an unknown outcome, what exactly the fine-graining reveal and the irrelevant-device round rule out, what is left standing afterwards, and where a decision-theoretic derivation has to take over from the game.

Honesty about limits. Tell me plainly which feature of the Born rule problem the activity cannot reproduce and which the facilitator must state in words. My suspicion is that the game rigs things so that amplitude-squared weighting is rewarded, and therefore begs the question at precisely the point where the live debate is — that the genuine claim is not that the weights are the squared amplitudes but that a rational agent who never mentions probability would nonetheless be forced to act as if they were. Say whether that is right and how to handle it without deflating the previous ninety minutes.

Failure modes. Give me an honest list of what will predictably go wrong: participants who quietly revert to treating branching as ordinary ignorance, participants who answer for the group average rather than for themselves, participants who insist they will simply be the one in the biggest branch, the physics students who try to compute their way out, and anyone who notices that the facilitator controls the reveal. For each, say what to do in the moment and whether to co-opt the objection into the debrief or shut it down. Include a short note on what to do if the room splits almost exactly in half on a coupon price and neither side can be moved.
