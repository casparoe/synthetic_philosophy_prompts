I've spent the better part of fifteen years working in adult social care, and over the last two or three years I've been reading moral philosophy on my own in the evenings — no university, just a small reading group that gets through a chapter a month and whatever I can borrow from the public library. I've drifted into something like a care-ethics position, and I've been asked to write up my "profile" so the rest of the group can attack it. Before I do that, I'd like to know whether the pieces actually hang together. Here, more or less, is where I've landed.

(a) The basic moral unit is the caring relation — someone attending to and responding to a particular other over time — not the character of the carer and not the sum of well-being produced. I hold onto this partly to stop care ethics being swallowed by virtue ethics, which seems to me to put the agent's excellences at the centre in a way care ethics shouldn't.
(b) It follows, I think, that care ethics has no need of principles, rules, rights or a calculus: what's called for is judgement from inside the relationship, and trying to spell that out in impartial terms loses the very thing. (I take this to be part of what the old justice-voice/care-voice contrast was gesturing at, though I may well be caricaturing it.)
(c) But I also want care ethics to be political. I want to say that institutions, labour markets and even whole economies can be assessed by whether they sustain or wreck relations of care, and that there is something wrong with the way caring work gets shifted onto the people who can least afford it, across entire countries.
(d) And I want to keep its being a feminist position — that it begins from the lives of the people who actually do society's caring work, and loses something if it's generalised into a neutral theory anyone can occupy from nowhere.
(e) A verdict I'm attached to: a parent who delegates nearly all the daily caring labour to a well-paid, highly skilled nanny and keeps a genuinely attentive relationship with the child at weekends has done nothing morally wrong. The child is loved, the worker chose the job and is properly paid, and nothing seems to have been lost.
(f) A second verdict I'm equally attached to, and about which I have a nagging worry that it sits badly with (a): imagine a carer I'll call the well-oiled carer. Over the years they have built such an exact model of the person they look after that they no longer need to attend in the moment at all — every need is anticipated correctly, the person is thriving, feels deeply cared for and would be miserable if the carer were replaced. There is, in the outcome, no failure of responsiveness. Still, I want to insist that something has gone missing. But everything I can find to say about what has gone missing is a claim about the carer — their attention, their attitude, whether they are really there — which is just the agent-centred framing that (a) was supposed to rule out. (Connected worry: if a second, equally well-oiled carer could have produced the very same history of care, does the particular identity of the carer matter at all? My instinct says it does, and I can't say why.)
(g) One more, which I suspect isn't really in tension with anything but which the group will go for: I hold that a carer who devotes themselves entirely to their own family and is simply indifferent to everyone else is open to criticism — but not because they have violated some impartial duty.

What I want is an audit, not an introduction to care ethics. Go through these and tell me which pairs genuinely pull against each other and how badly, ranked by severity; for each real tension, say what would have to give and what giving it would cost me; and, importantly, tell me if any of the apparent tensions dissolve on inspection and why — I would much rather be told that (g) is harmless than see it solemnly listed alongside the others. Where I'm leaning on something care ethics itself has no business saying, say so plainly. Please commit rather than catalogue: name the single revision that would clean up the most, and tell me which of my verdicts I should be most willing to drop. And please don't send me to the literature — I have no access to a university library, so reason it out from the commitments themselves rather than from what you take the position's defenders to have written.
