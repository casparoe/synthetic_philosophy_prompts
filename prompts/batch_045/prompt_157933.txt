I have been working through the rule-following paradox in Kripke's reading of Wittgenstein and have built an argument I cannot accept the conclusion of. I would like a diagnosis: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?

Let "quus" name the familiar variant on addition: it agrees with addition whenever both arguments are below 57, and returns 5 otherwise. Now:

(1) The sceptic's case rests on a single premise: for any candidate rule R and any finite body of evidence E about my past usage, there are arbitrarily many rules that "fit" E equally well. Nothing in my history, my introspectible states, or my dispositions singles out one of them. Underdetermination.

(2) Nothing in the argument turns on the vocabulary being arithmetical. Underdetermination is a thesis about rule-governed usage generally, so it applies to any expression with a normative role — including the terms the sceptic needs to state the challenge: "rule," "fits," "the same," "correct," "means."

(3) Define "schmorrect": a computation is schmorrect iff it either involves only numbers below 57 and is correct, or involves a number at or above 57 and issues 5. My entire history of using "correct" — every correction issued, every answer called right or wrong — is finite, and is equally well fitted by schmorrect as by correct, just as my arithmetical history is equally well fitted by quus as by plus.

(4) So by the sceptic's own premise, there is no fact of the matter whether I meant correct or schmorrect.

(5) Then the challenge loses its content. "Quus does not fit my past usage," or "5 is not the correct answer to 68 + 57," is now indeterminate between a reading on which it is true and one on which it is false. A challenge with no determinate content cannot be the substantive discovery the paradox purports to be; it cannot even tell me what I am supposed to worry about.

(6) And it gets worse: if the indeterminacy is global, the sceptic cannot so much as assert that there is no fact of the matter, since that assertion is indeterminate too. The paradox appears to consume itself.

(7) But the paradox plainly does not consume itself. It is a hard problem, the challenge is one we understand perfectly well, and its force is not dissipated by reflection on the sceptic's own words. So at least one of (1) through (6) is false.

I would like to keep (1) and (7). The natural move is to reject (2) and confine the sceptical argument to some proper subclass of vocabulary, but I cannot see how to draw that line without either conceding that certain expressions are immune because their meaning is somehow already given to me — which looks like a straight solution smuggled in under another name — or making the restriction unmotivated. I also suspect the flaw may lie later, in the slide from indeterminacy to contentlessness at (5), or in the iteration at (6).

Tell me which step fails and why it looked sound. Then price the alternatives: what does the sceptical solution have to give up if it accepts the generalization, what does a straight solution have to give up if it blocks (2), and is there a stable position that treats the paradox as undermining the practice of making meaning-claims rather than as discovering something about them?
