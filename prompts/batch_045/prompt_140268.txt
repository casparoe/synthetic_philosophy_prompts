I'm in a final-year metaphysics seminar and last week we went over Max Black's 1952 Mind paper on the identity of indiscernibles — the thought experiment in which a universe contains nothing but two iron spheres, some distance apart, agreeing in every qualitative respect: same diameter, same temperature, same colour, same chemical composition, nothing extrinsic to break the symmetry. After the session a classmate said the whole puzzle is idle, on the grounds that "no two things are exactly alike" — we only think the spheres are indiscernible because we imagine them too crudely. The TA half-agreed and added that in any genuine universe each sphere would occupy its own region of space, and that this alone would make them different. Neither remark struck me as answering Black, but I couldn't say precisely why, and I'd like to be able to.

So I want a précis of the slogan rather than a verdict on Black. Take "no two things are exactly alike" (it also gets put as "nature makes no duplicates" and "there are no two leaves alike") and set out, as a numbered list, the distinct precise theses it could be expressing. The distinctions I want separated at minimum:

- whether "alike" ranges over intrinsic or monadic properties only, or over relational properties as well, including relations to places or spacetime points if such things exist;
- whether the properties in question are purely qualitative, or whether non-qualitative ones are allowed in — haecceities, properties of the form being identical with this very sphere, being the thing someone is pointing at;
- whether the claim is a contingent generalisation about what the actual world happens to contain, a modal claim about what is or is not possible, or an epistemic claim about what anyone could ever know or reasonably believe;
- whether "indiscernible" is absolute indiscernibility (no monadic difference), weak discernibility (some irreflexive but symmetric relation holds between the two, as being some distance from does), or relative discernibility (an asymmetric relation holds one way but not the other);
- whether the slogan is a claim about things, or only a claim about what can be said of them in some language or picked out by some description.

For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible metaphysical thesis, or false. Then, which matters more to me: say which readings would actually do the work my classmate and the TA wanted from the slogan, and which leave Black's argument entirely untouched. If every reading strong enough to dispose of his case simply restates the principle he is attacking, while every reading with independent content leaves his scenario possible, say so explicitly. And tell me what the TA's version commits him to about the nature of space — substantival, relational, or something else.

One further constraint: people in the seminar said the principle was originally pressed into service in early modern arguments against empty space and against qualitatively identical corpuscles. Say which reading the slogan would have to bear for that use, and whether that is the same reading my classmate would need.
