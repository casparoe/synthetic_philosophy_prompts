An old university friend of mine — a corporate lawyer who keeps a blog on "the philosophical case for small government" — recently posted a short write-up on Robert Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain argument and ended it with the claim that the argument settles the question of economic redistribution once and for all. I'm reading *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* with a reading group and may end up discussing this with him, but I can't tell where Nozick stops and my friend starts. My own instincts are that the line about taxation being "on a par with forced labor" is doing more work than it can bear, and that "by definition" in sentence 6 sounds like a tell — but I can't say why, and I may be wrong about both. I also have a vague memory of hearing that some critic argued Nozick simply helps himself to self-ownership without defending it; if that bears on sentence 4, I'd like to know.

Here is the passage, which I've numbered for convenience:

"1. Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain argument is a five-line argument: start with whatever distribution you happen to like — say, strict equality — and let a million people each hand over a quarter they already own to watch Chamberlain play basketball, leaving him far richer than anyone else. 2. The new distribution no longer fits the pattern, and since neither the fans nor Chamberlain has wronged anyone, the only way a patterned principle of justice can survive is by forbidding people from spending their own money as they choose. 3. That is also why Nozick says redistributive taxation is on a par with forced labor: taking someone's earnings is taking hours of their life, so any tax-funded transfer is a rights violation. 4. The engine of the whole thing is self-ownership plus Locke's idea that you come to own unowned things by mixing your labor with them; once you grant that, the rest follows. 5. His positive theory is then simple: a holding is just if it arose through original acquisition and voluntary transfer, provided the acquirer left enough and as good for others — his slogan is roughly, from each as they choose, to each as they choose. 6. Philosophers object that none of this can handle a real world full of conquest and theft, but that objection is political, not philosophical: because Nozick's theory is historical rather than patterned, whatever people currently hold is what they are entitled to, by definition. 7. Which settles it: no principle that redistributes can be a principle of justice, and that includes Rawls's difference principle, since the difference principle would have to ban the fans from paying Chamberlain."
