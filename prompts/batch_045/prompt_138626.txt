I write fiction, and at dinner during a writers' residency last month two other writers fell into an argument I have not been able to settle in my own mind. I want to stage a version of it in the novel I am drafting, and before I do I need to know which speaker actually has the better of each exchange. The subject was Machiavelli's pairing of fortuna and virtù.

**Hal:** The trouble with reading virtù as a settled trait of character — an Aristotelian disposition, something built up over a life — is that Machiavelli's own explanations of failure are not stories about defective character. Princes fall because a cautious man meets cautious times and then the times turn; his mode of proceeding stays fixed while the world does not. So virtù must be a capacity to be remade by the occasion: conjunctural responsiveness rather than a stable disposition. And that is why fortune can be said to govern half of what we do. Whether your temperament happens to suit your era is not up to you.

**Naomi:** But if that plasticity is not cultivable — if it is simply something you either have or lack — then calling it virtù destroys the distinction it was introduced to make. He would merely be saying that the successful are the people lucky enough to be flexible. The flood passage tells against you directly: he says provision is made in advance, with dykes and embankments, while the river is quiet. That is settled, forward-looking preparation, not improvisation at the moment of the flood.

**Hal:** The engineering metaphor cuts the other way. Any system of dykes encodes a bet about which floods are coming, and the floods that actually ruin princes are the ones nobody designed for: a new weapon, a sudden death, an army no one expected. Building in advance helps only if your foresight is as mobile as the world is, and that is precisely the thing you are claiming as a fixed achievement. His admiration, in any case, runs to the impetuous — the young master fortune, on his account, because they are violent and audacious enough to seize her, which is a virtue of timing and intensity, not of accumulated habit.

**Naomi:** I think we are both treating fortune and virtù as two separable causal factors dividing up a single outcome, and that is the mistake. Machiavelli has a third notion in play, occasione: nothing is an opportunity except relative to an agent's capacities, and no capacity is exercised except in a moment that affords it. The moment is therefore jointly constituted — it is fortune for you precisely because it is virtù for me. On that reading the celebrated half-and-half remark is not a causal partition of outcomes at all, but a claim about how success and failure get attributed afterwards.

**Hal:** Which makes it unfalsifiable, and turns the discussion into exhortation dressed up as theory. If any outcome can be redescribed as either seized or missed, then the practical advice — prepare now, before the river rises — has no content left, and the reader is being told to be bold rather than shown anything.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate each of the five contributions from clearly mistaken to clearly right, saying what exactly each speaker gets right and where the reasoning slips, and then say what, if anything, both of them are missing.
