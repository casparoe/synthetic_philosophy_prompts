I want to think through a practice that has always struck me as philosophically strange, and I would like you to reconstruct what it presupposes about meaning.

The practice is statutory interpretation as it is actually done in common-law courts. As I understand it (correct me if I have the practice wrong, but I don't want the answer to turn on contested legal detail): when a dispute turns on what a statutory word means, courts apply something like a plain-meaning rule. Absent a statutory definition, ordinary words get their ordinary meaning, technical terms get their technical meaning, and judges routinely consult dictionaries and corpus evidence of ordinary usage, and other statutes, to establish it. Statutes frequently contain definitions sections, in which a term is defined by other terms of the statute, which are sometimes themselves defined further along. The meaning is treated as fixed at the time of enactment rather than as tracking later changes in usage. Where the term is a term of art, courts defer to the specialized community that uses it rather than to the general reader's sense of the word. And the ordinary-meaning inquiry is explicitly relativized to a readership: the meaning that competent speakers within the statute's intended audience would have taken the text to have. Finally, the whole apparatus has an escape hatch: a reading is abandoned, even when the words are clear, if it would produce absurd or cruel results.

Here is what puzzles me. The practice looks, on its face, like an attempt to fix meaning entirely by symbol-to-symbol relations — a statute defining its terms by its other terms, judges consulting a book that defines words by other words, the inquiry stopping at the walls of the legal corpus. But the canonical illustration of the symbol grounding problem is precisely that a dictionary cannot do this: someone who did not already understand the language would cycle endlessly from one uninterpreted definition to another without ever touching anything nonsymbolic. So I want the implicit theory made explicit, and I want it made explicit in both directions.

First, reconstruct it. What account of meaning does this practice only make sense under? Notice that the practice does not claim the statutory text is self-interpreting in the way a formal system is; it says the text means what a specified population of already-competent readers would have understood it to mean. That is not grounding in the demanding nonsymbolic sense — it looks more like a deliberate parasitism on meanings already in speakers' heads, with the legal system outsourcing grounding to the linguistically competent community and helping itself to the results. Is that the right way to describe it? And is it coherent, or does the outsourcing leave a hole exactly where hard cases live?

Second, I want you to work with a distinction I have been using and tell me whether it survives. Call *anchoring* the fixation of which thing or which set of items a term picks out — by stipulation, by a definition, by a precedent that settles one concrete application — and call *grounding* the possession of a nonsymbolic capacity to identify further instances on one's own. My sense is that legal practice is very good at anchoring and indifferent to grounding: a definitions section anchors by fiat, and a line of precedents accumulates anchored exemplars. But does a sufficiently dense set of anchored exemplars *converge* on grounding, the way a large enough labeled sample can leave you with a capacity you could not have stated as a rule? Or does exemplar-anchoring merely postpone the question, since each new application still requires someone who can already tell whether the new case is like the old ones? Say which, and say what it would take to tell the difference.

Third, the part I think is genuinely hard. Many of the terms statutes fight over are not the names of perceptible natural kinds whose grounding is the paradigm case; they are institutional or normative terms, whose content seems to be fixed by their role in a system of rules rather than by anyone's sensorimotor discrimination. If that is right, then the grounding demand may simply misfire for most of legal language, and the practice may be philosophically fine. But institutional terms typically piggyback on ordinary terms that do look grounded — the chestnut about whether a bicycle, or an e-scooter, or a toy car is a "vehicle" under a rule banning vehicles from a park is a dispute of exactly this shape. So: for which statutory terms does the grounding problem genuinely arise, for which is it a pseudo-problem, and is the boundary itself something the practice can mark, or only something we can mark from outside it? I would also like your view on what the absurdity escape hatch is doing: is it an admission that the symbol-to-symbol machinery has run off the end of the community's anchoring, or is it a separate normative principle with nothing to do with meaning?

Finally, the replacement question. Suppose we dropped the implicit theory and required instead that statutory meaning be answerable to the nonsymbolic, experienced capacities of those who apply it, and not merely borrowed from a readership's prior understanding. Which devices of current practice would then become illegitimate or pointless — definitions sections, dictionaries, fixation of meaning at enactment, textualism itself? What would take their place, and what would we lose? I have a particular worry I want addressed: grounding, on the standard account, is a property of agents with sensorimotor histories, not of texts, and different interpreters are grounded differently. Does taking grounding seriously therefore trade the law's aspiration to a single public meaning for something irreducibly interpreter-relative, and is that a cost the practice could bear?

Please be disciplined about the difference between what the practice strictly presupposes, what it merely sits well with, and what several rival theories of meaning would license equally — I suspect the practice is more underdetermined than it looks, and I would rather hear that than a tidy reconstruction. Where the symbol grounding literature has nothing to say about a question I have asked, say so rather than stretching it. I don't need citations, bibliographies, or a reading list; I know the standard sources, and I'm interested in the argument.
