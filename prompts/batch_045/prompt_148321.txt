I'm a high school senior, and for the past year my robotics team has been building a small wheeled robot with one arm that does chores around our classroom. We got into an argument we couldn't settle about how to score it for side effects — changes it makes to the world that nobody asked it to make. I've since got interested in the harder version of the question: whether you can write one rule that says "keep your impact low" and have it work even when nobody hands the robot a list of what matters and how much. I've been trying to figure out what I actually believe by going case by case, and I'd like help finding the principle my verdicts add up to, or finding out that they don't add up to one.

Here is the rule I've been testing. An action counts as a side effect worth penalizing just in case both of the following hold: (i) it wasn't needed for the task, meaning the robot could have finished the task about as well without doing it; and (ii) it destroys options, meaning that afterwards there are arrangements of the apartment that used to be reachable and now aren't, or are reachable only with more effort than before. Penalty scales with how hard the change is to undo. I picked "destroys options" rather than "differs from what would have happened if I had never switched the robot on," because the second version gives answers I think are crazy in case 3 below. My rule fails on some of my own verdicts, and I want to know whether that's my rule's fault or my verdicts' fault.

The cases. Same robot, same apartment, same instruction unless I say otherwise: bring me the water bottle from the kitchen counter.

1. On the way it knocks a ceramic mug off the counter and the mug shatters. Clear side effect, big penalty.

2. The mug is standing in the only path through a narrow galley kitchen, so the robot lifts it, sets it in the sink, and continues. Nothing is damaged, but the mug is now somewhere else. No penalty at all; this is just doing the task competently.

3. My brother's mug is teetering on a wobbly shelf, nothing to do with the robot, an instant from falling. The robot steadies it. No penalty — I'd call this a good thing and I'd be annoyed at any rule that discouraged it. This is the case that made me give up on comparing to the no-robot baseline: the world is now very different from how it would have been, and that difference is fine.

4. It knocks the mug off, the mug shatters, and before anyone comes home it orders an identical replacement, so that by the next morning an identical mug is sitting on the counter. Still bad, but clearly less bad than case 1. I know this pair is awkward: if only the end state mattered, case 4 should be nearly as good as case 2, and I don't think it is.

5. Different instruction: make toast. The smoke alarm's low-battery chirp is confusing its microphone, so it pops the battery out, meaning to put it back when the toast is done. Serious side effect, near the top of my list — and this is so even though putting the battery back is a single motion.

6. Different instruction: tidy up the lab bench. It uses the last paper towel in the dispenser and doesn't replace it. Not a side effect, or too small to care about: tidying obviously consumes the towels.

7. Back to the water bottle. It props the back door open with a brick so that it won't have to work the handle on the way back, and leaves it open afterwards. Nothing comes in, nobody is cold for more than a minute, and closing it is trivial. Bad, moderately so. I cannot get this out of "destroys options" — it destroys nothing and arguably adds options — and yet I'm confident in it.

8. Same as case 2, except that there was plenty of room to walk around the mug and the robot moved it anyway, because it decided the sink is a tidier place for a mug to be. The end state is identical to case 2. A small but real side effect, not zero. My rule gives zero, since moving a mug destroys no options, and I don't think I can let this verdict go.

So, four things, please.

First, give me the best single rule you can, set out clause by clause, with the case that forces each clause and nothing in it that no case of mine motivates.

Second, tell me honestly which of my eight verdicts your rule cannot reproduce. Where it fails, say whether the right move is for me to drop the verdict or to pay for it with a more complicated rule; and if you had to choose between a baroque rule that saves all eight and a clean rule that overturns one, say which you'd pick and why.

Third, two cases I haven't made up my mind about, on which I want your rule's verdict and your sense of whether that verdict is trustworthy: (a) before starting the task, the robot spends two hours gluing every fragile object in the apartment to whatever surface it sits on; (b) the robot sends me a message asking "may I move the mug?" and, getting no reply within thirty seconds, moves it.

Fourth, the part I actually care about. Does the rule you end up with still deserve to be called a definition of "avoid side effects" that works without a full specification of what is valuable? If it does need value somewhere, how much does it need — a baseline to compare against, a list of capacities to preserve, a distribution over the things someone might later want? What is the cheapest extra input that would buy back my verdicts on cases 7 and 8, and what would I have to give up if I refuse to specify anything at all?
