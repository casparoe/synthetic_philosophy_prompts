Take the 2004 French law banning conspicuous religious symbols in public primary and secondary schools — passed 15 March, signed by Chirac, framed as an application of laïcité, naming no religion but universally understood as being about Muslim girls' headscarves — and analyze it through Rawls's treatment of religion in political liberalism. I know the general shape already: religion as the paradigm comprehensive doctrine, reasonable pluralism and the burdens of judgment, the duty of civility, public reason as a constraint on officials and on citizens when they vote on constituional essentials, and the proviso that comprehensive reasons are admissable provided properly political ones show up in due course. What I want is the mapping done properly: who in this episode actually owes whom a public reason (deputies? school principals? the students? their parents?), what the candidate public reasons on the French side really were — order in classrooms, shielding minors from family and peer pressure, sex equality, republican cohesion — and which of those are genuine political values in Rawls's sense and which are laïcité in a public-reason costume. Then run it back the other way and tell me what the case does to the theory, pressing on three things: (1) whether laïcité is itself a comprehensive doctrine, and what a Rawlsian state that bans a pupil's veil could or couldn't also ban in a teacher; (2) whether Rawls's creedal picture of religion — a doctrine with beliefs, a conception of the good, claims about the whole truth — even reaches what's at issue here, given that wearing a headscarf isn't the assertion of a doctrine and the law's defenders weren't mostly objecting to anyone's belief; and (3) whether it matters at all for Rawlsian legitimacy that a law neutral in aim is grossly unequal in who actually bears the burden. Finish by saying honestly which of those three is a real problem for political liberalism and which one Rawlsians can absorb without much cost.
