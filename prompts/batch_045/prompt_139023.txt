I am putting together a lecture on the methodological critique of evolutionary psychology — the charge that its explanations are unfalsifiable narratives invented to fit whatever behavior happens to have been observed — and I want a case that runs in the other direction: an adaptationist hypothesis that a careful skeptic should concede is doing genuine scientific work, even though it displays every formal vice on the critics' list. So I would like you to build me one.

Take the following as stipulated findings from a single large, preregistered, multi-site study. I have invented them for the purposes of the exercise; treat them as given rather than as claims to check or to reconcile with the literature. Adults sleep measurably worse on the first night in an unfamiliar bedroom than on the second night in that same room, and worse than in their own bedroom. The size of the deficit tracks the novelty of the room — its layout and soundscape — and is unaffected by the room's comfort, temperature, and noise level. It is not reduced by giving subjects credible and true information that the room is perfectly safe, and it is unrelated to how safe they report feeling. It is much smaller in adults who slept in a large number of different rooms during childhood. It is asymmetric: on that first night one hemisphere responds more strongly than the other to unfamiliar sounds, and the asymmetry is absent on the second night and at home. And it appears with roughly the same magnitude in samples from five countries whose actual night-time risks differ enormously.

Now invent, strictly after the fact, an adaptationist explanation of exactly this pattern, subject to the following constraints. State the mechanism as an input-output rule together with an explicit error-management trade-off, in information-processing terms: no invented neuroanatomy, no fictional technology, no imagined fossil or archaeological discoveries. Do not rescue it by claiming that selection is powerful, or that complex functional organization must have an adaptive explanation; whatever earns the hypothesis its standing has to be a structural feature it possesses, not the adaptationist assumption itself. Do not reach for the standard parade of examples — no kin selection, reciprocity, mate choice, pathogen avoidance, or cheater detection. Every confirmatory test must be one a well-equipped laboratory could run this decade. And the case must be one in which the skeptic retains a real objection: I want a hypothesis that is good science, not one that has been stipulated to be immune.

Give me exactly five numbered points, no more than two sentences each, with no preamble and no closing summary.

1. The hypothesis, stated as an input-output rule with its error-management trade-off, including why the mechanism's failure to take safety information as an input is a prediction rather than an embarrassment.
2. At least two consequences it has that go beyond the stipulated findings, that the obvious alternative — discomfort, stress, or general hyperarousal — does not yield, and that could be tested now.
3. Why each of the three standing objections fails to land on this hypothesis in particular: that it was generated after the fact to fit data already in hand; that there is no evidence about ancestral environments; and that some other story, including a by-product story, could always be told instead.
4. The single feature doing the work: name it, and show that deleting it while leaving everything else in place turns the hypothesis back into the kind of story the critics are right to dismiss.
5. The strongest objection a skeptic who grants all of the above should still press, together with the most honest reply available, including a statement of what would refute the hypothesis.
