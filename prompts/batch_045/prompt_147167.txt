ok so — I'm the student rep on my school's scholarship committee (me, two teachers, the vice principal, three parents; we meet in the library on Tuesdays and there are always stale donuts). Every year we hand out one big merit scholarship, and over the last few years we've built up a kind of track record of who we say "deserves" it. I'm trying to work out what rule we're actually running, because this year's case has me stuck.

Our verdicts so far: Aisha — straight A's, hardest schedule the school offers, no job, no real obstacles, and she genuinely works for them — we said yes, she got it. Marcus — 3.85, same hardest schedule, works around 20 hours a week at his uncle's shop, basically raises his two little brothers, first in his family to apply to college — we said yes, and a couple of the teachers kept saying his 3.85 was "worth more" than somebody else's 4.0, so we ranked him above Aisha. Devon — 3.95, but he took the easiest classes he was allowed to take, no job, no obstacles, works hard — we said no, on the grounds that you have to actually challenge yourself. Priya — 2.9, hardest schedule, was homeless for part of junior year, works, and every teacher says she works harder than anybody in the grade — we said no, and it was a miserable meeting; the vp said something like we can admire her and still think this particular award isn't hers. (We also turned one kid down because he'd been caught cheating sophomore year, but everyone agreed that was a separate issue from whether his grades were deserving, so I don't think it counts as a precedent.)

This year's front-runner is Elena: 4.0, hardest schedule, no job, no family stuff, both parents are doctors, private math tutor since about sixth grade, extracurriculars are fine but nothing special — and every teacher who has her says school is just easy for her and she does her homework on the bus. So what I want from you: give me the rule or rules that fit all five of those verdicts at once. I can half-see two: one where what matters is how much you achieved plus extra credit for obstacles you didn't choose, and one where what matters is effort and taking the hard road with some kind of floor on actual results. Tell me which of those the committee looks like it's really running, what each of them predicts about Elena, what we should decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves, and which earlier case is the closest analog. If our precedents genuinely don't decide it, say so and say what principle we'd be committing to either way. Also — my sister's boyfriend is in a philosophy PhD program, and when I told him about all this he said the whole exercise is incoherent because nobody earns their natural abilities or the family they were born into, so at best we're rewarding effort and at worst we're just laundering luck into self-congratulation. Is that actually a third rule that fits every verdict we've made, or is he just being annoying? And most useful for me: what's the one extra hypothetical case I should bring to the next meeting that would force these rules apart?
