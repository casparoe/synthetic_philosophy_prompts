Hey — context first so the question makes sense. I've got roughly two years and enough money to fund three people (postdocs, or junior faculty on course buyouts) to work on one thing: whether Hume's arguments in the economic essays — money, interest, the balance of trade, the jealousy of trade, public credit, refinement in the arts — still hold up in an economy like today's, and what a Humean framework would actually recommend to a government now. The end I care about is pretty concrete. In two years I want to be able to say something defensible to finance-ministry types on two live questions: (a) should a middle-income country worry about running a persistent goods trade deficit, and (b) how scared should anyone be of public debt. So this is not a Hume exegesis project and not a survey of a field.

Which three or four questions should the program actually work on, and in what order? I care much more about the reasoning than the list. Here's my own rough set of pressure points; throw out or reframe any of them if you think I've framed them badly.

1. Hume's long-run story is that money is basically a veil and an inflow only helps during the interval before prices catch up. Under fiat money and floating exchange rates there's no specie to flow out. Is the interval story just nominal rigidity in period costume, and does it survive when the money growth is anticipated? And what about the apparent counterexample — commodity-boom countries where an inflow of foreign money seems to have permanently damaged their tradable sector, the thing now called Dutch disease. Does that kill the story or just delimit it?

2. The jealousy-of-trade argument: your neighbours getting richer is good for you. Does it survive industries with strong increasing returns and learning curves, where arriving second means never catching up? Does it survive a world where the deficit is in goods and the surplus is in services and licensing, so the balance statistic measures something close to nothing? Does it survive strategic dependence — buying from someone who might stop selling?

3. Interest: Hume thinks it's set by the rate of profit and the demand for borrowing, not by the quantity of money, and that low interest is a symptom of commercial maturity rather than a cause of growth. Modern central banks set a policy rate and move the whole curve. Is there anything left in the claim that you can't enrich a country by making money cheap, beyond long-run neutrality in different clothes?

4. Public credit: Hume's warning that debt either gets destroyed or destroys the state, plus his worry about a rentier bondholder class and about debt held abroad. What happens to that when the debt is in your own floating currency and held mostly by your own pension funds? Does the we-owe-it-to-ourselves move have any force against a Humean, or does the real Humean worry turn out to be about something else entirely — who gets paid, what the sovereign can no longer do, and so on?

5. Luxury: part of Hume's defence is that people need occupation, and refined sociable consumption is what keeps them busy and civil. Take it as given that we had decent evidence that a big chunk of modern consumption growth is positional and barely moves reported well-being. Does that break his argument, or is he committed only to more occupation and not to more consumption?

Rank whatever version of these you'd fund, and defend the ordering: what's upstream of what, what's tractable in two years for people who read philosophy and some econ but won't build a DSGE model, and which ones would actually change what you'd say to a finance ministry versus just being interesting. Also tell me what finding over the next couple of years would move something up or down — what would make you drop question 1 altogether, say. Argue with me rather than summarising, and please don't give me reading lists.
