Need help with a grant application. I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy training beyond arguments with a mate who did a PhD, and I'm applying to a small fund for independent researchers: six months part-time, deliverable is one ~5000-word paper plus a two-page practical annexe for people who build systems that generate text users actually read — not just chat models, but templated notifications, autogenerated support replies, model-written summaries.

The thing I can't let go of: the usual list of tests for conversational implicature — cancelability/defeasability, calculability, non-detachability, reinforceability — all seem to work by asking what the speaker could have said instead, or could have added afterwards. That needs a speaker who was there and could have done otherwise. With generated text there often isn't one: nobody can say "I didn't mean to suggest that", and with a stochastic generator there isnt even a fact of the matter about what it would have produced instead, so the counterfactuals the tests run on come out indeterminate. But hearers still draw the inferences, they still feel defeasible to us, and they still do real work in the world. My half-formed thought is that this is a genuine wedge: either those tests aren't constitutive of implicature but only symptoms of it, or the intentionalist picture where speaker meaning is the primitive has to give way to something like "what a competent hearer is entitled to infer, given how the thing that produced the text was built and the context it landed in".

So: turn that into a proposal. I need (1) the question stated precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, (2) why the obvious existing positions don't settle it — as far as I can tell, the intentionalist/Gricean line takes speaker meaning as basic and the tests as diagnostics, the neo-Gricean/default-inference line puts most of the work on the hearer side and will probably shrug at all this, there's some "the designer or the company is the speaker" move, and the AI side seems stuck on whether models can have intentions, which I think is a different and less interesting question, (3) the method, month by month — I'm imagining a taxonomy of cases (templated vs stochastic, is there an identifiable authorising agent, is a repair move even available) and running the tests across it, but tell me if that's the wrong shape, (4) why that approach rather than the obvious alternatives, e.g. why not just do an empirical study or just write about whether systems can mean things, (5) what the possible conclusions are and which considerations would decide between them, (6) what would count as the project failing — I'd rather find out now if it collapses into something people already answered, and (7) honest scope: what's realistic in six months part-time with no supervisor and no library access.
