Hi — I'm on the Clinical Analytics Review Committee at my hospital system. We meet Thursday and I've got to circulate a short decision memo beforehand, and I'd really like a draft to work from.

The case: our sepsis early-warning vendor, Corvid Health, wants us to upgrade from the model we've run for two years to their new "Corvid Universal." The pitch is a benchmark suite: 11,000 synthetic prediction tasks generated from what they call a uniform random prior over data-generating processes, and they report that Universal beats both our current model and a panel of standard learners on average across the whole suite. The deck concludes that this is domain-general evidence and that site-specific validation is therefore optional.

One of our members, a retired philosopher who sits on the committee, sent around a one-pager saying basically the opposite. Two claims in it. First, that by the no-free-lunch results, averaged over all possible problems every algorithm does exactly as well as every other one, so Universal's suite average can't be evidence of anything unless the suite was built to resemble wards like ours — and a uniform random prior over data-generating processes is definately not that. Second, that even setting that aside, no amount of past performance at other hospitals could ever justify expecting performance here, since the only argument for it is "it has worked before," which is circular. He wants us to reject outright and let the contract lapse in March.

I think he's right about something and wrong about something, and I want the memo to say which. Concretely: recommend approve, reject, or approve-with-conditions-and-a-hard-gate, with a timeline, and reasons the vendor could actually respond to in writing.

Things I want handled:

- The uniform-prior suite. My read is that it's a prior dressed up as a proof: doing well on it shows the model's biases match whoever built the generator. But I don't know what we should be asking the vendor for instead. One idea floating around is to make them name the fifty tasks in the suite most similar to our ICUs and report performance only on those — that feels like a trap, since they pick the fifty and would pick flattering ones, but maybe there's a version of it that works.
- Ranking versus calibration. Whatever we adopt fires a fixed threshold that triggers an antibiotics protocol, so what matters is whether the probabilities are right in our population, not whether the model orders patients well, and I don't think the vendor's numbers speak to that at all.
- The feedback problem. Once the score is displayed, nurses act on it, which changes which patients get labelled septic, so the data we'd collect during a pilot can't straightforwardly tell us how the model would behave once it's actually live. I want the memo to be honest about what a pilot could and could not establish.
- Give the losing side its best day in court. If we go with conditions, the strongest objection isn't the circularity one, it's that clinicians will anchor on a number they don't understand and that a "pilot" becomes a deployment by inertia.

Keep it under ~700 words, written for the committee — a CMO, two docs, a nurse manager, our data scientist, and the philosopher — with the recommendation up top. And please don't cite anything or point us at papers; we can't chase references and wouldn't know what to do with them anyway. Just the argument.
