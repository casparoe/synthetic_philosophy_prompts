I'm giving a job talk in three weeks and I need a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the view.

The talk defends what I call the Two-Tier Account of suffering's badness. Three claims:

1. Below a threshold, pleasant and unpleasant experience are commensurable in a single hedonic currency. There are exchange rates; they vary between persons but are in principle estimable, and interpersonal comparisons are good enough to do policy with.

2. There is a lexical threshold. For pains above it, no finite quantity of sub-threshold well-being — however distributed, across however many people — compensates for a single episode. My argument for the threshold is a revealed-preference test I call the interchange test: a sub-threshold pain is one for which, at the time of the pain and with the subject informed and not panicking, there is some amount of later well-being she would accept in exchange for continuing a while longer; a super-threshold pain is one for which there is none. In practice I locate the threshold where a subject's answer to "how much longer could you stand this?" collapses to "not another second."

3. For a life containing a super-threshold episode, the value of the whole life is set by that episode alone — call it the amputation rule — and sub-threshold content survives only as a tiebreaker between lives whose worst episodes are equally bad. Among super-threshold episodes, intensity ranks first and duration second, because in that range a subject's answer to the interchange test looks insensitive to how long the episode goes on.

The applied upshot I close with is that ordinary aggregation of the sort health economists do is legitimate below the threshold and breaks above it: severe-pain conditions take absolute priority over any number of mild ones, and duration matters far less than clinicians assume.

The room, in the order of how much they will matter:

- Two aggregative consequentialists. One is a straight total utilitarian. The other is a prioritarian with effective-altruism sympathies who already grants extra weight to the worse off but thinks that extra weight is best modeled by a continuous, steeply concave weighting function, so that lexicality is a needlessly rigid way to reach the same practical conclusions.
- One population ethicist in the tradition that treats the Repugnant Conclusion as the decisive test of an axiology. She will ask what a two-tier structure does to population-level aggregation — in particular whether it generates a pathology of its own, something in the same family: a conclusion that for any population with one person above the threshold there is a better population of vastly many barely-worth-living lives, or the reverse.
- One philosopher of mind who works on pain. His hobbyhorse is that "how intense is it?" and "how unpleasant is it?" are separable questions — the ratings dissociate under distraction, under changed expectation, and under some analgesics. He will ask where the cliff is supposed to live, in the experience or in the subject's reaction to it, and whether intensity is a scalar at all.
- One bioethicist in disability studies who is hostile to aggregative frameworks generally. She will press on what my view implies about people living with severe chronic pain, and on who gets to decide whose pain counts as above the threshold.
- A handful of graduate students who have all argued about the enormous-numbers case — vastly many barely noticeable annoyances versus one agony — and will raise it within thirty seconds of the end of my talk, for which I have about two minutes.

What I want, as a prep sheet I can rehearse from:

The eight or so objections this room will actually produce, ordered by how much damage they would do rather than by how likely they are, each attributed to the person who will raise it and phrased the way that person would put it — in a prioritarian's vocabulary, in a population ethicist's vocabulary, in a philosopher of mind's vocabulary — not in mine, and not the objections I would get from the online readership of these ideas. For each: a two-minute reply, a note on where the reply is soft, and a verdict on whether it is better to concede something up front or hold the line and pay for it.

Also: what I should concede in the first two minutes of the talk before anyone asks anything; the single reply that has to land if the project is to survive the hour; and the weakest point in my own argument that I should volunteer rather than hope nobody notices. I suspect it is either the interchange test's treatment of duration or the assumption that the threshold sits in the same place for different people, but tell me if it is something else. Close with the question I am hoping nobody asks, including at least one I will not see coming.

Where a reply would depend on an empirical claim about pain or about measurement that I would have to look up, say so explicitly instead of asserting it. Tag each reply as confident, reasonable, or speculative, and at the end single out the one claim in the sheet you are least sure of, with a note on what further challenge from the floor should make me fold rather than push back.
