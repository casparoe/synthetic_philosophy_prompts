I've ended up defending a package of views about expectation and rational choice more confidently than I can really support, and I'd like to know how exposed it is. The package is this. (1) The St. Petersburg-style cases don't merely show that expected value is awkward to compute or unintuitive; they show it is the wrong currency, so the quantity that should govern choice isn't E[U] at all. (2) The decisive case is the Pasadena-style gamble: a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads; if the first heads is on toss n you receive a payoff that alternates in sign and grows like 2^n/n, so that the expected value series is the alternating harmonic series, conditionally convergent, with both the positive and the negative tails divergent. Any criterion that goes silent there, or that returns different verdicts depending on how the terms happen to be ordered, has misdescribed what it was measuring rather than hit a computational wall. (3) The replacement I favor is what I call the stabilization rule: truncate both options at a threshold — cap magnitudes, ignore outcomes beyond it, cut the tail however you like — take the difference of the two truncated expectations as a function of the threshold, and let the threshold go to infinity; where that limit exists and is nonzero, prefer accordingly, and where it fails to exist, treat the options as genuinely incomparable rather than manufacture a number. (4) The consequence I actually care about is that expected-value reasoning about very long-run futures — existential-risk mitigation, arguments of the form tiny probability, astronomically large payoff — inherits the pathology, since that is precisely the domain where the tails are heaviest and the bounds least settled.

I was pressed on this by a friend who works on long-termist research funding and by someone in my department's decision-theory reading group, and I noticed I was doing most of the conceding. So: what would count against the package? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and separate what a sympathetic reader — someone who already grants that expectations can be undefined — would themselves concede counts against it from what only a committed expected-utility theorist would press. I'd like you to consider at least: whether the stabilization rule is extensionally equivalent to plain expectation wherever expectation is defined, since I take that as a desideratum I may not have earned; whether it can violate transitivity or stochastic dominance, or flip its verdict under changes confined to outcomes of arbitrarily small probability; my own suspicion that the rule just privileges one ordering of the terms — the order in which the tosses happen — without a principled story about why that ordering is the right one; whether incomparable is doing real work or is reluctance with a technical name; whether the whole package is otiose if the physical world bounds stakes and probabilities sharply enough that no real decision has an undefined expectation, which is where my friend pushed hardest and where I think I waved the question away; and whether (4) actually follows from (1)–(3) or needs an extra premise about how heavy the tails of real interventions are. If there is a result to the effect that a law-of-large-numbers style justification can be recovered for long-run reasoning even when only one of the two tails converges, tell me how much of (1) that would take down with it.

What would move me most is evidence that has arguably already arrived — a case, a proof, or an established modeling practice — rather than a merely possible world. And please tell me honestly whether I have insulated the position: expectation is the wrong currency is a normative claim, and I can't tell whether my insistence that no numerical criterion survives the Pasadena case is a discovery or a stipulation. If it is insulated by construction, say whether that is a defect for a view of this kind. Lead with your verdict in a single sentence — how exposed the package is, and at which joint — then give the ranked defeaters and the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of what I've asked.
