I sit on a small working group drafting guidance on expert statistical evidence for criminal trials in England and Wales — I came to it after serving on a jury in a case that turned almost entirely on a DNA match probability that none of us felt equipped to think about, and I took one philosophy of science course long enough ago that the interpretations of probability survive in my head only as slogans — and I have been asked to prepare the opening case study for a half-day seminar for judges and advocates. I want to use the Sally Clark case, because it is the one everyone in the room will already have heard of. Here are the facts I intend to hand out, so that we are working from the same picture: Sally Clark, a solicitor, was convicted at Chester Crown Court in November 1999 by a majority verdict of murdering her two infant sons, Christopher (born September 1996, died that December, a few weeks old) and Harry (born November 1997, died January 1998, aged eight weeks); the defence said cot death, and the prosecution called the paediatrician Roy Meadow, who testified that the chance of two cot deaths in an affluent, non-smoking family was about one in 73 million, a figure he reached by squaring a figure of roughly one in 8,500 for a single such death, and which he illustrated by comparing it to backing an 80–1 outsider in the Grand National four years running and winning each time; the Royal Statistical Society later said publicly that there was no statistical basis for the figure and warned about the misuse of statistics in the courts; the conviction was upheld on appeal in October 2000 and quashed at a second appeal in January 2003, after it emerged that the prosecution pathologist had not disclosed microbiology results indicating Staphylococcus aureus in the second baby's cerebrospinal fluid; Clark was released after more than three years in prison and died in 2007. If I have any of that wrong in a way that matters, correct me quietly at the start — but please do not rest the analysis on the disputed details, because the seminar cannot turn on them.

What I want from you is not the standard story. I know about the prosecutor's fallacy, and I do not need a lecture on the difference between the probability of the evidence given innocence and the probability of innocence given the evidence. I want a disciplined analysis of the episode through the lens of the question of what probability is — frequentist, subjective or Bayesian, propensity, and, where it matters, the separate question of the error-rate machinery that grew up around frequentist statistics — with three things done carefully in that order. First, sort the numbers floating around the trial into three objects that I suspect are being run together: a relative frequency in a specified reference class, a single-case chance ascribed to this infant or this family, and a degree of confidence in the hypothesis that the defendant killed her children. Tell me which interpretation can supply each, which can supply it only by stretching, where Meadow's figure migrates illicitly between them, and what his horse-racing analogy reveals about which of the three he thought he was offering. Second, take the squaring step seriously: it assumes the two deaths are independent, and they arguably are not, since a family that has already lost one infant to cot death is not exchangeable with one that has not. What does each interpretation make of that dependence, and what happens as the reference class is narrowed — affluent, non-smoking, already one cot death — toward a class with only a handful of members, at which point a frequency is undefined while a propensity and a degree of belief apparently are not? I also have a half-memory of someone arguing that two infant murders in one family are rarer still than two cot deaths, so that even taken at face value Meadow's number did not point toward guilt; tell me whether the comparison of two such tiny numbers, drawn from different classes, is even coherent on a frequentist reading, and whether every interpretation converges on the same claim about what the jury should have been told.

Then I want the case run back against the theory, which is the part I expect the room to resist. Does this episode tell us anything at all about which interpretation of probability is correct, or is it merely statistical incompetence that any interpretation would have condemned equally? I have two suspicions I want tested rather than indulged. The first is that a criminal trial seems to need, at the same time, an objective-sounding number about a one-off event (this family, these two deaths) and a threshold standard — beyond reasonable doubt — that looks like a posterior probability of guilt on a Bayesian reading but like a long-run error rate for a decision rule on a frequentist one; if that is right, then legal fact-finding makes demands that the frequency interpretation cannot meet, and I want that claim examined instead of asserted, including whether the error-rate reading of the criminal standard is really as available as it looks and what it would commit us to about the many cases that never reach a jury. The second is that the reforms everyone reaches for after cases like this — that experts should present likelihood ratios instead of probabilities of guilt, that courts should refuse to hear numbers about the defendant's guilt at all, that juries should be given base rates — each seem to presuppose an answer to the interpretation question; I want to know which interpretation each reform tacitly commits a court to, and whether the reform is defensible under more than one. Finally, and this is the part my group most needs, be explicit about where the interpretation debate has nothing to say: about the undisclosed microbiology, about the role and selection of expert witnesses, about how juries actually hear large numbers. Give me, at the end, a plain map of what the philosophy can and cannot do for us here, so that I am not selling the seminar as more than it is.
