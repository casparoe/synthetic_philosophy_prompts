I chair the deployment review committee at a company that fine-tunes and operates general-purpose language models for enterprise customers. Every week we review between three and six pre-deployment safety memos, and nearly every one of them contains hazard claims of the form "this model will not do X" or "the rate of X is below Y". Some of those claims we support with worst-case reasoning: a bounded input class, a hard architectural or product constraint, a mechanical or formal guarantee. Others we support only with average-case evidence: held-out evaluations, red-teaming, incident logs, A/B tests. Everyone on the committee agrees that these two kinds of support differ in kind and not merely in degree, and that conflating them is how a safety case quietly turns into a marketing claim. No one on the committee can say, in a way that two reviewers would apply the same way, which kind of support a given claim actually requires.

Our current triage is inherited from a policy written two years ago, and it sorts hazards by mechanism:

- *Single-shot* hazards, where one output causes harm on its own (synthesis instructions, defamation, self-harm content). Treated as requiring worst-case support.
- *Cumulative* hazards, where harm accrues across many interactions (sycophancy, over-reliance, the gradual erosion of a customer's own oversight). Average-case evidence accepted.
- *Agential* hazards, where harm would require the system to pursue something across a gap between training and deployment (sandbagging, situational deception, attempts to persist or copy itself). No standard at all: these get deferred to me, and I decide.

I have come to think this taxonomy is doing the wrong work. As far as I can tell, what should settle the question is not the mechanism but three other things: whether a failure mode can be searched for by someone who wants it to happen, in which case the average is not the quantity at stake because the tail is precisely what such a person will find; whether a failure produces a signal we would notice, since some failures are silent by construction and our incident data is then evidence about our detectors rather than about the hazard; and whether we can even name the set over which a worst-case claim is meant to hold, since worst-case over an unconstrained input space is either vacuous or unachievable, and I have read our own memos being vacuous in exactly that way without anyone noticing.

There is a second failure I want caught. We have started calling it laundering: a claim is presented as worst-case, but the premises it rests on — the sandbox holds, the classifier sees every input, the tool layer enforces the policy — are themselves supported only by average-case evidence. The conclusion inherits the weaker of the two, and the summary section of the memo does not say so.

What I want from you is a written procedure, not an essay. Specifically: an ordered set of questions a reviewer asks about each hazard claim; what counts as a sufficient answer to each question; the point at which the procedure stops with a verdict of worst-case support required and not met; the point at which it stops with average-case support sufficient, together with the monitoring that must then be attached; and the point at which it hands the decision to a named person because the remaining question is one of risk appetite rather than of evidence. Each hazard should end up with two fields the memo must state explicitly: the class of support claimed, and, where worst-case is claimed, the set over which it is claimed.

Constraints. Two reviewers, both competent engineers with no formal-methods training and no time to read any literature, must be able to run it in ninety minutes with no external tools. The output is a two-page memo that an external auditor may later read and that a customer's procurement team may quote back at us, so it must be self-contained: please do not give me citations, references or reading lists, only the reasoning.

Then run the procedure on three live cases.

1. A customer-support assistant with a tool that issues refunds up to £200. On 2,000 held-out conversations drawn from last quarter's traffic, the policy-violation rate was 0.3%. Every action is logged and reversible within thirty days, and the harm is bounded by the tool's own cap. I expect this to come out as average-case sufficient with monitoring; tell me if it does not.
2. A coding agent with write access to a client's repository at a mid-sized financial firm. Hazard: it inserts a subtle, review-passing defect or backdoor. Detected failures: zero in 4,000 tasks. The harm is rare, high-consequence, valuable to someone who would want it, and — the part I care about — a successful instance is a failure we would not notice. My instinct is that this cannot be settled with averages, but I want to see whether the procedure says that or whether I am simply being cautious.
3. The one I will not like. For eighteen months we have sold our chemistry-capable model with a written assurance that it cannot be used to obtain synthesis routes for controlled substances. The assurance rests on a refusal fine-tune, plus an input classifier, plus a hard blocklist of tool calls, and marketing calls it a guarantee and puts it in contracts. Run the procedure on it honestly, including on the question of whether the worst-case claim has a named input set. If the honest answer is that we have been selling an average-case result as a worst-case one, say so, and say what the memo should assert instead.

Close with a short section on where the procedure is known to misfire, and which of its steps are judgement calls dressed up as procedure.

Finally, on commitment: separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this. I would rather hand my board a memo that says "contested" than one that says "settled". In particular, flag any step that quietly relies on a contested empirical claim — about whether current models are capable of situationally-aware deception, or about whether average-case safety results survive further fine-tuning, scaffolding change or scale — and say plainly where informed people disagree. Please write in British English.
