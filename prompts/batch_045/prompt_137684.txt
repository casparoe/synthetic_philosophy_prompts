I've just come out of a long argument with a friend who works at a philanthropic advisory shop, and I want help reconstructing what the practice we were arguing about actually presupposes, because I suspect it is committed to more than my friend admits. The practice is the standard cause-prioritisation framework used by grantmakers in that world: candidate causes are scored along three axes — how much value would exist if the problem were solved (cashed out in something like lives saved, quality-adjusted life years, or some generic welfare unit), how much progress an additional dollar buys, and how few other resources are already flowing to the problem — and causes are then ranked by something like the product of the three. Around the scoring sits a cluster of further habits that I think matter. Evaluators decline to recommend an organisation that cannot usefully absorb the money: they ask how much room there is for additional funding, and once that room is nearly filled they move on, even though the programme itself is unchanged and still the cheapest known way to produce the good. The score of a cause goes down when other funders move into it; grantmakers say a cause has become over-subscribed and is therefore a worse bet than it was five years ago — while in the same breath running field-building programmes whose explicit aim is to attract more funders and more talent into the very causes they favour. Donors are told to concentrate everything on the single highest-scoring cause rather than to spread across several, even though everyone agrees the underlying estimates are uncertain by an order of magnitude or more. And in practice only causes that can be given a quantitative model get scored at all: considerations that resist quantification — dignity, coercion, effects on institutions or on culture, the moral significance of who exactly is helped — tend to drop out of the ranking silently rather than entering it as uncertain terms.

The crux of our argument was the third axis. My friend said it is just a proxy for diminishing marginal returns: my dollar does less good where other dollars are already going, and the framework is only trying to approximate the marginal expected value of the next dollar. I said that cannot be the whole story, because the same variable is also being used as evidence: grantmakers routinely treat the fact that no one is working on something as a warning sign that it is not important or not tractable, and the fact that many smart people are working on something as evidence that it is. Those two roles pull in opposite directions, and I want to know whether a single scoring framework can coherently use one input both as a multiplier on value and as evidence about the other multipliers, or whether that double-counts. She then proposed a rival that I found genuinely illuminating, and I want your verdict on it: replace the third axis with what she called credit-share accounting, where a cause is scored by its importance and tractability divided by the number of funders whose cooperation the outcome requires, so that we rank by our share of the credit for a joint achievement rather than by the difference our dollar makes to how the world goes. Her claim was that the practice already secretly runs on her view and not on the counterfactual one — that the room-for-more-funding check and the concentration advice only look sensible if you think you are buying a share in an outcome rather than making a marginal difference.

So: reconstruct the implicit theory here, and be disciplined about it. What theory of practical reasons — counterfactual difference-making, marginalism, fair-share-of-a-cooperative-venture, something else — does the practice strictly presuppose, and what does it merely sit well with? Could a non-consequentialist, or someone who thinks we should do our part rather than maximise, endorse the same scoring without inconsistency, which would show the practice is more underdetermined than it looks? Does the practice presuppose that values across radically different causes are commensurable and aggregable, and if so does it presuppose a total rather than an average, person-affecting rather than impersonal, view of aggregation? What does it presuppose about the epistemology of unmodelled effects — is the refusal to score what cannot be modelled a piece of expected-value reasoning, a sensible response to cluelessness, or simply a bias that the framework's own commitments condemn? Separately: is the advice to concentrate rather than diversify compatible with the acknowledged size of the uncertainties, or does it require risk neutrality (and if it requires risk neutrality, is that a substantive moral commitment the practice is smuggling in)? Finally, tell me which feature is doing the real work in the practice, what would have to change in grantmaking if we swapped the counterfactual view for my friend's credit-share view (be concrete: which of the four habits above would survive and which would collapse), and what the sharpest objection is that my friend should make to my charge of double-counting. Please don't answer by saying that the three-axis framework is just a heuristic for expected value — I know the framework and I know that line; I want the commitments that ride along beneath it, and where the practice is running on two theories at once.
