I'm a junior in a Latin reading course, and for our final paper I tried to reconstruct the argument of Cicero's essay on old age as one continuous piece of reasoning rather than as a list of replies to four separate complaints. It came out looking valid, and I think it proves far too much, but I can't find the break.

(1) Cato grants the two complaints about the body — that old age brings weakness and that it puts an end to bodily pleasure — and denies that either one matters, on the ground that a human being's good lies in the exercise of reason, which age does not wear down and may even improve.
(2) Where a pleasure really is lost, he says a better one takes its place: conversation, reading, farming, and the authority that comes with having been trusted before.
(3) A person is made worse off by a change only if the change takes more from them than it gives; if what replaces the loss is better than what was lost, they are not worse off.
(4) So old age does not make a person worse off.
(5) But nothing in this turns on old age. Any loss can be brought under (3): every loss either comes with some compensating good — illness with the chance to show endurance, exile with leisure for study, bereavement with the sympathy of others — or can be described as taking away something external that was never essential to a rational life anyway.
(6) So nothing that can happen to anyone is bad for them: no illness, no exile, no grief, no death.
(7) And then the essay has no subject. There was never anything in old age for Cato to console Scipio and Laelius about, and Cicero, who was famously inconsolable when his own daughter died, plainly did not believe (6) himself.

My teacher accepts (1) and (4) without complaint and rejects (6), so she thinks the damage is in the middle somewhere, but I can't see which step to surrender. If I have to choose, I would rather keep (1) and (4) than keep (3), because (3) is the only part of the argument I can see that separates real misfortunes from imaginary ones. Which step is actually broken, why does it look fine when you read it quickly, and what does giving it up cost me — including what it costs Cicero, since the authority, deference, and public usefulness he praises in old men are themselves the sort of external goods that fortune can take away?
