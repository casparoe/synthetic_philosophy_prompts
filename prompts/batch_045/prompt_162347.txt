I run the weekly "Reason and Choice" seminar for first-year PPE students at a UK uni — 22 of them, 80 minutes, one seminar room with movable tables, a whiteboard, index cards, envelopes, and one PhD student who can help me collect and match things. I've got a session coming up on what people call rational irrationality, and I want them to *feel* why it could be rational to arrange, in advance, to be irrational later, rather than nod along while I explain it. The case I'm building around is the hitchhiker one: someone is stranded in the desert, a driver stops and says he'll drive them to town but only if they promise to hand over £100 on arrival, they promise, they get to town, and now — being safe — they have no reason whatsoever to pay, and the driver knew that when he stopped. Design me a runnable activity for this group that makes the force of that land in the room. Constraints: no real money (department rules), so payoffs have to be points that convert into something they mildly care about — I was going to give the top third first pick of the essay titles for the assessed essay, but tell me if that backfires. Also, they did the one-shot prisoner's dilemma two weeks ago and walked away with "just defect, it's dominant" ringing in their ears, and there are three economics-ish types who will instantly try to turn whatever I give them into a repeated game with reputation — so the design has to be one-shot and anonymous in a way they can *see* (numbered envelopes, codes I match privately, reshuffled pairings, only aggregate stats ever revealed), or the whole session collapses into "obviously pay, reputation."

Two wrinkles I'd like built into the rules rather than said afterwards, because I think they're where the interesting stuff is. First, a pre-play stage where hitchhikers can privately buy what I'm calling a "bond": an unconditional, non-refundable chunk of points, paid whether or not anyone ever picks them up, which takes the arrival decision out of their hands — if they bought it, the £100 gets paid for them. Costs and payoffs are yours to set, but the bond has to be bought *before* they know whether they'll be picked up, and it has to be genuinely binding. Second, a cheap-talk channel: each hitchhiker writes one sentence that their driver reads before deciding whether to stop, and a hitchhiker who did *not* buy the bond is permitted to say in that sentence that they did — the driver has no way to check. I want to see whether drivers trust the claim, what the un-bonded hitchhikers who claimed it then do, and whether writing the sentence down changes anything for them. I'd also like drivers, at the moment they decide to stop or not, to write down a private probability that their hitchhiker will pay, scored for accuracy and paid out at the end, so the debrief has something to work with about whether people anticipated their own future change of heart. And please build in a short, genuinely unrelated filler task between arrival and the payment decision — I want the "I'm safe now" feeling to be an actual gap in time, not a formality. Give me the full rules, materials, a payoff table, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 80 (including how long each stage takes), and how to run the reveal.

Then the debrief, which is where I most need help: map specific moments in what happened onto the parts of the idea — the ex ante rationality of the arrangement, the difference between tying yourself to a mast from outside and changing what you want on the inside, and the point that this isn't the prisoner's dilemma again (it's sequential and asymmetric, and the "defect" reflex doesn't transfer). Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I can forsee the econ lot trying to meta-game the bond, someone declining to play a promise-breaker on principle, everyone buying the bond so I get no variation, drivers stopping for everyone out of politeness, the lying-about-the-bond thing poisoning the atmosphere — and what to do about each. Also be honest about what the activity structurally cannot reproduce: my instinct is that splitting driver and hitchhiker across two people loses the single-person-across-time thing that the real puzzle is about, and that the activity shows what pays rather than what rationality requires, but tell me what else the facilitator simply has to say out loud because no set of rules will make them run into it. Written in British English please, and laid out so I can literally run it off the page.
