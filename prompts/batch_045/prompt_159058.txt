I'm a senior in high school and I help run a small after-school ethics discussion group, mostly with friends from my biology and debate classes. We got stuck on something that sounds narrow but has swallowed two of our sessions: whether insects have experiences that matter morally, and, more generally, whether sentience is the kind of thing that comes in degrees or whether there are cliffs somewhere along the line from a thermostat to a human being. My friend Dev is close to convinced: he thinks a fly's pain-like states count for something real for the fly's own sake, that there are staggering numbers of insects, and that this should change mundane choices (what foods we buy, whether we care about the ants on the sidewalk). I'm at roughly 0.45 on the claim that a typical adult insect has valenced experience of a kind that should carry non-negligible moral weight once you aggregate over the very large numbers involved; Dev is at about 0.85. I'd like your own number, but much more than the number I'd like the decomposition that produces it.

Here is roughly how I have been carving the question up, so that you can tell me what I have got wrong or left out. First, the integration-window question: whether a valenced state has to be temporally thick, held together by memory and anticipation into something sustained, in order to matter at all, or whether a bare, momentary, unremembered negative state is already enough. If there is a minimum thickness, insect nervous systems may only manage brief flickers, and I genuinely do not know whether a million flickers within one life, or one flicker each across a million lives, sum into anything. Second, the architecture question: whether the capacities insects have, such as associative learning and flexible trade-offs between competing motivations, are close enough to the thing we care about in vertebrates, or whether the gap is one of kind rather than degree. Third, what I have started calling the denominator question: when someone says an insect's welfare capacity is a tiny fraction of a human's, is that a claim about intensity per moment, about duration of life, or about the number and richness of things the creature can be said to care about? Those three give wildly different answers once you start adding creatures up. Fourth, the small-weights question: whether very small per-individual weights should be treated as rounding to zero for practical purposes, or whether enormous numbers of them dominate everything else. And fifth, the thermostat end of the continuum: Dev says a thermostat is obviously off the list because it has no unified subject and no perspective, but I want to know exactly what it is missing, because it does have a goal, a reading of the world, and error-correcting feedback, and I would like to know whether the property that excludes the thermostat is the same property that, on his own account, includes the fly.

So please: give me your credence on the claim as I have stated it, decomposed into the sub-questions you think actually carry it, with a rough number and one line of justification for each, then show how they combine and say which single term the final number is most sensitive to. Tell me which term you are least confident in and what one discovery, behavioural, neuroanatomical, or purely conceptual, would move it most. Say where on the continuum from thermostat to housefly to crow to human you think the cliff sits, if there is one, and whether 'where is the cliff' is even the right way to ask. And finally, locate our disagreement: at 0.45 and 0.85, which of those sub-questions is most likely where Dev and I actually differ, and which of us is more likely to be wrong about where we differ?
