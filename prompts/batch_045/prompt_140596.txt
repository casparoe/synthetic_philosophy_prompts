Nāgārjuna's Mūlamadhyamakakārikā (usually dated to around the second century CE) opens with a dedication verse that is notoriously compressed. In a widely used modern English translation, the verse reads:

"I salute the Fully Enlightened One, the best of orators, who taught the doctrine of dependent origination, according to which there is neither cessation nor origination, neither annihilation nor the eternal, neither singularity nor plurality, neither the coming nor the going [of any dharma, for the purpose of nirvāṇa characterized by] the auspicious cessation of hypostatization."

I am trying to understand how this list of eight negations is related to the four-cornered logic of the tetralemma (catuṣkoṭi: affirmation, denial, both, neither) and to the Madhyamaka claim that all phenomena are empty of intrinsic nature (svabhāva). In particular, I have been working with an interpretation I call the unbound-variable reading, which I will state so that you do not have to guess at it. On this reading, the paired negations in the verse are not a set of metaphysical theses about what does or does not exist; rather, they are meant to show that, when a dharma is correctly understood as dependently originated, questions such as "Does it arise? Does it cease? Is it permanent? Is it annihilated? Is it one? Is it many? Does it come? Does it go?" lose any well-defined truth-value, because the hidden assumption that the subject-term has an intrinsic nature—a determinate, independent essence that could serve as the bearer of those predicates—has been removed. The analogy is to a formal language in which an open sentence containing a free variable has no truth-value until the variable is bound; the auspicious cessation of hypostatization would then be the cessation of the automatic, default binding of phenomena to putative essences. Emptiness, on this way of talking, would not be a further property that phenomena have, but the absence of any such default binding.

Please help me understand whether this unbound-variable reading is a good way to read the verse, and more generally how the eight negations should be mapped onto the four lemmas of the tetralemma. I want a committed response, not a survey. Then do the following: (1) apply your chosen reading clause by clause to the verse's negations (cessation/origination, annihilation/eternal, singularity/plurality, coming/going) and say what each pair is denying and why; (2) explain how your reading avoids both the extreme of reification (treating emptiness or dependent origination as a real, independent nature) and the extreme of nihilism (treating the negations as denying that anything exists or that language can say anything); (3) state, in one or two sentences, the exact sense in which, on your reading, the tetralemma's four alternatives are neither accepted nor rejected as a fifth option; (4) give your confidence in your interpretation on a scale from 0 to 100, and identify the single strongest objection to it, together with how you would respond to that objection. If you think part of the difficulty is verbal or stems from an ambiguity in the terms cessation, hypostatization, or intrinsic nature, say exactly which part and why.
