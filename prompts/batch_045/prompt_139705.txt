I am teaching the last session of a graduate seminar on theories of well-being. The session is on the experience machine, and I would like to close it by reading a poem rather than by summarizing the dialectic. Please write that poem.

Write it as a villanelle — nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain — and make its two refrains the two sides of the exchange.

Refrain A is the offer, spoken in the voice of whoever keeps making it: one plain, end-stopped line about what you will not be able to tell from the inside. Refrain B is the refusal, in the same register and at the same pitch, and it must state what a life has to have if it is to count as good — not a prediction about how the plugged-in life would feel. Since a villanelle has only two rhyme sounds, choose them so that the offer's sound and the refusal's sound are audibly different in weight; the refrains themselves should not rhyme with each other.

Each of the five tercets presents a different machine, and the new version should arrive in that tercet's middle line, so that the poem's argument advances in the middle lines while the two refrains stay word for word the same. The five should form an escalation in which each new machine closes off one more way of stating the objection:

1. The standard case: a life you would choose, supplied whole, body maintained, and no suspicion from within.
2. The machine you can leave: you go in and come out, you remember both, and the stretch inside is a chapter you chose and can narrate — a real episode of a real life with real deciding at its edges.
3. The machine everyone enters: the people you love are in there with you and their regard for you is genuinely theirs; no one is left outside to be deceived or deprived; only the world is furnished, not the people.
4. The machine in which you actually act: you do the deeds, at cost, with your own effort and failure, and they are real doings by you of exactly the kind you would want — though nothing they do reaches past the machine.
5. A machine that never touches experience at all. You live your ordinary unremarked life; the device does nothing to your days; but once, if you ask, it will tell you truly whether the things you hope are so about your life are so — whether the work reached the one person it was made for, whether you were forgiven, whether anyone noticed. Nothing changes. You cannot act on it, repeat it, or tell it to anyone. Call it whatever you like; I think of it as the deposition machine.

That last one needs the most care, and it is where I want the poem to be genuinely in the weeds. If what the refuser wants is reality rather than experience, then here is reality and nothing else: no feeling, no change in the days, only a fact, given once, useless. Whether accepting it would vindicate the refuser or expose the refusal as a taste for a certain picture of oneself is exactly what the poem should leave undecided.

Each version should reach the reader as a concrete image or situation — a person being offered something again, in a new form, by someone patient — not as a numbered list of cases.

The closing quatrain should bring the two refrains together as adjacent lines for the first time and should settle nothing. By then the offer has been made five times and the refusal returned five times, and the reader should not be able to tell whether that final line is the deepest thing in the poem or the sound of someone who cannot be comforted. Do not tip it either way, and add no title, epigraph, or prose note afterward to explain it.

Constraints: no proper names — neither the philosopher's nor the thought experiment's label — and as little technical vocabulary as possible; no "hedonism," no "wirehead," no "utility," and no "simulation" if a concrete word will do. Someone who has never heard the case should be able to follow the poem as a poem; someone who has taught it should recognize all five variants and see that the refusal is being tested rather than asserted. Keep it loose iambic with exact rhymes, keep the refrains end-stopped, and keep the whole thing short enough to read aloud in under a minute and a half.
