I teach an upper-level undergrad epistemology seminar, 16 students, one 80-minute session. They've met the tracking account in its subjunctive form — you know p only if, were p true, you'd believe it, and were p false, you wouldn't — but we haven't done closure and I don't want to front-load it.

Design a game for the room where they run that test themselves and run into the fact that it doesn't survive deduction: they should end up somewhere they'd describe as knowing p, seeing in one step that p entails q, deducing q, and having their own rule tell them they don't know q. I want them to get there by filling in the two counterfactual blanks claim by claim rather than by me announcing it, and I want it to cost them something — chips or points, no real money — so they have to decide whether to stake on a claim they've validly derived.

Then a second round that's deliberately mundane: the nearest not-p world is just a look-alike (a fake, a duplicate, a switched label), so the same rule declares ignorance somewhere their gut says they plainly know. The session should land on the idea that the counterfactual test buys its verdict about deduction entirely by letting "the nearest world where it's false" do the work, and that that's also what makes it misfire — and should leave students choosing between two repairs (one that makes the belief track the fact, one that requires the belief not to be easily wrong) without me resolving it.

Give me materials and setup, rules, a scoring scheme, a minute-by-minute timeline, a debrief that maps particular moments onto the two subjunctive conditions and onto the deduction step, an honest list of what'll go wrong the first time I run it (I fully expect someone to say the skeptical world isn't close, someone to say "I derived it, so of course I know", and someone to try to sneak a look at the actual state), and a note on what the game can't stage and I'll have to say in so many words.
