Write a specification for an account of causation by omission. I am not yet asking which account is true — I want the requirements list first, so I have something to hold the candidates to.

Fix the purpose, so that adequacy has something to be relative to. The account is being bought to do three jobs at once: (i) to underwrite singular causal explanations in ordinary and specialist discourse, of both the agential sort (the nurse did not administer the anticoagulant; the driver did not brake) and the non-agential sort (the rains did not come; the firewall was never installed; the backup generator did not engage); (ii) to underwrite the causal-attribution step in legal and moral responsibility, where an omission is pinned on someone and treated as what they brought about; (iii) to plug into the variable-based causal models used in epidemiology, engineering failure analysis and accident investigation. An account that works for one of these and fails the others is not adequate for my purposes, and part of what I want from you is a view on whether that triple mandate is itself coherent, or whether jobs (ii) and (iii) pull in opposite directions — the lawyer wants the causation to track duties and expectations, the modeler wants it to be duty-free difference-making.

The deliverable: a numbered list of requirements — the phenomena an account must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must respect, and the results or practices it must not contradict — each with the case or consideration that motivates it, stated tightly enough that I could use it as a checklist. For each requirement say whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp, and why. Flag any subset of the requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which one you would weaken or drop and what that costs. Then name the single requirement that does the most work in excluding otherwise-attractive views, and defend that choice.

Here are the materials I already think matter. Organize them, prune the ones you think are fake, and add what is missing:

- Selection. Every non-actor is a difference-maker in the same formal way: had the Queen watered the plant, it would have lived, just as it would have had the gardener watered it. Only one of them gets cited. So the account owes a filter — and it owes a story about whether the filter is semantic, pragmatic, or normative. If it is pragmatic, say plainly whether that demotes the whole topic to a footnote about language use.
- Agency-independence. The rains that did not come and the switch that was never thrown have to be handled by the same machinery as the nurse and the driver. Agency cannot be built in at ground level. But agent omissions dominate ordinary and legal practice; is that a fact about norms, or about the metaphysics?
- Chain structure. Omissions must be able to sit at intermediate nodes: the supervisor never trained the guard, the guard never threw the switch, the backup never engaged, the patient died. Does causal strength attenuate as a chain alternates between action and omission? Can there be second-order omissions all the way down, and if not, where is the cutoff?
- Preemption and overdetermination among omissions. Two guards asleep, neither of whom would have raised the alarm had the other stirred; and the variant where one of the two had a duty to act and the other did not. Both guards, one guard, or neither?
- Grain and location. The plant died because she did not water it — or because she did not water it on Tuesday, or because she did not water the fern. And where is the omission located: at the plant, at the gardener, or nowhere? An account that wants to mesh with anything spatiotemporal has to answer.
- Timing. A season-long failure to irrigate versus an instant failure to brake. When does the causal work happen — at the last moment at which acting would still have made a difference, or spread across the whole interval?
- The intervention asymmetry. On an interventionist treatment one can test what would have happened had the agent acted, but there is no intervention that sets a variable to the omissive value it already has: the counterfactual runs in one direction only. Is that a defect of interventionism, a feature, or a reason to deny that omissions are causes strictly speaking?
- The deflation worry. "Her failure to feed the child caused the death" and "had she fed the child, he would not have died" seem to have no distinct observable consequences. If the account cannot say what would settle the difference between them, is the stronger claim contentful?
- Univocality versus mechanism. Does "caused" mean the same thing in "the brake failure caused the crash" and "the failure to brake caused the crash," given that the second has no connecting process, no conserved quantity, no transmission? Which of these two demands is a genuine desideratum and which is a prejudice of one camp?
- Ontology. Are there negative events, or only positive events plus facts and absences? The account must say what makes "the failure to feed the child caused her death" true if nothing in the world corresponds to the failure — and whether that makes the failure a cause, or merely the correct answer to a why-question.
- Doing/allowing, and blame. The account must not collapse causation by omission into allowing, or into blameworthiness, but it must explain the very tight correlation between them.
- Norm-sensitivity. People are far more willing to cite an omission where there was a duty, a rule, or a statistical default. Must the account build that sensitivity into causation itself, or may it hand the work to a separate theory of responsibility and conversational relevance?
- Defaults. The alarm did not sound gets cited; the Sun did not explode does not. Is the background default causal, statistical, or normative — and who or what sets it?
- Contrast-hunger. "The plant died because you did not water it" is fine until one asks "rather than what?" Omissions look peculiarly dependent on an implicit contrast. Is that special to omissions, or general to causal selection?
- The fundamental-physics worry. Causal claims in fundamental physics seem never to cite omissions. Must an account secure a place for them there, or only in the special sciences and ordinary life?
- Empirical constraint. There is a developmental and experimental literature on when people will and will not attribute causation to an omission. What, if anything, does a metaphysical account owe to it? I expect you to mark this one as negotiable if you think it is.

Two things I especially want. First: I suspect the real fault line is between a requirement that causation be objective and interest-independent and a requirement that the selection among omissions track norms, duties, or defaults — set out that conflict as cleanly as you can, and say which side you would give up. Second: tell me whether, once a decent filter is in place, there is anything left that is distinctive about omissions, or whether "the problem of omissions" dissolves into the general problem of causal selection and was never a separate problem at all.

If the usual taxonomy — act versus omission, prevention versus double prevention, event versus fact versus absence — is missing a distinction your spec needs, draw it, name it, and say what case forces it.

Finish with a short scorecard, three rows only: counterfactual or difference-making accounts; process, mechanism, or conserved-quantity accounts; and views on which omission claims are perfectly true but not causal at all. Keep the weight on the criteria rather than on the scoring.
