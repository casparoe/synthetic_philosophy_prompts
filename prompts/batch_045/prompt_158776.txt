I teach a second-year undergraduate module on existentialism, and for the final seminar I want to set aside the papers for a week and read a novel through the lens of Sartre's account of radical freedom and bad faith. I have settled on Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day (the 1989 Booker winner; we have the 1993 film as well, but I would rather the discussion stay with the book). The obvious line of approach is to treat Stevens as a nearly perfect specimen of mauvaise foi: a man who has spent a lifetime treating the obligations of his office as if they were features of the world rather than choices he renews every morning, who claims not to have opinions of his own because a great butler does not, and who keeps a vocabulary of dignity and professionalism on hand precisely so that he never has to admit that he elected to be the man who misses his father's death upstairs and who stays in the pantry while Miss Kenton waits for him to say something about her marriage proposal. I would like that reading done properly, and I would like it anchored to particular scenes rather than floated as a general characterization: the dismissal of the two Jewish housemaids, the night of the conference, the proposal, the visit to Mrs Benn in Cornwall, and the closing conversation on the seafront with the stranger who tells him that the evening is the best part of the day, after which Stevens resolves to practise bantering for his American employer.

I also want the seminar to get past the obvious version, and I have two complications in mind. The first is an idea a colleague floated and that I cannot find a canonical version of, so I am interested in what your philosophical judgment makes of it: she distinguishes occurrent bad faith, the local act of hiding a particular freedom from oneself, from what she calls distributed bad faith, a standing arrangement in which an entire household, trade and class collaborate to hold the split steady, so that no single agent has to perform the deception alone and no single moment of sincerity is available to anyone inside it. Stevens seems to me to be a case of the second kind if it is coherent at all, and it is not obvious to me that it is coherent on Sartre's own terms: if bad faith is a project of a single for-itself playing at being in-itself, then a social arrangement cannot literally be in bad faith, and yet the later Sartre's notion of the practico-inert looks like an attempt to say something very close to it. Does the novel push a Sartrean toward that extension, or does it just show how much Sartre's ontology loses when the unit of analysis becomes a household rather than a consciousness? The second complication runs the other way: one of my students argued last year that the Sartrean lens is simply parochial here, that Stevens has a self constituted by his role and his service, and that calling that self-deception smuggles in a modern, individualist picture of what a person is. I want to know whether that objection is fair to Sartre, whether it is fair to Stevens, and whether the novel gives us any reason to decide between them.

Above all I want a reading that is faithful in both directions and willing to say where it stops working. Please tell me which scenes genuinely reward the Sartrean vocabulary of facticity, transcendence and the spirit of seriousness, and where the vocabulary flattens something the novel is doing better than the philosophy does: Stevens' grief, his class position, the fact that his heroism and his servility are the same trait, and the way the first-person narration puts the reader in an odd position, since we are at once the person Stevens is deceiving and the person he is confessing to. I would also like your view on the ending: is the final chapter a moment of authenticity, the re-appropriation of a situation he had been pretending was a fate, or is it a fresh act of bad faith that simply gives him a new role to hide inside? And I want an honest verdict on whether the book adds anything to the debate about bad faith that the arguments in Being and Nothingness do not, or whether it merely illustrates them more movingly.
