**The money pump doesn't show what it's usually said to show**

Setup: my preferences over three holiday destinations cycle — I prefer Rome to Oslo, Oslo to Kyoto, and Kyoto to Rome. A travel agent who knows this shows up while I'm holding an Oslo ticket and offers me swaps, one euro each: Oslo→Rome, then Rome→Kyoto, then Kyoto→Oslo. Every swap moves me to a destination I like better, and a euro is small, so I take all three and end up back where I started, three euros poorer. Repeat until I'm broke. Usual moral: cyclic preferences are irrational, because they turn you into a money pump.

I think the argument is doing something different from what it looks like it's doing.

*Step 1: the pump needs a fragmented agent.* Note that the "until I'm broke" version needs the tree to be offered over and over, and it needs me to re-decide at each node by comparing only the two options in front of me, without noticing that I'll do exactly the same thing at the next node. But suppose I'm a planner: I see the whole tree coming, I rank complete plans (refuse at the first node / swap once then stop / swap twice then stop / do the whole loop), pick one, and stick to it. Then the worst that can happen to me is one pass through some particular tree — a bounded loss that I could also avoid entirely by committing in advance, or by handing my wallet to a friend before the agent shows up. So the standard argument doesn't show that my preferences are defective; it shows that my preferences *plus* node-by-node re-deciding *plus* no commitment are expensive. That's a claim about a package, not obviously about rationality.

*Step 2: the obvious reply, which I think half works.* The reply is that commitment doesn't help me, because for cyclic preferences the ranking over plans cycles too. Rank plans by where they leave me: Rome-minus-1 beats Oslo-for-free (I prefer Rome to Oslo), Kyoto-minus-2 beats Rome-minus-1, Oslo-minus-3 beats Kyoto-minus-2 — as long as each of my preference gaps is worth more than a euro. But Oslo-for-free beats Oslo-minus-3, just by dominance. So there's a cycle over the plans as well: every plan I could commit to is beaten by another one. The planner has to pick arbitrarily, and if they pick in any random-ish way, or if the agent offers me the same tree again next week and I don't perfectly coordinate with myself, I bleed money in expectation.

This is the strongest version, but look what it quietly assumes: (a) that every one of my preference gaps is worth *some* positive amount of money, so the bribes can be shrunk until they never flip a comparison — i.e. no infinitesimally small gaps, something Archimedean-ish; and (b) that my tie-breaking among the plans (all of which are "not best") isn't perfectly stable, since a planner who always breaks the tie the same way — by refusing at the first node — never loses a cent.

*Step 3: so what's actually left?* Here's the bit I'd build the post around: what the pump really shows isn't "you'll end up poorer" (Step 1 shows that's avoidable) but "your preferences don't settle anything". Over the plans in the pump there's nothing your own preferences single out as best, so whatever you do, you do something your preferences don't endorse — and once money is attached, an arbitrary pick among non-optimal plans costs you in expectation. That's a failure to be a guide to action rather than a failure to be prudent. And the euros are basically decoration: even if every swap were free, the endpoints of the four plans still cycle, so there still wouldn't be a best plan. (Side thought: this is why a cyclic person might rationally pay to have transitive preferences installed even if they never lose money — deliberation never converges.)

*Step 4: where I'm least sure.* The same style of argument is meant to support the independence axiom, not just transitivity. But there the case looks structurally different. In the usual compound-choice setup — you go for the safe-ish option in one comparison and the gamble in another, and the travel agent arranges things so that after an interim stage you want to swap back and pay for the privilege — my preferences *do* single out a best plan up front. There's no cycle over plans. So the Step 3 reading can't be what's biting there; the bite looks more like "the plan you want now is one you'll want to abandon later." Which leaves me unsure: are transitivity and independence just being supported by two different arguments that happen to share the name "money pump," or have I misdescribed what the pump does in the transitivity case?
