My reading group is crawling through al-Ghazali's Incoherence of the Philosophers, the part where he argues that the fire touching the cotton doesnt produce the burning — God creates the contact and creates the burning, and what we call causation is just habit. One person in the group keeps explaining it with a flipbook analogy: reality is a stack of frames that God draws one after another, nothing in frame 1 brings about frame 2, a law of nature is just the fact that he draws them in a stable order, and a miracle is simply a frame where he draws something unusual. They then use it to conclude occasionalism is empirically indistinguishable from believing in real secondary causes — science goes on exactly as before, so the view costs nothing.

I think the analogy is doing a lot of quiet work, so please audit it. Lay out the correspondences explicitly (frame → what, the artist → what, the order of frames → what, a spliced-in frame → what), then the places it breaks. The three Im stuck on: a single frame already contains a whole spatial arrangement with apparent causal processes running inside it, so the analogy may smuggle back what it was supposed to remove; a film needs a strip or a reel, something persisting between frames, which occasionalism tied to atomism probably cant allow; and the artists drawing is itself one more event in time, so causation may just be relocated rather than eliminated. Which of these bear on the conclusion they want and which are harmless? Does the costs-nothing conclusion survive — does it hold only for the weak reading (no necessary connection) and fail for the strong one (creatures have no causal power), especially once you ask what Gods habit is even supposed to be on this picture? Bonus: does the flipbook make miracles too cheap? And if it's a bad analogy, what's a better one.
