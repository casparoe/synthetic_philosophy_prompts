I've spent the last two years as a research assistant for my doctoral supervisor, who has been slowly writing a book on dirty hands in politics, and I'm now assembling a casebook of the hypotheticals he has ruled on across seminars, referee reports and conference Q&As. I keep his verdicts in a spreadsheet and I'd like to predict what he'll say about a new case a colleague wants to contribute, since the casebook deadline is in three weeks and she needs to know whether her case will get a "dirty hands" tag or a "something else entirely" tag. The verdicts I'm confident about are these: (1) a security chief who authorises the torture of a detainee to locate a bomb that will otherwise kill hundreds, with no other route to the information, who reports herself the next day, resigns and serves a sentence — dirty hands, and, separately, still guilty, "she should be punished and she should have done it"; (2) a finance minister who knowingly breaches a campaign-finance statute to get emergency relief payments out within the week, announces exactly what he did that afternoon, asks parliament for retroactive indemnity and steps down — dirty hands, though he called it "the boring end of the spectrum"; (3) a wartime leader who orders an unrecorded strike on a civilian target to shorten the war while her own advisers had an embargo option on the table that would very likely have worked within a year, and who denied the order when the records surfaced decades later — not dirty hands, "an atrocity with an excuse stapled to it"; (4) a mayor who authorises a rescue during a prison riot that will foreseeably kill two hostages rather than all forty, testifies to the inquiry, asks to be charged, is not charged, and stays in office at the council's request — dirty hands; (5) a civil servant who leaks classified documents exposing an illegal surveillance programme, breaking his oath and the law, and serves two years rather than name his source — not dirty hands, "conscientious lawbreaking; he was repudiating his office's instructions, not discharging them"; (6) a mayor who takes a bribe from a developer and spends it on a clinic in the poorest ward, saying on arrest that the end justified the means — not dirty hands, "he didn't dirty his hands, he sold them; the wrong wasn't the price of doing the public's business, it was the price of not raising taxes"; (7) a governor who quietly reallocates vaccine doses from a rival's district to her own district's hotspots just before an election, honestly believing her outbreak is the worse one, in a reallocation that saves more lives overall than it costs — not dirty hands, "mixed motives poison it."

The new case is this. During a pandemic, the director of a regional health authority, given a modelling report saying that panic-driven crowding would kill far more people than the virus, orders two things she has no legal authority to order, and keeps both secret for six years: the public dashboard is fed false ventilator-availability figures so that the worst-hit hospitals are not swamped by self-referrals, and an undisclosed triage protocol gives priority to patients with more life-years remaining. The panic never materialises — critics later say it was never going to — and no one can now establish whether the deception saved any lives. Her private diary from the period is full of anguish about lying to the public. When the internal emails are leaked, she declines to apologise, says the policy was right, says she would do it again, and notes that she never profited personally and gave up two years of her life to the job.

I can think of roughly three rules that fit all seven verdicts, but I can't tell which he's running: one keyed to necessity, official role and public-regarding motive; one adding the condition that the agent own the wrong afterwards rather than vindicate herself; one adding the condition that the wrong be avowable in the end to the public in whose name it was done. I also can't tell whether the guilt condition is about inner torment or about actually paying a price, or how he handles necessity when it fails only in hindsight — I have a note, which may be wrong, that he once said necessity is judged from the agent's epistemic position rather than with hindsight. I can't reach the library until term starts, so please work from the track record rather than from anything published. Tell me what you take his implicit rule to be, what each of the plausible rules predicts about the new case, your best guess at his verdict with the reasons for preferring it, and which single further variant would best discriminate the rules — for instance whether it matters if she had published the protocol at the time and asked to be charged. Two further wrinkles I'd value your judgement on, since he has never ruled on either: an official who arranges the wrongful act through intermediaries who are not told the rationale, keeping deniability for herself, so that the "dirt" is, so to speak, delegated rather than touched; and an official who announces the wrongful act in advance, in public, and invites prosecution before carrying it out, so that the guilt is borne prospectively rather than retrospectively. Finally, if the new case isn't dirty hands at all, which of his other boxes does it fall into?
