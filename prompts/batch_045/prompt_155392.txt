I am preparing a two-page handout for a graduate seminar in environmental ethics on whether habitat destruction reduces the total amount of suffering experienced by wild animals. The seminar has stalled because participants keep discovering that they are answering different questions: some are weighing the animals killed during the clearing itself, some the animals who would have been born on the land afterwards, some the average welfare of the survivors, some only the intensity of suffering rather than the number of sufferers. Rather than commission another prose survey, I want a classification instrument that separates these commitments and shows where each one lands on the sign of the net welfare effect.

Please build it as a decision tree, constructed in two stages. First, lay out the branching questions themselves — seven to nine of them, generated from the logical space of the issue rather than from a list of named views — and state, for each, the range of answers it permits. Then locate the actual positions at the leaves, eight to twelve of them, naming each where a name exists and writing "unnamed" where it does not. For any combination of answers that the space allows but that nobody in fact occupies, mark it as empty and say in one sentence whether it is empty because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because it is unexplored.

Constraints on the nodes:

1. Every node must be phrased as a question a student could answer without already knowing which position it leads to. No node may take the form "do you value nature?", "are you a total utilitarian?", or anything else that merely asks for a label; the instrument should sort readers by what they say about cases rather than by what they call themselves.

2. At least two nodes must be empirical rather than axiological — about what conversion actually does to population size, to the taxonomic and body-size composition of the sentient biomass, and to how the animals in question die — since in practice this is where most of the disagreement about the sign lives. One such node should force the distinction between the pulse of harm during the clearing event and the steady-state welfare flow on the converted land afterwards. Another should force a choice of counterfactual land use, since conversion to pavement, to intensive monoculture, or to irrigated pasture and other anthropogenic habitats that favour synanthropic generalists can pull the answer in opposite directions.

3. One node must concern the currency of aggregation: number of individuals, welfare-range-weighted individuals, average welfare per life, or suffering only. Make the welfare-range weighting do real work here, because conversion typically shifts composition toward smaller-bodied, more fecund taxa, and the verdict therefore depends on whether those taxa are assigned non-negligible welfare ranges.

4. One node must separate views on whether the animals never born because the habitat is gone count as a loss at all, and one must separate views on whether death itself is a harm over and above the suffering of the dying. These two together should be what prises person-affecting and prior-existence style views away from impersonal total views.

5. One node should address displacement and leakage — whether the animals that would have lived on the site die earlier, are killed in the clearing, or relocate and reproduce elsewhere — and the tree should be explicit that the sign of the net effect can differ between these cases, including the case in which prevention of future births dominates everything else.

For each leaf, give the name or "unnamed", one sentence stating the position's verdict on the sign of the net welfare effect for a representative episode of habitat conversion, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment — the thing a holder has to swallow, for instance that the reasoning generalises to favouring less wild nature generally, or that the mass death of sentient animals during clearing is not itself a moral cost.

Finally, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot really separate rather than papering over the overlap, and add a short closing note addressed to the seminar leader identifying which single node you expect a class to actually disagree at, and why that node rather than the axiological ones. Format the whole thing as an indented tree plus a compact table of nodes and their permitted answers, sized to print on two sides of one sheet.
