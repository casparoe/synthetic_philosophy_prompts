I'm writing a novel about a conspiracy against a tyrant in a city that's sort of late-medieval-but-not-anywhere-real, and I've been reading Aquinas on tyrannicide — the commentary on the Sentences, where as far as I can tell he distinguishes the ruler with no title at all from the lawful one who governs rottenly, and is much more relaxed about the first. It felt tidy until I started listing who I actually want my readers to cheer for.

My gut verdicts:

- The captain who took the citadel by force and rules with no claim whatsoever, knifed by an ordinary citizen: fine.
- A different captain, also seized power by force, but rules well, fair taxes, keeps the peace — killed in order to restore the hereditary heir, who is a wastrel: not fine.
- A properly crowned and anointed king who has turned genuinely cruel: I flinch away from killing him and I don't trust the flinch. Genuinely don't know.
- A demagogue the majority adores, who's abolished the elections and is grinding down one minority: fine, I think, although most of the city would call it murder.
- The chancellor who actually runs the prisons but seized nothing himself: fine, yet it feels like a different kind of act — nearer war than punishment.
- Killing when everyone knows the successor will be worse and there'll be a five-year famine: not fine.
- Killing him out of pure private revenge, which happens also to free the city: fine as an act, but I don't want to admire the person.
- Killing him when he's visibly dying and will be gone in a month: not fine — and not because it's wrong, because it's something else.

Is there definately a principle that gets these, or gets close? Which of my verdicts does it force me to drop, and which of them would Aquinas's own version have to give up on? And what does it say about the case I keep dodging: my protagonist turns out to be the tyrant's daughter, and he has always been kind to her.
