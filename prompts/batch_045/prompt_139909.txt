I teach political philosophy, and this term I am co-convening a small interdisciplinary reading group with a social psychologist who works on cross-cultural variation in the strength of norms: the now-familiar contrast between "tight" societies, where norms are dense, unambiguous and strictly enforced, and "loose" ones, where they are thinner and deviance is tolerated, together with her working hypothesis that a society's position on that spectrum tracks ecological and institutional threat. Last week the group came apart over a case we could not resolve, and I want to hand the members something better than the exchange we actually had.

The case: one of our number is about to spend a semester at a university in a country whose academic and everyday norms are considerably stricter than ours — rigid forms of address, dress expectations, deference to seniority, tighter limits on what may be said in a seminar, and a strong presumption against contradicting a senior colleague in public. She said she intended to go on speaking and dressing more or less as she does here, and a colleague replied, with the air of closing the matter, that "when in Rome, do as the Romans do." I think that proverb was being made to carry a conclusion it does not obviously bear — that she is required, and not merely permitted, to conform, and required irrespective of whether she judges the local norms good — but I could not say in the room what premise would have to be added to get there, and I suspect the sentence is really several theses of very different weight stacked on top of one another.

What I would like from you is a disambiguation, arranged as a menu I can put in front of the group, of the distinct precise claims the proverb might be expressing, ordered from the near-trivial to the genuinely radical, with a verdict on each: which is a platitude, which is substantive and defensible, which is contentious, and which is straightforwardly false. I have some candidate readings in mind and would be glad to have them sharpened or replaced — conformity as mere compliance with expected sanctions; conformity as uptake of a coordination convention, where an act simply fails to mean what the agent intends except against local conventions; conformity as epistemic deference to people who know local conditions and consequences better than a visitor does; conformity as acknowledgment of the authority or legitimacy of a normative order as such; and conformity as an expressive gesture of respect owed to a community one is joining as a guest. For each, I would want to know whether it yields only a prudential or etiquette-based reason, or something that can be called an obligation, and whether it would actually support the use to which the proverb was put. Three complications should be addressed as well, since they are where the argument seemed to me to bite. First, the self-application problem: in a genuinely permissive culture the operative local norm may be tolerance of deviance, so a proverb telling one to do as the locals do appears there to require nonconformity — and I want to know whether that is a fatal instability or merely a sign that the proverb has an implicit parameter for what kind of norm is in play. Second, the "which Rome" problem: whose norms count when a society is internally divided, and in particular whether there is an asymmetry whereby deviation registers strongly with the strict while conformity registers hardly at all with the permissive, so that the practical grain of the proverb always drifts toward its strictest available reading. Third, the abhorrent-Rome problem: whether any of the readings survives when what Rome requires is something our colleague takes to be unjust — for example staying silent while a senior colleague asserts something she believes to be false, or observing a gendered norm she regards as demeaning — and whether the proverb's plausibility in ordinary cases depends on a suppressed assumption that the local norms are not seriously unjust, or at least not more unjust than the alternatives. Finally, tell me which of these readings you take people to have in mind when they deploy the line in order to end an argument, and whether that reading is one they should be willing to defend.
