You are a professional philosopher whose research is in the theory of reasons and in the particularism/generalism debate in metaethics. I need a piece of original case-design work, and I want you to think carefully rather than reach for the first example that comes to mind. Before you settle on anything, spend a few paragraphs brainstorming four or five candidate settings and candidate feature-pairs, testing each against the constraints below; tell me briefly which candidates you discarded and why, and only then present the finished design. I want the rejected ones reported, not silently dropped.

What the case is for. I am building it for a graduate seminar on whether holism about reasons really forces particularism. The dialectical target is a generalist who has already conceded more than the caricature does: she grants that the weight of a consideration varies with context, and she grants that considerations can be defeated by others. What she denies is that a consideration's status as a reason, or its direction, can vary. On her view every consideration that ever favours an action contributes pro tanto weight in every context in which it is present, and the true principles can be stated with finitely many hedges, each of which is motivated independently of the cases it is used to save. So I need a case that beats that opponent. If the case only shows weight-variation, it has failed, and I would rather you tell me that than quietly ship me a case that begs the question.

Background stipulations, so that we do not talk past each other. By holism about reasons I mean the thesis that whether a given consideration is a reason for a given action can depend on what other considerations are present. By silencing, as opposed to merely outweighing, I mean this: a consideration that would elsewhere favour the action is present but contributes nothing at all to the deliberative accounting in this context, not even a weakened reason, and leaves no residue of the form I had some reason to do it, just not enough. The particularist claims that the valence or existence of reasons is context-dependent in this strong sense; the generalist claims that only their weight is, and that everything else can be captured by hedged principles.

The design I want.

1. Setting. One realistic setting from ordinary professional or institutional life: an office, a clinic's rota, a school, an editorial meeting, a lab, a committee. Nothing exotic or science-fictional. The most that may be at stake is embarrassment, a small sum of money, a missed deadline, or a minor unfairness. No one's life, health, or livelihood may hang on the decision, and the case may not turn on a promise, a lie, a confidence, or a threat. I want the work done by structure rather than by stakes.

2. Structure. One agent, one action-type phi, and two descriptive features F and G. Build four variants in the same setting:
   - A: F present, G absent, so that the agent ought to phi.
   - B: F absent, G present, so that the agent ought not to phi.
   - C: both present, so that the agent ought not to phi, and F is not merely outweighed but silenced: it contributes nothing to the accounting, not a weakened reason.
   - C-plus: C with substantially more F, whether more instances of the same kind of fact or stronger instances of it, so that the agent still ought not to phi and still nothing changes: no residue, no sense that piling on F any further would eventually tip the balance.
   The contrast between C and C-plus is the saturation test. If F were merely outweighed in C, then by C-plus it should have tipped the verdict or at least made the balance noticeably closer; that it makes no difference at all is what is supposed to distinguish silencing from steep but finite weight-variation.

3. Vocabulary. F and G must be statable in plainly non-moral, non-evaluative terms. Anything of the form she had promised, it would be dishonest, he was being cruel is disqualified, because the whole point is to see whether valence-variation can be generated from features carrying no moral charge. If you find that you cannot state a candidate feature without moral vocabulary, say so and discard the candidate.

4. No stipulating verdicts. The verdicts in A through C-plus must be the ones the theories deliver, not stipulations dressed up as cases. Where you are relying on a considered intuition about the case rather than on something the theory entails, mark that explicitly and keep it separate from what follows from the theory.

After the scenario:

(a) Walk through what the particularist says about each variant and what the conceding generalist says, and show exactly where they come apart. I expect the divergence to be located in the C to C-plus contrast; show whether that is right and whether A through C on their own would already be enough.

(b) Take the three standard generalist repairs in turn and say, for each, whether it survives this case and what it costs: additive weights plus a ceteris paribus or unless clause; redescribing the reason so that its consequent is more specific, so that the principle is never really tested, as in a reason to phi later, or gently, or in writing; and reclassifying the silencing as merely very steep weight-variation. If the first or second repair can be made to work here, say so. I would rather have an honest result than a rigged one, so do not suppress a repair that succeeds merely because it succeeds.

(c) Name the single feature of the design that is doing the work, and tell me what happens to the case if it is removed. My own guess is that the saturation move is load-bearing and that without it the case collapses into the weight-holism the generalist has already granted; confirm that or correct it.

(d) Give the strongest attack a defender of the generalist would actually make on the case, not a dialectically feeble one. Include, if it applies, the charge that arguments from deliberative phenomenology or from the absence of residue are not evidence about reasons at all, and say how the particularist should answer it.

(e) An honest audit of the case itself: which of the four verdicts are you least confident about, and where does the case lean on an intuition you would not defend under pressure?

Finally, and this matters for how I use it: state, claim by claim, how confident you are in the substantive assertions you have made, using some plain scale such as confident, reasonably confident, guessing. Do this for the verdicts, for the claim that the generalist repairs fail, and for the claim about which feature is load-bearing. Then single out the one point you are least sure of, with a sentence on what would change your mind about it.

Keep the scenario itself, A through C-plus together, to roughly a single paragraph. The analysis may be as long as it needs to be, but do not let it sprawl.
