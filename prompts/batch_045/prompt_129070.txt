I direct a graduate seminar on institutional design and non-ideal theory, and for the last two terms the same argument has swallowed most of our meeting time: two of my best doctoral students — the Exporter and the Localist — have been going at each other over whether the Nordic model is portable. The Exporter holds that what does the work is a specifiable institutional package: universal rather than means-tested provision, a broad-based tax that everyone visibly pays, union-linked unemployment insurance, wage floors set through collective bargaining rather than statute, easy hiring and firing paired with real income security and active labour-market policy, and a state that delivers services well enough that people are willing to fund them. On her view these are design features, not folkways, and the reason they have not been copied is political failure of will, not any deep cultural precondition. The Localist holds that the package is the visible surface of something much older and much less transferable: a small, linguistically and religiously homogeneous population, a long inheritance of state capacity built before mass democracy, a dense associational life that gave unions their organisational muscle, and a reservoir of generalised trust that lets strangers pay taxes for strangers' children without needing to monitor them. On his view you can adopt the institutions and get the costs without the compliance. I want them to stop performing for each other and produce the document an adversarial collaboration would actually produce, and I would like you to draft it for me as a model of what I am asking for.

Please give me: first, a numbered section of agreed claims — substantive ones, not platitudes — covering at least what is to be explained (high employment, low disposable-income inequality, high revenue collection with low visible evasion, high self-reported trust and life satisfaction), what the candidate mechanisms are, what would count as a failure of an attempted transfer, and which popular claims about the Nordics both parties concede are unsupported. Second, a disagreements table: for each disputed point, the Exporter's formulation in the words she would sign and the Localist's formulation in the words he would sign, neither of them a straw version, plus your own judgement in a separate column of whether that row is a genuine disagreement or a merely verbal one — I suspect several rows dissolve once we specify whether 'trust' means survey-reported trust or revealed tax compliance, whether 'export' means the whole package or a component, and whether 'the Nordic model' denotes one thing when Norway has oil and Finland has a different wage-bargaining architecture. Third, for each surviving crux, the concrete case, natural experiment, or argument both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance so that neither can reconstruct their expectations afterwards. I have my own list of candidate cruxes, which you should feel free to reject or reorder: whether universal institutions manufacture trust or merely require it (call it the trust ratchet — universal provision builds the trust that then sustains the taxes that fund it, so the arrangement is self-reinforcing once running and very hard to start); whether the components travel independently or only as a bundle, and if independently, in what order (the sequencing hypothesis — a universal child benefit financed by a broad-based consumption tax first, labour-market reform later, rather than the reverse); whether a large informal sector and weak tax administration make the whole thing infeasible or simply make it slower and more expensive; whether ethnic and linguistic heterogeneity raises the cost of sustaining universalism, as the Localist thinks, or whether that claim smuggles in exactly the cultural essentialism the Exporter objects to; and, the one I care most about, whether feasibility constrains prescription at all here — if a population would presently reject and partly evade these institutions, is it still true that they ought to have them, or does that depend on whether the feasibility constraint is read as fixed or as something institutions can bootstrap their way out of?

To keep the cruxes from floating, anchor them in the case we have been using all term: a fictional middle-income country I call Meridia, with roughly 40 per cent of employment informal, tax revenue near a fifth of GDP, a commodity export boom that will last maybe a decade, a large emigrant diaspora that sends remittances home, a tax administration that is honest but thin, and a recent history of means-tested transfers that were captured by local brokers and are now widely despised. Meridia's finance ministry has asked, in effect, for a sequencing decision: which one instrument would you install first, if you had to, and what would you measure ten years later to find out whether it worked? Both parties should be made to answer that in their own terms, and to say in advance what result would make them concede. Please do not supply citations, data tables, or a reading list — I want the argument, and I want the empirical background stipulated between the parties rather than sourced, since we are testing whether the dispute is resolvable in principle before anyone looks anything up. Aim for something my two students could put their names to without either feeling caricatured; if part of their dispute is verbal, say plainly which part and why, and if a crux turns out to be undecidable with the evidence both accept, say that rather than inventing a tiebreaker.
