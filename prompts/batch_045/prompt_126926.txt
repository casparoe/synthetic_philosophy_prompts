I'm putting together a two-page "where I currently stand" handout for a graduate reading group I run on disagreement and higher-order evidence — the group is about half steadfasters and I don't want to get steamrolled in the first ten minutes — and I'd like someone to audit it before I hand it out. I've tried to write down only things I actually believe rather than things that sound defensible in a seminar room. Here's the set, roughly in order of how confident I am in each:

1. Conciliationism, moderately stated: if someone I antecedently count as my epistemic peer, working from the same evidence, lands somewhere different from me, that fact is itself a reason to move toward them — not merely a prompt to go back and re-examine the first-order evidence. And I want this to apply at the meta level too: the fact that plenty of people I respect steadfastly refuse to conciliate is itself some evidence against conciliationism, and I'm fine with that pushing me toward a milder version of it.
2. Permissivism, also moderate: for a good chunk of ordinary messy bodies of evidence there's a range of attitudes it's rational to take. Two careful people with the same evidence can rationally end up in different places, and neither is making a mistake.
3. Higher-order evidence has no independent force: it never hands me a new first-order reason about the subject matter. All it can do is change how I weigh the first-order evidence I already had. I'm suspicious of "weight of evidence" talk as anything more than a relabelling of this.
4. No epistemic akrasia: it's irrational to believe that p while simultaneously thinking that p isn't what my evidence supports, or that believing p is the wrong response to it.
5. Case verdict — global impairment: I've taken a drug I know makes me bad at weighing evidence, or I learn I did a stretch of reasoning on no sleep at all. The right response, I think, is to damp my confidence across the board in the verdicts I arrived at while impaired — not to try to go back and re-weigh the first-order evidence, since re-weighing it is exactly the thing I can no longer do well.
6. Case verdict — self-trust: my own belief that p, once I've formed it, is not an extra bit of evidence for p that I get to add to the pile. Other people's beliefs can be evidence for me; mine, in my own case, aren't.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture, and definately not a reading list — I can't look anything up right now and I don't want to be told to go read somebody. (Two people in the group have already told me the handout looks fine, which given (1) I guess shouldn't move me much, or maybe should, I honestly can't tell whether that's a case of what (1) is about or a seperate thing.) Concretely: tell me which pairs of these actually pull against each other, and rank those by how bad they are rather than listing them as equally bad. For each real tension, say exactly what would have to give and what that costs me — what I lose elsewhere in the set, what cases I can no longer give the verdict I want to give. Also tell me which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection and why, because I'd much rather you discriminate than flag all six. And if any pair is only in tension given some bridge premise I haven't stated, name that premise explicitly; I suspect at least one of them is. If you want to reach for the move where the answer is "just distinguish believing p from believing that you're justified in believing p," fine, but then say what work that distinction is actually doing rather than relabelling the problem. Don't tell me which commitment I personally should give up — I want to see the price of each option side by side. Under 800 words if you can manage it.
