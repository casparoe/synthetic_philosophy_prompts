I run a weekly reading group on minds and machines at my university — about 14 undergrads, half CS and half philosophy, one 75-minute session, a room with a projector and a whiteboard, no laptops. We're spending two sessions on learning a concept from almost no data, and I want the first one to be an activity where they run into the problem themselves rather than me naming it and us discussing it.

The set-up I had in mind: those Bongard-style puzzles — a bunch of boxes of simple shapes, one pile where the rule holds and one pile where it doesn't, and the job is to say what the rule is. I'd rather build my own then print the classic ones, because I want to know for sure what the intended rule is and I want to be able to produce extra boxes on demand; I can make cards with circles, triangles and squares, black or white, 1–5 shapes per box, big or small, touching or not touching. So design me a runnable activity, groups of 3–4, that makes them feel that the twelve boxes they're given don't pin down any rule at all, and that whatever makes one of the many fitting rules the right one is not fit to the data. I'd like at least three moments: (1) groups write a one-sentence rule for a problem, I collect them, and we see that the rules which all classify the twelve boxes correctly disagree with each other somewhere; (2) a scoring round where they have to commit in advance to how their rule classifies six held-out boxes I then reveal — small stakes, chocolate or a leaderboard, no money, and I want the scoring to punish confident wrongness more than hedging, but I'm not sure how to do that cleanly; (3) a meta round I half-invented and don't know how to run: give each group four candidate rules that all fit the twelve boxes and are about equally short, and make them bet on which one I actually had in mind when I built the problem — including at least one problem where the rule I really used is deliberately unnatural, like the total number of corners being prime, or the order I drew the shapes in, or something about seperate clusters — so that whatever they're betting on can't be simplicity or fit and has to be something else, and afterwards we can ask what that something else was. No lecture up front, and I don't want them to hear words like projectible, grue, simplicity prior or underdetermination until the debrief.

Then the debrief: map what actually happened onto the ideas and only name them there; tell me honestly which part of the point the activity can't deliver and that I'll have to say myself; and tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — my worry is that the CS people will brute-force the intended rule in ninety seconds and kill the whole thing, or that one group will announce that any rule fits and refuse to play, and also that the philosophers will just sit back and watch the CS people. If you think my three moments are the wrong three, say so and give me yours instead, and please keep the whole thing inside 75 minutes including the debrief.
