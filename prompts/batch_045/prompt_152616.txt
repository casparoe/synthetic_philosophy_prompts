I have been trying for some time to settle, by my own lights, what pleasures have in common, and I keep failing in the same place: every candidate unifying property either stretches until it admits things I refuse to call pleasures, or narrows until it excludes cases nobody would give up. Before I conclude that the kind is simply disjunctive or merely a family resemblance, I want to run my own verdicts through reflective equilibrium, and I would like you to do that work with me.

Here are the cases and the verdicts I am inclined to give. Sexual pleasure: pleasure, the clearest case I have. The taste of very cold water when one is parched, or the first sip of a well-made espresso: pleasure. Scratching an itch, including an itch one knows one should not scratch: pleasure, even though it makes matters worse. The burn of very spicy food: pleasure, and not merely tolerated for the sake of the rest of the flavour, but liked for itself. Being absorbed in a hard climb, where at the time there is no noticeable pleasant feeling at all, only effort, fear and attention: I still want to call the episode a pleasure, on the strength of the fact that I sought it out, would seek it again, and call it enjoyable afterwards — though this is one of my least confident verdicts. The faint contentment of lying in a warm bath: pleasure, although it is barely noticeable and easily displaced. The satisfaction of closing a proof or finally fixing a bug: pleasure, even though it seems to have no distinctive bodily feel and is in that respect quite unlike the first four; it strikes me as cooler and more cognitive. Being frightened by a horror film and enjoying being frightened: pleasure. Schadenfreude at a rival's small failure: pleasure, however unattractive. The relief when a threat passes or when pain stops: I am inclined to say this is not a pleasure but only the ending of something bad, even though at the time it feels as good as most things on this list — and I am aware that these two halves of that verdict pull against each other. The satisfaction of ticking items off a to-do list, or of a newly ordered desk: not a pleasure, but something more like a judgment that things are as they should be. But compare this with the proof case, which I do count: both are mild, both are cognitive rather than sensory, and neither has much of a distinctive feel, and I cannot say what separates them. Dream pleasure — dancing, flying, lovemaking in a dream, where nothing is actually happening and arguably nothing is really obtained: pleasure, and I do not think the dreaming makes any difference. A case in which someone reports being driven to pursue something in which they take no enjoyment at all (there are neurological and addiction cases of this sort, and I do not want to lean on their details): not a pleasure, even though the pursuit is compulsive and the person would say they want the thing intensely. Finally, a layering case: hearing my child laugh, and then enjoying the fact that I am enjoying it. I want to say that there are two pleasures here, not one larger one, and that the second takes the first as its object.

The principle I have been toying with, which I suspect is broken, is this: an experience is a pleasure just in case, while it lasts, it presents itself to the subject as something to stay with, where this is neither a belief that staying with it would be best nor a desire to stay with it, but a felt pull toward the continuation of the experience itself. On this account pleasures need share no felt quality at all: what unites them is a direction, not a texture. To deal with the case of pursuit without enjoyment I add that the pull must be toward the experience rather than merely toward the activity or object that produces it. To deal with the layering case I add that such a pull can take another experience, including another pleasure, as its object, so that pleasures can be stacked. Call it the continuance account.

What I want from you is this. Test the continuance account against my verdicts case by case, and tell me, for each clause, which case forces it and what happens if the clause is dropped. Repair it where it fails, and if the repair requires a disjunction or a clause that seems tailored to a single case, say so and name the cost. If no single property can be found, lay out the honest alternatives — a disjunctive list, an adverbial treatment on which pleasurably is a manner in which one undergoes an experience of any qualitative sort, a role-based account on which pleasures are unified by what they do in motivation, learning and deliberation rather than by how they feel, or the abandonment of the kind — and for each, say which of my verdicts I would have to give up and which of my distinctions it cannot draw at all. Then tell me what the account you recommend predicts for two cases on which I have not ruled: being tickled, where one laughs and squirms and asks to be stopped; and the glow of self-approval after doing the right thing at real cost to oneself. Finally, be explicit about whether the heterogeneity problem has actually been dissolved or merely moved: if the winning account unifies pleasures by a formal or relational feature rather than a felt one, I want to know whether it has thereby stopped being a theory of what pleasure is like, and whether that is a defect in it or simply what the problem forces.

I am not after a survey of the literature, and I do not want citations, reading suggestions, or references; I want the principle, the argument for it, and its costs. Please answer in continuous prose, as you would in a letter — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, and no enumerated lists in your reply — and aim for something in the region of a thousand words.
