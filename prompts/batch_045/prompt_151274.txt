A friend of mine who works on animal stuff walked me into this and I cant find the exit. Shes not even vegan, shes just annoyingly committed to consistency. Here is her argument more or less as she gave it, cleaned up so that every step is something I would separately agree to:

(1) When you choose between diets, the only thing that matters about the choice, as far as animals go, is the total amount of suffering it causes, counting every affected animal, farmed and wild, and weighting by how much suffering the kind of animal in question can pack into a day.

(2) So the suffering caused by a diet = the suffering of the animals raised and killed for it + the suffering of the wild animals affected by the land and the feed it takes up.

(3) On the first term, beef beats chicken by a lot. Chickens are small and live a few weeks, so a kilo of chicken meat or a gram of chicken protein takes far more chicken lives than the equivalent from a cow takes cow lives — somewhere between one and two orders of magnitude depending on whose numbers you use — and the conditions are the same kind of intensive conditions either way. If what you are counting is lives of a given bad quality per unit of food, chicken is much worse.

(4) The obvious reply is the second term. Beef needs way more land and feed per calorie, so beef means more cultivation, and cultivation kills field animals directly through ploughing, harvesting and poisoning, and it destroys habitat. So whatever beef wins in (3) gets eaten by the second half of (2).

(5) But that reply assumes the wild animals affected were having lives worth living. Most animals in a field or a hedgerow are the kind that produce dozens or hundreds of offspring nearly all of which die young, of cold, starvation, parasites or predation. Take such a life as a whole and it very likely contains more suffering than enjoyment. And for a life like that, dying early is not a harm done to it; it is a bad thing cut short. Having fewer such lives is better twice over, because each existing one suffers for less time and because there is simply less of the stuff.

(6) So the second term does not count against beef. It counts for beef: the more land a diet needs, the more it suppresses wild populations whose lives are net negative. (She even has an answer to the obvious pushback here: sure, farmland gets repopulated by synanthropic generalists like rats, pigeons and starlings, but those are the same kind of r-selected lives, so it is not clear the total goes up, and it is not clear it goes down either — it is roughly a wash, and the wash is the good case.)

(7) So if you want to minimize animal suffering you should not go vegan, and you should not switch to chicken either. You should eat the animal with the largest land footprint per calorie you can get: extensively grazed, grass-fed beef, ideally from a system that has converted a lot of habitat into pasture.

(8) And (5) does not stop at diets. Almost all the suffering in the world is out there in wild nature rather than on farms. If preventing net-negative wild lives is a good thing, then the best uses of land are parking lots, golf courses and distribution warehouses, and we should be cheering habitat conversion generally.

(9) I think (8) is nuts and I am fairly sure (7) is too, or at least I am not going to act on it. But each step from (1) to (6) sounded fine when she said it and I cannot point at the one that is doing all the work.

A wrinkle that makes it worse for me: I noticed she never says anything about the fact that farmed animals only exist because people eat them. She says that is already handled by (3), which just counts the lives involved. But if those lives are net negative and would not otherwise have happened, then (3) is not really about deaths at all, it is about lives created — and it is using the same net-negative assumption about farmed animals that (5) uses about wild ones. That symmetry feels like the hinge, but I cannot tell whether it is what breaks the argument or what makes it unbreakable.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost you? I do have preferences. I would most like to keep (1) and (3), because if I give up (1) I have no idea what I am supposed to be doing with any of this, and (3) is the bit I thought I already understood before I met her. (5) is the one I am most suspicious of, but I cannot say why it is wrong rather than just uncomfortable, and every time I try I end up saying something that sinks (3) as well. Please do not just tell me the empirical numbers are uncertain and leave it there — assume for the sake of argument that we know them, or tell me specifically which ones it matters that we do not. And if you think the problem is a slide between two steps rather than a premise, say exactly where, and what the two readings are that it slides between.
