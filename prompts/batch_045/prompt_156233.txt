I am writing a paper on whether "better than" is transitive, and I would like your comments on its central argument before I develop it further. The argument is meant to do three things: diagnose what continuum-style arguments for intransitivity actually trade on, offer a distinction that I think dissolves them, and show what that distinction does to the repugnant-conclusion style of population argument. I have written everything as a constructed case so that the issue can be argued without reference to the literature, so please do not supply citations or reading suggestions, and please do not spend your reply restating or praising the setup. I want criticism.

The case: one path, two slicings

Fix a population of 1,000 people and let two goods vary. *Vividness* is the felt intensity of each person's pleasures, on a scale from 0 to 100. *Harmony* is a relational good: the extent to which everyone's pleasures compose a single coherent shared life, also 0 to 100. Let A be the state with vividness 100 and harmony 0. Let Z be the state with vividness 50 and harmony 100.

Now consider the path from A to Z sliced into 1,000 steps. At each step vividness falls by 0.05 for everyone and harmony rises by 0.1. Call this the fine slicing. At this grain no one can tell that anything has happened to their own experience: 0.05 of vividness is below the threshold of notice. The gain in harmony at each step is real and, at that size, is at least comparable in value to the loss. So the local verdict at each step is that the successor state is not worse than its predecessor — arguably better. Chain the local verdicts and you reach: Z is not worse than A; indeed Z is better than A.

But the endpoint verdict, asked for directly, goes the other way. Halving the felt intensity of everyone's experience is a large loss, and a gain in harmony does not compensate for it. A is better than Z.

Now take the very same path sliced into ten steps: vividness falls by 5 per step for everyone, harmony rises by 10. At this grain the loss is plainly noticeable and the local verdict flips; each step looks worse than the last. Chain those verdicts and you get A better than Z, which agrees with the direct endpoint judgment.

The endpoints, the path and the total change are identical in both descriptions. Only the slicing differs. So consider:

*Grain-Invariance*: verdicts about a pair of states that are derived by chaining verdicts about adjacent states along a path must not depend on how finely the path is sliced.

Spectrum arguments violate Grain-Invariance. They take their premises from the fine slicing and their conclusion about the endpoints from a direct judgment, and the two disagree. Something has to give.

The distinction

What gives, I want to argue, is a conflation of two relations.

*Betterness* is a two-place comparative relation on states. It is transitive, it permits arbitrarily small margins, and it is indifferent to whether its relata are presented as a move or as a static comparison.

*Improvement* is deliberative and, in effect, four-place: state B is an improvement on state A *for someone situated at A who is considering whether to move*. It is sensitive to noticeability, to the costs of transition, to the status quo, and to the grain at which the move is described. It governs what may be done, not what is better.

The local verdicts in my case are verdicts about improvement. Read that way, "you may take this step; nothing noticeable is lost; something is gained" is true at the fine slicing and false at the coarse slicing, which is exactly the grain-sensitivity we observe. Nothing follows about betterness. Once the two relations are separated, transitivity and completeness together entail that somewhere along even the fine-sliced path there is a step whose successor is worse than its predecessor — imperceptibly worse, but worse. That residue sounds epistemicist, and I want to know whether you think it is fatal.

The payoff

Take the population case. Adding a life that is worth living but barely so looks, to many people, both permissible and not an improvement in the betterness sense. On my distinction that is not a contradiction but the expected shape of the verdict: the addition is permitted (a deliberative verdict, given that no one is harmed and someone benefits), and it does not make the outcome better. The repugnant-conclusion argument needs the additions to be *not worse* in the betterness sense, since only betterness is transitive. If they are merely improvements in the deliberative sense, and if permissions do not agglomerate across steps, the chain never gets off the ground. The same structure appears in the familiar case of trading a barely noticeable increment of pain for a sum of money, repeated over and over: each move is permitted from where you are, and the endpoint is terrible. My claim is that both puzzles are one mistake — reading a verdict about permitted movement as a verdict about comparative betterness.

Costs I can already see: the view owes an account of improvement that does not collapse into betterness plus conservatism; it owes an explanation of why permissions fail to agglomerate; and it seems to render the repugnant conclusion not false but unreachable, which may be less than I wanted.

What I want from you

1. Reconstruct the argument as an explicit numbered list of premises, marking which are definitional stipulations about my two relations and which are substantive claims that a hostile reader would contest. I want to see where the weight actually falls.

2. Is Grain-Invariance a genuine constraint, or does it beg the question? An intransitivist can say that of course the local verdicts are grain-sensitive, since that sensitivity is precisely the phenomenon to be explained. Say whether that reply works and why.

3. Give the strongest intransitivist case that my diagnosis cannot reach. In particular: can a cycle of "better than" verdicts be constructed among three states presented statically, with no movement, no path and no deliberative framing at all? If so, the improvement/betterness distinction does no work there, and I need to know how much of the debate that leaves untouched.

4. Assess the epistemicist residue. If betterness allows imperceptible margins, then somewhere on a fine-grained path there is a bad step that no one can locate. Is that a cost I can bear, or does it hand the intransitivist everything?

5. Is the improvement relation stable, or does it collapse? If "you may move from A to B" is ultimately grounded in reasons, and reasons are comparative, does permissibility just reduce to betterness plus deontic side-constraints? And if the status quo is doing real work in my account, am I smuggling in loss aversion and calling it a norm?

6. Test for over-generation. Does "permissible but not better" spread to ordinary sequential policy reasoning, where we do want to conclude from a chain of improvements that the endpoint is better? If it does, is that acceptable, or does it wreck more than it saves?

7. Does the framework actually block the repugnant conclusion, or does it merely relocate the burden onto an unexplained failure of agglomeration? Say which, and say what would have to be true for the relocation to be a gain rather than a punt.

8. Finally, an honesty check: how much of this is new, and how much is a redescription of moves that are already on the table — incomparability or parity views, imprecise or indeterminate betterness, person-affecting restrictions on addition, the failure of agglomeration for permissions? If it is largely a repackaging, tell me which existing position it collapses into and what, if anything, the repackaging buys.

If you think the argument collapses, say so and say where; I would rather have that than a sympathetic summary.
