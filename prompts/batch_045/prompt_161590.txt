I am a PhD student in philosophy working on responsibility gaps for autonomous systems, and I am putting together a set of discussion examples for an upper-level seminar on moral responsibility and artificial intelligence. Most of the standard cases in the literature are unsatisfying for my purposes: they tend to smuggle in negligence, an obvious design flaw, or a human who could have intervened but did not, so they never really isolate the structural feature I care about. I want a cleaner constructed example. Assume three simplified theoretical positions. A control theorist holds that an agent is morally responsible for an outcome only if, at the time of acting, they had the kind of control or avoidability that makes praise or blame appropriate; if the outcome was produced by a system whose behavior no human could guide or foresee in the moment, the control theorist denies responsibility and diagnoses a responsibility gap. A tracing theorist allows that responsibility can sometimes be traced back through earlier conduct, but only when that earlier conduct was itself faulty, reckless, or unreasonably risky; if the earlier design and deployment decisions were optimal given available evidence, the tracing theorist also finds no blame and thus no closure of the gap. A risk-imposition theorist, by contrast, holds that an agent can be morally responsible for a harm when they deploy a system that imposes risks on others in order to secure benefits for themselves, even when no one was at fault and no one violated a duty. Your task is to construct a realistic thought experiment, set in an ordinary environment such as a hospital logistics system, a warehouse, or a campus delivery service, with no science-fiction elements, no exotic predictors, and confined to one paragraph, in which a learning-based autonomous system causes a nontrivial harm, and in which the control theorist and the tracing theorist both conclude that there is a responsibility gap, while the risk-imposition theorist concludes that the deployer is morally responsible.

The constraints are as follows. The harm must not be the result of a violation of best practices: the developers used state-of-the-art safety methods, the operators had no opportunity to prevent the specific event, and the system's dangerous behavior emerged only because the operating environment shifted in a way that no reasonable amount of foresight would have singled out in advance, even though the general possibility of such a shift was known. The divergence in verdicts must therefore not be manufactured by the deployer having adopted an obviously bad policy or by any individual having been negligent; it must fall out of the structural facts of the case. After presenting the scenario, I want you to walk through what each of the three theories says about it and why, explicitly stating which feature of the case is doing the work. My hypothesis is that the key feature is the combination of an adaptive system, an unforeseen but not unreasonably ignored environmental shift, and a deployment that benefits the deployer while imposing risk on others; please confirm or correct this. Then tell me what happens if that feature is removed: for instance, if the harm traced back to a design flaw that was known or should have been known, or if a human had real-time control. Finally, I want you to explain how a defender of the view that comes out worst would attack the example: for example, how a control theorist might argue that the deployer did exercise control in choosing to deploy the system, or how a tracing theorist might try to find a fault in the decision to use a learning-based system at all.

Because I plan to use this example to test whether the three views really come apart in practice, please include a brief epistemic afterword. Tell me how confident you are that your scenario genuinely makes the three theories deliver the specified verdicts, rather than merely stipulating them or relying on an unfair description of one view. Say what would change your mind: which objection from a control or tracing theorist would lead you to revise or withdraw the example, and where your own application of the three positions is most likely to fail. I am especially interested in whether risk-imposition is better understood as legal liability rather than moral responsibility, and whether the notion of control is too indeterminate to yield a clear verdict. I am less interested in a polished final verdict than in seeing the reasoning and the pressure points, so please be explicit about the weakest step in your construction.
