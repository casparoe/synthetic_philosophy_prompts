Hi — I'm on the clinical ethics committee at my local hospital and we're about to sign off on a pilot of one of those ambient documentation tools: a mic in the room that listens to the intake conversation and writes the summary the GP reads before they walk in. I've sat through the demos and it's pretty obvious to me that the notes come out flatter and more hedged when the patient's speech is non-standard — strong regional accent, second language, working-class register, or just an older person who rambles. "Patient reports abdominal discomfort" where the patient actually said the pain is stabbing, it's in the same place as last time, and it wakes her at four every morning. And as far as I can tell the clinicians basically never go back to the raw audio.

A colleague on the committee keeps saying this should just be filed under algorithmic bias and patient safety, and that calling it epistemic injustice is a category mistake, because nobody has a prejudice and the software doesn't have beliefs about anybody. I think shes wrong, and I've got to make the case out loud in a fortnight.

So please write me the argument I should give. Argue that this is testimonial injustice in the full Fricker-style sense — a credibility deficit that tracks social identity and wrongs the speaker specifically in her capacity as a knower — even though there's no individual hearer with an identity prejudice and no system with beliefs. Argue that the deficit is best located in the arrangement (mic + model + note + the clinician's workflow) rather than in any person's judgement, and that the corrective Fricker proposes, the hearer cultivating the virtue of testimonial justice, does nothing at all here, because the person who would need to correct for the deficit never encounters the speaker's own words. Keep it distinct from the hermeneutical kind, too: these patients often have perfectly good words for their symptoms, the words just dont make it into the note.

Two objections you have to actually deal with rather than gesture at: (1) that credibility is a doxastic notion, so a pipeline with no beliefs can't assign anyone credibility, and (2) that we lose nothing by just calling it bias and regulating it as a safety problem. And if you can work it in — I think the worst part is that the ordinary one-on-one case at least leaves the speaker room to repair: you slow down, you produce your credentials, you repeat yourself, you watch the doubt on the doctor's face and respond to it. Here there's no one in the room to repair your credibility in front of. Tell me if you think that's the strongest version of the point or a distraction.

Max 550 words, British English, plain prose, no bullets or headings, and please don't cite or quote anything — I want your argument, not a reading list, since I'm the one who'll be standing up and saying it.
