i've been going in circles on this for about a week and it's feeding into a third-year essay on moral uncertainty that's due in a fortnight, so any help is very much appreciated. The setup: I have £20k I've decided to give away, and four options — (a) a bednet charity, (b) a long-run existential-risk fund, (c) a research fund for working out which causes are most cost-effective, (d) spend it on a extremely long holiday. I've got roughly 0.6 credence in something like a commonsense near-term view that cares a lot about identified lives and thinks (d) is genuinely, seriously wrong, and roughly 0.4 in a long-termist consequentialism that cares mostly about the far future and regards (d) as a bit wasteful but not a big deal. Here's the argument:

(1) If you're uncertain which moral theory is true, the rational thing to do is maximize expected choiceworthiness: score each option by each theory, weight by your credence in that theory, add them up, pick the winner. (2) The theories don't measure the same thing, so their scores aren't on a common scale, and to add them you need a conversion rule. (3) The only conversion rule that isn't pulled out of thin air is to use each theory's own ranking of the options on the table: rescale so the best option on the table gets 1, the worst gets 0, and the rest sit in between in proportion. That uses only information internal to each theory, and it's invariant to what units the theory happened to be stated in. (4) But now whether (a) beats (b) depends on what else is on the table: I did the arithmetic with made-up units, and with (d) available the long-termist view's scale gets squashed relative to the commonsense one and (b) wins; delete (d) from the menu and the commonsense view's spread opens back up and (a) wins. I varied the numbers a fair bit and the flip survives, which is the worrying part. (5) That's absurd — whether I should do (a) rather than (b) can't depend on the existence of an option I'm not going to take. (6) So (3) is out; but every other conversion rule is arbitrary in the sense that nothing in the theories fixes it, since multiplying one theory's units by ten changes nothing the theory says about the world and yet flips the sum. (7) So maximizing expected choiceworthiness delivers no verdicts at all, only verdicts relative to a stipulation I can pick at will.

That's already bad, but it gets worse when I look for an exit. (8) Fall back on the other standard view, do whatever the theory you're most confident in says. But which theory that is depends on how coarsely you carve up theory space: lump the consequentialist family into one theory and it's my favourite at 0.4; split it into four sub-variants at 0.1 each and the commonsense view at 0.35 becomes my favourite instead. Nothing in the epistemology seems to fix the right grain. (9) So no procedure for acting under moral uncertainty is well-defined, and therefore under moral uncertainty there's no such thing as one option being more rationally choiceworthy than another: the person who reads nothing and gives to their local animal shelter and the person who spends two years researching before giving are equally rational, and the whole idea of "taking moral uncertainty seriously" is empty — including, I guess, the idea of taking this argument seriously.

I definately can't accept (9), and every paper I read treats this as a live, tractable problem, so I assume something in the chain is broken. I've tried two fixes and neither quite lands: a friend says (2) is the bad step because you can aggregate the theories' ordinal rankings instead of their cardinal scores, but that seems to throw away how much better one option is than another, and I worry about cycling; my supervisor muttered something about treating moral uncertainty like imprecise probabilities and just using a permissibility rule — don't do anything that a theory you give more than some threshold credence forbids — but that just relocates the arbitrariness to the threshold. If I have to give something up I'd rather keep (1) and (5) than (3) or (6), because giving up either of the former feels like giving up the whole enterprise, though maybe that instinct is exactly the mistake.
