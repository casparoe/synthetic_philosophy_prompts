I'm helping put together a problem set for an intro philosophy class that spends a week on Hume's chapter on miracles, and I want one item that's a carefully built case rather than an open essay question. I was arguing with a friend about it last night and we kept going in circles, so I figured I'd just try to get the case written properly.

Here's the setup I'm working from. On the Bayesian reconstruction of Hume, everything turns on whether the reports we've got are more likely if the violation happened than if it didn't, weighed against how tiny the prior on the violation is to begin with. On Hume's own side there are two rather different things: his maxim, roughly that no testimony establishes a miracle unless the falsehood of that testimony would itself be a bigger marvel than the event it reports; and then the checklist he runs in the second half of the chapter, about whether any marvel has in fact ever been attested by enough witnesses of good sense, education and integrity, in public, with no motive to fool us and no enthusiasm or love of the marvellous doing the work. What I want is a case where the Bayesian comes out *more* skeptical than that checklist, which is the opposite of the direction these discussions usually go: the Bayesian says withhold, because the reports taken together just don't clear the prior, even though the witnesses are as good as Hume's checklist could possibly want. So individually excellent witnesses, quite a lot of them, public event, no hoax and no conspiracy, no religious enthusiasm, nobody with an interest in the marvellous, and — this is the bit my friend thinks is cheating — each witness's reliability independently established, so we know from unrelated work that these people are careful and honest.

What makes the Bayesian balk has to be that their error channels aren't independent. I've been calling it a calibration-linked witness pool: a shared instrument, or shared training, or a shared inference habit, or a reporting pipeline in which only the striking readings get passed up the chain, or shared perceptual conditions — something structural that makes it far less surprising that they all go wrong together than it would be if their mistakes were unrelated. Two more constraints. First, nobody downstream of the event, us included, gets to observe it directly or re-check the instrument ourselves; the evidence has to stay testimonial all the way through, or it stops being a Hume case at all. Second, pick your law so that the easy out of saying we simply had the law wrong isn't available, and say why it isn't (or if you'd rather make that escape route the interesting part of the case, flag it and deal with it). Give me the scenario in about one paragraph, in a realistic setting — a hospital, a lab, a cockpit, a weather station, a courtroom, whatever, but no sci-fi gadgets. Then show the work: state the claim, the total evidence, the prior on the violation and where that prior comes from, and the two likelihoods; show explicitly that a naive treatment of the witnesses as independent would flip the verdict to believe, and that the correlated channel is what flips it back. Then run Hume's checklist over the same witnesses and show it comes out maxed, and say what his maxim appears to imply given that. Then tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work and what happens to both verdicts if you remove it. And finally, give me the defense: how would someone who wants Hume to come out looking fine attack this case? I can see at least three moves — lean on the in-principle argument from the first half of the chapter instead of the checklist; argue that the checklist was never meant to be a list of merely individual virtues and that Hume's talk of contrary causes already covers shared error channels; or go after the prior by blurring violation of a law into law was wrong. Say which of those actually works and which is a dodge. The whole point of the exercise is to make students see whether the real weight in that chapter sits in the first half or the second, so build the case for that purpose. If a line from Hume would help, paraphrase it rather than quoting — I don't want invented quotations, and I've got the text in front of me anyway.
