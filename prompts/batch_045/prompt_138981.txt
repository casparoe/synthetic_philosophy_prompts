I volunteer on the technical advisory group for a newly chartered innovation zone whose assembly has voted to run housing and transport policy by futarchy: the assembly votes on a welfare metric, and then for each proposal a conditional prediction market estimates that metric under adoption versus non-adoption. The metric currently in the charter is a weighted index of median rent burden, mean commute time, and five-year small-business survival, measured four years after the policy takes effect.

A friend on the group sent me an argument that our scheme proves too much, and I cannot find the exit:

(1) In futarchy, the value question, which is which welfare metric to use, is settled by a vote; every factual question, which is what a policy will do to the metric, is settled by a market.
(2) The case for routing the factual questions to markets is that market prices aggregate dispersed, unverbalized information better than voting does.
(3) So on any question where there is a fact of the matter that markets are better positioned to know than voters are, the question should go to a market rather than to a vote.
(4) Whether the charter's composite index is a good measure of what residents care about is such a question: it is an empirical matter of what residents actually want and of how housing and transport propagate into the rest of their lives.
(5) So the choice of metric should be delegated to a market too, not to a vote.
(6) But a market can only pay out on a well-defined, measurable event, and specifying that event already requires a metric, so choosing the metric by market requires a metric to bet on, and choosing that one requires another, and so on.
(7) So either the regress never bottoms out, in which case no policy is ever legitimately adopted, or it bottoms out in a metric that somebody simply declares, in which case the vote-on-values, bet-on-beliefs split is just rule by whoever writes the index, with markets as decoration.
(8) Either way, futarchy cannot deliver what we advertised to the residents.

This also seems to sink our group's workaround, which was to let a market pick the metric by betting on what a deliberative assembly would endorse five years later; we call it a ratification market.
