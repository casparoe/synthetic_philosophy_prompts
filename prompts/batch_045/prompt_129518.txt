Im putting together a handout for a first-year PhD reading group on teleosemantics — the family of views that try to fix a mental state's content by what that state is *for*, usually via some kind of selection history. What I want is a decision tree that sorts someone into a position, built up from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first six or seven branch questions, each one phrased as a concrete case or a plain claim that a reader can answer without knowing which view it leads to, then six to eight leaves, each carrying the name of the position (or just say unnamed if you dont think anyone really holds it) plus one line on that position's most awkward commitment. Assume the readers know the frog or toad that snaps at flies, and the swampman case where lightning knocks together a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a person, and basically nothing else — so any further case you use has to be explained in a clause.

The two axes I actually care about, because they're what my chapter is on, so the tree has to separate views along them: (i) systems with a design history but no selection history — a hidden layer in a net trained by gradient descent to flag tumours on scans, a prosthetic or implanted device wired into the visual system, a lab population evolved for a few hundred generations — do these have content, and does it matter whether the history is artificial, short, or supervised; (ii) co-option, where a mechanism has a selection history for one job and gets reused for another, so that ancestral and derived function come apart — does content track what it was selected to do or what it now does, and what happens to misrepresentation in the reused case. Also make sure somewhere in the tree there's a question about whether a system that never had the right history can misrepresent at all, or only fail to represent.

Two more things. Flag any combination of answers your questions allow that nobody seems to occupy, and say briefly whether that's because it's incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored. And flag any two leaves your tree can't really separate rather than pretending it can — for instance, whether a view on which content needs an actual history and one on which content is what the state would reliably bring about if things went right genuinely diverge anywhere outside duplicate cases, or whether they only look different. Keep the whole thing under about 700 words, and write it in British English.
