Hey — I'm in a metaphysics seminar this term on powers and dispositions. We did the simple conditional analysis (x is disposed to M in C iff x would M if C obtained), everyone nodded, and next week we're scheduled to hit the fink/mask stuff. I volunteered to run the session before that, and I'd rather they walk into the problem than be told about it: 14 of us including the prof, 80 minutes, one big table, whiteboard, I can print cards but nobody's doing prep for it.

What I want is a game, not a discussion. Something where they feel that you cannot read whether a thing has a disposition off how it behaves when you test it — feel it as a practical problem, with something at stake, before anyone says "fink" or "mask" out loud. My rough idea: small teams, each team gets a sealed object file describing some object's hidden micro-mechanism, and a different team has to interrogate it — three questions of the form "what happens if we do X to it", answered truthfully from the file — then give a verdict (has the disposition / lacks it / can't tell). Scoring has to make bluffing and answer-key-lawyering pointless without anyone playing for money. Redesign it if you've got something better; the only hard constraints are printed cards, a whiteboard, and no lecture up front.

The deck is the part I really need. Include the standard cases (the thing that loses the disposition exactly when you go to test it; the thing that keeps it but is packed so nothing shows) but I want most of the deck to be cases we haven't seen, that make the same trouble in weirder directions. Ones I've been playing with:

- a wire that conducts fine, but the current itself trips a switch after half a second, so you get a brief current and then nothing
- a glass that is fragile, but the first strike destroys the packing and the second strike shatters it — so asking your three questions in the wrong order gives you the wrong verdict
- something with no disposition to explode at all, where heating it makes it explosive and then it explodes
- a fink that only fires half the time
- a case where the manifestation happens, but a week after the test window has closed
- one completely boring object whose file says "nothing weird here, it's just a normal fragile glass", because I want to see who over-thinks it

Give me 10–12 objects with a short file for each, an answer key that commits to has-it / lacks-it / genuinely-undecidable, and flag the ones where your key is really just stipulation. Plus the scoring, a timeline in minutes, the roles, and what will predictably go wrong the first time — I'm guessing at least one team asks a question that presupposes the answer, and someone will say "it depends what you mean by disposition" about ten minutes in. Tell me what to actually do with those.

Then a debrief that maps specific moments onto specific trouble: which cases break the simple conditional, which ones a conditional theorist can just bite the bullet on by holding the mechanism fixed in the counterfactual, and where the argument stops being about behavior and has to be about the categorical basis. And be honest about what the game cannot do. My worry is that a querying game only teaches them that evidence can be misleading, which is epistemology and not the point at all — if that's what this is, say so.

On confidence: tell me how sure you are that each weird case has a stable pre-theoretic verdict rather than just splitting the room, and where you're guessing about what my seminar will find intuitive.
