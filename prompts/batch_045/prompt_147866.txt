I work in operations at a mid-sized company that recently routed most of its customer-support email through a language-model assistant, and I spend a good part of my day reading transcripts of what it says to customers. An argument that keeps resurfacing among my colleagues is whether its sentences — I understand how frustrating this has been, I'm sorry we shipped you the wrong part, I checked and your refund was processed on Tuesday — should ever be read as reports of anything the system believes, knows, regrets, or intends. I came across Daniel Dennett's notion of the intentional stance in a podcast episode and found it helpful but too blunt for my purposes: it tells me that treating a thing as an agent with beliefs can be a useful predictive shortcut, but it doesn't distinguish among the several quite different shortcuts I actually catch myself reaching for, and it says nothing about which of them is liable to get me or my company into trouble.

So I have been trying to write down, from my own notes on the transcripts, a longer inventory of stances a person in my position can adopt toward such a system. Here is my list, with a one-line gloss of each:

1. Physical stance — think about it only at the level of circuits, voltages, and stored numbers.
2. Design stance — predict from how it was built and what it was trained and fine-tuned to do.
3. Intentional stance — treat it as a single agent with beliefs and desires, and read its sentences as its own reports of them.
4. Simulator stance — treat it not as one agent but as a machine capable of inhabiting many simulated agents, so that no one of them is *it*, and there is no fact of the matter about what *it* believes.
5. Corpus echo stance — treat the output as a sample of what the texts it was trained on would most likely say next, addressed by no one to no one.
6. Role stance — treat it as a performer playing whatever character the system prompt and the conversation have cast it as, and judge only the quality of the performance.
7. Cenotaph stance — treat the output as an inscription left behind by absent human authors (the annotators, whoever wrote the system prompt, the customer themselves), with no speaker present at all.
8. Attributive stance — treat whatever it says as the statement of the party who deployed it, the way a firm speaks through a press release written by many hands.
9. Adversarial stance — assume that any appearance of understanding, sincerity, or self-knowledge has been optimized to be persuasive rather than to be accurate, and discount it accordingly.
10. Instrument-reading stance — treat its self-reports the way one treats a thermometer reading or a survey response: not authoritative about itself, but still evidence about something else, in this case about its training text and its prompt.

I would like two things from you, kept separate. First, a score from 0 to 100 for each stance, reflecting how much real predictive grip it gives someone in my job who is trying to anticipate, in advance, what the assistant will say in a new and slightly unusual support conversation. Second, the same ten stances sorted into three or four tiers according to how much ethical, legal, or reputational risk you think my company takes on by relying on that stance when deciding what to do — for instance, when deciding whether a transcript shows that we knew about a defect, whether an apology from the assistant is an apology anyone has actually made, or whether a customer who says the assistant promised them something was promised anything at all.

Give me the numbers and the tiers, each with a sentence or two of justification, and please don't spread the first set of scores evenly: I want to know which of these stances you think is doing genuine work for someone in my position and which is mostly intellectual decoration. If you think two entries on my list collapse into each other, or that I have omitted the stance that would in fact be most useful to me, say so and rate that one instead.
