The question as set:

**Question 4 (20 marks: part (a) 7, part (b) 8, part (c) 5).**

**(a)** State Bertrand's chord paradox. Give three different ways of making the instruction "choose a chord of a circle at random" precise, compute for each of them the probability that the chord drawn is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, and explain, in no more than a few sentences, why the example is thought to be a problem for the principle of indifference.

**(b)** Take a circle of unit radius and an integer N at least 4. Mark N equally spaced points on the circumference, and let a chord be produced by choosing uniformly at random one of the N(N−1)/2 chords determined by the pairs of marked points.

  (i) Show that the chord joining two marked points whose shorter-arc angular separation is 2πd/N has length 2 sin(πd/N), and hence that it exceeds √3 if and only if d exceeds N/3.
  (ii) Hence obtain, as a function of N, the probability that the chord exceeds √3. Take care with the diameters.
  (iii) Evaluate your expression for N = 6, N = 12 and N = 60, and give its limit as N grows without bound.
  (iv) The limiting value coincides with one of the classical values from part (a). Does this show that the classical paradox is merely an artifact of the continuum, and that the value obtained here is the one the principle of indifference really supports? Discuss, paying attention to what the finite construction tacitly assumes.

**(c)** A straight straw, long compared with the diameter of the circle, is dropped onto a unit circle painted on a floor, in such a way that the midpoint of the straw is uniformly distributed over a large region containing the circle and the direction of the straw is uniformly distributed over [0, π). For a straw that crosses the circle, let p denote the perpendicular distance from the centre of the circle to the chord the straw determines; p is then uniformly distributed on [0, 1]. Compute the probability that this chord exceeds √3, and say what the example shows about the relation between physical randomness and the principle of indifference.

The candidate's answer:

**(a)** Bertrand's paradox is that the question "a chord of a circle is drawn at random; what is the probability that it is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle?" has several different answers, each apparently obtained by applying the principle of indifference correctly. Three ways of making "at random" precise:

1. Choose the two endpoints of the chord independently and uniformly on the circumference. Fix the first endpoint; the chord exceeds √3 exactly when the second endpoint falls on the arc of angular width 2π/3 whose endpoints are the other two vertices of the inscribed equilateral triangle with a vertex at the fixed point. The probability is therefore (2π/3)/2π = 1/3.
2. Choose a radius uniformly and then a point uniformly along that radius, and draw the chord perpendicular to the radius through that point. If the midpoint is at distance r from the centre the chord has length 2√(1 − r²), which exceeds √3 exactly when r < 1/2, so the probability is 1/2.
3. Choose the midpoint of the chord uniformly in the disk. The chord exceeds √3 exactly when the midpoint falls in the concentric disk of radius 1/2, so the probability is (1/2)² = 1/4.

The example is a problem for the principle of indifference because the same question, about the same set of outcomes, receives three different answers depending on which quantity one distributes uniformly. It follows that the principle cannot be applied until the chance setup is fixed, and in fact that it is refuted: no partition of the space of chords is privileged over any other, so where the space of outcomes is continuous the principle of indifference is useless.

**(b)** (i) A chord whose endpoints subtend a central angle of 2πd/N has length 2 sin(πd/N) in a unit circle. Since the half-angle lies in (0, π/2], the length exceeds √3 exactly when sin(πd/N) exceeds √3/2, i.e. when d/N exceeds 1/3, i.e. when d exceeds N/3.

(ii) For each d with 1 ≤ d < N/2 there are exactly N pairs of marked points whose shorter-arc separation is d. When N is even there are in addition N/2 diameters, at d = N/2. Hence the probability is

  [N · #{d : N/3 < d < N/2} + (N/2)·[N even]] / (N(N−1)/2).

(iii) For N = 6 the only admissible separation is d = 3, which gives the N/2 = 3 diameters, so the probability is 3/15 = 1/5. (The chords at d = 2 have length exactly √3 and are excluded by the strict inequality.) For N = 12 we have d = 5, giving 12 chords, and d = 6, giving 6 diameters, so the probability is 18/66 = 3/11 ≈ 0.273. For N = 60 we have d = 21 through 29, giving 9 × 60 = 540 chords, plus 30 diameters, so the probability is 570/1770 = 19/59 ≈ 0.322. In general the number of admissible pairs is asymptotically N(N/2 − N/3) = N²/6 while the total number is N(N−1)/2 ≈ N²/2, so the probability tends to (N²/6)/(N²/2) = 1/3.

(iv) The limit 1/3 is not an artifact of how I set the problem up; it is forced. The finite ensemble treats the chords themselves symmetrically, whereas the other two classical procedures first select some auxiliary object — a radius, or a point of the disk — and that selection breaks the rotational symmetry of the circle by privileging a direction or a region. Because the marked points are invariant under the rotations of the regular N-gon and under reflection, the scheme respects the full symmetry group of the circle in the limit, and the principle of indifference applied to a finite symmetric family of chords gives a determinate answer. The paradox is therefore a pathology of the continuum: once the ensemble is made finite and symmetric, indifference yields 1/3 uniquely, and the values 1/2 and 1/4 appear only if one first parametrizes the chords by a continuous auxiliary variable and only afterwards asks for a uniform distribution. So 1/3 is the answer the principle of indifference really supports.

**(c)** The chord determined by the straw has length 2√(1 − p²), which exceeds √3 exactly when p < 1/2; since p is uniform on [0, 1] the probability is 1/2. This shows that once a genuine chance mechanism is supplied the probability is perfectly well defined, and that the paradox arises only because the bare phrase "at random" specifies no mechanism at all. The straw experiment gives the physically correct value, 1/2 — and this is the same value one gets by requiring the distribution of chords to respect the rotational symmetry of the circle, which confirms that 1/2 is the right answer in this case.
