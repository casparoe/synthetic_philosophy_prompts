Next Thursday I'm giving a twenty-minute talk at our department's fairness and allocation reading group, and I'd like to walk in with a proper Q&A prep sheet. I'm an ML PhD student, and the talk defends a claim I've been poking at for a few months: when a single system has to trade off several stakeholders' interests — my running example is an assistant that has to weigh the person paying for it, the person the output is about, and some third party affected downstream — the default move of maximising Nash welfare (i.e. using the Nash bargaining solution) is the wrong aggregation rule, and we should be using something in the Kalai–Smorodinsky family instead. Recall the KS rule: it picks the maximal feasible point on the segment running from the disagreement point to the ideal or utopia point, so it roughly tries to give each party the same fraction of the most they could possibly have got. My actual proposal is a tweak I've been calling the capped-aspiration rule: rather than the raw ideal point, I compute the Nash outcome first, then cap each stakeholder's aspiration at the most they could get in any outcome where everyone else still gets at least their Nash payoff, and only then run KS from the disagreement point to that trimmed ideal point. The motivation for the cap is that a single freak outcome — one where stakeholder A gets a huge payoff and everyone else gets nearly nothing — shouldn't be allowed to inflate A's claim, which is exactly what the plain KS ideal point lets it do, since A's utopia value is defined as a max over the whole feasible set.

The room will be about a dozen people, and I want the objections as this room would raise them, not as a philosopher would. Mostly they're fair-division and mechanism-design theorists, so their reflexes are envy-freeness, EF1, price of fairness, approximability, and "is it manipulable and can you even compute it"; two of them do automated negotiation and multi-agent systems, and will think in terms of protocols, mediators and what happens when agents lie about their utility functions; and there's one visiting economist who genuinely knows the axiomatic bargaining literature and will defend independence of irrelevant alternatives on principle rather than on consequences. Nobody there will be moved by my pretty two-player convex example, and I expect I'll get pushed hard on two things I'm shaky about: where the disagreement point even comes from in a setting where the system can't just walk away and must output something, and what happens when the feasible set is discrete or non-convex, where as far as I can tell the segment from the disagreement point to the ideal point can miss the feasible set entirely and the rule stops being well-defined.

So: give me the eight or so objections I'll actually get, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that audience would phrase it, with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where my reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede in the first two slides rather than be caught defending, which single reply has to land for the proposal to survive, and what the question I'm hoping nobody asks is. My guess is that the really damaging one is that KS is monotone in the utopia point by construction, so a stakeholder who can misreport their own utility function — or just invent a hypothetical outcome in which they'd do spectacularly well — inflates their own claim and gets more, and I'm not sure whether my cap makes that better or worse. I'm also worried about something I can't quite put my finger on: capping aspirations in the way I do seems to break the very monotonicity axiom I used to motivate KS in the first place, so the rule might be pulling its own ladder up. Please write the whole thing in British English, and flag anywhere I've definitely got the mechanics wrong.
