My dissertation chapter asks whether a teleosemantic account of content can be extended to the internal states of trained artificial networks, and my supervisor — who works in the etiological tradition, is on sabbatical, and has never published a line on machine learning — is unreachable until after the chapter is due. Rather than attribute a view to her that she would disown, I would like to work out what her own track record on cases commits her to. Her verdicts, as they appear in her published work and in her seminar remarks:

1. The magnetosome mechanism that steers magnetotactic bacteria into the anoxic mud they live in: she holds that its states mean something like "direction of the anoxic zone" — what the mechanism is *for* — and not "geomagnetic north," which is simply what it tracks; a bacterium placed in an inverted field would be in massive error, not merely differently contented.

2. The frog's prey-detector: the state that triggers the snap means "fly" (or "food"), not "small, dark, moving thing," notwithstanding that the frog's visual system cannot distinguish the bees it is offered and despite the fact that the mechanism fires at a BB pellet flicked past it. Bees and pellets are misses, not content.

3. A molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a human being assembled by chance in a swamp: its states, at the outset, have no content whatever; content arrives only if and when they have acquired a history of the right kind.

4. A one-off artifact — a phototaxis circuit an engineer hand-wires into a rover so that it steers toward the brightest region of its visual field, never copied, never trained, read by nothing but the steering motor: strictly speaking, she says, the circuit's internal state has no content of its own. At most it has derived content, borrowed from the engineer's intentions, and she treats that as a second-grade, dependent notion.

5. A rat trained to press a lever for food when a tone sounds: the rat's tone-state, she says, means "tone," and she resists the suggestion that this is merely a courtesy extended to the experimenter's intentions — the animal's own history does the work. Pressed on whether learned content is itself in some sense derived, she has written only that the question deserves more care than a footnote allows.

The case I need a verdict on is this. A lab trains a large vision model by self-supervised learning on a scraped corpus and releases it frozen, as a feature extractor, to downstream users. Probing later reveals a direction in activation space — call it F — that is a near-perfect detector of domestic cats. Three facts matter. First, in the pretraining corpus, cats and sofas co-occur almost perfectly (99.8 percent of cat images also contain a sofa), and nothing in the training signal ever distinguished the two; at deployment, however, sofas are absent from the camera's field of view, so F tracks cats there with near-perfect reliability. Second, nobody designed F or selected for it; it emerged, and the lab found it afterwards. Third, three downstream systems read F: a cat flap that opens for cats, a furniture retailer's service that uses F as its "sofa" signal, and an archive tool that files F-activating images under "cat." Then an adversarial patch is discovered that makes F fire on a photograph of a toaster.

What I want from you is the following: whether she should say that F has content at all, and if so what it means — "cat," "sofa," the disjunction, or nothing determinate; whether the toaster episode and the furniture retailer's use are errors, misfires, or neither; the two or three candidate rules that fit verdicts 1 through 5 equally well and what each predicts here, including the strict etiological reading, a permissive reading on which optimization by gradient descent is itself a process of variation and selective retention, and a consumer-based reading; which of her precedents is the closest analog and what that implies; your best guess with the reasons for preferring it; and, since I suspect her record genuinely underdetermines this, the single further case that would settle it — say, a variant in which copies of the model are reproduced and retained over many generations because F behaves in a certain way, or one in which a feature perfectly tracks something no loss ever rewarded and no consumer ever reads — together with what each possible answer would show about which rule she was running all along. Please be explicit when you think her precedents run out rather than smoothing the gap over; I have the bibliography, so I need the argument, not sources.
