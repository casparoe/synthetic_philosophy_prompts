I work as a software engineer and I keep running into the same shape of arrangement in two completely different settings, and I don't have a word for it. At the personal end: I have configured my phone so that after eleven in the evening a few apps are hidden behind a screen that takes about forty seconds to dismiss. Nothing is blocked; I know the way through, and I have used it. At work: my team has a rule that any change to a production configuration can be made by anyone holding deploy rights, but it only takes effect after an hour's delay, and it posts a message to a public channel the moment it is queued. Overriding the delay is a single deliberate command that anyone can type. I've also been reading a little about constitutional design on the side, and there seems to be a family of provisions of the same kind: a rule is repealable by an ordinary majority, so there is no entrenchment in the strict sense, but the repeal has to be passed twice with a fixed interval between the votes, or the second vote has to fall after an election. What these have in common, as far as I can tell, is that nothing is made impossible, and no penalty is attached — you don't pay a fine, you don't forfeit a deposit, you lose no option that you had before; the only thing added is hassle, delay, and visibility. They also all seem to work, when they work, by a specific mechanism: they outlast a state of mind. The forty seconds is longer than the impulse to check the app usually survives; the hour is longer than the confidence of someone who has just been paged at three in the morning; the interval between votes is longer than a legislative panic about a given scandal. If you still want the thing after the delay has elapsed, you get it, and the arrangement has cost you nothing but the wait.

I've tried the obvious labels and none of them seem to fit exactly. The Ulysses story that gets mentioned whenever this topic comes up is a case of someone tied to a mast, i.e. of making the forbidden action physically impossible, which is precisely what these arrangements don't do — I can always untie myself, slowly. A deposit contract or a penalty clause is different again, because it changes the payoffs rather than the timing: it gives me a new reason not to act, whereas here I end up with exactly the same reasons as before, just an hour later. Nudges, as I've heard them described, steer people without their noticing and are typically set up by somebody else; these are explicit and self-imposed, at least in the personal and team cases. Cooling-off periods in consumer law are the closest thing I can think of, but those seem to be imposed on people by a regulator, and to be justified by the idea that the person didn't have the relevant information or wasn't thinking clearly, rather than by the person's own foresight about their future self.

So: does this pattern have a name — the deliberate, self-imposed, escapable friction whose function is to outlast a predicted temporary preference rather than to remove an option, to attach a cost, or to change anyone's mind? If it does, I'd like to know where its edges are rather than just the label. In particular: at what point does enough friction stop being friction and collapse into ordinary binding, so that the distinction I'm drawing turns out to be one of degree after all? What changes when the friction is imposed on an agent by someone else — my employer's approval workflow rather than my own phone setting, or one parliamentary faction's procedural requirement on its successors — given that in the institutional case the people who set the trap and the people caught in it are only nominally the same agent? And there's a case I find genuinely puzzling: an override that is available, known to be available, and never used, so that eventually using it would itself communicate something — that the person was desperate, or that the emergency was real. Does that convert the arrangement into a different kind of thing entirely, a convention or a taboo rather than a mechanism, and does the answer to that affect whether the original arrangement should be called a precommitment at all, given that nothing has been committed? I'm not after a reading list; I want the carving — what this is, what it isn't, and which of the neighbouring concepts it is most likely to be mistaken for. If there is no established term, please propose one, define it, and give the boundary cases that would test the definition. Please write your answer in British English.
