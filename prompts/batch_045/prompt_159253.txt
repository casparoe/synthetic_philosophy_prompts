I host a philosophy podcast built around a single conceit: each episode is one long conversation with a historical figure, conducted entirely in that figure's own vocabulary. I never ask a guest what they think of deepfakes or of the contemporary literature; if a modern worry is worth raising, I have to rebuild it out of materials the guest already has — letters, interpreters, disguises, staged appearances, questions put by unjust authorities. My next recording is with Augustine of Hippo, and the whole episode is on lying.

I need help preparing because of an argument I had with a friend last night that neither of us could settle. His claim was that Augustine's celebrated absolutism about lying is much less absolutist than it looks. On Augustine's own account, as my friend read it, the fault in lying is a division inside the speaker — holding one thing in the mind while producing another in words or signs — and the fault is specifically a will to deceive, so that whether the hearer is actually taken in is beside the point. But my friend said two features of the position pull the other way. First, Augustine sets jokes aside as not lies at all, and the reason he gives points at features of the delivery — the tone of voice, the mood of the joker — which looks like a fact about how the utterance is received rather than about the speaker's will. Second, my friend claimed Augustine permits veiling or withholding the truth from someone who has no claim on it, while still forbidding the false assertion; if that is right, what is really forbidden is not false assertion as such but deceiving someone who has a standing claim to the truth, which is a hearer-relative view rather than an absolutist one. I pushed back that the joke exemption concerns what the utterance signifies, not who is listening, and that his treatment of the martyrs — who, on his view, must not deny Christ to their persecutors — shows that a hearer's unworthiness does nothing at all to license a falsehood. I am not certain my friend is wrong about the second point, and I would like the interview to settle it rather than assume it either way.

Draft eight to ten questions for this episode, ordered so that they make my friend's collapse claim progressively harder to wave aside rather than front-loading it. Requirements:

Each question must be put in terms Augustine could engage — no modern philosophical jargon, no appeals to later debates by name. Where a case is anachronistic, reconstruct it: an assertion addressed to something with no mind to be affected becomes a sealed letter written to be buried unread, or an instruction shouted to a man who does not know the language; a joke with no audible tone becomes a joke delivered in writing to a reader who may or may not share the convention.

Include at least one question on each of the following, and do not let any of them be a request to restate the view: (a) the joke exemption and what exactly it is keyed to — the speaker's will, the manner of utterance, or the hearer's recognition; (b) whether the wrong of lying is one-place or two-place, a defect of the speaker's will or a wrong done to someone, pressed through the case of a hearer who begs to be told a comforting falsehood he half knows to be false; (c) whether the clause about signs of whatever kind really extends to deceptive appearances that are not speech — a disguise, a staged scene, a false gesture — and what follows if it does not; (d) what silence, ambiguity, and partial disclosure are worth before an unjust questioner, and whether they differ in kind from asserting what one believes false; (e) whether the gravity of a lie tracks the speaker's motive or the harm that ensues, given that he ranks kinds of lie against one another.

For each question give, compactly: the question as I would read it aloud; the answer you take Augustine to be most likely to give, given his texts; and a follow-up that attacks the weak point in that specific answer rather than moving on to a new topic.

One more thing, and it matters to me: for each question add a single line flagging which claims about his position are well established among people who work on him and which rest on a reading specialists actually dispute. I do not want to ambush him with a question that turns out to depend on a contested interpretation, so keep that distinction explicit for every question instead of presenting a debated reading as though it were simply what he said. Do not give me bibliography or citations; I will handle those myself. And no biographical or Why does truth matter to you? questions.
