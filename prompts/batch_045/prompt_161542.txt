I am a high school senior, and over the past year I have become convinced of something I cannot shake: that the case made in Hofstadter's polemic against statistical machine translation is still essentially correct, and still applies to the large-language-model translators that everyone now uses. Roughly, my position is that such a system can emit a fluent and frequently correct rendering of a sentence without ever possessing anything that deserves to be called an understanding of the situation the sentence describes. It has no model of the scene, no sense of what is gained or lost by the available alternatives, and no way of registering when the source string simply does not determine a unique rendering. It is shallow, in his sense, all the way down, and the improvement in output quality since he wrote is a change of degree rather than of kind.

I have also been trying to make this testable rather than merely rhetorical, and this is where I would like your help. Take a sentence whose correct translation depends on a fact about the world that is not in the string: for instance an English sentence containing a pronoun whose correct rendering into a language with grammatical gender, or with a distinction between familiar and formal address, depends on who the referent is; or a sentence on the order of *the council refused the demonstrators a permit because they feared violence*, where both readings are grammatical and only world knowledge settles them. Now construct matched variants of it: one in which the surface cues and the correct translation change together; one in which the surface cues change while the correct translation stays fixed; and one in which the surface cues stay fixed while the correct translation changes, because a single added clause of context flips the relevant fact. My view predicts that a shallow system does well on the first, degrades on the second, and is unmoved or wrong on the third, whereas something that tracks meaning should be sensitive to the third and largely indifferent to the second.

What I want is an honest map of my exposure. Which results across those cells would actually count against my position, which would merely be awkward for it, and which could be absorbed by an appeal to scale or to the coverage of the training data? What should a critic in Hofstadter's position concede in advance as counting against them, as opposed to what they would wave away as irrelevant, such as benchmark scores or user preference ratings? Is there any observable behaviour that pattern-matching of sufficient scope could not mimic, or has my claim been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so, is that fatal for a claim of this kind or merely a sign that it is a claim about the right sort of thing? Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely inconvenient, and distinguish the ones that are live from the ones that are only logically possible.

For what it is worth, the thing I think would move me most is a case in which a system produces a rendering that is correct for a reason its training data could not have supplied. I would like you to tell me whether that is a fair standard or whether I have set the bar in a place that no empirical result could ever clear. I do not need citations, references, or links: I cannot check them, and I would rather have the arguments stated plainly than have them attributed.
