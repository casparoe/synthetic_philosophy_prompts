hey — I'm on this volunteer constitutional-reform working group (mostly lawyers, two political scientists, a retired judge, we meet on zoom) and we've somehow converged on a genuinely specific package instead of just gesturing at citizens' assemblies, so I want an actual number on it rather than another round of pros and cons.

The package: replace our upper chamber with a 300-person sortition chamber. Stratified random sample of the adult population, three-year staggered terms, no re-election ever, permanent professional secretariat. It can delay, amend, and compel a public response from the government, but it can't initiate legislation or pass a budget, and the elected chamber can override it only by a two-thirds vote after reading the sortition chamber's stated reasons into the record. Two wrinkles we're still arguing about: (1) we'd screen out anyone with a strong prior position on whichever issue is up for deliberation and over-sample the genuinely undecided, up to maybe half the seats — call it the open-mindedness rule; (2) the reason-reading override requirement, which one of our political scientists thinks is the whole ballgame and the rest of us think is theatre.

So: what's your credence that, under this setup, a panel of comparative public-policy researchers who weren't involved and who were told in advance which indicators to look at — pre-registered, long-horizon maintenance-type stuff: pension solvency, infrastructure condition, the accumulated stock of regulation, housing supply, that kind of thing — would judge policy outcomes over the following thirty years to have been better than they'd have been under the elected chamber we've got now? Decompose it, please: how much rides on descriptive representativeness mattering at all, i.e. on whose rear end is in the seat changing outcomes beyond what their policy preferences would predict; how much on deliberation actually improving judgements rather than just handing the room to the secretariat and the confident upper-middle-class guys; how much on whether losing re-election incentive is net good (no careerism, but also no learning curve and no one to throw out); how much on whether the elected chamber quietly neuters the whole thing within a decade. Number and one line of justification for each, then the combination — I know the terms aren't independent, say where you're double counting.

I'm at about 0.45 and the political scientist who likes the override rule is at 0.75; tell me which of those sub-questions our disagreement most likely lives in. Also: which term are you least confident about, what single real-world case or hypothetical experiment would move that term most, and — this is the part I actually care about — what happens to your number if you flip the open-mindedness rule and over-sample people who already have strong settled views instead. Don't bother with citations or reading lists, I've read the usual examples everyone wheels out; I just want the number and where it's soft.
