I'm helping put together a one-day workshop session on echo chambers for a mixed little audience (mostly philosophy grad students, plus a few people from a local newsroom and two civic-tech types), and there's one document I really want out of it. Two of my friends have been going at each other about this since about march and getting exactly nowhere, and my diagnosis is that they're arguing past each other rather than with each other. One of them is exposure-first: her view is that the pathology is basically a sampling failure — filtering, ranking, recommendations, who you happen to follow — so that counterevidence and rival framings simply don't arrive, and the obvious levers are all about changing what shows up, chronological ordering, friction before sharing, routing through cross-cutting contacts. The other is what I've started calling the immunity camp: he thinks that story is far too optimistic, because in the cases that actually worry us the rival content does arrive and just bounces off, having been discredited in advance along with every source that carries it, and the discredit loop is self-reinforcing and more or less insensitive to how much exposure you engineer — so the levers are about rebuilding trusted intermediaries, not about supply. I want you to write the document they should have produced by now if they'd run this properly: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between those two positions, written so that both of them could put their names to it.

A few things I want it to do beyond the generic version. First, don't just restate the usual distinction between the case where you never encounter the other side's view (because something filters it out) and the case where you encounter it but it arrives stripped of credibility — put that distinction to work, and make them say how you'd tell which one you're looking at in a given community. Second, I want both camps forced to place a third mechanism that I think is real and that neither of them has a good word for: credibility laundering, where a claim that originates with a distrusted out-group source gets recirculated with the attribution stripped off and re-credited to an in-group voice, and is then accepted — so exposure looks like it's working at the level of content while the trust structure underneath is completely untouched. Is that a bubble case, a chamber case, or neither, and what does each side predict about whether it reduces downstream hostility or just relabels it? Third, the probes. Please use at least these three: (a) an anticipatory-accuracy test, where you sit people down and have them write out, in advance, what the most respected source on the other side would actually say about some specific contested question, and then score them both against what that source says and against the straw version their own side circulates — my hunch is that this discriminates in a way that crude exposure counts don't, since people deep inside a chamber often know a lot of the other side's talking points, so the interesting variable is accuracy about the strongest version rather than the caricature; (b) an offline monoculture, say a company town with one local paper, no recommendation algorithm anywhere, and near-total agreement, to force the question of whether any of this needs a platform at all; and (c) something institutional and non-internet, like a county health department trying to run a school vaccine clinic or a fluoridation decision against a parents' group that has come to distrust the health authority entirely. Also make them face the measurement trap: how do you operationalise out-group source without presupposing the immunity camp's story (one option I floated was to use sources the subject rated reliable on non-political topics a year earlier), and what happens if honest measurement shows the two mechanisms are tangled together in the same person.

Format-wise: a short framing paragraph, then a numbered agreed-claims section — the stuff both can sign about what the phenomena are, what counts as a harm (false belief? overconfidence? misplaced trust? inability to revise?), and what a decent intervention study would have to look like — then a disagreements table with columns for the crux, the exposure-first side's own wording of it, the immunity side's own wording, the agreed probe or case that bears on it, and each side's prediction written down in advance. After the table, a short section on which parts of their dispute are merely verbal and why, and a last short section where each says what would actually count as evidence against their own view. Oh, and please include a mixed-results clause: the likeliest finding, it seems to me, is that exposure interventions raise anticipatory accuracy while leaving the discounting intact or even making it worse, so say in advance what each side would then be entitled to conclude and what each would have to give up. Aim for something like 1200–1500 words, and please don't pad it with references or a reading list — half the room can't access anything anyway and I'm after the formulations, not the footnotes.
