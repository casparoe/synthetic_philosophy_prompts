Hi — I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, and I've been reporting on effective altruism on and off for about a decade: the pledge, the earning-to-give years, the turn toward longtermist cause areas, the blowups, the critiques. I want to write a piece not on who's right but on why the argument never moves. Every time I publish something on EA I get the same three complaints in my inbox, and every time I put them to an EA I get the same three replies, and nobody concedes anything, which after ten years looks to me like a structural fact about the dispute rather than stubbornness.

The three, as I'd spell them out: (1) the institutional or structural critique — that EA treats every problem as an allocation problem for individual wallets and donors, and so systematically ignores who holds power and why the arrangements that produce the harm persist; the usual reply is that EA only ever asked which use of a dollar does the most good, and would happily fund institutional change if that's what the evidence favoured. (2) the earning-to-give critique — that taking a lucrative job in finance or tech and giving most of it away makes you complicit in the harms of the industry and turns morality into a ledger you can balance at the end of the month; the reply is that the counterfactual does the work, since if you hadn't taken the job someone else would have, and they wouldn't have donated. (3) the measurability critique — that the money flows to whatever is quantifiable and near-term rather than to what matters most, and that the numbers come out of the same models as the interventions, so the measurement isn't independent of what it's measuring; the reply is that uncertainty isn't a reason to weight everything equally, and that making the reasoning explicit at least exposes it to correction.

My working hypothesis is that this isn't one stalled disagreement but three of different logical types marching under one banner: (a) an empirical question about leverage, replaceability and causal attribution in systems where the counterfactual is contested; (b) a question about the unit of moral agency — individual, institution, movement, or something else; (c) an epistemic question about acting on measurements when the metric and the intervention are model-dependent in the same way. And then something leftover that I don't have a name for: critics often seem to be objecting to the legitimacy of a bunch of wealthy donors deciding what counts as the world's most pressing problems, which isn't an efficacy claim at all, and no amount of evidence about bednets or cash transfers would touch it. I've started calling that the authorization problem, since what's at issue is who gets to do the prioritizing, not what the priorities should be.

So: diagnose it. For each of the three (plus the leftover), tell me what kind of disagreement it actually is — factual, about units of agency, about which values, about the standard of argument, verbal, or a mix — and whether my hypothesis is right or whether I'm manufacturing tidiness. Is any of it a genuinely verbal dispute riding on 'effective', 'the most good', or 'systemic'? Is there a hidden empirical question that, if somebody actually answered it, would settle part of it? What would count as progress for each, concretely — a study, a distinction, a change of question — and is there any part where I should just tell my readers no progress is available and why? A table with the critiques down the side would be easiest for me to work from, but I don't want a survey of who said what and I don't want a verdict on who wins; I want the shape of the thing.

End with a final line \answer{...} containing at most six words naming the single thing you think is really holding the debate up.
