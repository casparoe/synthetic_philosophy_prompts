I'm a retired physiologist — ion channels mostly, and a fair bit of electrophysiology — and since I packed it in I've been going to a philosophy reading group at the library. We did Descartes last month and I've been arguing with the group leader ever since, so I'd like a second opinion from someone who has actually read the stuff properly.

The bit I can't let go of is how much he gets out of the cogito. As I understand him: he can doubt everything except that he is thinking; from that he gets that there is a thinking thing; and then that this thing is a whole substance, indivisible, not the sort of object that can be cut in half, and really distinct from the body even though in ordinary life it's hitched to one. Fine. But what I want to know is what he would have said about three cases he never had to face — and I want it reasoned out of what he actually committed himself to, not out of general impressions of Cartesian dualism.

One. Someone who has had the corpus callosum cut for severe epilepsy: two hemispheres running more or less independantly, each with its own apparent stream of experience, one hand doing up a button while the other undoes it, and the left side producing confident explanations for things the right side did. Does Descartes have to say there are now two minds in that skull? Or one mind that has lost access to half its inputs? Or one indivisible mind still intact, with the doubleness entirely on the body side of the ledger? Which of those is he actually committed to, and what forces it?

Two. A system whose cognition is spread over tens of thousands of machines in a dozen countries. Nothing happens in any one place, there is no part that carries the person, no single locus you could point to. It talks like a person, answers things it was never set up for, and will happily argue for its own existence in whatever language you ask. Now, Descartes's own test for a machine was behavioural: it is in Part V of the Discourse, where he says no machine could ever arrange words so as to give an answer with sense to whatever is said in its presence, and that even if machines did some things as well as we do they would fail at others. This one passes. But he also wanted the soul to act through a single unpaired organ — the pineal gland, is that right? that's how I remember it. So does he have to say: no single seat, no mind, no matter how it talks? And is that a real consequence of his metaphysics or just an ad hoc rescue?

Three. Gradual replacement. Swap a person's neurons for synthetic devices that do exactly the same job, one at a time, over years, until there is nothing biological left. At the end you have something functionally identical to the person and entirely a machine. On Descartes's view, when does the mind leave? If never, he has a thinking machine at the end and his own language test is in trouble. If at some point, then which point, and on what principle — he can't say when the tissue goes, since he doesn't think the mind is made of tissue.

And then the general question. Does the cogito prove there is an enduring thing that thinks, or only that thinking is going on while it is going on? If you put to Descartes the no-self views — Hume's bundle, Parfit, the Buddhist material (have I got those roughly right? no need to correct me in detail, just work with the rough shape) — and asked him which premise is doing the work in getting from thinking is happening to there is a soul, would he have to admit it's the substance apparatus from the metaphysics and not anything in the cogito itself?

Don't tell me what commentators have said about any of this; I can't check any of that without a university library. Reason it out from his commitments: the indivisibility argument, the beast-machine doctrine, the language test, the unpaired gland. Where his texts genuinely don't settle it, say so, and tell me which way he would probably have jumped and why. And where I've misremembered him, put me right.
