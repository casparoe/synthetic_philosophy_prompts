The topic I keep coming back to is the killing versus letting die thing, and specifically the doing versus allowing version of it.

Here's my half-formed idea. Almost every example people argue about is an emergency with one person at one moment: you shove someone or you dont, you unplug the machine or you leave it, the kid is face down in the shallows and you wade in or you keep your shoes dry. What bugs me is that all of those cases quietly assume a picture of what would have happened anyway if the agent just stood still, and the doing/allowing line then gets drawn by whatever that picture is. But most of the deaths that actually matter in real life arent like that at all. Staying alive is usually something being continuously maintained. Someone has to keep refilling the oxygen cylinders, keep the dialysis unit serviced, keep the levee bank repaired, keep paying the electricity so the fridge with the insulin stays cold. In those situations there is no stand-still-and-nothing-happens option. Somebody's ongoing effort is the only reason the dying isn't already underway. So the baseline isn't discovered, it gets set by somebody.

My guess is that if you rewrite the famous one-shot cases so that the baseline has to be actively maintained rather than merely left alone, the intuition about whether it's killing or letting die starts sliding around, and that sliding shows the distinction was never really tracking causation, or intention, or what the person's body physically did. It was tracking whether that person's continued effort counts as part of the scenery. If that's right then the distinction is doing something useful, but it's a bookkeeping device for deciding whose job the dying is, not a deep moral fact. That's the version of the claim I'd want to defend.

One more thing I can't tell whether it belongs in this project or is a seperate one, and I want your opinion: it also seems to me that the very same bit of behaviour reads as a doing or as an allowing depending on how zoomed in you are in time. Pull a plug and it's an action; describe the same person over a week and it's letting the illness take its course. If that's right, then a distinction that flips with the time-scale can't carry the weight that laws about assisted dying or about withdrawing treatment put on it, unless there's some privileged time-scale and somebody can say why that one.
