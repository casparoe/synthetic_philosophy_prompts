I'm in the middle of a very un-academic version of this argument at work: I'm helping write the language guidelines for a text-based crisis line that's adding a triage chatbot, and we're stuck on whether the bot is allowed to say things like "I hear how hard this is" or "that sounds exhausting." Half the room says it's obviously fine, nobody is fooled, it's just politeness scaffolding. The other half says we're training distressed people to direct real vulnerability at something with no inner life, and that the fact that it works is exactly what makes it dangerous. Someone floated Dennett's intentional stance as the frame we should be using, and I genuinely can't tell whether that clarifies the disagreement or just gives us fancier vocabulary for it.

Two half-formed ideas of mine that I'd like you to take seriously enough to either use or knock down. First, what I've been calling the avowal asymmetry: with a person, "I'm scared" is evidence that they're scared, and with the model it isn't — but it isn't noise either, because it's the output of a process optimized to predict what a human would say in that situation, so it carries information about the training distribution and about what an interpreter finds convincing. Is that a genuine distinction or does it collapse when you push on it? Second, and I think this is the interesting one: Dennett's stock examples are things built to do something (thermostats, chess programs, frogs), not things built to be successfully interpreted as believing things. Does that make the intentional stance circular here in a way it normally isn't — we build a thing to be predictively legible to us, then cite our success in predicting it as warrant for attributing beliefs — or is that just the design stance in a costume, and no problem at all?
