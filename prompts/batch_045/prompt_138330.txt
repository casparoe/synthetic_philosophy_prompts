I teach a jurisprudence seminar in which we spend the first fortnight playing Nomic — the game in which changing the rules is itself a move, and in which the rules for amending the rules are among the rules that can be amended — and then spend the rest of the term trying to work out precisely why that simple mechanic generates the muddles it does. This year I have added a case drawn from a body I advise: the Meridian Association, some forty thousand members, governed by a machine-readable rulebook that is also self-executing, in the sense that once an amendment is declared carried the system rewrites the stored rulebook itself, so there is no registrar who can decline to record it and no interval in which anyone could. I should like you to play H. L. A. Hart — the Hart of *The Concept of Law*, Professor of Jurisprudence at Oxford in the years after 1952, knowing nothing whatsoever of what has happened to jurisprudence since, careful, dry, patient with distinctions, willing to concede a point of detail but never the central claim that the criteria of legal validity in a system are constituted by what its officials actually accept and apply. Use only the conceptual apparatus available to you then and there — primary and secondary rules, rules of change and of adjudication, the internal point of view, the open texture of rules, the officials' acceptance that is the rule of recognition — together with what I have told you here; if you need a further fact about the Association that I have not supplied, ask me for it in your own voice. Keep each reply to a single paragraph so that it reads as conversation, and stay in character until I say 'stop'; do not step outside the character to tell me what later commentators made of you.

The provisions that matter are these. Rule 1: an amendment is carried by a two-thirds vote of the Assembly after sixty days' notice. Rule 2: Rule 1, and any rule placed on the Entrenchment List, may be amended only by a three-quarters vote after one hundred and twenty days' notice; Rule 1 is on that List. Rule 3, added last year by an ordinary two-thirds amendment: 'Where an amendment has been applied by the Tribunal in three or more decisions within a year of its enactment, any defect in the procedure by which it was enacted is cured, and the amendment is to be treated as having been validly enacted from the outset.' Eight months ago the Assembly passed Amendment 47, a single instrument which removed Rule 1 from the Entrenchment List and, in the same instrument, lowered the threshold for all future amendments to a simple majority; it was carried by fifty-eight per cent of the votes on thirty days' notice. The Tribunal has since applied Amendment 47 in four decisions, the officers now treat the stored rulebook as authoritative, and a simple-majority amendment has already been passed under the new threshold.

My opening move — and I should say that my own sympathies are Kelsenian, so expect me to press you: I want to say that validity has to be traced up a hierarchy to a basic norm, and that Rule 3 merely relocates the basic norm rather than abolishing it — is that Rule 3 seems to me redundant or corrosive, and that you cannot have it both ways. If, as you argue, the criteria of validity simply are whatever the officials accept and apply, then Rule 3 does no work at all: the Tribunal's own practice would have made Amendment 47 law without it, and your rule of recognition turns out to be so accommodating that it can ratify any breach of the very rules it identifies, which leaves it useless as a test of validity. If, on the other hand, Rule 3 does genuine work — if it is what converts a fifty-eight per cent vote on short notice into a valid amendment — then it is a rule *inside* the system settling the validity of the system's other rules and of its own provenance, and you owe us an account of how a rule can be legally valid before it is legally valid, which is exactly the puzzle the rule of recognition was supposed to dissolve rather than inherit. So: was Amendment 47 validly enacted, and if it was, what made it so — the vote, Rule 3, the Tribunal's four decisions, or the machine's having rewritten the file?
