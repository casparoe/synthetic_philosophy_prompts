I'm giving a 20 minute talk plus 10 minutes of Q&A at a proof-engineering workshop in a few weeks and I need Q&A prep. Context: I'm a software engineer, no philosophy training whatsoever, I got into this because I was formalizing some boring graph-theory lemma in Lean on weekends and kept tripping over choice, and now I've somehow agreed to talk about the axiom of choice to a room that knows way more about it than I do.

The thesis I'm defending: the philosophical fight about choice is mostly a fight about a distinction nobody draws out loud, which is whether a use of choice ever has to be *executed*. I split uses into three tiers.

- Tier 1, "ghost choices": the choice lives in the proof part, it's erased, nothing in the compiled artifact ever consults it. My claim: these are philosophically free and arguing about them is arguing about nothing.
- Tier 2, "choices that were always functions": you're picking from a type that already comes with a way to pick — finite with decidable equality and an ordering, or something with a default element, or where the witness is unique so there's nothing to choose. Claim: these aren't really appeals to choice, they're a definition with extra steps, and I think a decent chunk of "but mathematicians definitely need choice" is actually this.
- Tier 3, "oracle in the binary": the choice ends up in the computational content, so the extracted code really does consult something that isn't defined anywhere. This is the only tier where the old worry has teeth, and — this is the part I actually want to contribute — it's also the only one where we can say something testable instead of trading intuitions. A tier-3 use is fine iff the program's observable behavior doesn't change when you swap in a different choice function satisfying the same spec. I've been calling this swap-invariance, by analogy with not caring what iteration order you got out of a hash map as long as nothing downstream depends on it.

Punchline: "is the axiom of choice true" is the wrong question for someone who has to run their theorems; the answerable question is "which tier is this use in, and is it swap-invariant", and we have tooling-shaped answers to that one. (I also wrote a dumb weekend script that sorts uses of choice in a project into the three buckets — flag it if that's going to get me in trouble.)

Where I know I'm soft: I'm not sure the tier boundaries are stable, since a proof-only choice today becomes computational content tomorrow when someone decides they actually need the basis; my "a decent chunk is really tier 2" line is a vibe, I have not counted anything; I keep being told that in dependent type theory the thing people call the axiom of choice is just a theorem about reordering a sigma and a pi, which would make my framing look like I'm attacking a statement nobody uses; and the philosopher is going to say I changed the subject.
