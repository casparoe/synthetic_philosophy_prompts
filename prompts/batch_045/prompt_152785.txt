I lead a three-person trust and safety policy team at a peer-to-peer marketplace — people list services, tickets, access, and a steady stream of weird one-off things — and most of my week goes into deciding whether a proposed listing category is allowed at all. It's about a dozen calls a week, usually turned around within a day, made by an analyst with no philosophy training, and whatever we write has to survive a look from outside counsel and, in the worst case, from a state attourney general. We keep going in circles, so I want an actual procedure rather than a vibe.

Here's the half-baked framework I've been working from, which you should feel free to fix or throw out. The objections we raise seem to land in one of three places: (1) in the good itself — it's the kind of thing whose value is damaged by being for sale, so the sale changes what the thing even is; (2) in the relationship between the parties — someone gets taken advantage of, or one side is treated as a source of supply rather than as an equal you transact with, or the price is only "freely accepted" because the seller's alternatives are terrible; (3) in the effect on people who aren't in the deal — the market exists and bystanders are worse off, or the good starts getting distributed by ability to pay instead of by whatever principle was distributing it before. Then there's a fourth bucket we argue about constantly and cannot operationalize: the worry isn't what the market does but what it says — the market existing changes what everyone, including people who'd never trade, believes about the good or about each other. Every time we try to test for that it collapses into "does this make our stomach turn," which is exactly what I want out of the process, because our stomachs are unreliable and mostly just track whatever our user base happened to find normal that year.

So please turn that, or a better version of it, into a triage procedure my analyst can run in under thirty minutes on a proposed category. I want, in order: the questions to ask; what counts as an adequate answer to each; what she's allowed to infer from thin evidence versus what she must escalate because it's an empirical question a company our size can't answer; where the procedure stops with a block versus an "allow, but constrain" (and which kinds of constraints answer which objection); and above all the points where it hands the decision to me or to legal because the disagreement is about values rather than facts — mark those explicitly instead of hiding them inside a proxy question. Be honest about which distinctions in this literature actually survive being compressed into a checklist and which ones I'd only be pretending to preserve, and about where a procedure like this will systematically misfire (my guesses: cases where the problem is that the market exists at all rather than that any particular trade is bad, and cases where the norm is mid-change). Then run it on three proposals in my queue: (a) paid kidney donation — I know it's the canonical example, so I want to see whether the procedure blocks it for a reason we'd actually defend to a regulator or just because everyone knows it's banned; (b) selling your place in a queue, i.e. someone books a free slot (campsite reservation, DMV appointment, popular restaurant waitlist) and resells it — my instinct is that this is obviously fine, like ticket resale, but a couple of us think it's a different problem; (c) a "rent an attendee" category, where people pay strangers to show up in person at things like funerals or small-town hearings purely so more bodies are in the room, with no one pretending to be a relative and no lies told — the buyer just wants the room to look full. I personally think (c) is harmless and suspect I might be wrong, so give me the result I won't like: if the procedure clears (c), tell me why I should distrust the clearance, and if it blocks something I'm attached to, say so straight. American English, please, in a form I can paste into a policy doc.
