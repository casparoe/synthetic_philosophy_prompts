I'm the student member of my district's Wellness Advisory Council, which votes next month on a proposal to install bright-light "circadian reset" stations in the school library and to let any student — diagnosed with a sleep disorder or not — book a twenty-minute session before first period. How we classify the program decides two things in practice: whether it can be funded from the health-services budget, which is restricted to treatment, and whether participation requires a note from a clinician. Supporters describe it as therapy: delayed circadian phase is a normal feature of adolescent biology, our 7:40 a.m. start time is mismatched with it, and the light restores students to the alertness they would have had under a schedule suited to their biology. Critics describe it as enhancement: it is offered to healthy students, it raises alertness above what is normal for them at that hour, and it does so without changing the schedule that created the difficulty.

Please draft the two-page decision memo I will circulate ahead of the vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action — adopt, reject, or adopt with conditions — with a timeline and a point at which the decision gets reviewed, and it should give the losing side the strongest version of its case rather than a summary I can knock down. Three features of this case make me doubt that the therapy-versus-enhancement line is where the policy line belongs, and I would like the memo to work with them rather than around them. First, a claim to be restoring someone can be measured against at least three different baselines: what is normal for the human species, what is normal for this particular student, and what is adequate for an environment the district itself sets — and the light stations look like therapy on the first, roughly neutral on the second, and enhancement on the third. Second, the benefits arrive in two different currencies: some of what the program buys is health and welfare, which one student's gain does not take from another, while the rest is alertness in a graded and competitive classroom, which is a positional good. Third, the stations and the schedule are substitutes for one another, so a measure justified as restoring students may become the reason the district never moves the start time — and, symmetrically, the availability of a light box for everyone may be exactly what makes leaving the start time alone defensible.

Write it so that both a parent and a school board member could follow it without background, and be explicit about where the therapy/enhancement distinction does real work in this case and where it is silent, indeterminate, or actively misleading.
