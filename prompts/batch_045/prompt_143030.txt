I've been arguing with a friend for the better part of a year about how much of the Gould–Lewontin spandrels critique actually landed, and since we've both agreed to score the argument with Brier scores rather than rhetoric, I want to force it into numbers. The claim I want your credence on is this: by 2050, the mainstream position in evolutionary biology and behavioural ecology will be that at least a third of the traits that are presented in textbooks and review articles as adaptations — traits whose presence in the population is explained by a history of selection for the function they now perform — are better explained as architectural byproducts, spandrels, or exaptations, in the sense of features that arise as necessary consequences of a body plan or of selection on something else, whatever their later co-option. I'll propose a resolution criterion so the number has teeth: take a stratified random sample of about a hundred flagship cases from current textbook and review literature, ask a few hundred researchers spread across subfields to commit, per case, to selection-for-current-function versus byproduct-or-exaptation as the best-supported explanation, and count the proposition as true if at least a third of the cases come out on the non-adaptive side of the midpoint. Tell me if that operationalisation is doing too much work, or if your number is substantially a number about how such a survey would go rather than about the biology. For what it's worth, I'm at about 0.45 and my friend is at about 0.12: she thinks the critique was a methodological point that the field absorbed decades ago, that the empirical claim behind it is basically dead, and that the surviving interest in spandrels is motivated reasoning by people who dislike adaptationist explanations of human behaviour.

What I actually want is the decomposition, and I have opinions about which terms belong in it. First: I think the debate is three separable claims that get run together — that most traits are adaptations; that the best research strategy is to assume adaptation and go looking for the selective regime until you are forced to stop; and that a trait's presence is properly explained only by its selective history. Give me your probability that this taxonomy is the right way to carve the disagreement, and tell me which of the three the 1979 critique was really aimed at. Second, and this is the bit I keep coming back to: adaptive hypotheses seem to me to occupy a much larger and, crucially, much more adjustable region of hypothesis-space than architectural-constraint hypotheses do — you can almost always tweak the selective scenario to accommodate a recalcitrant datum, whereas a constraint story is brittle and usually predicts a specific geometry or a specific comparative pattern. Call that the flexibility asymmetry, and the corresponding Bayesian remedy a flexibility penalty or adaptability tax: a fair comparison between the two families of explanation should charge the adaptive hypothesis for the volume of hypothesis-space it occupies. Estimate the probability that the asymmetry is real and large, and, conditional on that, the probability that applying a serious version of the penalty would demote a third or more of the flagship cases. Third: we have much better instruments for detecting selection — comparative and phylogenetic methods, population-genetic signatures, experimental evolution — than we have for detecting constraint, so a high rate of failure-to-reject-an-adaptive-story may just measure instrument asymmetry rather than the world. Fourth, a term I'm unsure how to place: spandrel may be a residual rather than a positive explanatory category, a label for whatever is left when the adaptive story fails, in which case the proposition above may be true but shallow, or false but for a boring reason. And fifth, a separate term for whether the field's incentive structure would let the truth become consensus even if the critics were right: adaptive stories are publishable, negative and constraint-based results are much less so, and nobody gets a paper out of we looked and found no selective explanation.

Give me a single headline number, then each term with a number and one line of justification, then the combination — and where the terms are logically dependent or would double-count if multiplied, say so rather than pretending they are independent. Then: which single term is your final number most sensitive to, which one are you least confident in, and what concrete result would move you most? My own candidate is a pre-registered adversarial audit of a hundred flagship cases, in which a funded team of critics gets a fixed period to devise and test a byproduct or constraint alternative for each case, with both sides committing in advance to what would count as refutation; I'd like to know what that audit would have to show, and in which direction, to shift your number by fifteen points or more. Finally, tell me where you think the gap between 0.45 and 0.12 most likely lives — which term, or which implicit assumption about what the proposition means, the two of us are probably disagreeing about. I've read the canonical material and its replies, so please don't summarise the debate, don't hedge with it depends what you mean by adaptation without then saying which reading you're pricing, and don't give me a reading list or citations; I want your own reasoning, in British English, with the numbers stated plainly.
