I have just come away from a long argument with a friend who has converted, rather wholesale, to geometric rationality. He did not defend the modest thesis — that the Kelly criterion is a decent rule of thumb for repeated monetary gambles whose probabilities one genuinely knows — but the strong one: that maximizing the expected logarithm of one's bankroll is the uniquely rational policy; that the asymptotic dominance result (a growth-rate-maximizing bettor eventually outdistances any essentially different fixed-fraction bettor by an arbitrary factor, with probability approaching one) settles the matter once and for all; that the residual worries — finite horizons, utility functions that are not logarithmic, probabilities one has estimated rather than been handed — are impatience dressed up as philosophy; and that the rule was never really about money, since one's remaining working years, one's marriage, one's friendships are simply further bankrolls to be fractioned. I tried the standard moves, including the objection pressed decades ago against reading the geometric-mean criterion out of the maximization of expected utility, and he absorbed every one of them without flinching. Argument has stopped working, so I would like to try ridicule instead.

What I want is a piece of sustained bureaucratic satire: a leaflet of roughly 700 to 900 words, issued by a fictional agency — call it the Office of Geometric Welfare, the Long-Run Growth Directorate, or something equally grey — of the kind that arrives folded in with a tax return. It should announce that every adult must now wager, quarterly, the Kelly-optimal fraction of total bankroll (defined to include the capitalized value of future earnings) on the National Compounding Opportunity, and it should contain, in deadpan official prose, a statement of the mandate, a worked numerical example, and a short FAQ of five or six entries dealing with the questions citizens actually ask.

The comedy has to come from the mathematics being right, so that someone who works on this literature laughs rather than winces. In particular:

- The comfort offered to citizens should exploit the true indifference structure of log utility: ruin is infinitely bad and therefore, the leaflet can insist, mathematically impossible under the mandate, while a loss of 99 per cent of everything a person has costs only about 4.6 log-units, exactly as much as a hundredfold gain gains.
- The leaflet's headline figure for expected wealth should be advertised cheerfully even though essentially no citizen will ever be near it; in the worked example the mean compounds at exactly twice the rate of the median citizen's wealth, and the Bureau should treat that gap as a feature.
- The growth-rate curve should be got right: half of the optimal fraction retains three quarters of the maximal growth rate, twice the optimal fraction returns it to zero, and overbetting is therefore punished far more savagely than underbetting — which matters because the Bureau's official probabilities are official rather than true, and the mandated fraction was computed from an estimate of the edge that the leaflet describes, without embarrassment, as forward-looking.
- The FAQ should extend the mandate to non-monetary bankrolls — the career change, the marriage, the remaining years of health — and should do so without ever noticing that the theorem justifying the policy has an indefinitely repeated, fully reinvested gamble as its hypothesis, and that a marriage is not one.
- The logarithmic utility function should be decreed, not argued for: it is the Bureau's utility function, and there is only the one.

Two constraints on fairness. First, do not let the leaflet claim that Kelly underperforms at finite horizons; with logarithmic utility it is optimal at every horizon, my friend knows that, and a strawman would break the joke. The pressure on the reader must come entirely from elsewhere: from what log is indifferent between, from the gap between an ensemble average and a single life, from the asymmetry of the growth-rate curve, and from the absence of any long run in the things the leaflet has taken to calling bankrolls. Second, keep it deadpan and legible: no winking, no sneering asides, no footnotes telling the reader what to think. Every reassurance the leaflet offers should follow from a premise my friend actually endorses, so that a reader recoils from the policy while being unable to point to a single step that is not orthodox Kelly. The piece should be funny to a specialist and intelligible to someone who has never heard the criterion, and it should stand entirely on its own: no citations, no named real authors, no suggestions for further reading — invented officials and a fictional agency only. I would like it to end on the Bureau's complaints procedure, which I leave to you, though I hope it involves a horizon no applicant will live to see.
