I'm auditing a graduate reading group on agency and artificial intelligence, and I'm trying to get clear on Dennett's intentional stance before I have to give a short response to it next week. A colleague who teaches a separate course on human–AI interaction circulated a one-page handout on "mental-state talk about machines," and its core paragraph struck me as confident in a way I don't trust, but I can't localize what's wrong. Here it is, more or less as written:

"Dennett's intentional stance says that there are three ways of predicting a system's behavior: the physical stance, from laws of nature; the design stance, from what the thing was built to do; and the intentional stance, from its beliefs and desires. The intentional stance is really just the design stance with the internal mechanism blacked out — you don't know or don't care how the thing works, so you put beliefs and desires in as placeholders for the machinery you can't see. That is why Dennett holds that there is nothing more to having a belief than being usefully predictable in these terms: intentionality is not a genuine feature of the world but a façon de parler that earns its keep only when it beats its rivals at prediction. This is no longer merely philosophical. Engineers now report that predicting a language model's continuations by attributing to it a belief that P outperforms every purely mechanical predictor they have tried, and on Dennett's own criterion that settles the matter — the model believes that P, and the same will hold of any future system that passes the predictive test. The old worry that this makes belief too cheap, since a chess program or even a thermostat can be predicted by ascribing beliefs and desires to it, the handout sets aside in a clause: Dennett, it says, 'happily restricts the stance to systems with goals of their own.' The upshot the author draws is that either we accept that today's chatbots have beliefs and desires in the ordinary sense — are, in some sense, conscious of things — or we abandon Dennett's criterion altogether."

What I'd like from you is an accuracy audit rather than a verdict. Go through the paragraph more or less sentence by sentence: which claims would someone sympathetic to the intentional stance accept as stated, which are fair simplifications of a more careful thesis, and which would they reject as misrepresentations? I'm especially unsure about three things. First, a friend in the group insists Dennett is some kind of realist about patterns rather than the outright instrumentalist the handout describes, and that this changes what "nothing more to having a belief" can mean; I don't know whether that distinction matters for the conclusion. Second, several of us got stuck on what exactly the belief is being attributed to: the trained model, the model together with its prompt, or the fictional character whose voice the model is continuing — and on whether Dennett's criterion has anything to say about how to draw the boundary of the system. Third, someone floated a result (I can't reconstruct the source) in which two mutually incompatible belief-ascriptions to the same model predicted its outputs equally well, and another person argued that the whole predictive success is an artifact of the training data consisting of sentences written by creatures that do have beliefs. I'd like to know whether either point touches something real in the view.

End with the practical question I actually care about: does the handout's bottom line — that the question whether a chatbot believes anything is settled by running prediction benchmarks and seeing whether intentional ascriptions win — survive the corrections you've made, or does it depend on the parts that are wrong? If it depends on them, say what a defender of the stance would propose instead as a way of making the question tractable. I don't need citations or further reading; I'd rather have the argument laid out and the errors identified precisely enough that I can say, in my response, exactly which sentence I'm objecting to and why.
