I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy, and for the third year running I've been handed the second seminar of our department's 'Rationality and Choice' course: 25 students, 75 minutes, mostly second-years, and the room splits almost down the middle between economics/PPE majors and philosophy majors. Last week I gave them expected utility theory, so they are already comfortable taking a utility function over outcomes as given, computing expected utilities, and noticing that their own answers to the Allais and Ellsberg cases don't line up with the theory — which means they use the word 'utility' fluently and have no idea where the numbers come from. Next week I have to do revealed preference: choice first, preference as what choice reveals, utility as a numerical representation of that choice, and then the part I actually care about, which is what the utility numbers mean. My problem is last year's run. They left able to state the weak axiom and able to check it against a two-good example, but with two beliefs perfectly intact underneath: that utility is a technical name for how happy or satisfied an outcome makes someone, and that once we've fitted a utility function to a person's choices we've thereby found out something about their welfare. Both are wrong in ways I can articulate, and I have not managed to make either of them land.

So what I need is a plan for the session, not a lecture I could deliver. Specifically: the order the pieces have to come in and why — what has to be in place before what, and where, if anywhere, I should bring in the fact that the 'utility' of expected utility theory has to be cardinal (defined only up to positive affine transformation) in a way that utility-as-representation-of-choice is not, given that utility-as-representation is only defined up to any increasing transformation. I genuinely can't tell whether that distinction is the linchpin of the whole session or a distraction that will eat fifteen minutes and lose the room, and I'd like you to say which and why. I also want the one example that should carry everything, with a note on what it must have and what it must not have: the fruit-basket thing I used last year got me the definition and nothing else, and my hypothesis is that apples and oranges are too obviously proxies for some underlying quantity, so students quietly supply the quantity themselves and never notice they've done it. Then the misconception map — for both halves of the room separately, including, I suspect, that this is behaviorism about the mind, that indifference is revealed whenever someone could have chosen either way, and that a larger opportunity set can never make a chooser worse off — together with the point in the sequence where each should be headed off rather than refuted outright. And one question at the end that would let me tell whether the idea has landed instead of been memorized.

Two more things. Some of these students will be doing cost-benefit analysis for government agencies within two years, putting numbers on lives and commute times, and I want them leaving genuinely uneasy about where those numbers come from without turning into people who think the whole apparatus is worthless because the numbers aren't handed down from heaven; I don't know how to hold those at once, and I'd like the plan to hold them for me. I'd also like you to say what I should openly refuse to get into at this level — I'm assuming Afriat's theorem, the strong axiom, and anything about integrability are out, but tell me if I'm wrong — and, because I have to defend this plan to the course convener on Friday: which piece of your proposed ordering you would abandon if I had to cut ten minutes, and what would change your mind about the ordering (for instance, if you thought the gap between choice and welfare had to come before the representation result, or that the session should be built around a single case rather than a sequence of moves). Be explicit about where your advice is least confident.
