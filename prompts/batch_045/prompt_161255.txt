**3. The Simplicity Condition**

Standard presentations of Descartes on animal minds offer the two tests of the fifth part of the *Discourse on the Method* (1637) as though they were the argument. The first is the language test: no brute, not even a parrot, arranges its words so as to reply appropriately to whatever is said in its presence, whereas the dullest human being does. The second is the adaptability test: brutes surpass us in some operations, but fail in others, which shows that they work by the disposition of their organs rather than by reason, which is a universal instrument serviceable in all sorts of encounters. I want to argue that both tests are symptoms rather than grounds. The ground is a condition on what can be a subject of experience at all, and Descartes never states it. Call it the Simplicity Condition: whatever is a subject of experience is not a composite whose unity is borrowed from the arrangement of its parts. Its unity is primitive.

Two considerations support attributing this to him. First, his own physiology threatens to make the experiential difference idle. In the *Treatise on Man* and the *Passions of the Soul* he explains the withdrawal of a foot from a fire, the stirring of the heart in fear, and the acquisition of habits by the flow of animal spirits through nerve tubes and by the traces those flows leave in the brain. He holds the same story to be true of us, up to the point at which the soul is affected. If the mechanical account is complete for the animal's behaviour, then there is no work left for felt pain to do, and the familiar charge — that Descartes is moved by a crude appeal to parsimony — would be fair. But Descartes is unmoved by parsimony arguments of that shape; he does not doubt his own thoughts because a mechanical story can be told of his body. Something stronger must be doing the work, and the Simplicity Condition is the natural candidate. A machine has no single thing that the pain could belong to. The pain belongs to the person, not to the hand, and a machine has hands but no one whose hands they are.

Second, the condition explains why the two tests take the form they do. Speech and flexible action are not definitions of thought; they are the observable traces of a subject whose states are available to it as a whole. A creature that can say what it thinks on any occasion, and that can turn its resources to unforeseen cases, exhibits a unity of access that mere clockwork cannot counterfeit, because the responses of clockwork are keyed to particular configurations of its parts.

If this reading is right, the familiar objections do not land where their authors suppose. Trained parrots, signing apes, and the flexible behaviour of corvids and cephalopods press against the tests. They do not touch the condition. A Cartesian can concede the performance and deny the subject, and the dispute relocates to the question of what unity consists in rather than to the question of what animals can do.

And here, I shall suggest, the reading turns against Descartes. For the condition admits of two readings, and neither is comfortable.

Read as a primitive, non-functional requirement, it is evidentially inert. Nothing observable could tell us whether the unity of a candidate subject is borrowed or primitive. The doctrine of the beast-machine then reduces to an assertion, and the two tests lose even the corroborating role I have assigned them, since whatever they detect could in principle be detected in a machine.

Read as a functional requirement — unity of access, of the kind that makes one state available to the operations that draw upon others — it is satisfiable in principle by a machine, since integration is precisely what machines can have. Worse, Descartes is committed to saying that animal bodies are integrated in the relevant respects: the whole force of his case for mechanism is that animal behaviour is as well explained by his physiology as ours is. If integration is what makes a subject, and integration is present in the animal's nervous organisation, then the beasts are subjects after all, and the soul is left doing no unifying work.

To make the second horn vivid I introduce a case, which I call the Joined Controls case. Imagine two animal-like creatures whose nervous systems have been grafted together, so that a single control system drives both bodies and each body's states are available to the operations governing the other. Intuitively there is now one subject with two bodies. Now imagine one body divided between two control systems that never communicate: intuitively there are two subjects in one body. If those verdicts are right, we are counting subjects by functional integration and not by the simplicity of a substance. But functional integration is exactly what a machine can possess. So the Simplicity Condition, once we are forced to say what it comes to, has become a condition that Descartes cannot use.

I therefore close the section with a dilemma: either Descartes' condition on subjecthood is primitive and evidentially idle, or it is functional and self-defeating. On neither reading do the two tests of the *Discourse* carry the argument they are usually credited with.
