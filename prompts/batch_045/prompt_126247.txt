I spent most of last evening arguing with a friend about the 2008 rescue of AIG, and I have come away unsure whether we were even disagreeing about the thing I thought we were. Her claim was that AIG was, as she put it, a utility monster, and that the episode therefore shows what is wrong with any moral view that simply aggregates well-being. My instinct was that the analogy is doing considerably more work than it earns — but I could not say exactly where it breaks, or what survives when it does. I would like you to settle the mapping properly.

You are a professional philosopher who works in normative ethics and who also has a solid working knowledge of the financial-crisis period. Take your time and be exact. Before you write the analysis proper, spend a few paragraphs brainstorming in scratchpad form: list every candidate mapping you can think of, mark the ones you reject and say why, and note which questions will have to be settled for the rest of the argument to hold together. Then give me the analysis. Please do not supply citations, references, a bibliography or a reading list: you cannot look anything up from where you are, and I cannot check it from where I am, so I want the argument itself rather than pointers to arguments.

Stipulate the following facts for the sake of argument, since I would rather you spent your words on the concepts than on the record. AIG had written a very large book of credit default swaps on mortgage-backed securities while holding little collateral against them; when house prices fell, it faced collateral calls it could not meet. Lehman Brothers was allowed to fail on 15 September 2008. The next day the Federal Reserve extended an emergency credit line to AIG and took a large equity stake, with total federal support usually put at somewhere around 180 billion dollars. A substantial part of the relief flowed through, not to AIG's policyholders, but to the banks and other institutions on the other side of those swaps, which were reportedly paid close to the full value of their claims. That last point is the one my friend kept returning to. I am not certain of every figure; if you think one is materially wrong, correct it in a single sentence and carry on.

Then work through the following.

1. Build the mapping rigorously. In the original thought experiment the creature is an entity with an unusual capacity to convert resources into welfare — call this a welfare monster. Say precisely what, in September 2008, would be the entity, the resource, the welfare function, and the comparison class, and then say whether AIG fits. Be willing to report that the fit is poor, and to describe what a good fit would have looked like.

2. Consider whether one kind of monster is enough. I want to introduce two further labels, and you should argue with them or replace them if you think they are wrong. A leverage monster is not unusual in its capacity for welfare at all; it occupies a causal node such that its failure propagates, and its continued functioning is worth far more to everyone else than to itself. An endogenous monster exists only because the allocation rule creates it: it is made, not found. Which of the three is AIG, if any? Does the objection reach all three, and if it reaches only some, is it narrower than its reputation — or does it reach the others in a form nobody bothered to write down?

3. Handle the timing seriously. The creature in the textbook example is simply given, and the question is whether to feed it once. The bailout decision was made under radical uncertainty, in a sequence, by an actor whose own future conduct would shape what such entities do. If the monster is endogenous, can a utilitarian who cares about rules, expectations and incentive effects legitimately refuse to feed it — and does that dissolve the objection or merely relocate it one level up?

4. Say what the framework illuminates and what it cannot reach. I suspect it illuminates the specific ugliness of the episode: sacrifices extracted from people who gained nothing, and gains concentrated in parties who had made bad bets. But I doubt it can reach several things that obviously mattered — coercion, fairness, democratic authorisation, the difference between paying someone and merely declining to punish them, and the fact that most of the benefit accrued to third parties rather than to the entity being fed. Tell me which of these the framework is simply silent about rather than forcing it to speak.

5. Run the case back against the theory. Is the episode a confirmation of the utility-monster objection, an anomaly for it, or a case it was never built for? Use the Lehman contrast carefully: it is the nearest thing we have to a natural experiment, but the counterfactual is contested, so tell me how much it is allowed to count as evidence and what it cannot show.

6. In a short closing section, tell me which of us was closer, or whether the disagreement was verbal. Take a stand where the argument licenses one; where it genuinely does not, say so plainly rather than hedging everything uniformly.

Aim for 1,000 to 1,400 words, in numbered sections matching the above.
