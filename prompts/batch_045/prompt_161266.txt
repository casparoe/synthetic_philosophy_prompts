I am a retired physical oceanographer who, having spent a career among buoy data and storm-surge models, sat in last term on a graduate seminar in moral philosophy and came away with a habit of testing myself against the old lifeboat example: a ship goes down, there is one boat rated for twelve, open water, no second boat, no one can swim to shore. Assume throughout that the overload danger is real rather than a manufacturer's caution, so that with more than twelve aboard the boat swamps and everyone dies. My verdicts, given on intuition and with no principle in hand, are these. (1) Fifteen have got aboard; unless three go back into the water all fifteen die. I think it is permissible to draw lots and put three over the side, though I am not certain I could do it; and if one of the fifteen volunteers, I would let him go, which feels notably better than the lottery even though the same man dies and the same twelve live — I don't know why it feels better. (2) Twelve are aboard and three are in the water hanging on the gunwale; hauling them aboard sinks the boat. Refusing them seems to me permissible and troubles me far less than (1), despite the outcomes and the bodies being identical; one of the graduate students says this is "just the doing/allowing distinction," which strikes me as a label rather than an answer, since the question is exactly why that distinction should carry weight here when the arithmetic is the same. (3) Now make the risk graded instead of a cliff: each person beyond the twelfth adds about four percentage points to the chance of swamping, so two percent at twelve, ten at fourteen, eighteen at sixteen, twenty-six at eighteen. I would take two extra and stop there, and I notice that on my own numbers the expected number of survivors is still rising at sixteen and eighteen; I stop because the possibility that the boat goes down with everyone in it counts for more with me than its probability warrants. (4) The mirror image of (3): fourteen aboard, and putting two back into the water moves the risk from ten percent to two. I think that is not merely distressing but wrong. (5) The overload was caused by one man who panicked, forced his way aboard and dragged others after him: he goes first, before any lottery — and I genuinely cannot tell whether I am moved by his culpability or by the thought that he is the one who can be removed with least resistance. (6) If the culprit is also the only person aboard who can navigate, I keep him and draw lots among the rest; if he is the only one who can work the radio, I keep him too; if he is only the doctor, I am uncertain. (7) One of the fifteen is unconscious and will certainly be dead within the hour whatever we do: he goes first, and this seems to me obviously right. Yet when the choice is between a healthy seventy-year-old and a healthy twenty-year-old, I refuse to choose, and I would not put the older one over the side. (8) One place left, and in the water are my wife and a stranger: I take my wife, and I do not think this needs defending; but I do not think I may put a stranger who is already aboard into the water to make room for her. (9) One child or one adult: I take the child, and I suspect that is sentiment rather than judgment; two adults or one child: I take the two adults; one twenty-year-old or one eighty-year-old: I have no verdict at all, and I resent the question.

What I would like from you is one principle — or at most two, with a definite recommendation between them — stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I have not thought about, with each clause of it motivated by the verdict it is there to save, its verdicts on the cases I have not listed, and an honest account of which of my verdicts it cannot reproduce and which you think I ought to surrender. The gap I most want closed or closed off is between (2), (3) and (4): can any single principle treat leaving a person in the water as permissible while treating the identical risk reduction achieved by putting that person out as impermissible, or must I give up one side? My own candidate, such as it is, runs: minimise expected deaths subject to a constraint against substantially raising the risk of total loss; break ties first by culpability, then by whether the person will die anyway, and never by age or by relationship to me; and treat refusing admission as permissible wherever ejecting the same person would be permissible. Repair it if it can be repaired, replace it if it cannot.

Two more things. First, the case I keep returning to and cannot settle: three must go, but there is a small raft for them and perhaps a one-in-five chance that a passing ship finds them before they die of exposure — does that transform the case, does the permissibility of ejection vary continuously with the ejected person's chance of survival, and does it matter whether that chance is twenty percent or half of one percent? Second, please commit: tell me plainly which of my verdicts you think is simply mistaken, give your reasons rather than a survey of the options, and state how confident you are — both in your verdicts and in the claim that your principle is a genuine principle rather than a restatement of my intuitions with a formula bolted on.
