I sit on a grant panel that funds field work in animal behaviour, and a large share of the proposals we read contain the same move somewhere in the background section: some trait — a patch of colour, a call, a bout of vigilance, an unwieldy structure — is described as a costly signal, and its cost is said to be what makes it honest. The move is load-bearing. It is what licenses treating the trait as an adaptation for communication rather than as a weapon, a byproduct, a cue, or a symptom, and it usually arrives with no more support than the observation that the trait is expensive or risky to have. Expensiveness alone does not separate types: a cost that every individual pays equally, however large, tells a receiver nothing. But we have no shared standard for when the claim has been earned, and I would like one before the next round.

Please write me the specification I should be applying when a proposer claims that trait T in species S is maintained as a costly signal of quality Q. That is: the requirements a case must meet before the claim is anything more than a just-so story, each with the case or consideration that motivates it, sorted into what is non-negotiable, what is a strong presumption that can be rebutted with argument, and what is merely the taste of one research tradition. The purpose is fixed, so please keep it in view: this is an evidential standard for empirical claims about particular traits, not a general theory of communication, and "adequate" means adequate to license the word *signal* in that case — as against cue, index, armament, or epiphenomenon.

These are the demands I keep half-formulating and then abandoning, to show you where I am stuck. I would rather you rebuilt the list than annotated mine, but I want your criteria to be answerable to them:

- Not "the trait is expensive" but "the extra cost of a somewhat larger or more intense trait is smaller for a higher-quality individual" — the graded or single-crossing condition that the strategic models place at the centre. A flat tax on all bearers cannot separate types.
- Costs counted at the margin of the signalling act, so that a cost the animal would incur anyway — a display given from a perch it would occupy regardless, a migration route that doubles as an advertisement — cannot be charged to the signal.
- A currency requirement: the cost must be one that Q genuinely makes cheaper to bear, so that the link between quality and cost runs through Q rather than through some third variable that happens to correlate with it.
- A declaration of which cost mechanism is being posited: production cost, receiver-imposed or social cost, or vulnerability cost such as attraction of predators — since these make different predictions when receivers are experimentally removed.
- A timing condition: whether the cost must be realised at the point of the receiver's decision, or whether a cost that is merely pledged and falls due later does the same work.
- A receiver-side requirement: that someone actually reads the trait, that the reading feeds back onto the signaler's fitness, and that the response is not better explained by a preference that predates the trait.
- An alternative-hypotheses requirement: why the trait is not a weapon or a direct fitness benefit, and why a cheaper equivalent producing the same response has not invaded.
- A say-which-it-is requirement: is the trait mechanically tied to Q, in which case the machinery of cost is idle and the evidentiary burden shifts to the mechanism, or is it conventional and cost-maintained?

Test the list on three composite cases from the current round, since a standard that cannot decide cases is not one:

(1) A lizard in which males develop a carotenoid-based throat patch, drawn from stores that depend on foraging success and parasite load, only in wet years; the display is performed from basking rocks that males occupy irrespective of patch size; patched males receive more attacks from territory holders; females prefer larger patches; and females of a closely related species lacking the patch also prefer blue-baited models.

(2) A fish with male parental care in which the courtship display itself is cheap, but displaying commits the male to weeks of egg-guarding during which he loses substantial mass and runs elevated predation risk — a cost that falls due only if the female accepts him, and that he could in principle escape by deserting afterwards.

(3) A bird whose dawn chorus costs every male, in every condition, roughly the same four per cent of body mass per morning.

Two further things matter more to me than the list itself. First: which of the things people demand in this area are genuine desiderata and which are inherited rhetoric — in particular whether the cost has to be *wasteful*, something that lowers fitness with no compensating return, or whether that is Zahavi's polemical framing rather than a result, and whether full separation is required or reliability on average is enough. Second: whether some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, in which case that is itself the finding I want. My suspicions are that a requirement that the signal mean the same thing across habitats and seasons cannot be met jointly with the fact that costs are context-dependent, and that a requirement of unfakeability by construction cannot be met jointly with one of evolutionary flexibility plus affordability for the lowest-quality bearer; tell me if these are right and whether there are better ones.

Finally, name the single requirement that does the most work in excluding candidate traits, and — in a few lines only, since the criteria are the deliverable and not the scorecard — say how the main families of account in the area fare: Zahavi's original handicap principle, the Grafen-style strategic models, the index or mechanical-constraint approach, and the results showing that honesty can be stable without cost when interests partly coincide.
