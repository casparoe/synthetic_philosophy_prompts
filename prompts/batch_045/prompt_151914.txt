I'm one of five people on a committee rewriting the allocation policy for a regional health system's annual exceptional-funding budget: a single pool of money, no realistic chance of topping it up, and a claimant list whose stakes differ as much in shape as in size — one patient facing ten years of a disabling condition, ten patients facing one year each, a thousand patients each carrying a small probability of a large loss, and a prevention programme that takes a trivial amount from everyone for a large gain to a few. Every time we work a case through, someone quietly switches framework halfway: complaints get added like quantities, then someone insists the numbers are irrelevant, then someone divides a single person's stake into parts and it suddenly weighs more. So before I read any more of the contractualist literature on whether and how the numbers count, I want the specification I should be reading it against. Write me the desiderata for a theory of interpersonal aggregation: what such a theory has to deliver to be good enough for this job — good enough, that is, to be operated by a committee that must actually reach a decision in the cases that arise, that must give the same verdict when the same situation is described at different levels of grain or carved into different numbers of claimants, and that must be able to offer each losing claimant a justification rather than only a total.

Here is my rough start, which I'd like you to correct, collapse, reorder, or discard rather than just endorse. (i) No weight from re-description: a single patient's stake must not gain moral weight merely by being described as several harms, and two claimants must not collapse into one merely because they share a bank account, a decision procedure, and all their aims — so the theory needs an account of what makes stakes separate, not just an insistence that they are. (ii) It has to say whether intrapersonal aggregation over a life runs by the same algebra as interpersonal aggregation across lives, with the ten-years-for-one versus one-year-for-ten case as the test. (iii) It has to handle very large numbers of very small costs without either licensing anything at all or licensing nothing, and say whether the trade-off has a limit. (iv) It has to say whether it aggregates probabilities or outcomes: a thousand people each with a one-in-a-thousand risk, versus one person certain to suffer. (v) It has to have something to say when the stakes tie and the good is indivisible — lottery, rotation, or silence. (vi) It has to say whether merely possible future claimants enter the sum at all. (vii) It has to avoid making the worse-off-anyway case look better, or explain why that appearance is benign. (viii) It has to tell us whether aggregation belongs in the moral weight of reasons or only in the procedure we adopt, since my committee may be able to use a summing rule even if nothing morally speaking sums. For each requirement give the case or consideration behind it, mark whether it is non-negotiable for a body like mine or merely the preference of one camp, flag any subset you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and name the single requirement that does the most work in excluding otherwise popular candidates. If you think my framing has already smuggled in a contested commitment — for instance by demanding determinacy, or by asking for a justification addressable to each individual loser — say which, and how the list would look without it.

Please skip reading suggestions entirely; I have no library access from here and can't follow them up. Please also mark your confidence: separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and don't present the contested part as the consensus. If you have room, add four or five lines scoring Scanlon-style reasonable-rejectability, total-utilitarian summing, prioritarian weighting, and a Taurek-style refusal to aggregate at all — but keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases, which is the part I can't do for myself.
