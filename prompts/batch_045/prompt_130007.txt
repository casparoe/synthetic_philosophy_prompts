Hey — I'm stuck on a chapter I'm trying to finish on responsibility gaps for autonomous systems, and I keep going back and forth on how to frame the two main fixes, so here's my half-baked setup and a question about it.

The case I keep coming back to: a hospital deploys a diagnostic model that outperforms its radiologists on average. One patient is harmed by an error that nobody was negligent about, nobody could have predicted in its specific form, and nobody can fully explain afterwards, because the systems internals aren't interpretable enough to say why it landed where it did. Everybody was behaving sensibly; somebody is badly off; nobody seems to *deserve* blame.

Call that first problem the responsibility gap proper. Then there's the neighboring one people call the retribution gap: our punitive and blame-directed responses still get triggered, but there's no fitting target for them, so the reaction is left hanging. I want to treat those as separate problems, because I think a lot of the literature definately runs them together.

Two families of response as I see them:

(1) Keep desert, move it upstream. Blame doesn't attach to the outcome but to the *risk*: the deployer knowingly put a risky thing into the world, and if that risk-creation was justified, then there's no blame but also no gap — we were right to run the system and we say so. Blame gets relocated to whoever had the most control over the risk, not to whoever was nearest the patient.

(2) Get revisionary about the practices themselves. Drop desert as the organizing notion and treat responsibility practices as forward-looking instruments: deterring careless deployment, compensating victims, giving people somewhere to take a complaint, maintaining trust in the institution. On this view we may end up sanctioning things that aren't agents at all — pulling the model, retiring the product line, publishing the failure — and the fact that our retributive itch goes unscratched isn't really a moral cost, it's just a habit we should outgrow.

My own candidate, which I'm calling the guarantor view, is this: deployment is a status-conferring act. By putting the system into a high-stakes setting you *become the guarantor* of its outputs, and that role carries standing duties to explain harms, repair them, and change the system — plus it makes you the default addressee of complaints. The point is that the gap is closed *before* anything happens, by making sure the role is filled, funded and ackknowledged at deployment time, instead of going hunting for a culprit afterwards.

So: compare (1) and (2) properly — where exactly do they disagree, and what's the strongest version of each, not the strawman? Then tell me whether the guarantor view is really a third option or whether it collapses into (2) with nicer packaging, or into (1) with the blame-language mostly stripped out. I have a suspicion it's just (1) minus the desert condition, and if so I should probably drop the chapter's third section, but I can't quite argue myself into a verdict.

Two things I specifically want you to weigh while you do this. First: whichever view you land on, does it actually avoid dumping everything on the lowest-level person in the room? The classic failure mode is that the nurse or the junior doctor who was merely the last human in the loop absorbs all of the blame simply because she's the only available target, while the people who chose the system and set its thresholds walk. Second: is there a *residual* gap that none of the three closes — some leftover of the responsibility problem that survives even once liability, repair and deterrence are all sorted out — and if there is, does it matter morally, or is worrying about it just retributive nostalgia?

Don't sit on the fence: I need an actual verdict (something like "the guarantor view is just (2), and (2) is right about X but wrong about Y" is fine, as is splitting it by case type). Aim for roughly 1000–1200 words, in prose rather than bullet points, in American English. Please don't pad it with references or a literature review — I've read the survey articles, what I need is someone to argue from the structure of the case.
