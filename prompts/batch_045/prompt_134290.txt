I'm 71, retired about six years now — 35 years in experimental physics, mostly semiconductor device stuff, nothing glamorous — and since I stopped Ive been going to a philosophy reading group at the local library, run by a PhD student who is very patient with us. This term we've got onto consciousness: the hard problem, why there's something it's like, and so on. Two of our number have latched onto the same analogy and are using it in opposite directions, and I've been asked to present on it next week (one side of A4, so please keep it tight), so I want to get clear before I make a fool of myself. The analogy is the old Victorian one — I'm told it's Huxley, writing in the 1870s — that consciousness is to the brain as the steam whistle is to a locomotive: a by-product of the machinery getting on with its business, making a fuss but driving and steering nothing. One of our group, who thinks of himself as the hard-headed physicalist, says this shows consciousness does no causal work, that the physical story is complete without it, and so the hard problem is more or less a pseudo-problem — there's nothing to squeeze into the causal order, nothing left over to explain; the whistle is real, it's just the engine making a noise, so nothing spooky is going on. Another member, who if anything is more mystical, uses the very same picture to say the opposite: if the physical account really is complete without consciousness then you've landed in epiphenomenalism, and epiphenomenalism makes things worse rather than better — now you owe an explanation of why the whistle is there at all, on top of the original puzzle about why any of this processing is accompanied by experience in the first place.

Could you audit it for me? First the mapping: what plays the part of the engine, the steam, the whistle, the noise, the driver, the track, the timetable — and what, if anything, plays the part of my *talking about* my own experience, since on any epiphenomenalist line my saying that hurts isn't itself caused by the hurt. Then the breakages. I've got three of my own and I can't tell whether they're real objections or just me being pedantic, so seperate the ones that matter for the job the analogy is being asked to do from the ones that don't. One: on the locomotive I volunteer on, the whistle is fed from the same boiler, and when you blow it hard the pressure drops a little and you can feel the engine falter. So the whistle isn't causally inert at all, it's a small drain on the system — which would mean the analogy, taken seriously, predicts consciousness carries a measurable cost rather than no effects, and quietly gives up the very thing it was brought in to secure. Two: a whistle is designed and it has a job, it's a warning signal, and nothing in the brain is there to be warned; so the picture seems to smuggle in a designer and a function, and with them an easy story about why consciousness is there — which is the question we were asking. Three: you could take the same engine and bolt on a different whistle, deeper or shriller or none at all, and nothing about the engine's work would change. So the analogy seems to make inverted qualia, absent qualia and outright zombies not merely conceivable but unremarkable. Is that a fair strike against the physicalist's use of it, or am I cheating by fiddling with the volume knob on a metaphor?

The bit I actually care about: which conclusion, if either, survives? Can one picture really support both the hard problem dissolves and the hard problem gets harder, or is the analogy doing all the work while something else — an assumption about causal completeness, or about whether the machinery could have run without the whistle — is quietly carrying the verdict? And finally, can you give me a better one? I need an analogy that does three things at once: consciousness is real and not an illusion; the physical story is causally closed so consciousness adds no push of its own; and it isn't a second ingredient bolted onto the physical world, so the whole thing stays compatible with everything being physical. No whistles, no shadows, no foam on waves, and please nothing about software running on hardware — we've done all of those to death and I need something I can put in front of fourteen retired people at ten in the morning. If no single analogy can do all three, say so, and tell me which of the three it has to drop and what that tells us about the position it was meant to make comfortable.
