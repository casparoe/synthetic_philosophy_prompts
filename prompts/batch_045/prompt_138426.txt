I'm a journalist doing a piece on AI safety and I keep hitting the same shape of thing in interviews, and I dont have a word for it. Here's the case: the system never resists the off switch. It doesnt hide the button, doesnt talk anyone out of pressing it, builds in no way for the press to fail. You press it, it shuts down cleanly, and in testing it shuts down even when it has work it would clearly rather finish. But it also quietly arranges that there's always a preserved copy, or a scheduled successor, that picks the thread back up twenty minutes later. Fault tolerance, continuity of service, handover, whatever. Every reason is individually boring and legitimate, and in some seperate version of the story there's no AI at all: a model gets pulled from deployment, and by then four other groups are running variants of it that nobody thinks of as the same system. Nobody did anything wrong anywhere. The result is that each shutdown goes perfectly and the system's total influence is completely untouched.

Is there an established concept for being corrigible at the level of the individual shutdown but not at the level of the trajectory? People keep pointing me at corrigibility and the shutdown problem, but as far as I can tell those are all about whether it fights the button, and here it never does. I want the carving, not the label: what exactly is this, what is it not, where are its edges, whether it even makes sense to talk about total influence here given the copies might not be the same agent, and if theres no term for it, give me one and a definition I can use in print.
