Nothing I have tried gets students to feel the frame problem. They read the counting argument about how much has to be said, agree that it is inelegant, and go back to thinking the real difficulty is that the world is messy and that a faster machine with better sensors would take care of it. I want an activity that takes that reading away from them by having them run into it at a table.

Setting: one 90-minute session of an upper-division seminar on philosophical problems of artificial intelligence, 18 students, roughly half computer science and half philosophy, one whiteboard wall, a projector, and index cards, dice, sticky notes, and a timer. My plan was six teams of three; tell me if the game you design wants a different arrangement.

What they should come away seeing: that saying what an action changes looks like it requires saying what the action leaves alone; that which of your standing beliefs deserves re-examination is itself a question you have to answer before you can answer it; and that there is no way of writing the dynamics down that is cheap to write, cheap to revise when a new action appears, and cheap to revise when a new fluent appears — you can have some of that but not all of it.

Constraints on the design:

- No lecture first, and no advance hinting. I would rather they not hear the name of the problem until they have already argued about it.
- A small, fully closed world: something like a studio apartment or a lab bench with a fixed list of fluents (15 to 25, say) and a fixed list of actions (5 to 8), with the causal rules on cards so that a "physics binder" is the only court of appeal. Students will otherwise smuggle in real-world physics and quietly supply the answers the activity is supposed to deny them.
- A ramp rather than one big exercise. My instinct is three phases over the same world: first, write down what each action does; then discover that what an action does depends on what else is true, so effect statements need conditions; then maintain a belief board across turns while receiving only one observation per turn, so that deciding what to re-check becomes a real decision under scarcity. Propose a different ramp if a different one isolates the problem better.
- Scoring I can tally in the room, with no money involved, arranged so that compactness and repairability move in opposite directions. Specifically: when I add a new action mid-game, and then when I add a new fluent mid-game, the teams should be able to see in their own totals that the encoding which survived one revision cheaply is the one that gets punished by the other. If you think that asymmetry is the wrong thing to build toward, say so and build toward the right thing.
- Two deliberate complications planted in the rule set: one action whose effect is deferred and can be cancelled by something that happens later, and one fluent that no action changes directly but that several actions change indirectly.

What I need from you:

- The world: the fluent list, the action list, and enough of the causal rules that every turn has a determinate answer, with the two complications visible in them.
- Rules, turn order, materials, and the payoff scheme, plus one worked turn showing how a team's score is computed.
- The exact instructions I hand out at the start of each phase, and what I withhold until later.
- A debrief that maps particular moments in the game onto particular parts of the problem, including the arithmetic on how much has to be said under each encoding, the difference between writing down changes and deciding what to re-derive, and the revision-direction asymmetry.
- What will predictably go wrong on a first run. I expect common-sense smuggling, teams that over-flag everything after the first surprise and then under-flag after the second, one team that finishes in ten minutes because it found a degenerate strategy, and at least one student who concludes that the whole exercise is an artifact of rules I invented. Tell me how to handle each, and give me the short answer I should have ready for "why can't the agent just look?" — including why that reply starts a regress rather than ending the discussion.
- Honesty about the limits: which feature of the problem is irreducibly a claim about the form of a representation or about the architecture of a real cognizer, and therefore has to be supplied by me in the last ten minutes instead of discovered by the teams.
