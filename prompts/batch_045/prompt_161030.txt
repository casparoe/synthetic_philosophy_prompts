The question:

Question 3 (25 marks). Defenders of what we will call the scale argument reason as follows: the number of insects alive at any one time is astronomically larger than the number of vertebrates, so even if our confidence that insects have valenced experiences at all is low, the expected amount of insect suffering is enormous, and insect welfare should be a major moral priority.

(a) State the argument in a form precise enough to assess. In particular, say what kind of probability is involved — is it a frequency across individual insects, or is it something else? — and say what the argument must assume about the magnitude and the sign of the welfare per insect.

(b) Compare two ways of modelling the uncertainty. On the per-individual model, each insect independently has probability p of undergoing valenced experiences of intensity m. On the taxon-level model, p is your confidence in a single hypothesis about a kind of organism: every insect of that kind undergoes experiences of intensity m if the hypothesis is true, and none does if it is false. Does the choice between these models affect the expected total? Does it affect the spread of possible totals? Does it affect which objections the argument faces? Which model better describes our actual epistemic situation, and does it matter which one we use?

(c) A common objection is that the argument proves too much: the very same reasoning can be run for any candidate bearer of experience, and there are many of them — nematodes, mites, plants, fungi, bacteria, artificial systems. Suppose that there are k such candidate classes, with populations N₁ … Nₖ and priors p₁ … pₖ, and suppose the corresponding hypotheses are not mutually exclusive: several of them, or all of them, could be true together. Does this undermine the argument? Does the fact that the hypotheses can all be true matter to whether the objection succeeds?

(d) Suppose the argument's conclusion is granted: there is a very large expected stock of insect suffering. Does that support any specific practical priority? Say what else would have to be established before it did.

The student's answer:

(a) The argument is best stated as an expected-value calculation: expected welfare-relevant experience from insects = p × N × m, where N is the standing insect population, on the order of a quintillion, p is our confidence that insects have valenced experiences, and m is the welfare per insect per unit time. The probability here is not a frequency across insects; it is a degree of confidence in a hypothesis about a kind of organism, so it attaches to all insects at once rather than varying from one insect to the next. That matters, because it means we multiply by p once, not once per insect, and we do not discount again for each additional insect we consider. The argument also has to assume that m is not negligible relative to the welfare of the vertebrates we already count, and that wild insect lives are on balance bad: if insect lives were net positive, the same arithmetic would tell us to increase insect numbers rather than to reduce insect suffering. Given how much larger N is than the number of vertebrates, even a p of about 0.1 lets the insect term swamp the vertebrate term, and this is why the argument is taken to show that insect welfare has been badly neglected.

(b) The choice of model makes no practical difference. On either model the expected total comes out at N × p × m, so nothing in the argument turns on which one we adopt. In fact the worry about p being low is something of a red herring: with N this large, the probability that at least one insect has valenced experience is 1 − (1 − p)^N, which is effectively 1 for any p worth taking seriously. That means we can be as good as certain that insect suffering exists on a vast scale, and the low value of p is not a reason to discount it. This is precisely what makes the argument robust against the objection from uncertainty.

(c) The serious objection is the proves-too-much objection, and I think it is best understood as a version of Pascal's wager. Just as Pascal held that an infinite payoff makes even a tiny probability of God's existence decisive, the scale argument holds that an astronomically large payoff makes a low probability of insect sentience decisive. And the standard reply to Pascal transfers directly: this is the many-gods objection in a new form. There are many candidate bearers of experience, and no principled way to single out insects. The candidates cannot all be pursued at once, and they compete with one another for our attention and our resources, so the expected values cancel out and the argument defeats itself. The simple form of the scale argument therefore fails, and its defenders have not answered it.

(d) Even so, the conclusion still points somewhere. What the numbers really support is treating insect welfare as a research priority rather than as a target for direct intervention: a large expected stock is not the same thing as a large expected difference made by any particular thing we might do. For the latter we would need evidence about marginal effects — how many insect lives are created or prevented per dollar spent, whether the net effect of an intervention is positive or negative, and how intense insect experiences are if they exist at all. Since we have almost none of that evidence, the honest conclusion is that we should fund research into insect sentience rather than reallocate money from, for example, farmed fish welfare.
