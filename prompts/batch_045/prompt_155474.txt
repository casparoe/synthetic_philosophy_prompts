I would like an explanation of a puzzle about Death in Damascus that I have not seen discussed, together with an assessment of whether the puzzle is fair.

The base case. You are in Damascus and have learned that Death, who has never mispredicted the movements of a victim, has come for you and will be in whichever city you are in tomorrow. Your options are to stay, or to travel to Aleppo at some small cost c. Survival is worth a very large amount L and death is worth 0. Two background points can be taken as given. First, because the prediction is perfect, each pure option is certain to kill you, so the substantive difference between them is exhausted by c: the case is interesting less for what it recommends than for what it does to the reasoning of each theory. Second, on the usual diagnoses, evidential decision theory delivers a verdict that survives being conditionalized on itself (stay, and do not waste the fare), whereas the causal theorist's recommendation is unstable: conditional on staying, fleeing looks better, and conditional on fleeing, staying looks better, so no pure act is ratifiable.

The variant. Suppose that, for a further small cost d, you can hand the choice of destination to a physically indeterministic device — call it an oracle ticket — that Death's powers do not extend to. Stipulate that Death still predicts perfectly whatever you would do by deliberation alone; that when you use the device Death is reduced to guessing and, by symmetry, is in either city with probability 1/2; and that the outcome of the device is therefore probabilistically independent of Death's location. You now have three options: stay, flee, or randomize. Randomizing gives you a genuine 1/2 chance of survival at an expected cost of c/2 + d. Each pure option gives you none.

What I want explained is the following apparent inversion, and the mechanism behind it. Once you are certain that you will use the device, your credence that Death is in Damascus is 1/2. Causal decision theory then ranks staying (expected value L/2) above randomizing (L/2 − c/2 − d) above fleeing (L/2 − c), and so recommends the one option that guarantees death over the one option that offers an even chance of life. Evidential decision theory does not invert: it still assigns 0 to staying and −c to fleeing, assigns randomizing the same L/2 − c/2 − d, and so puts randomizing first. The two theories agree on the mixed option and disagree only about the pure ones, and the randomizer appears to cure the causal theorist's instability only by way of producing a ranking that is wrong in a way that now matters.

Please do three things. First, say precisely which assumption does the work. My own guess is that it is the requirement that the probabilities weighting dependency hypotheses be unconditional on the act, so that an act the agent is certain not to perform gets evaluated against a distribution over Death's location that obtains only if that act is not performed — but I would like that stated properly rather than gestured at. Second, explain how the randomizer interacts with the instability of the original case: is using the device ratifiable, and does the answer change depending on which conditionalization you use? Third, tell me where the argument is unfair to the causal theorist: what can a proponent say in reply about imaging or counterfactual revision of credences, about the formulation of dependency hypotheses, or about whether a deliberating agent can ever be certain of randomizing while still evaluating pure acts — and is that reply convincing, or does it merely relabel the problem?

Keep the discussion conceptual but exact, with the numbers made explicit where they carry weight. I do not need references or a survey of the literature; I need the structure of the disagreement. Six hundred to nine hundred words.
