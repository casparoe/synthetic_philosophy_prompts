I teach an upper-level philosophy & economics seminar and I'm putting together the take-home final, and I want one question on whether markets can be trusted as epistemic institutions given the no-trade theorems. Level is advanced undergrad / early PhD, 90 minutes, answers capped at about 900 words, 20 marks total.

Heres the setup I want students to work with, restated cleanly at the top of the question. Call it an *oracle market*: one security that pays $1 iff some proposition P is true; zero transaction costs; every trader starts with no position and no other exposure to P, so nobody is hedging anything; all orders clear through an anonymous central book, so you can see price, volume and the spread but never who you're trading with. Traders are expected-utility maximizers, and everyone knows the standard no-trade result: if priors are common and it's common knowledge that each trader trades only when she expects to gain, no speculative trade occurs at all, because the fact that someone is willing to take the other side is itself evidence that they hold information unfavourable to me.

The question itself: an outside observer — the bystander — has her own prior on P and is deciding how much to defer to the market price. Real oracle markets trade heavily, and trading volume is usually sold as a sign of informational health. Should she defer *less* the more the market trades, on the grounds that heavy volume is evidence the no-trade conditions fail and that what she is looking at is noise rather than aggregated knowledge? Or is that inference a howler?

I want the question to force three things. (a) Which no-trade condition must be failing, and why the market design blocks the easy answers — risk sharing, liquidity needs, transaction costs, heterogeneous endowments. (b) The awkward point that if prices fully reflected all the dispersed private information out there, nobody would have had any reason to gather that information in the first place, so some departure from the ideal looks constitutive of an epistemically useful market rather than a symptom of its failure. (c) What exactly the bystander is supposed to be deferring to — the level of the price, its movement over time, the spread — and whether 'the market believes P to degree x' is even well defined once priors aren't common.

Give me the question, then a model answer of roughly 900 words, then the marking scheme: five must-hit points with marks apiece, plus one line each on the usual way people miss it. I'd also like a short note separating an A from a B — it should be possible to get 13/20 by knowing the theorem cold and saying nothing new, and I want to know which specific move has to be there for the top band.
