i'm a political philosopher by training but they've put me on the first-year public ethics module, and i've stupidly agreed to do the corruption week, so i want to walk in with something better than the NGO-style definition everyone opens with — entrusted power used for private gain — because maybe half my cases fall outside it. Let me just say what i actually think case by case and see what you can make of it.

A nurse in a public hospital who hasn't been paid in three months takes cash from a family to find a bed for their mother: i won't call that corruption, it feels more like a toll, or like a wage. Same nurse, same envelope, but this time to push their mother ahead of another patient in a queue that otherwise works fine: corruption. A building inspector takes forty dollars to sign off on wiring that will burn a house down: corruption, and worse than the queue-jumping, even though the money is smaller. A mayor awards a contract to a firm run by his cousin's husband — lowest bid, good work, no kickback — and i still want to call it corruption, though i can't say what was stolen. A different mayor steers a bridge to her own district to win re-election, and i don't want to call that corruption, just politics, though it's also an office used for someone's advantage. A minister takes a job with a company he used to regulate; nothing was agreed in advance, but i call it corruption anyway and can't point to the moment it happened. A firm pays someone inside the city to nudge the training data of the algorithm that ranks permit applications, so theirs comes out on top: no official exercised judgement, no rule was broken, and i still say corruption. A procurement framework drafted so that exactly one domestic firm qualifies, drafted by people who'll go on to work there, legal at every step: i say corruption, and worse than the inspector, but there's no payment and no individual victim i can name. A company pays an impoverished village to consent to a waste facility: i go back and forth, and i lean toward not corruption, which worries me, because the thing being bought is the one thing i thought couldn't be bought. Gifts: a goat to the midwife after a safe birth, fine; a two-hundred-dollar gift card to a teacher from a parent, i flinch; corporate hospitality at a tennis tournament for a regulator, corruption. An official who quietly lets a widow skip a queue out of pity, taking nothing: i don't call it corruption, but only because nothing was gained, and that feels like the wrong reason. And then a border clerk in a state whose only real function is extraction, who takes money to lose the paperwork on a shipment of grain: i want to call that sabotage rather than corruption, but i suspect i only get to say that if i've already decided the state is illegitimate — which seems to make corruption depend on a political judgement i'd rather keep out of the definition.

My tentative principle, such as it is: an act is corrupt when someone occupying a role lets a consideration that the role exists to exclude override the considerations the role exists to answer to. So: does that actually deliver my verdicts, and where does it break? It looks like it can't reach the algorithm case, where nobody's reasoning was moved at all, only the inputs; and it looks like it might clear the procurement framers, who could say they were serving development policy all along. Does "private gain" need to be a separate clause, or should i drop it and accept that the pity case is corruption with an excuse? And is the principle culturally neutral, or does it smuggle in a specifically Western administrative picture — impersonal offices, rules that bind against relationship — given that in plenty of places a gift to an official is how you acknowledge a relation rather than how you buy a decision? Two of my colleagues say that calling those gifts corruption just exports one administrative ideal and calls it a moral standard, and i'd like to know whether my principle has the resources to say the gift cases are genuinely different in kind, or whether it has to say the difference is only in what the local norms happen to be.

Give me the principle in a form i could defend in a lecture, with the case that motivates each clause, verdicts on two cases i haven't listed (including one i'd definately find counterintuitive), and an honest list of which of my verdicts i have to give up. I'd rather drop a verdict than add a clause that only exists to save it.
