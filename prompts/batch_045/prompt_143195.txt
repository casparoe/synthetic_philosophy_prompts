I am in a philosophy of religion seminar that has spent the last three weeks on the modal version of the ontological argument, and I have managed to dig myself into a hole of my own making. My study group wrote out the axioms as they came in the handout: positivity is closed under necessary entailment; for every property, exactly one of it and its complement is positive; positivity is necessary if had at all; being God-like, i.e. having every positive property, is itself positive; necessary existence is positive; with essence defined in the usual way and necessary existence defined as having each of one's essences necessarily instantiated, and S5 in the background. The proof does go through to the necessary existence of a God-like being, and I followed every line. The trouble is that on Tuesday night I noticed the machinery seems to prove a great deal more than that, and I cannot see where to stop it.

Here is my version of the extra argument, as tightly as I can put it. For any proposition p, let [p] be the property of being such that p: everything has it if p is true, nothing has it if p is false. (1) By the second axiom, either [p] is positive or [not-p] is positive. (2) If [p] is positive, then any God-like being has [p], so p holds; and since a God-like being exists necessarily, p holds necessarily. (3) If [not-p] is positive, the same reasoning gives that not-p holds necessarily, hence that p is false. (4) So, for a true p, the second disjunct is unavailable and the first yields that p is necessary: every truth is necessary. (5) Applying that to not-p and contraposing, every possibility is actual. (6) So nothing at all is contingent: donkeys, my sitting in this seminar, the number of planets, everything, holds of necessity, and there are no merely possible worlds. (7) The theist in my group, who is the one who wanted this argument, is now unhappy for a reason he cannot dismiss: on this result God could not have created any world other than this one, could not have refrained from creating, and a Leibniz-style best-of-all-possible-worlds theodicy is vacuous because there is nothing to compare this world with. The argument seems to have eaten the doctrine it was meant to support.

My own repair was to restrict the second and first axioms to purely qualitative properties, those whose instantiation conditions make no reference to particular individuals or to the actual truth value of some proposition, so that [p] never gets to be positive and the collapse lemma has nothing to work with. But the friend who built the original argument says that the second axiom is also load-bearing earlier, in the lemma that every positive property is possibly instantiated: if a positive property could have no instances, then it necessarily entails its own complement, the closure axiom makes the complement positive too, and only the dichotomy axiom turns that into a contradiction. I also tried a devil-parody in which positive is systematically swapped for negative, and it broke on the axiom that necessary existence is positive rather than on anything structural, and I cannot tell whether to find that reassuring or damning.

So: which step should I actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Candidate targets I can see are the dichotomy axiom, the closure axiom, the necessity of positivity, the assumption that there is a property [p] for every proposition, the definitions of essence and necessary existence, the underlying modal logic, or the abundant conception of properties the lemma helps itself to. For each one I would like to know why it looked mandatory in the first place and what I lose by dropping it. I would also like a verdict on my repair: does restricting positivity to the qualitative really block the collapse, or does it merely relocate it, given that for a purely qualitative p the property [p] may well be qualitative itself, in which case I have bought necessitism about every qualitative truth rather than escaped it? And if the restriction does hold, does the conclusion still deliver a being with the traditional attributes, omniscience about contingent facts, providence, the ability to answer prayers, or have I saved the argument by emptying it? If I have to choose, I would most like to keep the closure axiom, the necessity of positivity, the claim that being God-like is positive, and the validity of the main proof; the dichotomy axiom is the one I would give up if I must, but I do not understand what I would be paying for it.
