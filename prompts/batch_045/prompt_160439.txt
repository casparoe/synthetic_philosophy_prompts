For the past two years I have been circling a novel whose central object is a manuscript that nobody wrote: a short prose text that gets copied, translated, and eventually filmed, every physical copy of which can be traced back to an earlier copy, and whose origin, whenever anyone bothers to investigate, turns out to be just another copy. In the book this is meant to be eerie rather than impossible, and the reaction of almost everyone who has read the draft has been to say that of course it is easy to imagine. That reaction is what I now have to teach against. I have agreed to run a single ninety-minute session in March for eight adult writers at a writers' centre — mostly commercial fiction, no philosophy beyond whatever they half-remember from school — nominally on premise and possibility. What I actually want them to leave with is the distinction between what they can picture or narrate and what can be coherently and completely described, plus the thought that conceivability is a graded, defeasible, and non-uniform guide: vivid imagining is not the same as conceiving a scenario under full specification, and failing to imagine something no more refutes its possibility than succeeding establishes it.

So please do not explain the epistemology of modality to me; plan how I get these eight people there in ninety minutes. What I need first is the dependency structure — which distinctions have to be on the board before which, and why. I have in mind the split between epistemic and metaphysical possibility, the difference between a scenario I have merely narrated, gaps and all, and one I have specified exhaustively, the move from a first-pass judgement to whatever survives sustained reflection, the difference between conceiving that p and conceiving of a thing that it is thus-and-so, and the point that the guide runs in two directions with different strength in each. Tell me in what order these must come, and which one, if introduced even five minutes late, collapses everything built on it.

Then give me the one case that should carry the session, and argue for it. My instinct is to use my own unauthored manuscript: they will all concede its conceivability in the first minute, and I can then spend the hour making that concession expensive, while staying inside a craft conversation they care about. But I worry that because it is a causal-loop case, the real dispute — whether the information in it has a source — will surface as a quarrel about causation and I will lose the thread. Is the unauthored manuscript load-bearing, or better used as the opener, with something else carrying the weight: an inverted spectrum, a colour no one has ever seen, arithmetic going wrong, a necessary truth discovered empirically? Whichever you choose, say what the example must have and what it must lack to do this job. After that, map the misconceptions. The ones I expect are that picturing something settles that it is possible, that being unable to picture something settles that it is not, that possible slides unnoticed between nomological and metaphysical, and that narratability settles whether a story's world is possible at all; tell me where in the sequence each must be defused, whether any can be headed off before it forms, which one is worth letting them commit to out loud so the correction lands harder, and which misconception I have not anticipated. Finally, give me an exit check that would show the distinction has actually landed rather than that they have learned to repeat the phrase conceivable but not possible — ideally something they perform on their own work in progress, since eight writers will want to leave with a usable rule — and a short explicit list of what I should refuse to get into at this level and say aloud that I am refusing. I would rather end with them unsettled about one premise in their own manuscript than armed with a slogan.
