So there's this group chat I'm in — mostly backend engineers who drifted into the effective-altruism-adjacent stuff — and they keep using the phrase "moral trade" like everyone knows what it means. The setup, as far as I've pieced together: two people disagree about what's actually worth doing. My friend Priya thinks factory farming is the top priority; my friend Marcus thinks it's existential risk from AI. Neither thinks the other's cause is worth anything. So they trade: Marcus donates a couple hundred bucks to an animal charity, Priya spends a weekend doing something Marcus cares about. Both of them think they got a steal. Apparently this is a known move and not just something they made up.

The framing they keep leaning on is that this is *just arbitrage* — like currency exchange between value systems, or two people who each overvalue what they hold and undervalue what the other holds, so there's a trade that leaves both better off. And they draw a pretty strong conclusion from it: moral trade is a gigantic unexploited resource, the only thing holding it back is transaction costs and lack of trust, so the highest-leverage move is to build the plumbing (matching, escrow, reputation, maybe even something price-like), and then you get gains from trade basically for free. One of them also said there's no way to exploit anyone in these deals, because by construction both sides walk away better off by their own lights.

I half buy it and half think the analogy is doing all the work. Can you audit it?

What I want:

1. The correspondence table, made explicit. What plays the trader, the asset, the currency, the price, the bid-ask spread, the arbitrageur, the exchange. In particular: what is supposed to be the numeraire? I suspect that's the load-bearing part.
2. What the market picture quietly imports that the target doesn't have — the stuff nobody says out loud but that the argument needs.
3. Where it breaks, and specifically which breaks matter for the conclusion they're drawing and which are cosmetic.

Some things I've chewed on and can't settle:

- In a real market my gain is denominated in one unit and I can tell whether I gained and by how much. Here each side's gain is measured against their own standard, so "both sides gained" isn't one fact, it's two facts in units that don't convert — and I can't even verify the other side's report, since what they're reporting is how much something matters to them.
- A friend who did some mechanism design mumbled something like: markets work because money gives you transferable utility, which is what lets you separate "is this deal efficient" from "who gets most of the surplus," and moral trade has nothing that plays that role. I didnt really follow it. Is that right, and if it is right, how badly does it damage the analogy?
- Arbitrage normally pushes prices toward the truth. Here, if I think Priya's moral view is flatly false and I trade with her anyway, I'm acting on her error rather than against it — and unlike shorting an overpriced stock, acting on it doesn't nudge anything toward correct, it seems to make the false view *more* effective in the world. So the analogy looks like it flips sign exactly where it's supposed to be most attractive. Marcus says that's fine, it's just taking money off a counterparty who's mispriced something, nobody's harmed. Someone else said it's closer to blackmail-adjacent, because you can also manufacture the bad thing the other side wants you to stop doing. I genuinely can't tell who's closer to right.
- Delivery and enforcement. In a market I get paid up front or I have recourse. Here the counterparty's performance is often unverifiable, or unverifiable *by me*, or something they'd have done anyway — and once the trade is done I have no reason to trust their report.

Then the part I actually care about: does the "build the plumbing, and there's no exploitation worry" conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table? Does the analogy survive as a *heuristic* even if it fails as a model? And if the market picture is the wrong one, what's a better analogy — someone in the chat suggested legislative vote-trading / log-rolling, someone else suggested cross-chain atomic swaps, which I liked but I write distributed systems for a living so I would. Something where the failure modes are known and I can reason about them.

Please don't give me reading lists or citations — I'm on a plane and cant follow up on anything, so just argue it out. Assume I'm fine with probability, expected value and incentive talk, and assume I've never taken a philosophy class in my life, so spell out anything that's standard background in that world.
