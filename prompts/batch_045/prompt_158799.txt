I am a novelist. I need a plan, not an explanation.

My new novel turns on a conservation biologist who concludes that the restored wetland she spent eight years building for a local land trust is, per hectare, a net source of suffering: her word for it is a "welfare sink," a habitat whose added carrying capacity is filled mostly by very short lives that end badly, so that creating or maintaining more of it adds suffering on balance, while the degraded scrub it replaced was, on her accounting, a "welfare source." She quietly lets the site revert and has to defend that choice at a public meeting.

Next month I am giving a forty-minute author talk co-hosted by a land trust, to roughly forty people — retired birders, a high-school environmental club, a few farmers, and general readers who have not read the book. I want them to be able to follow how a decent person arrives at that conclusion, not to accept it. Give me the plan: what has to be in place, and in what order, before the sink/source distinction can even be stated without sounding like a paradox — I suspect the difference between total and per-capita welfare, and the standing of the animals who are never born, has to come very early; the one concrete site that should carry the whole argument and what it must have and lack; the objections in the order this audience will actually raise them — the balance of nature, "so she wants to bulldoze," the unmeasurability of wild animal welfare, suffering not being the only thing that matters, and the sharper one about whether adding lives that are on the whole decent can be bad — and at which point in the sequence each should be answered rather than absorbed; what I should openly decline to settle in forty minutes; and one question I can ask at the end that would tell me the idea has landed rather than merely unsettled people. Tell me how confident you are that this arc works with this audience, and single out the step you are least sure of. Please write in American English.
