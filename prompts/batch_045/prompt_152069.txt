hey — I need help building an actual research agenda, not a lit review. I've got about four people (all philosophy/political-theory backgrounds, zero ML) and one year inside an AI lab, and the deliverable is internal writing that could plausibly change what the lab does about whose values the system ends up being tuned to serve. So the question is: what do we work on, and in what order?

The map I'm starting from has four clusters, and I'm not attached to it: (a) who counts as a value-holder at all — only currently living humans, or also future people, also people whose existence depends on which values get implemented (which feels like a genuine trap, since the choice of values changes who shows up), also non-human animals; (b) what the stuff being aggregated even is — felt preferences, idealized or informed preferences, values proper, interests, welfare; (c) how to combine it — voting-ish schemes, bargaining, some overlapping-consensus or veil-of-ignorance style construction; and (d) the thing I keep getting stuck on, which is that disagreement about the aggregation rule is itself just more moral disagreement, so any procedure you advertise as neutral is a contested first-order moral choice, and "aggregate over the aggregation rules" looks like it just regresses one level and starts again. One of the engineers here keeps saying the real answer is to just take the revealed preferences of actual users and stop overthinking, and I want the agenda to say explicitly whether that's the view to beat or a distraction.

Two half-baked ideas of my own that I'd rather you push back on than politely incorporate. First, value escrow: for the questions we genuinely can't settle, don't aggregate — train the system to hold them open, to preserve future people's ability to decide, and to treat destroying an option as the main thing to avoid. It sidesteps (d), but I worry it's not really neutral either (whoever builds the escrow mechanism still decides what counts as keeping an option open versus quietly closing it) and that deferring everything is indistinguishable from having no values at all. Second, a stake-weighted lottery across aggregation rules: instead of picking a rule, give each constituency a probability of the whole system going its way, weighted by how much they have at stake, so nobody gets diluted into a compromise nobody endorses.

So: given four people, twelve months, and the goal of actually changing practice rather than just being correct, what goes on the list and in what order? I care much more about the reasoning than the list — tell me what criteria you're using (how much else depends on the answer, whether philosophers without empirical data can actually make progress on it in a year, whether it's already crowded, whether the lab would ever act on an answer), where those criteria conflict, and what result or evidence in the next year or two should move something up or down. And please do tell me if one of my two ideas is a dead end — I'd rather kill it in January.
