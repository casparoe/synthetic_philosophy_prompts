I'm coauthoring a paper on epistemic arguments for democracy with my advisor, and I keep tripping over the same question: when exactly does he think the Condorcet jury theorem licenses deferring to a majority? I want to predict his reaction before I bring it up, so here's his track record on cases we've actually talked through. All of them are groups making a yes/no call, everyone votes at the same time unless I say otherwise, and in every case the question is whether the group should just do what the majority says.

1. 11 voters, independent, each about 0.60 reliable: yes, defer, and he'll add that the majority is more reliable than any single member.
2. 11 voters, 0.60 each, but they all got their information from the same misleading news source, so their votes move together: no. He says the group is effectively one voter and the theorem has nothing to say.
3. 1,001 voters, independent, each 0.501: yes, and he's happy to say majority reliability is near-certain even though no individual is better than a coin flip by any amount you'd notice.
4. 21 voters, independent, competences all somewhere between 0.70 and 0.80: yes, majority comfortably beats the best member.
5. 3 voters, 0.60 each, but they vote one at a time seeing the earlier votes and end up following the first voter: no. He says sequential voting is worse than simultaneous here and independence is gone.
6. 51 voters, 0.60 each, except that the 20 who feel least confident abstain, and being unsure is exactly what makes them abstain: yes, more emphatically than in case 1. He says voluntary abstention by the unsure helps rather than hurts.
7. 11 voters, three options instead of two, each voter 0.60 likely to pick the best one with errors split evenly between the other two, independent: yes, defer to plurality. He says the binary version is just the clean case, not the limit of the idea.

The new case I want to bring him: a 7-person committee taking an up-or-down vote, voting simultaneously, votes independent. One member has read the full file and is about 0.90 reliable. The other six skimmed the one-page summary and are each about 0.55. If I've done the arithmetic right, the majority comes out around 0.71 — better than the 0.61 you'd get from seven people all at 0.55, but well short of 0.90 for just asking the one person who did the reading. So what does he say, and more importantly what's he actually running?

What I want from you: lay out the two or three rules that fit cases 1 through 7 about equally well — I can see something like "independence plus everyone above chance," and something like "independence plus roughly homogeneous competence," and something comparative where the majority only wins if it beats the best alternative rule available (the expert, or a weighted vote where each person's weight scales with the log of their odds). Say what each predicts about my 7-person committee, give me your best guess at his verdict with the reason for preferring it, tell me which of his earlier verdicts is doing the most work in your prediction, and name the single variant case that would separate the rules cleanly — I was thinking of re-running the same committee with the expert at 0.60 instead of 0.90, or holding her at 0.90 and growing the number of 0.55 voters. Do the arithmetic if it helps, and please answer in American English.
