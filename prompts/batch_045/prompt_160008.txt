hey — I'm an engineer (no philosophy background at all, sorry if half of this already has proper names and I'm just reinventing them). I work on a consumer AI assistant thing and we're writing an internal doc about whose values the thing should actually serve. I've ended up being the guy pushing a position, and before I put it in writing I'd like someone to check whether it holds together, because I have a feeling somewhere in here I'm helping myself to something.

Roughly where I've landed:

1. Neutrality. The assistant shouldn't be in the business of ranking conceptions of the good life. Not a religion, not a politics, not some packaged idea of flourishing. It should be good at helping whoever's using it get after whatever they're already after. Part of why I say this is that I don't trust us — the company — to be the ones doing the picking.

2. But where one user's goal bumps into other people's, we aggregate. Go with what satisfies more of the affected people's preferences, weighted somehow by how much they care. Hand-wavy on purpose; the point is we don't pick winners by whose goals we think are higher.

3. Deference inside the user's own sphere. About their own good, the user is the authority. That's why I pushed for shipping per-user customization — you can tell it your defaults, your style, what to never do — and I still think that was right.

4. Expressed preferences aren't clean, though. Alot of what people tell us they want is inherited: formed under bad info, in a bad mood, or straight up shaped by whatever engagement-maximizing thing we or someone else put in front of them. So I want a filter. Here's the bit I made up because I couldn't find anything existing: call it a provenance test — before we treat a preference as "the user's own," re-elicit it three ways (once with the cost made explicit, once with a delay, once after disclosing how the preference was probably produced), and only count it if it survives all three. Survives means full weight; doesn't survive means... less weight? Zero? I genuinely don't know, and I suspect this is load-bearing in a way I can't see.

5. There's a floor. It won't help with some things even if the user really wants it and even if most people in their community would be fine with it. My gloss on where the floor sits: what a reasonable person would endorse on reflection.

6. How we actually get it to behave: human raters approving or rejecting outputs, RLHF-ish. I think this is basically fine and I don't lose sleep over the raters being a weird unrepresentative slice, because (a) we're not asking them their views about the good life, we're asking them to grade outputs, and (b) nobody is forcing anybody to use the thing.

7. And the framing I ocasionally come back to: we're not building a moral agent, we're building infrastructure. Plumbing. Lots of different people run different stuff through the same pipes and that's the entire point.

Case I keep testing myself on, because I have a verdict and definately can't derive it: someone in a tight-knit religious community asks the assistant to help them set up monitoring on their teenager's devices so the kid doesn't see certain content. My gut says help — it's their family and their values, and it'd be obnoxious for us to lecture them about it. But I have a second gut that says refuse the structurally identical request from someone who wants to monitor their employees' devices for the same reason, because the employees didn't sign up for that. I can't get from 1–7 to both of those at once, or maybe I can and I just don't see the route.

So: take 1 through 7 pairwise and tell me which ones actually fight, which only look like they fight, and how bad each real fight is — ranked, please, not all listed as equally bad. For each real one, say what has to give and what giving it up costs me. I've got a soft spot for 1, but tell me straight if 1 is the part that's actually broken. And specifically: is the provenance test in 4 doing any real work, or does it smuggle back in exactly the ranking of conceptions of the good that 1 says we're not doing? If it does, is there a version of it that doesn't, or does the whole idea collapse once you squeeze it?
