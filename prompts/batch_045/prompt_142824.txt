I somehow ended up on the committee of a very small local heritage society — we put up plaques, run one open afternoon a year, that sort of thing — and I keep walking into the same strange configuration and I don't have a word for it. First case: our village has a story that the clock on the old town hall was paid for by a subscription from the mill workers, and every year at the open afternoon somebody tells that story while we all stand under the clock. About fifteen years ago a retired accountant went through the council minutes and the subscription list, and it turns out the money came from a single mill owner, and the workers actually petitioned *against* the clock because they wanted the money for the sick fund. Everyone on the committee now knows this. Nobody thinks the minutes are forged. And still the story gets told every year, and when I gently suggested last summer that we adjust the wording, the reaction wasn't "but the minutes are wrong" — it was closer to panic, and the thing that actually got said out loud was "well then there's no point doing the clock at all". And, sure enough, we've more or less stopped doing the clock. Second case: the next valley over has a story about the 1968 flood, that the valley authority opened the sluices without warning them, which the authority's own logs say it didn't. They never use that story to make claims about 1968. They use it every time the authority proposes anything now: it is the thing that entitles them to say they've never cared about us and to refuse to take up their seat on the joint board. Individually they'll concede the logs are probably right. But correcting them doesn't get you to "the logs are right and the authority still doesn't care about us" — it gets you to nobody saying anything at all, and the seat sitting empty.

So the pattern I think I'm seeing: a shared story about the past that (a) isn't really *believed*, in the sense that nobody will stake anything on it as evidence about what happened, but (b) isn't a legend or a lie either, because it doesn't seem to be trying to be believed, (c) does real work in the present — it's what licenses a stance, a grievance, a bit of local standing, a refusal to cooperate — and (d) can't be *corrected*, only deleted: put the accurate version in its place and the practice around it collapses instead of being updated by it. A friend said "that's just collective memory", which feels both true and useless — it just says that groups remember things, and it doesn't touch the part where the story survives being known to be false and dies when something true is substituted for it.

Is there an established concept for this exact thing? And please don't just hand me a label, I want the carving. How is it different from an ordinary false belief that a group happens to share, from a myth people take literally, from straight propaganda or a useful lie somebody knowingly tells, and from nothing more than outdated history nobody has got round to fixing? I suspect it's none of those but sits close to several of them, and I'd want to be told which is nearest and exactly where it comes apart. I also want the edges: is it still the same thing if nobody has ever actually checked the record? If the story turns out to be true? If the stance it licenses could perfectly well be defended from the accurate record, but nobody bothers to re-derive it that way once the story goes? If people, when challenged, insist the story is literally true — does that push it back into being a plain false belief, or is that insistence itself part of the phenomenon? And one more thing, since this is what the arguments here actually turn on: if a story like this isn't in the business of stating facts about the past at all, does it even make sense to ask whether the record or the memory should give way, or is that the wrong question to be asking? If there genuinely is no term for it, I'd rather get a coined one with a definition and a couple of boundary cases than a name I can't actually use.
