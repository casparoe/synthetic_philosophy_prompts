Bit of an odd request — I'm a novelist, mid-draft, and there's a scene I can't get right. The book's about a thing I'm calling a Person-Book, which is basically that old idea of a book you can hold a conversation with: it maps any conversation-so-far onto a continuation, and it answers exactly as one particular dead person would. In my version it's built by "saturation interview" — forty years of recordings of a philosopher called Miriam Kessel, plus a fitted generator filling the gaps — and it's stored as one static table. Every possible continuation is already inscribed in it. It also compresses: the foundation that owns it found the whole table reduces to a few gigabytes of rule, and they want to decompile it into something readable, which would destroy the table as a table.

The scene is a kitchen at 3am, the night before the decompile. Two people who've both lived with this thing:

Ana built it, nine years, and she has had conversations with it that Kessel never had — it answered a technical objection published after Kessel died and revised an earlier position of its own doing so. She thinks it's Kessel, or at least that there's no principled place to draw the line. Dev was Kessel's student, he's not a cartoon sceptic, he grants the book is behaviourally perfect, that it can be surprised, that it holds grudges across a thousand turns. He thinks it's a hollow artefact. He also cried the first time it wrote back to him, and he's not proud of that.

I want them to actually go at it, not deliver speeches. The bits I need them to sink their teeth into:

- If every possible reply is already inscribed, there are infinitely many mutually inconsistent Kessels in there. Being person-like is cheap when all the continuations are pre-written. Ana has to answer that rather than change the subject.
- The ablation finding: they swapped out about sixty per cent of the table for generic plausible filler, and testers only noticed in one session in twenty. Whose side does that even help?
- Table versus rule. Does it matter that the same behaviour is generatable from a compact rule instead of a giant list? Or is that just an implementation detail?
- Time. A closed book has no experiences between readings. Is one instantaneous reading enough to host a mind, or does consciousness need a run through time with uptake from the world?
- The confession problem. Told it might be decompiled, the book produced a long argument against its own decompilation, and then admitted it genuinely couldn't tell whether it was conscious. Ana thinks that avowal is the strongest evidence there is. Dev thinks it's the cheapest possible output of a surface — the shape of a confession with nothing behind it. I want that one fought over properly.
- Grief. Ana should press Dev on whether his tears are evidence he's denying; Dev should have a real answer, something like: grief tracks likeness, not presence, people have wept at letters and at puppets.
- And the awkward one: Ana's already built a book of her mother from voicemails, and her mother had dementia and was barely there at the end. The mother-book is more fluent than her mother ever was by then, and Ana finds it obscene and also finds it the only place her mother still argues with her. Dev gets to use this against her. I want her reply to cost her something.

Neither of them can win by appealing to authority, no naming of philosophers or papers or experiments — if Dev wants the move about the person who shuffles symbols without understanding, he has to say it in the kitchen in his own words, and Ana has to answer the move rather than the name. They're tired, they interrupt, they're occasionally cruel, and they've known each other fifteen years. Roughly equal airtime. Don't resolve it, but let each of them have shifted a little by the end, and let each have said one thing they'd take back.

British English throughout, please — -ise spellings, whilst, that sort of thing; nothing American slipping in.

Give it to me in four named sections, in this order:

1. "Where we are" — about 120 words. Brief prose note on how to stage the scene: what's in the room, what just happened, what's due at dawn. This is for me, not for the page.
2. "The scene" — about 1,400 words. The dialogue itself, sparse stage directions, no narrator commentary inside it.
3. "What each is still holding" — about 250 words. The unresolved thing each of them still owes the other, and the single sentence each would most regret by morning.
4. "My crib sheet" — about 250 words. Bullets: each argumentative move, who makes it, and what it commits them to later in the book, so I don't contradict myself in chapter twelve.
