I've been chewing on the paradox of voting on and off for years and I think I've finally located the part that actually bugs me, but I cant get the pieces to line up. So: explain the conceptual structure to me like I'm someone who's read all the standard stuff and still feels like the standard move is hiding something.

Assume I'm a fairly hardline expected-value consequentialist about whether to drive to the polling station, and assume a big electorate. The familiar move is: my vote only changes anything in the knife-edge case where it breaks or creates an exact tie, the probability of that is tiny (roughly on the order of one over the square root of the number of voters, or whatever the scaling actually is), so even multiplied by enormous stakes the product is smaller than the cost of the trip. Fine.

Now make the election *margin-sensitive* instead. There are thresholds sitting around with discontinuous consequences attached: an automatic recount if the margin is under half a percent, a runoff if nobody clears some plurality, a party losing ballot access or public funding if its share drops below some small number, a seat-allocation threshold, whatever. Now "did my vote matter" isn't one event, it's a union of a bunch of events, each a lot more probable than a tie, and near a threshold the counterfactual isn't "which candidate wins" but "does a whole extra process get triggered at all" — a recount, a second election, litigation, a party's long-term viability.

Here's the bit I really cant get straight, though. The pivot event and the value term don't look independent to me. Conditional on my vote mattering in any of those senses, the electorate is almost exactly at a boundary, which is strong evidence that my private guess about which side is actually better is close to worthless — and also evidence that the "stakes" I multiplied in aren't the stakes I'd have assigned unconditionally. The usual calculation treats the probability and the payoff as separable and multiplies them. I want an account of what actually goes wrong there and what the right replacement picture is: is this just a decision-theory choice (causal vs evidential vs "what does the version of me who decides to go vote tell me about the world"), is it a value-of-information point wearing a costume, or is the whole single-shot EV frame the wrong frame and the real answer something like "you're picking a policy for a large population of near-copies of yourself"?

And separately: how is the correlation stuff supposed to interact with the threshold stuff? The kind of person who reasons "p is tiny, I'll stay home" is statistically a lot like me, and abstentions cluster within a demographic, so my decision looks like evidence about a bloc rather than about one token — but that sounds like it either dissolves the problem entirely or proves way too much, and I can't tell which. Please walk me through what's doing the real work and what's a red herring, and don't just list considerations: I want to know how they compose.
