I write a column on science and ideas, and our desk has been having one of its recurring arguments about how firmly to play single new studies: one colleague wants to run the surprising primate-cognition result essentially as reported, another wants to bury it in a paragraph on how few findings of that kind survive replication, and I keep noticing that both of them are reaching for the same vocabulary — inside view, outside view, base rates, epistemic modesty — without either of them having said what a rule for weighing these against each other would actually have to do in order to be worth adopting as desk policy. I have read enough popular treatments of the inside/outside view distinction and of the replication crisis to know the slogans and the cautionary anecdotes; what I lack is a specification. So before I recommend any particular heuristic to my editor, I want the requirements list.

Please write the desiderata that any workable norm for weighting outside-view (base-rate, distributional) evidence against inside-view (case-specific, mechanistic) evidence would have to meet, where the purpose is fixed as follows: it is to govern a science desk deciding, usually within a day and without access to a statistician, whether and how firmly to run a single new result, and it must be defensible to an editor who has not done the reporting. For each requirement give the concrete case or consideration that makes it a requirement. The ones I think belong on the list, though I want you to add, cut, and reframe freely: what the rule owes us when there is arguably no reference class at all (a genuinely unprecedented result, where the outside view appears to have nothing to say); how it settles reference-class shopping, i.e. several defensible reference classes yielding different base rates, and whether the winner has to be specifiable in advance rather than after seeing the answer; what I have started calling the selection shadow, the fact that the published and press-covered record from which any base rate would be drawn was itself assembled by people who were not using the outside view, so the distribution is contaminated by the very enthusiasm the rule is meant to discount; what happens when the rule is performative, because adopting desk-wide modesty would alter the population the base rate is drawn from; how it handles a case in which the inside-view evidence is itself evidence about the heterogeneity of the reference class, so that discounting by the class average looks like ignoring the very information that distinguishes this case; what the output of the rule even is, given that a credence, a hedge in print, and a decision to publish come apart, and given that the writer's costs of a false alarm and a missed story are not the reader's; what I would call reception sensitivity, the fact that hedged prose is systematically read back as assertion, so a norm that is calibrated in the writer's head may be mis-calibrated once published; how cheap the rule must be to apply under deadline; whether an editor can audit its application without redoing the inside view; and finally that it must retain discrimination — a rule that licenses nothing is not modest, it is just silent. For each requirement, say what would count as cheating in satisfying it (for instance, one that handles the unprecedented case by permitting whatever the reporter already wanted to do has not handled it).

Two further things. First, mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as the preference of a particular camp, and identify any pair or subset you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, since that tension is the part I most want to see; if the list turns out to be internally unsatisfiable, say so rather than quietly dropping a requirement. Second, tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding the candidate heuristics people actually reach for — anchoring on the base rate and adjusting, the reference-class forecasting style of procedure, an inside view gated by a mechanistic-plausibility check, and some precision-weighted pooling of the two — and then give a brief scorecard of those four against the list, counting as unsatisfied only what can be judged without new data. I am less interested in the scoring than in the criteria and their motivating cases, so keep the weight there.
