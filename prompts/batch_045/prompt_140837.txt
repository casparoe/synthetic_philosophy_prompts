I teach a graduate seminar on moral objectivity, and next week we reach the cross-cultural literature on folk moral judgment: the now-familiar complaint that the subject pools are Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic, alongside the argument that runs from documented variation in folk responses to a conclusion about whether there are objective moral facts. My students can recite both the critique and the standard replies in their sleep, and I want something that makes them *feel* the weakness rather than report it. So I would like a piece of satire to hand out.

The piece: an internal memo-report from a fictional funding body, the Bureau of Convergent Intuitions, written in Year 12 of its Convergence Mandate and addressed to its oversight committee. It announces that the Bureau has at last established an objective moral fact by means of the Residual Sample Protocol. Deadpan bureaucratic register, in-universe throughout, roughly 800–900 words, with numbered sections, a small attrition table, and an annex defining the Conceptual Discount Rate applied to returns from non-WEIRD cohorts.

How the Bureau got there should be visible in the report without ever being confessed. Its sample is winnowed through a series of exclusion stages, each individually defensible in the way real methodological choices are: participants who fail the comprehension gate; participants who re-describe the vignette in local idiom and are therefore scored as having construed it non-morally; cohorts whose answers shift under rewording (the Bureau reworded them); cohorts whose variance exceeds a threshold and are consequently down-weighted rather than reported; and so on down to the Cohort of Last Resort, N = 41, whose composition should be quietly discoverable from the report's own metadata — they are the Bureau's staff, its graduate students, and their relatives. The proposition on which this cohort converges should be a sentence so heavily hedged that no one could dissent from it, and the word "objective" should not appear until the penultimate paragraph. Include one quoted fieldwork officer whose objection is being administratively processed rather than answered.

Constraints: the methods must be recognizably real methodological moves, exaggerated in degree only — no science fiction, no villainy, no named real researchers or papers. The Bureau must never use the words "circular," "gerrymandered," or "cherry-picked," and must never state an objection to itself; a reader should nonetheless be able to say what is being ironized, and a specialist should find the satire fair rather than a caricature. The jokes have to come from the content. Invented technical vocabulary is welcome where it helps.

After the piece, on a line of its own and outside the fiction, give me "Target:" followed by a single sentence naming the inference or practice being satirized, so that I have an answer key when I read these aloud.
