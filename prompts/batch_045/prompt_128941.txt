so I'm on the committee for this small Rousseau-themed weekend we run every summer (long story — there's a lake, a lot of amateur flutes, and one guy who insists on coming as a beaver) and I need a fake primary-source document to print in the programme and have somebody read aloud before the opening ceremony. Can you write it?

The setup: a present-day small town — say eight thousand people, on a lake, somewhere in the Jura — voted three years ago to shut down its municipal theatre and replace it, on principle, with civic festivals in the open air. The thing I want is a report from the town's "Office of Public Amusements" to the council, taking stock in year three. 700–900 words. Deadpan, warm, sincerely pleased with itself, in the voice of a convinced follower of Rousseau's case against the theatre who has not noticed that anything has gone wrong: the reader should get the joke and the author should not. Invent a name for the town and for the person signing the report. No dystopia — no police, no arrests, no grimness; the whole thing should be municipal, mild, and administrative, with the horror entirely in the paperwork and the good cheer.

The doctrine I want the jokes to come out of — work from my sketch below rather than from anything you remember, and I'd rather you paraphrase than put anything in quotation marks as if it were from the text:

- the theatre's emotions are wasted: people weep copiously at invented misfortunes and are then hardened, or just annoyed, by real ones. The feeling discharges on the stage and produces nothing.
- the actor's trade is precisely to appear to be what he is not, and a town that pays people to do that has created a class of professional counterfeiters.
- the spectator is the real problem: he sits, in the dark, passive, separate from everyone else, and pays money for the privilege of feeling something at a safe distance.
- comedy is worse than tragedy. A comedy has to make an audience laugh, and what audiences laugh at is... not virtue. Rousseau's exhibit is Molière's Misanthrope, where the honest man is turned into the butt of the joke and the smooth manipulator is the one who gets the good lines.
- the theatre is expensive, it manufactures tastes the town didn't have before, and it distracts people who would otherwise be content with simple pleasures after work.
- the alternative he proposes: festivals in the open air, under the sky, with no paid performers and no audience — everyone is at once actor and spectator, everyone is visible to everyone, and the community is a spectacle to itself. That transparency is supposed to be what keeps people decent, modesty included.

Here's the bit I want you to build the piece around, which is my own addition and not Rousseau's: the Office has concluded that the "wasted emotion" objection applies just as much to their festivals as to plays, and has fixed it with a Veracity Rule — a festival may only depict a real misfortune that actually happened in the town, and it must be staged by the people it happened to. So there is now an annual re-enactment of the flood of 2019, performed by the flood victims, and the report is delighted to announce that the tears are no longer wasted. Take that seriously and follow where it goes: what does the Office have to do next to keep the rule satisfied (only certain misfortunes are "festival-grade"; the Office has to keep some in reserve; people who have had nothing bad happen to them have nothing to contribute; the same people have to keep re-living the same year).

Some other things to work in:

- a loophole for the ban on actors: the Office employs "animateurs," who are not actors because they represent only themselves and are merely paid to be watched. There are now eleven of them, they have a union, and there is a three-year training programme.
- since a spectator is by definition someone who watches without being watched, the Office has had to do something about a hill on the edge of town from which you can see the square without being seen from it.
- the comedy ban, and what people are laughing at instead now that there's no comedy to laugh at, reported as a success.
- the closing figures: participation certificates issued, spectators fined, and — the report's proudest number — attendance, which is measured by counting the people watching, and is up 40%.
- one moment, near the end, where the report brushes right up against the obvious objection and swerves away from it without ever stating it.
- one quoted objector — an invented local, maybe the old prompter from the demolished theatre, or a schoolmaster — quoted dismissively in the report and answered, badly, on doctrine.

Hard rules: nothing in quotation marks from any real text, no footnotes, no citations, no bibliography, and don't let the author wink — no "ironic" italics, no punchlines that admit the game. It has to be readable as straight-faced municipal self-congratulation while still being obviously, to us, a disaster.
