Her argument, numbered, and I've tried to be fair to it:

(1) All three verdicts are computed from the same four cell rates; they're just weighted averages with different weights. So no dataset "contains" a direction of effect — the direction comes from the weighting, and the data on their own never pick out a weighting.
(2) A weighting is admissible only if the thing you weight by isn't itself caused by the programme. Centre and prior attainment are both pre-allocation, so both weightings are admissible — and admissible weightings disagree.
(3) So the only way to settle it is to control for more, not less: when two admissible partitions disagree, the finer one wins. Call it finer-is-fairer — keep refining until the strata are homogeneous enough that the weighting stops mattering.
(4) You can always refine. Add more covariates, and for a continuous one — score on the diagnostic, age, travel time to campus — there's no principled stopping point short of one student per stratum.
(5) At one student per stratum there's no within-stratum comparison to make: no student got both programmes. And by (3) every coarser verdict has already been overruled.
(6) So no finite body of data ever supports a causal comparison. Which is absurd — trials, and plain common sense, do deliver causal knowledge from data.

I'd rather keep (1), (2) and (6) than give up any of them, so I suppose (3) is where I get off — except that (3) is exactly what people seem to do with the kidney stone example, and if I drop it I've no rule at all for which verdict to print in the report. My suspicion is the rot sets in earlier than (3), but I can't put my finger on where.
