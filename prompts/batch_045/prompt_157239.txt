I write about science and ideas, and I keep coming back to the great toilet-paper panic of March 2020 as a test case for the methodological individualism vs. holism fight. The facts as I understand them: nobody actually ran out of pulp, there was no real shortage of the stuff, households just bought way more than usual for a few weeks, and the shelves stayed thin for months partly because residential and commercial tissue are seperate products made and shipped on different lines — the commercial side collapsed overnight and the residential side couldn't retool fast.

What I want is a disciplined mapping, not vibes. On the individualist side: who are the individuals — households, store managers, mills, truckers — and does the choice of unit change the explanation? On the holist side: what are the social-level items — 'the supply chain', 'consumer confidence', restocking norms, the just-in-time conventions that keep buly low-margin goods moving — and do any of them do real explanatory work, or are they shorthand for a pile of individual actions plus a stock-flow identity?

Then the part I'm stuck on. I've been carrying around a test of my own: call it the non-paraphrasability test — a social-level claim earns its keep only if you can't restate it as a claim about particular individuals plus an aggregation rule without loosing something explanatory. By that test 'there was a shortage' seems to survive, since it looks like a fact about a distribution system's operating rules (what counts as in stock, how often shelves get reset) rather than about how many rolls anybody wanted. But I can't tell whether my test is letting in too much or too little, and I'd rather find that out than be told I'm right. Also say what kind of evidence would actually settle it — an agent-based simulation, a natural experiment, some counterfactual.

And run it the other direction: what does the episode do to the theory? Does it vindicate holism, because brittleness and cascade look like system-level properties no individual-level model would have predicted, or does the individualist just retell the whole thing as a chain of locally sensible decisions and win? Be plain about where the frame is silent: does individualism vs. holism have anything at all to say about who was to blame, or about what anyone should have done?
