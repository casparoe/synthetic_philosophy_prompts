I am supervising an MPhil thesis on whether the distinction between doing and allowing — and, more narrowly, between killing and letting die — carries any moral weight of its own, and before my student writes the chapter that sets out what an account of the distinction would have to achieve, I want that specification written properly rather than assembled ad hoc from whichever cases happen to come to mind. So I would like from you a list of desiderata that any adequate theory of the doing/allowing distinction must meet, with the case or consideration behind each, and a view on which of them are genuinely required and which are merely the preferences of one camp (for instance, demands that the distinction be metaphysically prior to any normative facts, that it be describable without appeal to causation, that it be binary, or that it be neutral between a rights-based and a consequentialist framework).

The list should be pushed into territory that the standard examples do not cover well, since that is where the accounts I have read seem to come apart. In particular I want it to handle: cases of double prevention, where an agent does not create a threat but diverts an existing one onto a different person, or removes something that was shielding them, so that the harm runs through the agent without originating in them; the difference between withholding support and withdrawing support one is already providing, and why that difference, if it is one, should matter when the patient's prospects are held fixed; cases in which someone's allowing is itself constituted by a long chain of small commissions — budget decisions, triage protocols, default settings in a system nobody individually chose — so that the description of the event as an omission is contested rather than given; and cases in which the agent is an automated or artificial system, where doing and allowing come apart as the difference between running and declining to run a computation, and where there is no bodily movement, effort, or phenomenology of agency to anchor the intuitive contrast. I also want the list to say what the distinction must not collapse into: it must not simply track causation, since some allowings look causally necessary for the outcome, and it must not simply track intention or foresight, since those are standardly treated as separate; and it should say something about the weight question, namely whether the distinction is to do independent moral work or is at best a proxy for other factors such as the cost to the agent, the existence of a duty of aid, or the difference between infringing a right and failing to confer a benefit.

Two things matter to me most in the answer. First, tell me which pairs of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — if, for example, requiring that the distinction be sharp and non-negotiable is in tension with requiring that it handle distributed and automated agency, I want that stated as a result about the specification itself rather than smoothed over. Second, identify the single requirement that does the most work in ruling out existing contenders, and say why. The purpose the theory is being built for is fixed: it is meant to be usable by someone writing policy for an institution — a hospital trust drawing up guidance on withdrawing treatment, or a team specifying the default behaviour of an autonomous system that could intervene in a harm — who needs to know whether declining to act is, in the case at hand, morally different from acting, and if so how much of a difference it makes. If you think some of my cases are bad tests of a theory because they build in the very contestable description at issue, say so and tell me what should replace them. Please write the whole answer in British English.
