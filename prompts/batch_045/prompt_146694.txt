I have been going back and forth with a friend for about a year now about whether the sheer amount of cross-cultural disagreement about right and wrong should make us give up on the idea that moral claims can be objectively true, and I would like a number rather than another round of assertion. Neither of us has any academic background in this — I do database work, she is a hospital doctor — and we got into it through that familiar complaint that almost everyone psychologists actually run experiments on comes from Western, educated, industrialised, rich, democratic societies, together with the widely reported cross-country surveys in which people in different places answer the same sacrificial-dilemma scenarios in measurably different ways. Here is the proposition I want your probability on, stated as precisely as I can manage: that the amount of variation in moral judgement across cultures that we would actually find if we sampled the world properly is strong evidence against moral claims being objectively true, where by objectively true I mean true or false independently of what anyone happens to believe, and such that anyone who knew all the non-moral facts and reasoned well would converge on the same verdict. I am at about 0.6 on this, she is at about 0.15, and I want your number — but I mostly want the decomposition that produces it, and I do not want to be told that it depends on what you mean by objective. Use my definition, or say which definition you would rather use and why, and then give me the number on that one.

The terms I would like broken out, roughly as probabilities, are these: first, how likely it is that there is substantial real variation at all, over and above the sampling artefact — and in particular how much of it is merely different applications of shared principles to different local facts rather than genuine divergence in the principles themselves; second, conditional on there being real variation, how much of it would survive matching people up on what they believe about the non-moral facts and on their material circumstances; third, whether objectivism as I have defined it actually predicts convergence among finite, historically situated, socially influenced human beings at all, or only among idealised inquirers, since my friend keeps saying that a truth being objective comes with no guarantee that anyone will ever find it; fourth, whether whatever variation is left over is of a kind that objectivism cannot accommodate. On that last one, here is a distinction I invented to try to locate the argument: call a difference input variance if it disappears once you equalise what people believe about the non-moral facts; output variance if it survives that equalisation and people will defend it with reasons when asked; and drift if it survives but nobody defends it — people say, in effect, that this is just how we happen to do things here, and they shift when shown a coherent alternative. I want to know whether drift is really evidence against objectivity or merely noise that an objectivist is entitled to expect, the way someone who thinks nutrition is an objective matter is untroubled by the fact that most people historically ate badly. Finally, I want a term for whether the question is well posed in the first place — whether objectivity is doing real work here, or whether the whole framing quietly dissolves into something else. Add or replace terms as you see fit.

Two more things. Suppose a large pre-registered replication across fifty-odd societies, using scenarios well beyond the standard sacrificial dilemmas — how to allocate a scarce drug, what you owe a stranger who saved your child, whether a bad intention makes an otherwise identical act worse — reported that about two-thirds of the cross-cultural spread vanished once respondents were matched on what they believed the consequences actually were in their own setting, that a real residue survived that matching, and that roughly half of the surviving residue collapsed when respondents were asked to justify their answer to someone from another society who had answered differently. If we actually had that result, would your headline number go up or down, and by how much? And then: show me how your terms combine — multiply them out if that is the right operation, and tell me if it is not — and tell me which single term the final number is most sensitive to, which term you are least confident about, what observation would move that term, and which of the two of us, me at 0.6 and her at 0.15, is most likely to be the one with the wrong number on it. Please also tell me honestly whether this question can be settled at all, and by what — a study, a conceptual distinction, a formal result, nothing — or whether it is one of those questions that simply hangs however good the evidence gets. And please skip any reading list or citations: I have no access to the literature and would not know what to do with them if I did.
