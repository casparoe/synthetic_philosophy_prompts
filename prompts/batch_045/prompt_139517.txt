I should say at the outset that I have no academic background in philosophy or in agricultural science. I work as a management accountant, and for about two years I have been reading about animal ethics and wild animal suffering in my spare time. Below are the notes for an essay I would like to write and try to place in a small magazine. I would be very grateful for comments on them.

The working title is "Double Entry," and the question it tries to ask is narrow. I am not asking whether it is permissible to eat animals. Taking it as given that I will continue to eat some, I am asking: does my choice between beef and chicken make any difference to wild animals, and if so, how would I go about working out which way it cuts? The answer one meets most often is that beef is clearly worse, because of feed conversion — the usual quoted figures, which I am not vouching for, are a few kilos of feed per kilo of chicken and several times that for beef — so beef means more cropland, and cropland means field mice, voles, and insects killed by ploughing, harvesting, and pesticides. Chicken is therefore the efficient choice. My notes argue that this comparison is less settled than it looks, and that the reason it is unsettled is the interesting part.

**1. An allocation problem.** Chickens are largely fed on soy meal and grain. Soy meal is a co-product: the bean is crushed for oil as well as for meal, and both end up in the human food system. So when we charge the harms of a soy field to my chicken breast, we have to split those harms between the oil in the dressing and the meal in the feed. Split by mass, by market value, by protein content, by calories — each of these is defensible and each gives a different number. In my professional life this is the textbook problem of allocating joint costs, and the textbook conclusion is that such allocations are arbitrary and should not be used to guide a decision. If that is right here, then part of the gap between beef and chicken is not a fact about the world at all but a bookkeeping convention. The honest comparison would be at the level of the whole system rather than the single product — the diet rather than the item — and at that level the gap narrows, because the oil has to go somewhere whatever I eat.

**2. Stock and churn.** This is the part I think might be new, and the part I am least sure about. It seems to me that the debate counts the wrong quantities. People count hectares disturbed, or animals killed, and I want to say that both are downstream of a mistake. For a population living at the limit of what its habitat supports, a death is not a subtraction from the population. It opens a vacancy, and something fills it. On this picture, deaths determine births rather than population size; population size is set by the habitat. If that is right, then how many animals are alive on a field at a given moment is roughly fixed by the field, while how many die there in a year is not — it is set by how fast the population turns over. Two habitats can carry the same standing stock while one of them produces several times as many deaths per year, which means several times as many short lives, each ending in whatever way that habitat ends lives. So the right unit of account is not the hectare and not the head count but the annual death rate that a piece of land sustains: what I have been calling churn. And churn can be raised by things that look benign — irrigation, winter cover, hedgerows, a milder microclimate, anything that lifts the birth rate without lifting the ceiling.

**3. Where that leaves the comparison.** If churn is the right unit, then pasture and arable differ in kind and not merely in size, and I do not think the figures in circulation were collected with churn in mind. I genuinely do not know which way it goes. Grain may hold more small mammals per hectare through the summer and then expose them both to harvest machinery and to a hard winter; pasture may hold fewer and turn them over more slowly. My provisional conclusion is that the beef-or-chicken question, as ordinarily posed, may be underdetermined by the evidence people cite for it, and that the deeper reason is that we have all been keeping these books on a cash basis — recording deaths as they happen — when what we need is an accrual basis that charges a diet with the annual death rate it sustains.

**4. The suspense account.** Cropland carries insecticides and an enormous number of very small deaths whose moral weight I cannot settle. I keep these in a suspense account: I do not know enough to enter them, but I decline to write them off, and I want to say plainly why a ledger that cannot price its largest line item by volume may still be worth keeping.

What I would like from you:

- Is the stock/churn distinction coherent as I have stated it, or does it collapse under pressure? In particular, is the vacancy claim true often enough to bear the weight I am putting on it? If the distinction already has a name in this area, tell me what it would most likely be called, and say so if you are not confident.
- Is the joint-cost analogy fair? My worry is that allocation is arbitrary for internal decision-making but not for causal attribution: my demand for chicken may genuinely cause a specific number of hectares to be planted, in a way that no joint cost is caused by one product. Does that break the analogy, or is the causal chain too tangled for that to help?
- Which single claim in these notes is weakest, and if someone who works on this professionally would dismiss the whole thing in one sentence, what would that sentence be?
- Is the accounting framing a help to a general reader or does it read as a gimmick pasted onto an argument that does not need it?
- Is ending on underdetermination an honest destination or a way of avoiding having to say anything?

Please do not rewrite the notes for me, and please do not try to supply figures or sources — I have deliberately kept the numbers out until I know whether the structure holds. What I want is criticism of the structure.
