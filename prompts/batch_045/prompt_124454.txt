So I've been arguing with a friend about the GameStop squeeze and I think the no-trade theorem is relevant to it, but I can't tell whether it's relevant in the deep way I half-believe or whether it just amounts to shrugging and saying "people trade for non-informational reasons." I know the pop version of the story (dumb money vs. hedge funds, greater fools, reddit vs. citadel) and I don't need that. What I want is a careful mapping, and then an honest account of where the theorem goes quiet.

The facts as I have them: roughly 140% of GME's public float had been sold short; the stock went from about $17 at the start of January 2021 to over $500 pre-market on Jan 28; Ryan Cohen joined the board around then, which is actual news about the company; on Jan 28 Robinhood and some other brokers halted buying (not selling) of GME, saying they couldn't post enough collateral at the clearinghouses; Melvin Capital took enormous losses and wound down not long after; and then on Feb 24 the stock doubled in about ninety minutes with, as far as anyone could tell, no news at all. Correct me if any of that's off.

What I want:

First, do the mapping properly. In the Milgrom–Stokey style setup — common prior, common knowledge of rationality and of the information structure, no noise traders, and I think traders are price takers — who are the agents here, what is the state space, what would the theorem predict about the volume we saw, and which assumption does the most work in the failure? I suspect "price takers" is doing more work than people admit, but argue it.

Second, a distinction I want you to use, since it's what I keep circling: call it informational alpha, profit from knowing more about the terminal payoff of the asset, versus impact alpha, profit from knowing how your own and other people's orders will move the price before the terminal payoff arrives. My claim is that the squeeze is almost pure impact alpha, and that the no-trade theorem is definately a theorem about the first kind only, so it's not just wrong about Jan 2021, it can't represent it. Is that right, or is there a version of the result that bites on impact alpha too?

Third: a lot of buyers seemed to be buying something other than a financial claim — they wanted a particular hedge fund to lose money and were willing to pay for that, which is why so many of them refused to sell at the top. If the two sides are literally trading different goods, what does the theorem even say? Is there an allocation it's supposed to be ruling out?

Fourth, the Robinhood halt: when buying is switched off but selling isn't, that's involuntary no-trade imposed on one side. Does that do anything to the epistemic story about markets aggregating dispersed information, or is it just a plumbing failure the theory was never about?

Last: the Feb 24 doubling with no news. Which of the theorem's exits does a defender take there — noise traders, heterogenous priors, or something else — and what observation would ever tell those apart? I don't want "noise traders" as a conversation-stopper. And then the honest part: is Jan 2021 a confirmation of the theorem, an anomaly, or a case it was never in the business of describing?
