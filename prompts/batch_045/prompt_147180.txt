I'm a fourth-year PhD student in epistemology, and I've just turned a dissertation chapter into a job-market paper defending a diagnosis of the bootstrapping problem that I want stress-tested before I send it out. Here is the view. Take the paradigm case: an agent forms the belief that some source S — a measuring instrument, a memory, a perceptual faculty — is reliable, and her only evidence for that belief consists of S's own outputs: a long run of consistent readings, say, or the source's own repeated reports that its reports are accurate. I agree with everyone that she does not thereby come to know, or to warrantedly believe, that S is reliable. But I also deny that anything about the circularity of the reasoning is doing the explanatory work. What is doing the work, I argue, is a modal failure: S's outputs are by hypothesis just what they would be if S were systematically unreliable, so her reliability belief is insensitive and unsafe, and that — not the fact that the premise was supplied by the very source it concerns — is what disqualifies it. Two consequences I'm committed to. First, the bootstrapping problem is not, contrary to the usual framing, a special difficulty for externalism: it is a special case of a sensitivity failure, and wherever an agent's belief in a source's reliability is safe, an externalist should simply grant that it is knowledge, easy-knowledge worries notwithstanding. Second, and this is the part I take to be my real prediction: bootstrapping should be perfectly good reasoning when the source is what I call "self-auditing" — when the source's outputs would themselves differ if the source were unreliable, and differ in a way the agent can read off without appealing to any independent source. An instrument with an internal drift check that flags its own malfunction, or a memorial system that reliably returns a "no record" response for exactly the period during which it is degrading: in such a case, I claim, reading the flag and concluding that the source is reliable is fine reasoning, structurally circular though it is.

What I want from you is the strongest available case against this, ranked: not the worries a hostile referee might raise in passing, but the observations, arguments, and constructed cases that should actually move me, ordered from fatal to merely awkward, with an explicit note on which of them a defender of my view would have to concede as counting against her and which she would reasonably dismiss as presupposing an internalist framework she has already rejected. Along the way please address the following, since these are where I think the view is most exposed. Is the self-auditing prediction really an extension of the sensitivity diagnosis, or is it an extra premise I haven't earned — and can you construct a case in which a source is self-auditing in my sense and the bootstrapping reasoning still seems clearly illegitimate? I have a nagging worry about who audits the audit, and about sources whose self-reports are accurate only in precisely the conditions under which the source itself is accurate. Conversely, is there a bootstrapping case that plainly yields knowledge with no self-auditing anywhere in the picture, which would show the diagnosis is not even extensionally correct? Does the diagnosis survive the cases I want it to generalize to — an agent concluding that her memory is reliable by remembering many things, a research community certifying its own methods, a philosopher trusting his intuitions about what is intuitively plausible — or does it look convincing only because instruments happen to be the easy case? And is there some novel case in which my diagnosis and a genuinely structural, justification-theoretic explanation come apart, which would show that I am getting the right verdicts for the wrong reason?

Two things I should flag about my own situation. The commenter on the conference version of the paper said the view is nothing but reliabilism plus a safety condition, and so inherits every trouble the safety theory has — the problem of which method fixes the relevant nearby worlds, the problem of trivially unsafe true beliefs — and I want to know whether that is fatal, merely awkward, or something I should welcome and advertise as a reduction. Separately, my advisor's standing objection is that the view is now insulated: whenever a counterexample appears I can always say that the source wasn't really self-auditing, or that the belief wasn't really safe, and so the view predicts everything and risks nothing. Tell me honestly whether that charge is fair, whether the insulation is worse than what rival positions in this debate enjoy, and what I should be willing to give up in order to make the view genuinely exposed. For what it's worth about my own psychology: I am much more likely to be moved by a well-constructed case — ideally one where sensitivity and self-auditing pull in opposite directions — than by any general argument about the nature of justification or about what knowledge is for, so weight your answer accordingly and say plainly if you think the view is in better shape than I fear.
