I had a long argument last night with a friend who works in formal epistemology. Her position, as I came to understand it, is that a body of credences is rational just when it maximizes expected accuracy, where accuracy is measured by a proper scoring rule — the log score, or the Brier (squared-error) score — and that the justification is entirely one-shot: no money, no betting, no sequence of repeated trials, nothing asymptotic.

I am a professional sports bettor, and the only normative framework I think in fluently is Kelly: edge and odds, the fraction of bankroll that maximizes expected log wealth, fractional Kelly when I distrust my own model, the growth rate, drawdown, ruin, and the standing conviction that maximizing expected wealth rather than expected log wealth is a sucker's game.

So: translate her position into that vocabulary as faithfully as you can. What does "maximize expected accuracy" become if I treat a credence function as a stake allocation across possible outcomes, a scoring rule as a payoff schedule, and Bayesian updating as rebalancing? Give me the mapping item by item — in particular, is the log score literally my growth rate or only structurally similar to it, and what does the Brier score become on my side: a different payoff scheme, a different kind of bet, or something my framework has no room for?

Then give me the residue report. Tell me what in her framework cannot be said in mine — accuracy as worth having for its own sake rather than as growth, credences about propositions that will never be resolved twice so that there is no sequence for growth to act on, a justification that is synchronic where mine is asymptotic — and what in mine cannot be said in hers — that I have a principled reason to pick log rather than Brier, namely multiplicativity, whereas she has to choose a scoring rule, and that I cannot state "accuracy" at all without a bankroll to grow or to bust. For each mismatch, say whether it exposes a hole in the framework that cannot express the claim, or whether the claim was never as clear as it sounded.

Finally, settle one thing from the end of the evening. She proposed scoring credences by the geometric mean of their accuracy across possible outcomes rather than the arithmetic mean, so that being badly wrong in one corner costs you even when that corner is improbable. I said this just collapses into maximizing expected log accuracy — log scoring with extra steps. Which of us is right, and if it does collapse, does it leave her with anything genuine, given that on my side the geometric mean is justified by what happens over many rounds while she has only one?
