I am in a third-year seminar on moral responsibility — a few weeks ago we worked through the Strawson essay in which he contrasts the stance of someone caught up in ordinary interpersonal relations with the stance of someone who treats a person as an object to be managed, handled, trained or avoided — and I keep running into a pattern in my own dealings with people that I cannot match to anything we have read. I would like help identifying it, and if it has no established name, help defining it properly.

Two instances.

(i) A friend has cancelled on me five times in about a year. Each time the excuse was genuine: a sick child, a car that would not start, a deadline moved at a day's notice. I accept each of them. I do not think he wronged me on any one of those five occasions, and if he cancelled tomorrow with an equally good excuse I would have to say the same again. But I notice that I have stopped making plans with him. I arrange things and quietly keep a fallback; sometimes I do not mention the plan at all. I do not think I am angry with him. What I have done feels more like what I do with a rail service that is often late: I have not concluded anything about the driver, I have simply adjusted. Except that it does seem to be about him. If he asked me why I have gone quiet, the honest answer would mention him, not the world.

(ii) I run a small tutorial group. One student has missed four of six deadlines, each time for a reason I accepted and told her I accepted — a death in the family, then a flu that lasted weeks. I have no complaint I can state. But I now read her essays with a lowered expectation before I have read them, and in class I catch myself treating what she says as background noise in a way I do not with the others. Nothing she did is something I think she should have done differently, and yet my orientation toward her has shifted, and it has shifted in a way that I think she could reasonably object to even though I could not name a single thing for which she owes me an answer.

What I want a name for is this: a shift in how one is oriented toward a person, which is licensed by the run of their behaviour taken together but by no item in it, where each item is fully excused, and where the shift is not blame — it is a slackening of the expectations that go with seeing someone as the kind of thing that answers for what it does, without any accompanying judgment that they have failed to answer for anything.

Four things I have considered, none of which seems to me to be quite it. I would rather be told that one of them is right than be given a new name.

First, the objective attitude itself. As I understood Strawson, the cases he reaches for are ones where the person's grip on things is compromised — a child, someone deranged, someone under abnormal strain — or where the reactive attitudes are suspended for a particular episode and can be resumed afterwards. My friend's capacities are entirely intact, and what does the work in my case is not any episode but the shape of the sequence. So either this is a species of that attitude with extra conditions I cannot specify, or it is something else that merely resembles it.

Second, that it is blame in disguise. Perhaps the run of cancellations licenses an inference about character — that he is unreliable, that she does not take the work seriously — and unreliability is a failing, so my "adjustment" is really a verdict about the person wearing the costume of a scheduling decision. If that is right, there is nothing new here and my description is a rationalisation. I would like this pressed as hard as it can be.

Third, that nothing has happened to my stance at all. What has changed is reliance or prediction: I have revised a forecast on statistical grounds, which is no more a moral matter than revising a forecast about the weather. But this leaves unexplained why the change feels like one the person could reasonably resent, and why I would be embarrassed to describe it to her.

Fourth, a structural parallel I half remember from a different part of the syllabus: a case in which several people each turn a dial by a tiny amount, no individual turn makes any difference to the victim, and together they produce agony. There, nothing is true of any individual act and something is true of the collection. Is my case the same shape, or does the parallel break down precisely because what is in question here is a stance toward the individual rather than a harm divided among many?

The possibility I take most seriously is that there is nothing to name, because what I am describing is a defect in me: an unwillingness to bear the cost of going on taking someone at their word when the evidence concerns their circumstances rather than their will, a kind of laziness about the effort of maintaining an attitude. If that is right I would like to know what the defect is called.

If you do think there is something here, I am not after a label. What I need is the carving: what exactly this is, what it is not, and where its edges lie. In particular I would like a definition to handle three boundary cases. (a) Suppose my friend's cancellations are the predictable upshot of a standing policy he could have changed — he says yes to everything and then cannot deliver. Then there is something to blame him for after all, and the phenomenon seems to collapse into ordinary blame: so apparently it requires that the pattern itself not be attributable to him. How sharp is that requirement? (b) Two cancellations instead of five — at what point, if any, does the shift stop being warranted and become the defect? (c) Can the shift be warranted and yet be something I owe it to the person not to act on or disclose, the way some true judgments about a person are things one should keep to oneself?

Finally, since much of this is a request about what does and does not exist in the literature: please say how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the claim you would be least surprised to be wrong about.
