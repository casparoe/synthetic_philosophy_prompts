I sit on a charter-reform commission for a city of about 400,000 people. Our remit is the franchise for municipal elections only, but we have been asked to say how our recommendations bear on the national rules, which we cannot ourselves change. Before I draft my section I want my own position audited, because I have a feeling it is tidier in my head than it is on paper. Here it is, as six commitments plus one methodological preference.

(C1) Turnout should be legally required, but not by fine. I favour what I have been calling forfeiture-based compulsion: a registered elector who does not vote, and has not filed a standing declaration of conscientious abstention, is struck from the electoral roll and must re-register before voting again. The declaration is available on the day at the polling place, needs no justification, and carries no penalty, so that nobody is ever made to record a preference they do not hold.

(C2) The voting age should be sixteen for municipal elections; I would support sixteen nationally if that were open to us.

(C3) Long-term residents who are not citizens — three or more years of lawful residence — should have the municipal franchise.

(C4) But not the national franchise.

(C5) The ground for (C3) is the all-affected principle: anyone durably subject to a government's coercion must have a say in who wields it. I pair this with a procedural presumption: the burden of proof always lies with whoever would exclude someone from the franchise.

(C6) I take seriously the objection that an electorate's judgement degrades as less-engaged voters are added, and I think that cost is real and should be weighed. But I reject competence tests, plural voting, and every other device that requires the state to grade voters' competence, so I treat the cost as something to be mitigated rather than priced into the rule that defines the franchise.

(M) Methodological preference: I am unmoved by defences of compulsory voting that treat it as civic ritual or as an instrument of nation-building. I want the case for (C1) to rest on interests — above all on the way voluntary turnout skews whose interests get registered — and not on symbolism.

Please audit this package rather than defend it. I want to know where it binds on itself. Concretely:

1. Rank the pairwise tensions by severity instead of listing them all as equally bad. For each, state exactly which commitment has to bend and what bending it costs me downstream.
2. Identify any pair that merely looks tense and dissolves on inspection, and say what makes it dissolve. I would rather you discriminate than flag everything.
3. Tell me whether any of the case verdicts — (C2), (C3), (C4) — is a verdict my own stated principle in (C5) cannot actually deliver, and if so which verdict has to move.
4. Give me the single revision that removes the most tension at the least cost, and say plainly what I lose by making it.

Two constraints. Keep this an audit of my commitments: I do not need a tour of what is usually argued about compulsory voting, voting age, or resident voting, and I do not want anything settled by appeal to how actual jurisdictions have drawn their lines. Where a tension depends on an empirical claim, say what the claim is and how much of my set rides on it.

Present the ranked tensions as a table with columns for the pair, severity, which commitment gives way, and the downstream cost. Close with one final line in exactly this form:

Verdict: <the label of the single commitment I should give up or revise if I want the set to hang together>
