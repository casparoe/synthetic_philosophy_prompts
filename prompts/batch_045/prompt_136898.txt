So I'm a novelist — four books out, slogging through a fifth — and I've realised I have really firm gut feelings about when "good enough" is genuinely good enough and when stopping early is just laziness in a nicer coat. What I don't have is the rule. And I think two of my feelings flatly contradict each other, which is why I'm asking.

Here's my list, roughly in the order I thought of it.

- Stopping work on a chapter after eight passes, when a ninth would probly make it three percent better: fine. Zero guilt.
- Signing with the first agent who offers, without waiting on the two bigger agencies that still have the full: fine.
- Abandoning a draft at 30k words because the voice never showed up: fine, obviously.
- Abandoning a draft at 30k words because a *better idea* showed up: not fine. I think this is a character flaw. But I can't say why — same 30k words in the bin, same better book on the other side.
- Stopping my research on 1970s Belfast the moment I can write a convincing scene, and inventing the rest: fine.
- Not reading the one source that would tell me whether my plot's central mechanism is even possible: not fine. This one I'd be ashamed of.
- Picking a title by taking the first one that doesn't make me wince, instead of generating forty and testing them: fine.
- Sending the manuscript to my editor knowing two scenes still don't work: not fine. Even though I'd defend the eight-passes thing above.
- A week spent choosing a typeface for the paperback: fine, no regrets. A week spent comparing health insurance plans: I felt like an idiot afterwards. They came out the same.

My own half-baked rule: it's fine to stop looking when you couldn't tell the difference between what you've got and what more searching would turn up; it's not fine to stop when a better option is already visible and cheap to take. I think that covers the archive one and the manuscript one. I don't think it covers the two 30k drafts, which look identical under it, and I'm not sure it covers the insurance — I couldn't tell those plans apart either, and the week was still wasted.

There's one more thing I've noticed and named, badly: what I call a sinking bar. The level drops, but not because I learned anything — because it's hour nine and I'm tired. I've always treated that as the moment to quit and I've never quite trusted it. Maybe it's irrational, or maybe fatigue is genuine information: it tells you your discrimination has degraded, so there's nothing left to choose between.

So: what's the principle? Is this satisficing with an aspiration level, and if so what sets the level and when should it move? Is my couldn't-tell-the-difference clause the same thought as the take-the-best stuff, or a different one? Does the principle come from the economics side — search costs, optimal stopping, take the first one past some threshold — or from the cognitive side, where the mind is limited and the environment does half the work? Because those feel like they'd disagree about the sinking bar.

Give me the principle clause by clause, with the case that forces each clause. Then tell me what it says about the decision I'm actually in right now: take the two-book deal on the thing I've already written, or spend three years on the harder novel I think I might be able to write. And tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle can't swallow, and why it's right and I'm wrong.
