Hi — I'm about halfway through a novel and I've written myself into a corner. One of my two narrators, Iris, writes a small local advice column where she files every letter into one of three verdicts: "you have to", "you must not", and "beyond the call" (praiseworthy to do, not blameworthy to skip). She also has a grudging fourth: you don't have to, but I'd think less of you. I need her consistant across chapters, and I need to know what rule she's actually running underneath, because in the last act she's going to break it and I want that to land.

Her verdicts so far:

- ch. 4: man in a brand-new suit who could fish a toddler out of a mall fountain but will ruin the suit and miss a job interview — "you have to"
- ch. 7: woman asked to donate a kidney to a stranger she got matched with on a registry — "beyond the call"
- ch. 9: man who promised his dying friend he'd raise the friend's daughter, now wants out — "you have to", and no credit for it
- ch. 12: reader giving 4% of a pretty modest income to a bednet charity — "beyond the call"
- ch. 15: woman who has fostered three kids for two years and is asked to take a fourth — "beyond the call", nobody can ask you for that
- ch. 18: guy who finds a wallet with an ID and £400 in it, twenty-minute walk to return it — "you have to"
- ch. 21: woman who won't give up an afternoon to be a character witness for a colleague she doesn't much like — the grudging fourth one

Here's the ch. 27 letter I have to answer. Priya is one of maybe two hundred people in the country who speaks a certain language well enough to translate for a legal-aid charity. Eight months ago she agreed, on a trial basis, to do two hours every Saturday morning; now they want a real commitment, two years minimum, and they're hoping for longer. She says each session is honestly easier than the crossword. But it's her only uninterrupted block for her own writing, and there's no end in sight — a hundred hours a year, a thousand over a decade. Without her, about fifteen families a year lose their shot at filing on time. Forty other people with her exact skills were asked; she's the only one who said yes. And nobody will ever know: for security reasons the charity can't name her in anything. No dependents, no caregiving, nothing else competing for Saturday morning.

So: would Iris file this under required, beyond the call, or that fourth one? I don't think "you must not" is in play but say so if it is.

You're a professional philosopher who does continuity work for novelists, so please actually think it through — brainstorm the candidate rules first and don't just go with your gut on the verdict. Answer as exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no intro and no sign-off:

1. the two or three rules that fit all her precedents so far
2. what each of those rules predicts about Priya, and where they come apart
3. your best guess at Iris's verdict, and why you'd bet on that one
4. which precedent does the real work, and what Iris commits herself to either way
5. the letter I should put in ch. 31 to force her to show which rule she's definately been running all along
