I host a tiny philosophy-of-mind podcast (a few hundred listeners, mostly grad students) and I'm recording thrusday with a guest who defends a pretty specific line: that the access-versus-phenomenal-consciousness distinction is a false divide, because once information ignites into a global workspace and is sustained-broadcast to the rest of the system, that broadcasting just *is* what makes the state phenomenally conscious — not a mechanism that merely makes an already-conscious state available for report, reasoning and action. She's good, and she's heard every version of "you've changed the subject," so I need questions that make her earn the view instead of restate it. Eight to ten total, for a 45-minute episode, each short enough to read out loud, with the follow-up written next to it rather than as a separate item.

I also want to spring one thing on her that I doubt she's prepped. Call it blind broadcast: a stimulus that does everything the workspace story says a conscious stimulus does — ignition, the late global response, long-range fronto-parietal synchrony, the subject can name it, draw inferences from it, act on it, and it turns up in later memory — but when you ask, the subject insists there was nothing it was like; they say they knew it was there the way you know a fact, without ever seeming to see anything. I know that's a philosopher's fantasy case, that's the point. Give me at least two questions built around it, and cover both ways she can jump: if she says the state is conscious and the subject is simply misreporting, press her on what introspection would have to be for that dismissal to be legitimate rather than just convenient; if she says it isn't conscious, press her on whether anything recognizably global-workspace-ish is left.

For the rest, roughly:

1. Make her state outright whether broadcast causes phenomenality, constitutes it, or only distributes it — my bet is she says constitutes, so follow up by asking what the workspace adds, in terms of what-it's-like-ness and not in terms of function, that a purely feed-forward cascade through the same areas wouldn't add.
2. The overflow argument: phenomenal richness seems to outrun what you can access, on the partial-report style evidence. Her most likely reply is that the surplus is a high-capacity pre-conscious buffer, a precursor to experience rather than an experience. Follow up by asking what the difference is supposed to be between a precursor of an experience and an experience you can't get at, and who bears the burden of proof.
3. The posterior-versus-frontoparietal fight: if content-specific sensory activity keeps going while the frontoparietal broadcast signature is gone, which one is the conscious one on her account? If she answers that the prefrontal end is about report and self-monitoring and not about phenomenality, follow up by asking whether taking report and self-monitoring out leaves a theory of consciousness or just a theory of attention and memory.
4. No-report paradigms: if the field spent decades calibrating the neural signatures of consciousness on data contaminated by reporting, does that undermine the evidence base for the identity claim or only for the localization? And what result would actually make her give up the claim that broadcast and phenomenality come to the same thing?
5. Internally generated broadcast — dreams, mind-wandering, imagery with no stimulus — and the mirror case where ignition looks absent but people report experience on waking. Ask which direction of mismatch is more damaging to her view and make her say why.
6. The zombie-workspace question: if we built a system with a real global architecture, one capacity-limited buffer, competition for entry, widespread availability, self-monitoring of its own contents, would the theory have to count it as phenomenally conscious? If she says it would need the right sort of physical implementation, follow up on what happened to the functional claim.
7. Close with a clean falsification question: name one finding she'd treat as refuting her, and get her to say whether she'd actually abandon the view or reinterpret the finding.

No warm-up questions, no how-did-you-get-into-this stuff, and please don't write anything that lets her answer with "that's an empirical question for future research" and move on — block that exit where you can.
