I sit on the board of a small wildlife trust. I am a retired accountant, I have never studied philosophy, and I have been reading and arguing about this on my own for a couple of months because I have to put something in front of the other trustees soon.

We manage a coastal reserve of roughly 900 hectares where a resident deer herd of about 400 animals is eating out the scrub that ground-nesting birds depend on. Our ecological adviser has given us eight options, A1 to A8. A1 is to do nothing. A2 removes twenty animals a year, A3 forty, and so on up to A8's hundred and forty; from A5 onward each step also bolts on one more management intervention, so A5 adds winter feeding, A6 a perimeter fence to stop animals drifting in from neighbouring land, A7 veterinary screening of the remaining herd, and A8 a fenced paddock with a hand-reared breeding group. Each step therefore buys a real but small improvement in the habitat at the cost of a real but small increase in how managed the whole business is. The far end, A8, is in effect a deer park.

Two things have happened. At our September meeting we compared the options only in neighbouring pairs, and each time we preferred the more intensive one by a small margin: the habitat gain seemed to outweigh the extra killing and the extra fuss. The minutes record seven such preferences. Then someone put A1 and A8 side by side, and the room agreed that A8 is worse than doing nothing, because the herd would no longer be a wild herd on a reserve and none of us would have voted for it. But if better than chains — A2 better than A1, A3 better than A2, and so on — then we have already committed ourselves to A8, and rejecting it now looks like either bad faith or incoherence.

I do not think we should adopt A8, and I do not think we should conclude that the seven votes were worthless. What I cannot do is say where the chaining stops without it sounding like special pleading: at whichever step I stop, someone can ask why that one and not the next, given that I have already agreed the next is an improvement.

Three further thoughts, which may or may not help. First, our seven votes were all comparisons between neighbours, and I suspect two different claims are hiding inside them: that we cannot see a real difference between neighbours, and that they are equally good. I would like those kept apart rather than run together. Second, I am bothered by what happens over time rather than in one meeting: if a future board approves one more step each year, each time paying for it and each time justified by the step before, then in eight years we would be running a fenced, hand-fed deer park that nobody would have voted for in 2024, and no single meeting would ever have decided that. That seems wrong even though every individual decision seemed all right. Third, I have been playing with a rule to stop this, and I would rather you tested it than agreed with it: treat options as tied when the difference between them falls inside some band, and always assess a new proposal against a fixed description of the reserve as it stood when we last adopted a management plan, never against the option we happen to be running now. My guess is that the second half is what does the real work, and that it may also mean a slow genuine deterioration forces a decision on its own once the gap from that fixed description grows past the band. But I cannot tell whether that is right, who is supposed to set the width of the band, or what stops them setting it wherever they like.

What I need is the substance of a written opinion I can circulate to the board before our 30 November meeting. It should recommend one of A1 to A8, or say plainly that we should defer the vote and think again. It should say whether we should adopt a rule like the one above, in what form, and for how long. It should give the strongest version of the argument on the other side and answer it rather than talk past it: our longest-serving trustee holds that if each step really is an improvement then refusing to take it is simply irrational, and that if we refuse we are admitting our seven votes were never real judgments. And it should say what would show that the recommended rule had failed, so that we can review it honestly at the end of three years.

Please do not name philosophers, schools of thought or books, and do not cite anything; I have read a little, but references would only slow the discussion and I would rather argue from the case. Give me the substance of the opinion as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences long, with no preamble and no closing paragraph, because I will paste them straight into the minutes.
