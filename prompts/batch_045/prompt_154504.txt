My friend and I argued until well past midnight about a decision she has to make, and I want to turn that argument into something better than an argument. She has been offered two things: a two-year research fellowship abroad that would let her finish a collaborative project she has been running for years, and a permanent, teaching-heavy post in the city where her mother lives, whose health is visibly declining. Both of us agreed that neither option is better than the other. Both of us refused to say they are equally good. At that point we discovered we meant different things by the word "comparable," spent an hour talking past each other, and went home annoyed. We have now agreed to write a joint statement instead of having the same argument again, and I would like you to draft it.

The two positions to stage are these. Call the first the parity view: in a hard choice of this kind the options stand in a genuine fourth positive comparative relation — not better, not worse, not equally good, but comparable nonetheless, where comparability is not a matter of degree; a small improvement to either option makes it better than the other; and the choice is rationally resolvable, though not dictated by the comparative facts, because the agent's own will or commitment supplies a reason that the values at stake do not. Call the second the incomparabilist view: in a hard choice of this kind there is no positive comparative relation at all — the values in play are not measurable against any single covering value, so the failure is in comparability itself and not merely in the usual three-way classification; the agent must simply pick, no comparative verdict is available to guide the picking, and nothing about the options is made true by the agent's having picked. Please define both views carefully at the top of the document so that neither side is a caricature: the incomparabilist is not committed to "anything goes," and the parity theorist is not committed to a hidden cardinal scale.

I want four things.

First, a numbered section of claims both sides can sign. I expect at least these to appear in it, if you agree they are genuine common ground: that the standard trichotomy fails in some cases; that some hard choices are not merely epistemic, so that more information would not always settle them; that in such cases either option may be permissibly chosen; that both relations, if they obtain, leave the clearly worse options excluded, so neither view licenses indifference or arbitrariness about the whole menu; that comparability is not the same as measurability on a scale; and that the disagreement is not a disagreement about how agonizing the choice feels. Add anything else you think both could honestly sign, and drop any item on my list that turns out to be slanted once you try to write it.

Second, a table of the residual disagreements. For each one, give the issue in neutral terms, then the formulation the parity theorist would accept for her own position and the formulation the incomparabilist would accept for his own, and mark whether you think the disagreement is substantive or merely verbal. I strongly suspect at least one row is verbal and turns on two different things "comparable" can mean — roughly, standing in some positive comparative relation versus there being a fact of the matter that settles which option is to be chosen — but I want you to work out which row that is and why, rather than announce it in advance.

Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them. These are the ones I care most about, and I want each paired with a concrete test both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down before the answer is discussed.

- Take her actual case and vary it by a small amount of money attached to the fellowship abroad, small enough that neither of us thinks it is worth rearranging her life for on its own. Does that settle the choice? The parity theorist presumably says yes, with the improved option now better; the incomparabilist has to say something more careful, and I want to know whether he says the improved option has become comparable and better, or that it remains incomparable and the money is merely a tie-breaker with no comparative content. Then run the mirror variant: worsen the home option by the same small amount rather than improving the other, and have both sides predict separately. If either side's account predicts an asymmetry between improvement and deterioration, say so explicitly and say how confident that side is.

- Now repeat the choice. Suppose she faces a structurally identical choice two hundred times over — two hundred small, separable occasions on which she must allocate an afternoon either to the collaborative project or to her mother, with no option to do both and no diminishing-returns complications. Consider three packages: all project, all mother, and a hundred of each. Which packages are comparable, and is any of them better than another? Both sides should record predictions about the all-project versus all-mother pair and about the mixed package versus each pure package. I think this one hurts somebody, and I want to see whom.

- Consider what a well-informed third party — a colleague who knows her situation thoroughly — can permissibly say to her. Can he say "neither is better, but there is a verdict here: choose either, and your choosing it is itself part of the reason"? Or can he only say "no verdict is available; you must pick, and your picking settles nothing comparative"? Have both sides predict, and use this row to test the verbal-dispute hypothesis from the table above.

- Consider what happens after she chooses. Does her having chosen the post at home, and having begun to live as someone who did that, generate a reason she did not have before — a reason not to revisit the decision, or to make the same call next time? The parity theorist has resources here that the incomparabilist may not; both should say what they predict and how much of their view rests on the answer.

If there is a further crux I have not thought of, add it, but do not pad the list.

Fourth, a short closing section on what each side would count as evidence or argument against its own view, and whether there is anything both agree would actually move the debate rather than just refine it.

One constraint: this has to be usable without a library. Do not cite, attribute, or gesture at the literature; I have read enough of it and it did not settle last night's argument. Argue from the cases and from what each side is committed to. Where a formulation would only work if you smuggled in a technical term from a particular author, rewrite it in plain terms instead. And please keep both parties equally competent throughout: if one of them gets the better of an exchange, it should be because the argument went that way, not because you made the other one slow.
