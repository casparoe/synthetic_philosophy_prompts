For the reading group I run with physics and philosophy grad students I want a decision tree that starts from one commitment — a system's evolution is deterministic and it has exponentially diverging nearby trajectories — and lands on a named position about what chaos actually does to predictability. Six or seven branch questions, around eight leaves, each leaf with one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

The thing I most want the tree to do is separate three senses of prediction that everyone in this literature runs together: predicting which state the system will be in at time t, predicting statistical or ensemble-level stuff (time averages, the invariant measure, which lobe of the attractor it ends up in), and predicting enough to act on for some concrete purpose like station-keeping or deciding whether to cancel the picnic. My sense is that several positions only look like positions because nobody says which of the three they're about, so force that choice early in the tree.

Related: please make the tree distinguish three different sources of unpredictability that I think get conflated — sensitivity to initial conditions, sensitivity to the model itself being slightly wrong (no shadowing, structural instability), and computational cost or outright uncomputability. If two leaves differ only in which of these is doing the work, say so rather than letting them pretend to be rivals.

Leaves I want represented: straight Laplacian determinism with chaos as a mere epistemic annoyance; the standard textbook view that determinism survives and prediction dies; the mixing or statistical view on which chaos buys you a new kind of predictability that smooth systems don't have; a view on which the continuum idealisation is the culprit so chaotic systems aren't really deterministic in the first place; something where uncomputability means not even a demon given exact inputs could produce the prediction; and an instrumentalist leaf where determinism never amounted to more than a predictive claim and chaos therefore dissolves it. Name them or mark them unnamed. I've also been using the label model-relative determinism for the view that determinism only has to hold inside the model's own state space and owes nothing to anything physically measurable — give it a leaf if it earns one, or tell me it collapses into something else.

There's one combination I can't find anyone holding and I want your read on whether it's incoherent or just unpopular: determinism true, trajectory prediction swamped, action-guiding prediction also swamped, and statistical prediction unavailable too because the system isn't ergodic or the measure isn't computable — and yet determinism is still supposed to matter. If your tree has that cell and it's empty, diagnose why: bad view, unattractive view, or merely unoccupied?

Phrase every node as a question about a concrete case (double pendulum, weather, three-body, a chaotic circuit), not as do you accept indeterminism, and I dont want any node that someone could only answer if they already knew which leaf it leads to.
