I run a short "cases in miniature" humor page for a newsletter on applied ethics, and I need a piece on everyday conventions: whether things like tipping, queueing, and waiting for the walk signal are merely coordination devices or genuine moral obligations, and whether a visitor is bound by the local versions.

Please write a short joke — setup and punchline, at most about 150 words — either as a brief exchange between a visitor and a local or as a three-sentence anecdote. Two requirements on where the humor comes from:

1. Build it around a convention you invent, not around a familiar tourist-abroad gag, a pun, or the mere fact that customs differ. If it helps, start from this one: in the town the visitor has just arrived in, crossing against the light at an empty intersection is the polite thing to do, because waiting at an empty crossing is read as announcing that you do not trust the drivers to stop; the visitor, anxious to behave well, waits devoutly at every crossing and is therefore the one giving offense. Adapt or replace this freely, but keep some concrete invented convention at the center.
2. The punchline must turn on one of the actual structural claims in this debate: that a convention can be arbitrary in its content yet non-optional in its force; that the non-complier free-rides on everyone else's compliance; that a visitor's non-compliance offloads interpretation costs onto locals who now have to work out what the visitor meant; or that "I was following the rules where I come from" answers a different question from whether one owes anything here.

Structure the response as follows:

- **The joke** (150 words or fewer): the piece itself, with the punchline clearly landing.
- **Commentary** (150–200 words): which of the four structural claims the punchline exploits and how; why the invented convention is a fair test of that claim rather than just an exotic-customs gag; and what the joke quietly concedes about the visitor's obligation.

Please write in American English throughout.
