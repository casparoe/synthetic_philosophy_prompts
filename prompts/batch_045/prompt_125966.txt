Doing a forty-minute slot at a crime-writing festival in Bristol in October — about thirty novelists, no philosophy between them, no slides, a flipchart if I can be bothered — on the wrong kind of reasons. It's billed as 'writing characters who get paid, blackmailed or nagged into feelings'.

The case I keep coming back to is one of my own: my detective is offered fifty grand by a tabloid to believe a suspect is guilty, and then, in a seperate scene, the same money to *admire* him. Which makes me wonder whether one example can carry the whole thing, or whether the admiration one quietly breaks the machinery, since admiration doesn't seem to have the sort of thing-it's-meant-to-get-right that belief has.

So don't explain it to me — plan the session. What has to be in place before 'wrong kind of reason' means anything at all to them, and in what order do the pieces have to land and why? They'll definately arrive thinking the fifty grand obviously *is* a reason, and that 'wrong kind' just means 'not a very good one'. Which example should carry it, and what does it have to have and what must it lack? The misconceptions I'm braced for: that the money is merely outweighed; that this is really only about belief, or only about evidence; that 'you have a reason to make yourself believe it' is just the same claim as 'you have a reason to believe it'; and that a bribe can't cause a belief anyway. Where in the sequence should I head off each one? Give me one question to end on that shows they've got it rather than just nodding along, and tell me what to flatly refuse to get into at this level — I assume the argument about what 'good' means that all this grew out of, but say if something else will swallow the hour.
