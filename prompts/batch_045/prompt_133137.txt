I'm a PhD student writing a dissertation chapter on population ethics, in which I defend a person-affecting restriction on beneficence: an agent's choice can be criticized on grounds of beneficence only if some individual is worse off under the chosen option than that very individual would have been under the available alternative. A commentator on an early draft sent me the argument below. As far as I can tell it is valid and its conclusion is one I cannot accept, but I cannot locate the mistake. I would be grateful for a diagnosis: which step should I reject, why did that step look sound, and what does each of the remaining exits cost me?

(1) An agent's choice can be criticized on grounds of beneficence only if some individual is worse off under the chosen option than that same individual would have been under the available alternative.

(2) Which individual a person is depends on that person's actual history: had the history been relevantly different, the later person would have been numerically distinct from the actual one.

(3) A person's psychology — dispositions, memories, attachments, values, cognitive style — is largely a product of upbringing and social environment. So, by (2), an upbringing sufficiently unlike the actual one yields not the same person differently shaped but a numerically different person.

(4) Consider a policy under which a state removes children from their families at age seven and raises them in institutions designed to produce obedient, intellectually narrow adults. The resulting adults have lives well worth living but markedly worse than the lives the children would have had under the counterfactual policy. Assume the institutional upbringing is sufficiently different that, by (3), the adults who grow up under it are numerically distinct from the adults who would otherwise have existed.

(5) Under that policy, no one is worse off than they would have been: the adults who exist would not have existed otherwise, and the adults who would otherwise have existed never exist. So by (1) the policy cannot be criticized on grounds of beneficence, no one raised under it has a complaint about having been raised there, and the state owes no one compensation for it. Any legal ban on such institutions must rest on grounds other than the good of the children.

(6) That is false. The policy is a paradigm of harming children, and its wrongness consists precisely in how it makes them fare.

(7) So at least one of (1) through (6) is false. But (2) is orthodoxy in the metaphysics of identity, (3) looks like an empirical claim that developmental psychology supports, (4) is a stipulation about the case, and (6) is not negotiable. Hence (1) must go, and with it the person-affecting restriction everywhere, including in the standard future-people cases where I had thought it was doing useful work.

My commentator drew out one further consequence that I find especially hard to stomach. Because the psychological divergence in (3) comes in degrees, the argument works better the more damaging the institution is: a mild deprivation leaves identity intact and is therefore criticizable, whereas a severe one carries the cohort past the threshold at which a distinct person begins to exist, and is therefore not. On this picture, the worse a childhood policy is, the less it can be criticized for the harm it does, and past some threshold of severity the criticism vanishes altogether.

I would much rather keep (1) than (3), since (1) is doing work elsewhere in my chapter and (3) is where the metaphysics seems to be doing all the damage. But I do not see how to deny (3) without denying (2), and I do not see how to deny (2) without losing the standard nonidentity cases I set out to explain. Note also that my commentator has a fallback I cannot answer: run the case on a cohort that does not yet exist, and the argument collapses back into the ordinary nonidentity problem, which I already grant. My worry is specifically about its reach into the case of children who already exist when the policy is applied.

Where should the weight fall, and what am I paying either way?
