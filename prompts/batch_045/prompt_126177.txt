I am in the fourth year of a philosophy PhD, and the chapter I am currently failing to write is about explanation in the social sciences — specifically about when it is legitimate to cite a social-level fact (a rate, a structure, a norm, an institution, a climate of opinion) as part of the explanation of what individuals do, as opposed to treating such facts as mere shorthand for a story about those individuals. My supervisor treats the question as closed in favour of some form of methodological individualism; a friend in the sociology department does network modelling and treats it as a pseudo-problem about levels of description. I do not want either of those answers. What I want is to stop flip-flopping: I notice that I permit some social-level explanations and gag at others, and I cannot say what the difference is. So please do not give me a literature survey, a reading list, or citations — I can find those myself. Give me the principle, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case we have not discussed, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot save.

Here are my verdicts, as concretely as I can state them. (1) A modeller explains a city's residential sorting by giving each household a mild individual-level threshold — move if fewer than about a third of your immediate neighbours are like you — and showing that the macro pattern follows. Verdict: a paradigm of good explanation; nothing social-level does any causal work beyond the individuals' thresholds. (2) An urban economist explains rising house prices by the fall in average household size. Verdict: fine, but I want to say this is not an instance of the thing I am worried about at all: it is aggregative bookkeeping, arithmetic over individual-level facts, and calling it a social-level cause would be a category mistake. (3) A public-health paper explains low rates of walking to work in one district by the street-network layout: the grid has no through routes to the centre, so walking is infeasible for nearly every origin–destination pair. Nobody's beliefs or desires differ between the districts; what differs is what the built arrangement makes available. I call these *affordance explanations*, and my verdict is that they are fine. (4) A Durkheim-style explanation of a suicide-rate differential between two religious groups in terms of the weaker social integration of one of them. Verdict: I want to allow it — I would hate to have to call a century of social research confused — and yet I cannot produce the mechanism: I cannot point to the individuals whose integration was weaker and whose despair was thereby greater. (5) Hearts are there because pumping blood circulates oxygen. Verdict: no problem at all. (6) The punishment system has the shape it has because it maintains social solidarity. Verdict: I gag, even though it is the same sentence form as (5) — and note that I would stop gagging if a selection story were supplied, e.g. that polities whose punishment systems lost cohesion were conquered or replaced. (7) The spirit of the age turned against the regime; neoliberalism produced the rise in precarious work; the market decided. Verdict: I gag; these feel like redescription with a capital letter. (8) Investment in an industry falls because a published index crosses a threshold, when everyone knows that everyone else watches that index and everyone knows the others know it too. I call these *tracked variables* — social-level quantities that individuals respond to precisely because they are public and mutually known to be watched. Verdict: I allow this one, and I cannot say why it differs from (7). (9) The case I go back and forth on: a currency collapses because enough people expect it to collapse, where each person's expectation is largely a response to what they expect others to expect, and the object everyone is tracking is the aggregate outcome of everyone's own behaviour. I have argued myself into both corners on this and cannot settle.

My tentative principle is a test of in-principle replaceability: a social-level explanation is in good standing if and only if there could in principle be an individual-level mechanism story — beliefs, desires, opportunities, and interactions — that would do the same explanatory work, where the story need not be known, filled in, or even statable in our current vocabulary; anything not so replaceable is either bookkeeping or mysticism. I suspect this is broken in both directions: it looks too permissive, since a determined theorist can always sketch a mechanism for the spirit of the age, and too restrictive, since neither the street layout in (3) nor the tracked index in (8) seems replaceable by a beliefs-and-desires story without loss of explanatory power. Please test it against my cases, then repair it or replace it. I would like: the resulting account stated clause by clause, each clause paired with the case that motivates it, including any clause you need only to save one verdict; its verdict on (9); its verdicts on at least two cases I have not listed, of which the first should be a network-topology explanation (a behaviour spreads because of the degree distribution of the contact network, or because a few weak ties bridge otherwise separate clusters — a structural property that no individual has beliefs about) and the second is yours to choose; and an honest statement of which of my verdicts the account cannot accommodate, with the trade-off made explicit — I would rather overturn one verdict than accept an ad hoc clause per case, but I want to see what the overturning costs. Finally, please flag anywhere I have misdescribed what one of these explanations is actually doing, i.e. anywhere two cases I have treated as the same shape are not. A thousand to fifteen hundred words is fine; precision matters more to me than coverage.
