I am teaching a graduate seminar on self-knowledge, and for the session on Williamson's anti-luminosity argument and its bearing on the KK principle I would prefer to open by reading a poem aloud rather than by handing out a sheet of premises. Please write that poem.

The argument as we have been reconstructing it: call a condition *luminous* when, in every case in which it obtains, the subject is in a position to know that it obtains. Take a substantive condition — one that holds at one end of a series of cases and fails at the other, where neighbouring cases differ too little for the subject to discriminate between them. Grant luminosity, and grant that being in a position to know something puts one in a position to know that one is in a position to know it; then the second-order claim marches step by step along the series and arrives at the far end, where the condition does not obtain. But being in a position to know is factive. Contradiction.

The case I want is not the canonical one about feeling cold but one the seminar chose because it is the strongest candidate anyone could offer for a luminous condition: a woman's love for a man, draining away. Nothing dramatic happens to it; it goes by increments too small to notice.

What I would like:

**Form.** A ballad in six quatrains, ballad metre, with the second and fourth lines rhymed, built by the folk device of incremental repetition: each stanza repeats the shape of the one before it and advances by the smallest possible change. If strict metre would force padding, let the sense win.

**The step.** The opening line of each stanza should be the previous stanza's opening line altered by one word or one small phrase, so that the poem itself walks the series from a state anyone would call love to a state anyone would call its absence. Every step must feel too small to be the one that mattered. A second line in each stanza may carry the clock forward — one more evening, the light one shade lower.

**The refrain.** The third line of each stanza is a refrain that climbs by one iteration: in the first stanza the bare first-personal claim ("I love him still"); in the second, I know that I love him still; in the third, two iterations; in the fourth, three. At the fifth stanza — this is the volta — the climb must falter: the line will not carry another iteration, or breaks off mid-climb, or comes out in a form that cannot be taken a step further. The poem must not say why it falters.

**The close.** In the sixth stanza the bare claim from the first stanza returns in the same words, in a situation the poem has made unmistakably one in which she does not love him. The final two lines must be lines that a reader who abandons luminosity, a reader who abandons the KK premise, and a reader who denies that the steps were ever really indiscriminable could each hear as confirming their own diagnosis. Do not name the culprit.

**Two-sided.** I also want it audible, somewhere in the last two stanzas, that she cannot certify the opposite either: at the far end she is no more in a position to know that she does not love him than she was to know that she does. The seminar's name for what the argument costs us is *bilateral luminosity* — a condition is bilaterally luminous when, in every case, whether it obtains or not, one is in a position to know which — and running the argument on the complement condition takes both sides. So the closing should be over-emphatic rather than merely uncertain: not a failure to certify a love that is quietly still there, but a failure on both sides at once.

**Accuracy.** Two things an epistemologist should not wince at. The speaker is not the victim of a global scepticism: in the early stanzas she genuinely knows, and the poem should concede that first-order knowledge is plentiful; what goes is the second-order guarantee. And the condition must stay substantive — the final state is one in which the love is genuinely gone, not one in which it is merely doubted.

**Framing.** The words "luminous", "KK", "sorites", "margin for error" and "factive" must not appear in the verse, and there should be no footnotes in it. It should work as a poem for someone who has never heard the argument, and as a map of the argument for someone who has. After the poem, add no more than five lines of prose keying the incremental step, the refrain, the volta and the close to the four premises, for the handout.
