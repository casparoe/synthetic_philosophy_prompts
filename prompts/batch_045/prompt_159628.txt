Bit of an odd request. I'm a retired experimental physical chemist — 35 years of corrosion and electrochemistry — and about eight months ago I started going along to a philosophy discussion group at the lifelong-learning place in town. We've just hit dispositions, and I keep running into the simple formula: to be fragile is just to be such that you'd break if you were struck, to be soluble is just to be such that you'd dissolve if you were put in water, and so on. Every time I hear it my hands go up, because half my working life was spent noticing that putting the thing in the water changed the thing. A thermometer in a small warm bath warms or cools it a bit; a cheap meter across a high-impedance cell reads low because the meter is now part of the circuit. The test isn't a window, it's an intervention.

I gather the jargon for two of the standard counterexamples is "fink" and "mask" — I may have that backwards. One is where the stimulating event itself removes the disposition before it can show up, like a live wire wired to a gadget that cuts the current the instant anything touches it. The other is where the disposition is really there and the stimulus really happens, but something else eats the result — genuinely fragile glass, genuinely dropped, lands on foam. I want the group to feel why each of those wrecks the simple formula, and I especially want them to feel that they wreck it in different ways, needing different repairs, not just "ah, conditionals are hard."

But there's a third thing I don't think fits either of those, and I'd like them to run into it rather than be told about it. Take a bit of platinum gauze wired to a piece of magnesium and drop the pair into dilute acid: the platinum fizzes beautifully. Only it isn't doing anything — the hydrogen is coming off the magnesium, it's just nucleating on the platinum because that's the cathodic surface. So here's an object that passes the test outright: stimulate it, get the manifestation. And it isn't disposed to do that in its own right at all. The difference from the foam-padding case is that nothing interferes with the result; the difference from the cut-out case is that nothing destroyed the disposition. The basis is simply somewhere else.

So: could you design a runnable game or structured activity for my group — 16 people, mostly retired scientists and engineers, three or four humanities types, one ex-lawyer who will argue about the rules if given half a chance. 90 minutes, one room with a flipchart, index cards, a kettle, no homework and no lab. I'd be running it and I'm the least qualified person there, so the rules have to carry it: I don't want to stand up and explain anything at the start, and I don't want them hearing "fink" or "mask" until afterwards — I'd rather they invent their own names for what went wrong. Some of them are operationalists to the bone and will say if you can't measure it it isn't real; I want the activity to give that something to chew on rather than just contradict it.

Ideally the force comes from playing, not from the debrief. Something with hidden setups and a limited menu of things the experimenters are allowed to do, where a positive result doesn't settle the question and where "can't tell from here" is sometimes the correct verdict but can't be the safe answer every time — otherwise they'll just say it every round. No real money, and please nothing that feels like a children's party game: this lot are in their seventies and extremely competitive, so losers-put-the-kettle-on is about the right level of stakes. Materials, rules, scoring, a timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments onto the three case types and onto why the simple formula fails for each.

What I'd like them to walk out seeing: that the formula breaks not because of far-fetched metaphysics but because an assay is an intervention; that the three failure modes are genuinely different and need different repairs; and that the borrowed-basis case raises the question whether a disposition has to live inside the thing that has it — which, come to think of it, is what half of our lab discipline of controls, replicates and isolating the system was always for. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about which bit of the idea the game can't deliver and I'll have to say out loud myself — my guess is that it can't make anyone feel why dispositions would be real properties of objects rather than just convenient summaries of what objects do, but you tell me.
