I've been reading up on the Sally Clark case again and I can't stop thinking about it in terms of Lewis's triviality results, and I want someone to do that properly rather than just tut about the prosecutor's fallacy.

The facts as I remember them (correct me if I've got anything skewed, but don't go looking anything up): Sally Clark was convicted at Chester Crown Court in November 1999 of murdering her two baby sons, Christopher and Harry, who died a bit over a year apart in 1996 and 1998. The defence said cot death. The prosecution called the paediatrician Roy Meadow, who said the chance of two cot deaths in a family like theirs — affluent, non-smoking, mum over 26 — was about 1 in 73 million, which he got by squaring a roughly 1-in-8,500 figure for a single such death. He also had that catchphrase about one cot death being a tragedy, two being suspicious and three being murder. The Royal Statistical Society put out a statement saying there was no statistical basis for the figure. Conviction upheld on the first appeal in 2000; quashed at the second in 2003, after it came out that the Home Office pathologist had sat on microbiology results showing Harry had a staph infection in his spinal fluid.

What I want is a two-way case analysis, and I want the mapping done carefully, not gestured at.

First, say what conditional quantity the jury actually needed, and what quantity Meadow actually gave them, in the same notation — name the hypothesis, name the evidence, name the conditioning event on each side. Second, and this is the bit people blur: keep apart (a) transposing the conditional, i.e. offering P(E|H) where the jury needs P(H|E), and (b) treating a conditional probability P(B|A) as if it were the probability of some conditional proposition A→B — the thing Lewis showed can't be done if P is to stay a decent probability function and the conditional is to have truth conditions at all. Tell me which of those two the conviction really turned on, and why. My hunch is that everyone reaches for (b) when they hear "triviality result" but the case is mostly (a) plus an independence assumption, and if that's right I want to be told that Lewis is something of a red herring here — but argue it, don't just agree with me.

Third, something I've not seen anyone say: there's a mismatch of suppositions that isn't quite either fallacy. Meadow conditioned on a demographic profile; the juror who wants to use his number has to suppose a hypothesis — that these deaths were natural. Those are different suppositions, and the gap between them does independent damage. Does Lewis's set-up, where conditional probability is a two-place function over propositions with the conditioning proposition fixed, have anything at all to say about which supposition is the right one? I suspect it's simply silent, and I'd like that stated as silence rather than papered over.

Fourth: the standard rap against the suppositional / no-proposition side (Adams, Edgington and co) is that it can't handle embeddings — conditionals inside negation, inside disjunction, inside "the probability that...". Legal reasoning is stuffed with embeddings: jury directions, burdens of proof, the structure of "if you are sure of X then you must convict unless...". So check whether this case actually triggers any of them, or whether the whole sorry business runs on flat, unembedded conditional probabilities. If it's the latter, that's interesting in itself, and I want to know what it means for using courtroom cases as evidence about the semantics of conditionals.

Then the reverse direction. Does the episode confirm anything about the triviality results, is it an anomaly for them, or is it just a case the theory was never in the business of covering? Be straight with me if it's the third. And I want a closing note on what a defence barrister with a working knowledge of all this should actually have objected to at trial — which objection was load-bearing, and whether it would have made any difference to twelve people who'd just been told "one in seventy-three million".

I'd rather have 800 words that does the mapping honestly than 2000 that surveys the literature. I know the literature; I don't need a map of it.
