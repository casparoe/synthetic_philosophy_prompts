I cover science for a newspaper, and a great deal of what I do amounts to deciding how much confidence to project when I describe a model as having explained something rather than merely predicted it. Before I start leaning on a position in print, I'd like an audit of the working view I've arrived at. Roughly, I hold the following. (1) I reject Friedman's as-if move as any kind of general license: that a model predicts well does nothing on its own to show that its false assumptions aren't carrying the result. (2) What licenses a false assumption, I take it, is that it be Galilean in spirit — a distortion with a distinguishable route back, so that a less idealized model can be recovered and the damage checked. (3) I also want to keep a class I call hereditary idealizations, where a model explains by dropping causal detail and showing that the outcome is inherited under every way of filling that detail back in. (4) I treat success in a domain the model was not fitted to as evidence that its distortions were licensed. (5) I don't think a critic is entitled to object that a model leaves something out unless they can say, at least in outline, how it would be put back in. (6) For models that will be used to set policy — climate, epidemics, that sort of case — I want the burden reversed, so that the modeler has to show the distortion is harmless before the model is acted on. Does this package hang together? Which of these commitments genuinely conflict, which only look as though they do, and for each real conflict what is the least costly thing for me to give up and what would giving it up cost me? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equal, and answer in continuous prose with no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, the way you would in an email to a colleague.
