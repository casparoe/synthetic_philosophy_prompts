Hey — I'm an ML researcher, mostly vision these days (convnets, ViTs, some recurrent / predictive-coding-flavoured stuff, lots of pretrain-then-finetune). No philosophy training beyond what I absorb from podcasts, but I keep bumping into what I think is a philosophy argument and I want it said in my own language.

The argument, as best I can tell: does what you know, believe, want or expect change what you actually *see*, or does it only change what you say, decide or feel after seeing? One side apparently holds that perception is sealed off — it takes its input, does its thing, hands over a result, and your beliefs never get inside to tweak the result itself. The other side says of course they do: people see ambiguous figures differently depending on what they were told beforehand, an expert sees structure in a chess position or an X-ray that I definately don't, someone expecting danger sees it. And there seems to be a whole sub-fight about what would even count as an example that settles it. A philosopher friend gave me the name for it — something like "cognitive penetrability" — so hopefully that's enough to pin down which fight I mean.

Here's the vocabulary I actually think in: feedforward vs. recurrent/feedback computation; weights as compressed statistics of a training distribution; priors, and specifically the difference between a prior baked into the weights during training and a prior injected at inference time (a prompt token, a context image, a conditioning vector); test-time compute and in-context learning; layers as stages; linear probes, ablations, lesioning feedback connections; loss functions and training distributions; overfitting and dataset bias; ground truth and calibration. Assume I'm fluent in all of that and impatient with anything else.

What I want, in order:

First, restate both sides — and the disagreement about what would count as evidence — as faithfully as you can in that vocabulary. Don't just gesture at analogies.

Then the part I actually care about, the residue report: what translated cleanly, what only went through because you fudged it, and what has no expression in my vocabulary at all. Where a translation would only work by smuggling in an assumption my framework makes (that everything is inference, say, or that a "representation" is just an activation vector), flag that instead of quietly doing it.

Some places I suspect the trouble is:

1. We can localize. If I want to know whether an effect is "perceptual," I look at which layers' activations moved, probe early vs. late layers, ablate the feedback pathway and see what survives. Is the philosophers' perception-vs-judgment distinction that same kind of claim — a claim about where in the system the change happens — or is it a claim about something my methods can't touch at all (what it's like for the subject, whether they could have resisted it, whether the change answers to reasons)? If it's the latter, say so plainly, and tell me whether that makes their dispute empirical-but-hard or not empirical at all.

2. My lab has this running distinction between priors that are in the weights (I trained on data where red things were usually edible; now my net responds to red) and priors that show up at test time (a prompt, a context image). We joke that the first is "having been trained" and the second is "having been told." My hunch is this lines up with a distinction the sealed-off side keeps making: something like, learning can reshape what you see, but that's not the interesting case — only something like a belief acting *at the time* counts. Is there a real distinction there or am I confusing myself? And is the timing line principled or does it look ad hoc once you say it out loud? Does weights-vs-context map onto it, or have I just redescribed something else?

3. Here's my own case — tell me if it's even a case of the thing they're arguing about. I take a net pretrained on ordinary photos and fine-tune two copies on the same images, except that in copy A's training set every image containing a faint gray blob is mislabelled "dog." At test time I show both copies a clean image with a faint gray blob and no dog in it. Copy A's mid-level feature maps go dog-ish and it sometimes outputs "dog." Copy B doesn't. Neither gets any context at test time; the difference is entirely in the weights. Is that "penetration" on anybody's definition, or is it just having learned a bad prior, which everyone already grants happens? And either way, what does the answer tell me about the fight — does it show the interesting claim was never really about priors at all?

4. Last thing, the bit I can't get a grip on. In my setup the whole question "did the belief change the perception or merely the decision?" feels like it might collapse, because there's no principled place to draw the line between the perceptual part of the network and the decision part, and the training objective certainly doesn't care — gradient flows wherever it helps. If that's right, does it transfer? Does it suggest these people are fighting over a distinction that their own best models of the mind don't support, or does it just show that a trained net isn't the right object to think with here? I genuinely can't tell whether I've stumbled onto something or reinvented a confusion, and I'd rather be told it's the second one.
