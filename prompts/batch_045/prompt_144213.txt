I'm running a fifty-minute discussion section whose only job is to get first-year students to stop picturing consciousness as something that happens at a place and a time in the brain. They arrive having read a popular-science chapter on split-brain patients and Libet-style experiments, so they come in with a half-formed image of a central observer being fed by the senses. Don't explain the multiple drafts model to me — plan how I'm going to get them there.

The case I want them able to handle by the end is one I've invented. A subject is shown a red dot in one position, then two hundred milliseconds later a green dot in another, and she reports a single dot that moved and turned green about halfway along. Three things come apart: a speeded button press registering "now it's green" lands only after the second dot has been shown; a read-out of her visual cortex turns up no content corresponding to green-at-the-midpoint until after the second dot; and she insists, unprompted and calmly, that she saw the dot turn green at the midpoint. My students will want to say either that she is simply wrong about her own experience, or that she is right and the neural data are beside the point. I want them to end up seeing why the question "when did that content become conscious?" has no determinate answer until you specify what is doing the probing, and why that is neither a discovery that she was mistaken nor a reason to say consciousness is an illusion.

Give me the order in which the pieces have to arrive for that to be possible: what distinction has to be in place before the case is even discussable, and the one simpler example that should carry the first half of the session and what it must have and lack. Then give me the three misconceptions I should expect, in the order they'll arrive — that the view amounts to denying that consciousness exists, that it claims several streams of consciousness run side by side, and that it shows our choices are made before we know about them — and the exact point in the sequence at which each should be defused. Finally: one closing question that separates a student who has understood from one who has merely learned to repeat that there is no finish line, and one thing I should openly refuse to settle in fifty minutes.
