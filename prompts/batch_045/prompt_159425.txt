I'm a novelist and I'm running one session of a weekend workshop for other novelists — 11 people, 90 minutes, Saturday morning, one long table, all adults, mostly literary fiction with a couple of crime writters. The novel I'm supposedly procrastinating on is set half in a village in Hesse and half in a town in upstate New York, and the session is on writing characters whose morality isn't the reader's. Specifically this thing I keep bumping into: a German character who treats the rule as the thing that makes shared life possible, versus an American character for whom the individual conscience is the final court and "I was following the rule" is just what cowards say. I don't want anyone to leave having decided who has the better of it. I want them to leave unable to decide, and with a scene fragment where both people are right and neither one is the villain.

So design me something they *do*, not something I explain at them.

The constraints that matter to me as a novelist:

1. Nobody is allowed to say the words rule, duty, conscience, should, ought, right, wrong, moral, guilt, or responsibility during the exercise. They have to argue from concrete facts about the town — who gets wet, whose cellar, what time the warden gets back, what the mechanism does if you force it. The moral grammar has to show up in what people reach for and what they notice, never in what they declare. That's the whole craft point; I can't have them produce a character who announces his ethics.

2. Everyone gets a private card with something small and particular they stand to lose either way — not money, not a life: the baker's cellar, the deputy's promotion, the newcomer's invitation to the next thing, the clerk's sense of being someone who can be trusted with a key. Plus one private fact the others don't have.

3. There's a clock. Real time maps onto fictional time, and if they talk too long the window closes and something mild but real happens. I want them to feel the cost of deliberation itself, because one of these grammars buys time and the other spends it.

The scenario I keep circling is a river town and a sluice. The gates can be forced open now by anyone with a bar, or left for the warden, who is unreachable and forty minutes away. The rule saying only the warden opens them is genuinely obsolete — written for a mechanism that was replaced years ago — so the consequentialist case for forcing them is clean. I want it clean. If the rule turns out to be secretly wise, the whole thing collapses into "actually, follow the rules."

Here's the bit I can't get to work, and it's the reason I'm asking. The second half has to make the opposite move. My half-formed idea: when you break a rule on conscience you aren't only doing the deed, you're implicitly claiming authority to judge the rule, and that claim is addressed to your neighbours — they now have to ratify it or repudiate it. So the American move isn't solitary heroism at all, it's a bid, and it puts everyone else in the position of either endorsing you or being judged by you. And the German refusal isn't submission, it's declining to make that bid: it keeps the shared rule whole so that nobody has to stand under anyone else's private verdict. Which would mean one grammar protects people from drowning and the other protects people from each other, and neither protection can be cashed in without losing the other.

I need the second half to be something they do that lands that in the gut rather than the head. My instinct was a mirror version of the same town where the rule is gone — the bylaw lapses, the warden resigns, no office covers it — and everyone now has to decide alone. But I think that plays as liberation and just hands the session to the American side. Do something better if you've got it.

What I'd like back:

- both halves of the scenario, with the roles and what's on the cards
- the actual mechanics: what people do, what they write, what the clock does, how a decision gets made and by whom, and how the second half turns the first one inside out
- what each writer should have in hand at the end — ideally a scene fragment, 300 words max, plus two sentences on what their character noticed rather than decided
- a debrief that maps specific moments onto the parts of the idea, including where someone will say one of the words I banned
- an honest list of what will go wrong. My bets: the crime writers try to solve it like a puzzle, and somebody stages a walkout for the drama
- and what the exercise fundamentally can't do, that I'll have to say out loud afterwards

Last thing: don't resolve it. If your design has a correct answer, its the wrong design.
