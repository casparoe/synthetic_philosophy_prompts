Bit of background before the actual question. I'm 69, retired limnologist — spent thirty-odd years pulling sediment cores out of lakes, so when someone says "the payoff is two hundred years out" my instinct is that that's a perfectly ordinary timescale, not science fiction. Since retiring I've been going to an evening class on ethics and public policy, and I got into an argument at a department reunion with an old colleague who now does cost-benefit work for a water utility. He was defends utility discounting on the grounds that a benefit delivered in 2250 is just worth less, full stop, even after you've corrected for inflation and for the risk that nobody's around to recieve it. I've ended up at the opposite view: that pure time preference — marking a benefit down *merely* because it lands later — is morally indefensible, and that the only discounting that survives scrutiny is discounting for the chance the benefit never materialises (no people, no lake, no receptor) plus whatever you want to say about opportunity cost of capital, which my class notes suggest is a lot more contested than my colleague treats it. I also have my own pet argument, which I'm suspicious of precisely because it comes from my old field: physics doesn't pick out the present as metaphysically special, and we don't think spatial distance discounts a person's welfare, so why should temporal distance?

So: please take the role of a philosopher who works seriously on intergenerational ethics and decision theory, think it through properly — do a bit of brainstorming before you write, and don't just hand back the standard discounted-utility formula and a summary of the two famous economists everyone already knows disagree. What I want is a map of my view's exposure. What would count against "the pure rate of time preference should be zero (or near enough)"? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Include the one I half-remember from a lecture and can't reconstruct: something to the effect that in a standard growth model, a zero pure rate plus a growing economy implies an optimal savings rate that's absurd — most of current output, or something like that. If that's roughly right, does it sink me, or is the absurdity coming from assumptions about growth and about how consumption converts to welfare rather than from the zero rate? Also please tell me whether my block-universe symmetry argument is actually a good argument or a physicist's category error — I can imagine a defender of pure time preference saying temporal position matters because of causation and agency (I can affect the future and not the past, I act *from* here), not because of metaphysics, and I don't know how strong that reply is. And tell me whether my view has any risk at all, or whether I've quietly defined it into unfalsifiability by shoving every awkward case into the "that's just uncertainty, not time preference" box — and if I have, whether that's fatal or fine.

One more thing I'd like you to engage rather than dodge. Suppose a large, well-run cross-cultural study found that when you ask people to compare a benefit now against the same benefit in, say, 150 years, and you carefully strip out the reading "it probably won't happen / I won't be there," the residual preference for earlier comes out at about zero and if anything slightly negative. Is that evidence for me, against me, or neither — given that my claim is normative and not a prediction about what people want? I keep going back and forth on whether empirical results like that are even admissible. (Related puzzle I can't settle: a 400-year-old college or a cathedral fabric fund is an agent whose practical "now" spans centuries, and we'd think it was behaving badly if it spent everything this decade. Does that kind of case support my side, my colleague's, or is it just a different question in a trenchcoat?) Finally, since I'm new to all this and can't tell confidence from convention: close with a short note on how confident you are in the main lines of what you've said, and single out the one claim you're least sure about — and say whether what you're unsure about is a factual matter about the literature or a judgement call.
