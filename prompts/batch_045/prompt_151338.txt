At a reading group I sit in on, two members got stuck on Dennett's real-patterns criterion — the idea that the difference between a pattern that is really in the data and one we merely project onto it is a matter of compression: a pattern earns its keep if describing the data as the pattern plus a list of exceptions is cheaper than describing it item by item, and earns nothing otherwise. Their disagreement was about what gets charged for.

Ines opened with a case she called the Almanac. A station has logged a million bits; every short, principled scheme anyone can find gets essentially no compression on it. But an eccentric archivist published that very bit string in 1903 as a procedural poem, and the book is in every library. So relative to what is already on the shelf, the data compress to a one-line call number with zero exceptions, and by the criterion the pattern is as real as they come. Her conclusion: the test as stated is wrong, because a pure noise string can be turned into a real pattern simply by being published in advance under another description.

Tobias replied that this just shows the pointer is not free: charge the codebook to the description, as any minimum-description-length or Kolmogorov-style accounting does, and the Almanac totals more than the raw million bits, so there is no pattern. Ines came back that charging everything is too strict: the orbits of the planets count as a real pattern only because we are not charging geometry, arithmetic, or the rest of the physical background they presuppose, and a rule that charges all presuppositions will find compression nowhere, since the shared background dwarfs any data set. So the criterion needs partial charging — and, she said, no partial-charging rule is principled, because the line between shared background and data-specific material moves depending on who is doing the describing. Pattern-hood then becomes relative to a community's library, which is close to the eye-of-the-beholder view compression was supposed to replace. Tobias answered that this relativization is benign and indeed the point: Dennett's realism about patterns is mild and perspectival; the three stances are three licensed budgets — the physical stance may draw on physics, the design stance on the assumption of good design, the intentional stance on rationality and folk psychology — so disputes about which stance applies are disputes about which budget pays, and there is no further fact. Ines's last move was that on this reading the stances are not on a par in the way the view needs: the physical stance's budget contains the others, so it can never lose, and saying that the intentional stance is warranted here collapses into a remark about our bookkeeping rather than about the system. She added that the design stance is the odd one out anyway, since the Almanac has a perfectly good design story behind it that yields no prediction at all.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each of the four moves say which side is right about what, and how decisively. Then say what both of them are missing, if anything. Finally, state how confident you are in each of your verdicts and single out the one point you are least sure of.
