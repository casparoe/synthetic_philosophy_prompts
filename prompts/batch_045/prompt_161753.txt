I moderate a fairly large online community that is hosted on a platform run by a small company. The company recently published a public commitment that it will never sell or share member data, and the reaction in our community was almost uniformly "talk is cheap." A week later the company tried to answer that reaction: it placed a sum of money in escrow with a trustee, to be forfeited to a digital-rights charity if the company is ever found to have sold user data, with the amount set at roughly a tenth of what people estimate a full member database is worth on the market. That satisfied almost no one either, and the argument that followed had three moves in it that I couldn't untangle. One person said the escrow changes nothing because the amount is small relative to the profit from cheating, so the promise is still cheap in the only sense that matters. Another said that a signal doesn't have to be large to be informative, only more expensive for the kind of company that intends to cheat than for the kind that doesn't. A third said the escrow is beside the point in a different way: unlike a burnt-money gesture, it costs the company nothing at all unless it breaks its word, so it isn't the kind of cost that makes signals reliable in the first place. All three were using the same four-word proverb and clearly meaning different things by it.

What I'd like from you is a disambiguation rather than a verdict. Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses that "talk is cheap" could be expressing — for example, that words have negligible production costs; that words are revocable and unenforceable; that credibility requires a cost; that credibility requires a cost that is differentially higher for the sort of speaker who lacks the quality being claimed; that credibility requires cost measured net of whatever the speaker gets back from signaling; that credibility requires a cost the speaker cannot recoup by later cashing in on the deception; that cost is doing the work of enforcement, so a promise backed by a forfeitable bond is really a different mechanism from a costly signal; or the much stronger reading that a costless assertion carries no evidential weight at all. For each reading, say whether it is a near-platitude, whether it is substantive and defensible, and whether it is simply false, and then say explicitly which readings would actually support each of the three moves in our argument, and which would undermine them. I'm especially interested in whether the distinction that seems to me to be doing the real work — cost that is incurred up front and wasted versus cost that is contingent on being caught — is one that the "cheapness" framework handles well, or whether it shows that two quite different mechanisms are being run together under one slogan.

A few things I'd like you to address along the way, since they are where I keep getting stuck: whether a signal's absolute size can ever matter, or only its size relative to what the dishonest type would gain; what happens when the cost of a signal is partly or wholly reimbursed by the audience it is meant to persuade, as when a certification pays for itself through a price premium, or when a rich company finds an expensive gesture affordable in a way a poor honest one does not; and whether costless messages can nevertheless carry information when the speaker's and the listener's interests partly overlap — I have a vague memory of something like this from game theory but I can't reconstruct it, so please flag it as a point of uncertainty rather than as settled if that's what it is. I'd rather you reason the cases out from scratch than point me at literature I can't check without a library. Finally, for each reading on your menu, tell me how confident you are in the verdict you've attached to it, and single out the one item where you think you are most likely to be wrong or where you think reasonable people would disagree with you, and say why.
