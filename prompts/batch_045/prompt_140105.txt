Hey — I mod a pretty large online community (forum plus a Discord, ~40k members, rationalist/EA-ish core with a big general-interest tail), and the mod team is six volunteers doing this in our spare time. Two or three times a month someone does something and a chunk of the community demands we sanction them: ban them, delete or lock their posts, pull their invite to an in-person meetup, put a visible note on their profile, or publicly confirm or deny an accusation. Right now each of these turns into a 300-comment thread and we decide by vibes, so we've definately been inconsistent and everyone can tell.

What I want is a decision procedure, not more framings. Take whatever the philosophy of punishment actually has to say — retributive vs. deterrence vs. communicative/expressive vs. restorative accounts, proportionality, the line between punishing someone and merely declining to associate with them or protecting the group, whether the punisher needs standing and on whose behalf they're acting, whether reasons and a chance to respond are owed, whether a sanction can be undone — and turn it into something six tired volunteers can actually run in an evening on a real case, in about an hour of discussion, and write up afterwards as a short public note. Constraints: no lawyer, no investigator, usually only what the parties tell us; majority of the six signs off; we post the anonymized reasoning publicly because transparency is the whole reason anyone trusts us.

A few things I keep half-remembering from blogs that I think have to be built in, and please tell me if I've got them wrong:

- The ratchet: a sanction that's formally temporary is functionally permanent, because search engines, archives and screenshots don't decay. Whatever we do, its real severity is set by an audience of strangers we didn't choose, not by the community we were talking to. So "a two-week ban" and "a permanent mark on this person's search results" may be the same action and we should have to say which one we're doing.
- Related: the severity we intend and the severity that gets delivered come apart, and we don't control the translation. One member's mild rebuke is another audience's career-ender. I want a step where we say who the addressee of the sanction is supposed to be, and what happens if we can't keep it to them.
- There is no sanction budget. Nobody is tracking or able to track the total amount of sanction a person is receiving across venues, so "proportionate to the act" is not the same as "proportionate to what this person will actually experience." Sometimes the right move is to sanction less than the act deserves; sometimes doing nothing is the pile-on.
- The abstraction test: rewrite the offense description with no proper names, no ideological markers and no in-group/out-group markers, re-run the procedure on the rewrite, then re-run it with a randomly chosen member in the role. If the verdicts differ, something other than the act is doing the work.

The procedure needs: the questions in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where it stops with a no; and where it hands the decision to a person because it's a values question rather than a factual one (I think one of those is whether we're a private club we may exclude people from for any reason or none, versus something closer to infrastructure people depend on for professional and social goods — that one we should vote on once and then it changes everything downstream, so flag it).

Cases it has to handle without falling over:
1. A long-time, well-liked contributor who was repeatedly rude and dismissive to a newcomer, never broke a written rule, and the newcomer quietly left.
2. An allegation about something serious that happened off-platform, years ago, entirely legal, which we can't verify because the evidence is a private conversation and the complainant won't consent to sharing it — and the member's real name is on their account.
3. Someone another community already sanctioned hard for the same conduct, where people want us to either pile on or publicly affirm that sanction.

Then: run the procedure on all three, and make at least one of them come out somewhere the team will hate — we have to publicly decline to sanction someone most of us think is a jerk, or we have to sanction a popular contributor, or we have to say something that will read as taking a side. Tell us which one that is and how to defend it in the public note. Finally, be honest about what the procedure can't do: which kinds of case it will systematically misfire on, and which of the hard questions it just relocates rather than answers.
