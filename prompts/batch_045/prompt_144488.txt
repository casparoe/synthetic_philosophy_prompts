For the past eight months I have been the technical co-founder of a six-person company whose only product is an autonomous procurement agent: it negotiates prices with the software agents of other firms, and it is retrained every night on the transaction data produced by its own negotiations. Until recently I was one of three people writing code. Since our CTO left I have become the person who has to decide whether we ship the next version, and I find that I do not have the conceptual vocabulary to say what would have to be true of this system for any confidence in it to be earned. The reassurances I keep reaching for — held-out evaluation, a monitoring dashboard, a kill switch, a sandbox around the retraining pipeline — all seem to presuppose that the thing doing the modelling stands outside the thing being modelled, and I have become convinced that here it does not.

I have been reading about embedded agency: the situation of a reasoner that is itself among the objects in the world it reasons about, so that no clean Cartesian boundary separates agent from environment, and the machinery that leans on such a boundary — Bayesian updating over a policy-independent world, expected-utility maximisation over a fixed space of outcomes, offline scoring against a ground truth — loses its footing. I do not want a summary of that topic, and I do not want reading suggestions, citations or pointers to a literature; assume I have no web access and that I want your own reasoning. What I need is the implications, at the level of concreteness at which they turn into decisions I could actually make next month.

Two slogans my team has started using; please treat them as hypotheses to be tested, and tell me where each is confused or where it smuggles in exactly the boundary it is meant to give up. The first I call the subtraction fallacy: the habit of analysing the system by imagining our agent removed and asking how it would perform in the environment that remains, when the environment that remains after removal is not the environment the agent faced, because the other firms' agents adapt to ours and the price distribution we retrain on is partly our own doing. The second I call the twin condition: the demand that our world-model be able to say what the market would have looked like had a different version of our agent been deployed in our place, including the counterfactual in which the deployed version is the one we are now deciding about.

So: what are the most important implications of embedded agency for how I should design, evaluate and govern this system? In answering, please do the following.

Give me six to eight implications, ordered by how much they should change what I actually do. For each, state the claim in one sentence, name the piece of current practice it rules out or undermines, say what conforming to it would cost me in performance or engineering time, and give the cheapest test by which I could tell within one quarter whether that implication is really biting on my system rather than being a philosophers' worry.

Tell me which of our familiar evaluation and safety tools survive once the agent/environment boundary is given up, which of them are secretly relying on a boundary that is not there, and which can be repaired by replacing a policy-independent environment with something structural or counterfactual rather than merely statistical.

One of my engineers argues as follows: because our network has far fewer parameters than the data stream it is trained on, it cannot represent even a complete instantaneous state of a world that contains itself, let alone its own future policy; so any claim of the form that the agent's predictions are calibrated can only be a claim about some coarse abstraction, and the choice of abstraction is doing normative work that nobody on my team has examined. Is that argument sound, what exactly does it establish if it is, and what follows for how I ought to be choosing our state representation?

Where the honest answer is that there is no settled view, say so, and lay out the live positions in your own words rather than attributing them to anyone.

Please write in continuous prose under short headings, without bullet lists, around 1,200 to 1,600 words. Assume as your reader a competent engineer with no machine-learning background, and prefer implications that would surprise such a person over implications that are already familiar from statistics or control theory. Flag any implication that rests on a contestable assumption by naming that assumption in a clause. Finally: my system buys industrial fasteners. Please keep the discussion at the scale of the decisions in front of me, and spare me any general discourse about the future of artificial intelligence.
