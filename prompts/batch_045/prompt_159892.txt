hey — I need a hand with a funding application and I'm stuck on the framing.

It's a tiny three-month summer grant (about 7k, no renewal, no teaching buyout) and I need a two-page proposal for a project on free-floating rationales: the idea that there might be reasons that aren't anyone's reasons, reasons that obtain even though there's no agent they're a reason for. Every time I describe it I recieve the same two objections — that it's either just realism with a spooky name, or a footnote to the realism/antirealism dispute — and I think it's neither, but I can't yet say why.

Here's the half-formed bit. The standard fight is about whether reasons need minds at all. I want to change the question to a case where everyone agrees a reason *existed* and then ask what becomes of it once its owner is gone: promises, commissions, bequests, treaties, a dead composer's unfinished manuscript, a will that says "burn these letters," a request made by a collective that was never formally incorporated. Call them orphan reasons. Do they die with the person? Do they go vacant and get picked up by whoever happens to be able to discharge them? Do they accrue? Can they be inherited without consent? And does their force depend on how faithfully the heir is continuing the author's project — a fidelity-scaled inheritance, where the reason to finish the dead composer's symphony *in her style* weakens the more you turn it into your own piece? My suspicion is that the realist about free-floating rationales is quietly committed to the accrual picture, that this is far more costly than it looks (a world knee-deep in unowned, undischargeable, pro tanto reasons, none of them anybody's), and that this yields a new kind of argument against them — but also that the fidelity-scaled version may be a genuinely unstated third position rather than a compromise.

What I need from you is the proposal itself: the question put so that it has a determinate answer instead of being a gesture; why the two standard camps don't settle it even though both have a story about it; the method, month by month over three months, working alone; what a good result looks like; and an honest statement of what would count as failure — including the boring failure where I've just redescribed the realism dispute in new vocabulary, which is the one I'm most afraid of. Say straight out if three months can't hold it and which slice would fit.

Two constraints: no citations or bibliography, I'll add those myself; and don't give me "survey the literature" as the method, that's not what I'm asking for. Also give me a couple of sentences on why anyone who doesn't already care about the metaphysics of reasons should care about the answer — my committee will definitely ask.
