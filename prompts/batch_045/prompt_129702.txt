hey — I'm putting together a special issue on levels of selection and I've talked myself into trying something: instead of another round of reply-and-counter-reply, I want two of the contributors to actually produce one joint paper. Nadia is a gene's-eye / selection-among-replicators person who thinks group talk is bookkeeping that can always be cashed out in relatedness terms. Owen does multilevel selection and thinks the between-group term is sometimes where the actual causal story lives. They've been extremely polite to each other for years and have moved approximately zero inches, and I want the document they'd both have to put their names to.

You're a philosopher of biology who's refereed this literature forever and is bored of the same three moves on both sides. Think carefully, and before you draft anything, brainstorm two things: first, what the real shared ground is given the modern machinery (Price-style partitionings, relatedness coefficients, and the distinction between groups as contexts that sort individuals' fitness versus groups as things that themselves reproduce); second, what a case would have to look like to be genuinely diagnostic instead of one more example each side can retrofit into their own framework in an afternoon. Then write the statement.

The case I want them to work through is one I made up, so nobody has a canned answer lying around:

An engineered consortium of gut bacteria in a lab mouse colony. A strain carries a plasmid with a gene for a secreted enzyme that breaks down a fiber the mice otherwise can't digest. The enzyme leaks out of the cell, so every cell in the gut gets the sugar whether it carries the plasmid or not, while carriage costs the cell roughly 8% of its growth rate. The plasmid also transfers horizontally by conjugation between cells in the same gut, at a rate you can dial up or down with an inducer. Mice live in family cages, pups are colonized from a small number of founder cells whose number you control (1 to 1000), and each generation some adults get moved between cages, so migration is a knob too. There's also a mutant plasmid in circulation: it transfers about twice as fast but has the enzyme gene knocked out, so it's a defector at the cell level while being better at being a plasmid.

So you can independently vary the cost of carriage, the conjugation rate, the colonization bottleneck, and the migration rate, and you can watch enzyme output per cage and plasmid frequency within guts over a few hundred bacterial generations.

What I want, as the document:

Include what they agree about the decomposition of change in the population (that it's a partition, not a causal story in itself), about the baseline prediction for a costly public good under low relatedness, and about what would have to be true for cage-level differences in productivity to feed back into the global frequency of the plasmid.
2. A disagreements table. Columns: the crux; the formulation Nadia would accept for her own side; the formulation Owen would accept for his; the one experiment or measurement both agree bears on that crux, stated as an actual manipulation of the four knobs; Nadia's prediction up front; Owen's prediction up front. I'd guess among the cruxes are whether horizontal transfer makes plasmid carriage a trait of the cell at all, whether the group here is the gut, the cage, or the set of plasmid copies in a host, and who counts as the beneficiary when the mutant plasmid spreads.

4. Rules for yourself: no citations or reading lists, invent no equations beyond what both sides already accept, and if you can't find a manipulation both of them would accept as bearing on a crux, say so plainly rather than inventing one. Every prediction has to be about something measurable in this setup. And don't let either side win by definitional fiat, e.g. by stipulating what a 'group' or a 'cheater' really means.
