I co-teach a graduate seminar on scientific modeling with a colleague who is, by temperament, an instrumentalist about models, and we have agreed to convert our recurring argument into an adversarial collaboration. I need the document.

The parties: an as-if defender in the spirit of Friedman's F-twist, for whom the only test of a model is predictive success within its intended domain and for whom the unrealistic character of its assumptions is not merely tolerable but often a sign that the model has isolated what does the work; and a de-idealization realist, for whom a model earns its keep only if we can say in what respect its falsehoods are false, show that they are controlled, and recover the target phenomenon as they are relaxed.

Use the following taxonomy, which we have both provisionally accepted. An assumption is subtractive when it drops a factor known to be present but small; distortive when it retains a real factor but misdescribes it in a known direction; fictive when it posits a mechanism or entity absent from the target system altogether — a representative agent, costless arbitrage, an infinite population. We agree these are different, and that the dispute may have a different answer for each.

Draft the joint statement we could both sign: a numbered section of agreed claims, covering what models are for, what prediction, intervention and explanation each require of a model, and what no-miracles-style reasoning can and cannot establish here; then a table of the residual disagreements, each stated twice, once in wording each side would accept for its own position, and flagged where you take the disagreement to be verbal rather than substantive.

Then the cruxes. I expect at least three: whether the F-twist's license extends to fictive assumptions or only to subtractive and distortive ones; whether success at novel prediction under intervention supports different conclusions about the assumptions than success on passive observation inside the calibration range; and what a systematic de-idealization audit would find, given a protocol fixed in advance — take a large sample of much-cited models, relax one false assumption at a time, and record whether performance degrades monotonically, improves, or survives outside the calibrated range.

For each crux supply one concrete case both of us would accept as bearing on it, and record in advance what each of us predicts, including what each of us would count as a result that moved them. One shared case we both already accept as relevant: a metapopulation model of a coastal fishery that assumed uniform larval dispersal along the whole coastline and forecast a northern stock's collapse four years ahead, to within a season; its high-resolution successor replaced uniform dispersal with measured currents, matched that forecast, and additionally predicted a second collapse in a stock the first model had folded into the same unit.

Aim for under 900 words, and do not cite anything.
