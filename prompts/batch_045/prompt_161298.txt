"(a) State what the common prior assumption does and does not say, and explain why the Milgrom–Stokey no-trade result needs it. (b) Do these results, together with Grossman–Stiglitz (1980), support or undermine the claim that markets aggregate dispersed private information into prices? And here's the answer, copied verbatim, typos and all:

"Start with the common prior assumption (CPA). It says that at the ex ante stage — before anyone gets their private signal — all traders assign the same probability distribution over states of the world. It does not say traders have the same beliefs when they actually trade: once private information arrives their posteriors generally differ, and they can have different utility functions and different endowments as well. Milgrom and Stokey (1982) combine CPA with common knowledge of rationality, common knowledge that the trade is voluntary and mutually accepted, and the requirement that the pre-trade allocation be Pareto optimal relative to the prior. Conclusion: no trade.

The mechanism is basically Aumann's (1976) agreement theorem. With a common prior, agents cannot end up disagreeing — as soon as they communicate their posteriors have to coincide — and trade is a form of communication. If I learn that you are willing to buy from me at price p, that is evidence you have seen good news, and once I condition on it I no longer want to sell. By Aumann, the two sides' posteriors must be identical at the moment of trade, so neither can expect to gain. The trades that survive, then, are exactly the ones where posteriors can stay apart, and that means the parties must have started from different priors.

Note what this does not say. It rules out speculative trade driven purely by differences in information. It does not rule out trade for insurance or liquidity reasons — a farmer hedging her harvest is not claiming to know more about the world than her counterparty, she is offloading risk — and it does not rule out trades that are not common knowledge to the parties, e.g. trades against noise traders or through an anonymous limit order book where I don't know who I'm dealing with or why. Those loopholes are what keeps volume alive in real markets.

(b) Grossman and Stiglitz (1980) is the natural sequel. If prices fully aggregated everyone's private information they would be fully revealing, and then there would be no return to paying for information; but if nobody gathers information there is nothing for prices to aggregate, and the market collapses. Their resolution is the noisy rational expectations equilibrium: price is only partially revealing, informed traders earn back the cost of their information, and uninformed traders effectively free-ride on the price. So the Hayekian dispersed-knowledge story survives, but in a weaker form than it is usually stated: aggregation is real but necessarily incomplete, and the incompleteness is exactly what pays for the information in the first place.

(c) That gives us the answer on volume. Since MS shows that information-based speculation is impossible under CPA, and since real markets trade enormous volume every day, real traders cannot be sharing a prior — the volume has to come from heterogeneous priors. We could also appeal to irrationality, but we don't need that hypothesis. And this refutes the Hayekian picture rather than weakening it: the point of markets is not that they aggregate knowledge, it's that they let people with incompatible worldviews bet against each other."
