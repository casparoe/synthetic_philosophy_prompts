I am writing a literary novel whose narrator is a neuroscientist who has come to believe that consciousness is a user illusion: a display that offers her a simplified, actionable summary of processes she is not in fact running, the way a desktop offers files, folders and a pointer while the real work goes on where she never looks. The sentence has started to feel like several different theses to me, and the difference matters for scenes I need to write. Is it the claim that there are no phenomenal properties, only the appearance of them? The claim that there is no unified subject who has experiences, only the appearance of one? Or the claim that whatever does have experiences is not the author of the actions it seems to initiate? I want a theory precise enough to generate first-personal predictions: given a perturbation, what would my narrator avow, and what would she do? So please write the specification I should hold such a theory to.

Concretely: set out the desiderata any adequate user-illusion account must meet, each with the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark for each whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of one camp. The cases I care about are the ones where the interface and the machinery visibly come apart: visual subjects who deny seeing what they can still point at and discriminate; patients who disown a limb that acts on their behalf; the right-hemisphere phenomena in split-brain subjects, where one person seems to house two partly independent centres of report and action; the way reported decisions appear to lag the neural events that precede them; and a fictional scene of mine in which my narrator is trained, over months, to stop attributing her experiences to a user at all, and is left uncertain whether anything has been unmasked or only relabelled. I would also like the list to include three problems I have been carrying around under my own labels, if they are genuine desiderata at all and not artefacts of how I have framed things: the grain problem, of what fixes the resolution of the display and why it sits exactly where it does rather than finer or coarser; the persistence problem, of what makes the same user resume after sleep or anaesthesia rather than a fresh one take over; and the informativeness constraint, that an illusion which misleads about its own author should still track the underlying states closely enough to be worth having, and should predict the specific, systematic errors of introspection rather than mere confabulation. Finally, whichever theses the theory asserts, it must say to whom the display appears, or explain why that question is empty, since my narrator is precisely someone who seems to herself to be appeared-to.

A table with a column for the motivating case and a column for status would be fine, and you may score the main existing contenders against the list at the end, but keep that brief: the criteria are what I want, since I can do the scoring myself. If some subset of the desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied, say which and why, and how confident you are about it, because that is the part I most need to understand: I have a suspicion that a theory good enough to be written from the inside, in terms my narrator could herself accept, is in tension with a theory that denies there is anyone the illusion happens to, and I would rather have that confirmed or dissolved than politely worked around. And if the whole framing is confused — if the user-illusion claim collapses into a straightforward denial of phenomenal properties, or into a denial of a central executive that nobody defends anyway, or if the desktop metaphor is doing work the theory cannot actually do — tell me so plainly instead of constructing a list on top of it.
