Hi — I write novels, mostly literary fiction, and the one I'm stuck on has a marine insurance underwriter as its narrator; there's a chapter where she has to put an actual number on something she knows almost nothing about, and it's turned into a proper rabbit hole. It started as one line of dialogue, but my partner (maths degree, now writes pricing software) told me the scene was philosophically loaded, and I've been going back and forth on it for weeks. I know the names involved — Bertrand's paradox, the principle of indifference — but what I actually want to know is what I think, because my gut keeps giving inconsistent answers and I need my character to sound like someone with a settled view rather than someone hedging.

So here's where I land on cases, going completely by feel. A ordinary die, no reason to think it's loaded: 1/6 a face, no hesitation. A chord of a circle, probability it's longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle: I refuse to answer — 1/3, 1/2 and 1/4 all strike me as answers to three different questions, and the honest thing is to say the problem is underdescribed. A vat of wine and water where all I'm told is that there's somewhere between one and three times as much wine as water: I refuse too, and I notice that being indifferent over the ratio gives 1/2 while being indifferent over the proportion of wine gives 2/3, which looks like exactly the same disease as the chord. But now: a machine tool turning out cubes whose side length I know is between 1 and 2 cm, asked for the probability the volume is under 3 cubic centimetres — here I *do* want to answer, and I want to say roughly 0.44 rather than roughly 0.29, on the grounds that the machine is calibrated in millimetres of side, not of volume; the answer, I feel, comes from how the cube was made, not from how ignorant I am. A coin of unknown bias, not yet tossed: 1/2 heads, and that one feels rock solid, though I think it's solid because of the heads/tails symmetry rather than because I'm indifferent. Same coin, tossed once, comes up heads: I say 2/3 for heads on the second toss. And that last one makes me queasy, because I can't say why indifference over an unknown bias is fine while indifference over an unknown wine-to-water ratio is a mistake. My partner says the fix is to choose the parameterisation that behaves well under changes of variable, which sounds to me like picking the answer you fancy and then finding the maths to justify it. He also says I should stop saying 'no answer' and say instead that the answer depends on a choice of measure, and that choosing the measure is a modelling decision rather than a discovery — I half like that, but I don't know whether it survives contact with the cube case, where I think there really is a fact of the matter.

What I'd like is this: is there a single principle that gets all six verdicts, or, if not, two or three candidate principles I could choose between? State whichever you recommend precisely enough that I could hand it to my character as a sentence she'd say out loud, and tell me which of my cases motivates each clause. Then tell me honestly which of my verdicts it can't keep — I'd rather drop a verdict than lug around a baroque principle, but I want to know which one is the weakest link and why, especially if it's the 2/3, since that's the one I'm least sure about. And I need a verdict on the case the book actually turns on, which I haven't made up my mind about: a point is dropped at random inside a circle, and then a chord is drawn through that point in a random direction — is there a fact of the matter about whether it beats the inscribed triangle's side, and if so what does your principle say it is? I defintely can't see it. Please write the whole thing in British English: my editor is a stickler, and I'll probably lift sentences straight into dialogue.
