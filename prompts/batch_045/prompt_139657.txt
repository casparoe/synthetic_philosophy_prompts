hey — i run a small reading group (six of us, mostly history-of-science and history-of-ideas people, going for maybe four years now) and our last two sessions were on Whig history. we read Butterfield's *The Whig Interpretation of History*, the 1931 one, and spent most of the second night on something i've started calling the ratchet in my notes — not a term of art, just mine — which is the observation that every debunking of a Whig narrative gets absorbed as the newest stage of the same narrative: we used to tell it as a march toward us, now we know better, is itself a march toward us, and the guy congratulating himself for having seen through teleology is standing on a rung that's going to look exactly like the rungs below it. i posted a version of this on my blog and someone commented that it's just the map/territory distinction with footnotes, which i think is wrong but i cant say exactly why — it isn't that we read the past through our categories, it's that the correction has the same shape as the thing it corrects, and it does that indefinitely. one guy in the group is a convinced Hegelian and thinks the ratchet is just spirit coming to know itself, which is unfalsifiable and also annoyingly hard to wave off. my partner's read is that the whole thing is an excuse to feel smarter than everyone who came before you, and i'd rather the poem not prove her right.

what i want to close the run with is a sestina. i think the form is actually doing something here even though i cant articulate it well: the same six end words come back every stanza, but the rotation keeps shoving them into new slots where they dont mean quite what they meant last time, and then the envoi forces all six into three lines and you find out they were never six separate words. that feels like the right machine for "the vocabulary of progress survives every attempt to retire it." the constraint i actually care about is this: all six end words have to be words a Whig historian would reach for completely unironically AND words that the person debunking him still has to use anyway — i was thinking along the lines of progress / then / we / inevitable / freedom / forget, but take better ones if you've got them. dont let it resolve into either "the past was leading here" or "its all contingency and nothing means anything," and dont let it turn into a scold about presentism: someone who's never heard of Butterfield should still just find it a good poem, and someone who has should be able to point at the envoi and say, there, that's the ratchet catching.

give me the poem first (39 lines, envoi included), then a section headed "What I was going for" — 250 to 350 words — on the choices you made: which end words you picked and why, and where in the poem the form is doing work that a paragraph of argument couldn't.
