Bit of background before the actual question: I retired four years ago after thirty-odd years as a physical chemist — heterogeneous catalysis first, then process safety and incident investigation at a chemicals plant — and for the last couple of years I've been sitting in on a philosophy of science reading group at the state university mostly to keep from going stale. We've been stuck on causation for about six weeks and I've realised I keep holding a pile of things at once that the group seems to treat as rivals, so I'd like an audit of my own position before I put my foot in it in front of people who've read this stuff properly. Here's roughly where I've landed, written out more or less the way it came out of my mouth last week:

(1) The best account of causation we've got is the interventionist one: X causes Y roughly when there's a possible intervention on X that would change Y, or the chance of Y, with the rest of the setup held fixed in the right way. (2) Omissions are perfectly good causes. The coolant wasn't flowing, and that's why the reactor ran away — that's a causal explanation of exactly the kind we used to write in incident reports, and I dont see why philosophy should make me give it up. (3) But I also have this physicist's gut feeling that causation has to be local: there's got to be a continuous chain of goings-on between cause and effect, something along the lines of a conserved quantity getting handed along the chain. No action at a distance, and no causation from nowhere. (4) Causal claims are always contrastive, always implicitly X rather than X-star caused Y rather than Y-star, and a lot of puzzles evaporate once you say out loud what the contrast is. (5) Once the contrast is fixed there's no seperate metaphysical difference left between the match being struck and the sprinkler not going off; whatever asymmetry remains is pragmatic, a matter of which of the two we bother to mention. (6) None of this should depend on anybody's obligations. Whether I was supposed to check the gauge is a moral question and has no business in the metaphysics. (7) Two case verdicts I'm attached to. First, the watchdog: the coolant pump on a reactor I used to be responsible for was held running by a watchdog circuit that had to recieve a keep-alive pulse every thirty seconds; the pulse never arrived because of a firmware bug, the pump tripped, the core overheated. I want to say the pulse's not arriving caused the overheating, and I want to say it as a plain matter of fact, not as shorthand for something else. Second, the jammed relief valve: an engineer deliberately jammed shut the relief valve that would otherwise have vented the overpressure, and the vessel burst. I want to say both that the engineer's jamming caused the burst and that the valve's failure to open caused the burst.

So: does that lot hang together, and if not, which pairs are actually pulling against each other and how badly? Could I use that to let (4) and (5) do the work I need without sliding into it's all just pragmatic, or does that only move the bump in the carpet? I don't have a favourite commitment here, so don't assume I'd rather drop any particular one; tell me which has to go and what it costs me elsewhere in the set.
