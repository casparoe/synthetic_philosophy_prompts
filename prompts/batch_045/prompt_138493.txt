I'm a fourth-year PhD student in cognitive psychology, and I've been drafted onto a small departmental committee that maintains an open textbook for our first-year graduate methods sequence. Every few weeks we have to decide whether a finding from the literature goes into the chapter, goes in with a caveat, or stays out, and the decisions are published as an appendix with our reasoning, so we have to be able to defend them to the original authors. The catch is that we get about forty-five minutes per finding, one member of the committee is an actual statistician who has to sign off, and the other three of us are substantive researchers who can run a t-test and read a confidence interval but will never read a word of philosophy of statistics. So I need something operational: an ordered set of questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, an explicit rule for when we stop and say no, and a point at which the procedure hands the decision back to us because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts.

What I'd like it to draw on is the real structure of the disagreement between frequentist and Bayesian ways of reading evidence, not a list of warning signs about bad practice. In particular I want it to force us to keep separate three things we've noticed we routinely collapse: the claim that an effect exists at all, the claim about how large it is, and the claim about the mechanism that's supposed to produce it — because in our last few meetings we found that a replication often kills a magnitude claim while leaving the existence claim untouched, and we had no vocabulary for that. The procedure has to say what we do when the numbers we'd want aren't available (no effect size reported, no interval, no preregistration, only a p-value), it has to say what to do with a replication whose point estimate is in the same direction but a third of the original size, and with one whose interval excludes zero but also excludes the original estimate, and it has to be honest about which steps are judgment calls wearing a lab coat. One of our members keeps talking about severity in the error-statistical sense, another wants everything converted into a Bayes factor, and I'd like a procedure that doesn't preempt that argument but doesn't stall on it either. I'd also like it to make us state out loud the trade-off we're implicitly choosing between wrongly including a finding and wrongly excluding one, rather than having the procedure quietly pick it for us.

Then I want it run on three cases. First: a preregistered, generously powered replication of a finding that my own dissertation builds on has just come back with a point estimate very close to zero and an interval that excludes the original effect size; I think I already know what the procedure should say and I'd like to watch it say that without flinching, and then tell me whether there is any legitimate way for me to keep using that finding in my third chapter. Second: a multi-site replication across a dozen countries that reports a pooled effect near zero but substantial heterogeneity, with the original effect reappearing at something like its original magnitude in two of the sites — is this a hidden moderator, a badly specified original, or a fluke, and what would the procedure need us to go find out before it can answer? Third: a single unregistered study with about forty participants per cell reporting a just-significant interaction, with no effect size given anywhere in the paper, which a colleague wants to build a grant around. Finally, I want to know what the procedure can't do: where it has to stop and hand the question to a person, and which kinds of finding it will systematically misjudge.
