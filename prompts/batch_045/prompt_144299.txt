I sit on the coverage committee of a large academic health system, and we are trying to write a policy that turns on the distinction between therapy and enhancement. A colleague and I built the argument below to show that the usual way of drawing that line does not hold. It now seems to me to work too well: it takes down distinctions I need in order to write the policy at all. I would like to know which step to reject, why that step looked unobjectionable, and what rejecting it costs me.

Take one case as given, without worrying about whether it is realistic. Call the intervention TR-1. TR-1 halts and partly reverses age-related thymic involution, so that a 62-year-old patient's output of naive T cells moves from roughly the 10th percentile for her age to roughly the median for a healthy 25-year-old. It repairs no lesion, fights no pathogen, and the patient carries no diagnosis. It raises a part-function from a level that is species-typical for her age to a level that is species-typical for the species, but that she does not now have and that almost no one her age has.

(1) If the therapy/enhancement distinction is to do the work we need of it — deciding what medicine must provide, what a physician may refuse to prescribe, what a sport may forbid, what parents may not demand for a healthy child — then it cannot be drawn by appeal to what anyone happens to value. That work consists precisely in constraining what people may demand on the basis of what they value, so a line drawn from values cannot perform it.

(2) The only candidate for a value-neutral line is a functional one: therapy restores or maintains a part-function at its species-typical level; enhancement raises a part-function above that level.

(3) "Species-typical" is a statistical notion: the level of the part-function found in the statistically normal run of members of the species, with the reference class narrowed by age and sex where the function varies with them.

(4) Reference classes are adjustable without contradiction. TR-1 in a 62-year-old is, relative to healthy adults, a restoration to typical; relative to 62-year-olds, an elevation above typical. Nothing in (2) and (3) selects between the two verdicts.

(5) So (2) and (3) classify by choice of reference class, and the choice of reference class is a practical decision about which population we take as the standard. It is not a value-neutral fact about the patient.

(6) Therefore the therapy/enhancement line is value-laden after all, and by (1) there is no version of it that can do the work. Every appeal to "this is treatment, not enhancement" is a disguised appeal to values.

(7) In particular: TR-1 is therapy or enhancement depending on which population we count; growth hormone for a short but healthy child is therapy in a society that regards short stature as a defect and enhancement in one that does not; the same goes for stimulants for a healthy student, SSRIs for dispositional shyness, and cochlear implants for a child in a signing community. Preventive medicine — vaccines, fluoride, statins for a person with average cholesterol — is a clean case of enhancement, since it raises a part-function above species-typical without restoring anything.

(8) But I cannot accept (7). It makes a physician's refusal to prescribe growth hormone to a healthy short child a bare imposition of contested values rather than a medical judgment, and it makes the repair of a cleft palate an enhancement in any community that does not stigmatize cleft palates. Medicine plainly does something other than ratify local preferences, and prevention plainly is not enhancement.

So I have proved too much, and I cannot see what I bought it with. Which step do you reject? Explain why the step looked fine. Then price the alternatives honestly: I would rather give up the details of (2) and (3) than give up (1), but I want to know what each of (1), (2), (3) and the step from (4)–(6) costs me if I have to abandon it, including what my coverage policy would then be able to say.

One refinement has been proposed and I do not think it helps. Replace (3) with a historical or counterfactual criterion: an intervention is therapy only if it restores a level the patient once possessed, or would have possessed but for some specifiable disrupting event. TR-1 counts as therapy on this criterion, since she had the thymic output of a 25-year-old once; but correcting a congenital enzyme deficiency a child has never had the function for now counts as enhancement, which is absurd in the opposite direction. Does the failure of both candidate refinements tell me that (2) is the wrong place to look for a value-neutral line, that the functional line has to be pluralist or disjunctive, or that (1) was the mistake and the distinction was always going to be a normative one?
