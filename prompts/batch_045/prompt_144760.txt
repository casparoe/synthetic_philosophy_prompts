I run the clinical ethics consultation service at a mid-size hospital — about forty consults a year, one hour-long case conference each, and the attending physician plus a three-person committee has to sign off at the end. Our accreditation body has started requiring that every consult close with a one-sentence "articulable rationale" that could be applied to future cases, and that's exactly the thing I'm not sure is always available.

I've been reading up on moral particularism versus generalism and the holism of reasons — the thought that a feature counting in favour of an action here might count against it, or count for nothing, somewhere else. Turn that into a procedure my committee can actually run inside an hour: the questions in the order we should ask them, a real test for whether a feature is doing work in this case or just travelling along with it, and a way of dealing with the fact that we keep a register of past consults (I pair each new case with its closest near-miss from the register, and we routinely can't agree whether the difference between the two is a reason or just noise).

It has to say where it stops with no answer, which steps are judgment calls dressed up as steps, and what we write on the form when the honest answer is that there is no generalizable rationale — I'd rather flag that than invent one. It also has to tell me which parts of the theory I can drop in translating it to a one-hour meeting and which I can't drop without the whole thing collapsing into a plain checklist.

Then run it on three of our cases: a family insisting on continued ICU treatment the team calls futile; a case that is the near-identical twin of one we decided last year except the patient has no family at the bedside; and a patient with a history of violence toward staff, where everyone in the room is sure it matters but nobody can say whether it's a reason or a biasing factor. Include at least one where the procedure lands somewhere I won't like, and say plainly what the procedure can't do for us.
