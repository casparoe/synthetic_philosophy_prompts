I've been defending a hardline version of the revealed-preference line in welfare economics — call it the measurement-commitment view: utility ascriptions are licensed only by choice behaviour, full stop, and there is no second quantity (happiness, experienced well-being, what's really good for them) that utility is trying and failing to measure. When someone says "yes but the person isn't actually better off", my reply is that they've changed the subject rather than found a counterexample.

I want you to map how exposed that position actually is. What would count against it, ranked from fatal to merely annoying.

A few things I want you to deal with rather than wave at:

1. The insulation worry. Every apparent counterexample gets absorbed by saying "preferences changed", or "that's a different budget set", or "they had new information" — the person who chooses B and reports A made them happier, the person whose choices flip once they've actually experienced the good, the person who buys a commitment device to stop themselves choosing what they're about to choose. I can feel the view hardening into a definition. So: (a) is there any observation it genuinely rules out, or is it a stipulation in a lab coat? and (b) if it is a stipulation, does that cost it anything? My own sense is that the damage isn't the unfalsifiability but that an unfalsifiable utility concept may be too thin to do the normative work cost-benefit analysis and compensating-variation numbers are supposed to do. Press hard on whether that argument really lands — that's definately the thing that would move me most.

2. The case I loose sleep over. Imagine we get a measurement — call it the shadow-order probe — with these features: it's built from no choice data at all; it predicts the person's future choices better than their own past choices do; it yields something formally well behaved (complete, transitive, stable over the window we care about, and cardinal in the sense that gaps on the scale predict how large a compensating payment the person will accept); and in a stable minority of cases it disagrees with what their choices reveal, always in the same direction. Is this a defeater or a gift? A hardliner can say: fine, then the probe is what measures preference and choices are just a noisy readout of it. Is that a legitimate move or the sound of a view protecting itself? And does the answer turn on whether you read revealed preference as an identity claim about what utility *is* versus an evidential claim about what we can access?

3. The extrapolation gap. Choice data pins down an ordering only over the region the budget sets actually visited, and policy questions are nearly always about new goods, new price regimes, big changes outside that region. The standard consistency conditions can be satisfied and still leave the recovered ordering wildly underdetermined exactly where it matters. Fatal defect, scope limitation a defender should just concede, or something that bites revealed preference harder than it bites any rival account of utility?

4. One more: all of this assumes a single chooser. We routinely run this machinery on households and firms, where there may be no one ordering there to be revealed. Does that count against the view or is it just a modelling shortcut everyone is entitled to?

Be straight about which of these a committed defender would themselves concede as counting against them versus which they'd dismiss as confused — those aren't the same list — and tell me if there's a defeater I haven't listed that's stronger than any of mine. Also say plainly if you think the position is basically unexposed and fine as it stands. Don't spend words explaining what revealed preference theory is; I know the textbook version.
