I spent eleven years in demand planning for a grocery distributor, and in the years since I left I have been arguing with people online about whether individual consumption choices make any moral difference at all. I have ended up with an argument I cannot fault and whose conclusions I cannot accept, and I would like someone to tell me which step to give up.

The background is the part I actually know from the inside. Orders do not trickle up the supply chain unit by unit. A store's order to us was generated against a forecast and a reorder point, and what we ordered from the producer moved in fixed increments: a pallet, a container load, a flock. So there is genuine lumpiness in the causal path between my shopping and anything that happens to an animal or to a tonne of carbon, and each lump has a real edge.

(1) An act is permissible if it makes no one worse off: if nothing would go better, and no one would be better off, had I not done it. Call this the no-difference principle.

(2) Whether my buying one package of chicken matters depends on whether my purchase changes anything upstream: the size or the timing of an order, the number of birds bred, the number of trucks dispatched.

(3) Because of the lumpiness described above, my single purchase almost certainly changes nothing upstream. The order is placed in increments of hundreds, and I am somewhere inside an increment rather than at its edge.

(4) So my purchase makes no difference. (from 2, 3)

(5) So my purchase is permissible. (from 1, 4)

(6) Nothing in the reasoning turns on anything special about me. Every other buyer stands in the same position and can run the same argument.

(7) So no individual purchase in this chain is wrong — and yet the chain is nothing but individual purchases, and what it produces is enormous and, on anyone's view, bad.

The part that bothers me most is what happens when you look closely at the lump I am inside. Say it contains four hundred packages, all of which get bought, and one of which is the four hundredth: the one that tips the system into placing the next order. Call that buyer the tripwire.

(8) By (1) through (5), the tripwire does something wrong and the other three hundred and ninety-nine do nothing wrong.

(9) But beforehand each of the four hundred was in the same position: the same act, the same information, the same foresight, and roughly a one-in-four-hundred chance of being the tripwire. And being the tripwire is nobody's achievement and nobody's fault. It is a property of where you happen to land in a queue fixed by what everyone else happened to do. Had all four hundred stayed home there would have been no tripwire at all.

(10) So on this account whether I have done wrong is settled by a fact I could not have known, could not have found out, that no amount of care or conscientiousness would have revealed, and that is fixed by other people's behavior. Wrongness has come apart from anything an agent could use to decide what to do.

And (1) does not stop at shopping. If it holds, then joining a collective project whose harm will occur whether or not I take part is permissible as well, and every participant can say the same; so we get harms that really happened and that nobody did anything wrong in bringing about.

I would rather keep (2), (3) and (7) than keep (1): the lumpiness and the badness of the aggregate are the two things I am most confident of. But I have two worries about giving up (1). First, some version of it seems to be doing the work in every ordinary judgment about harm — the reason it is fine to buy rice and not fine to punch someone is precisely that the one hurts nobody and the other does. Second, a friend of mine says the phrase makes no difference is doing two different jobs in my argument and that the argument only looks sound because I slide between them; I did not follow him, and I would like to see exactly where the swap is supposed to happen, if he is right.

One more wrinkle, in case it matters. Someone in the thread pointed out that reorder points at my old company were recalculated every few weeks from recent sales, so a sustained change in what people buy can shift the whole schedule even when no single purchase sits at an edge. I cannot tell whether that breaks (3) or merely relocates the lump to a different place in the chain.

So: which step do you reject, and why did that step look safe? If it is (1), what is meant to replace it — what is it about an act that changes nothing that could make it wrong? If the fix is to restate the argument in terms of expected rather than actual difference, tell me what magnitude that leaves my purchase with, and whether it can support anything stronger than the claim that buying the chicken is a very small wrong. And tell me what I am committing myself to about the tripwire if I keep (1) instead.
