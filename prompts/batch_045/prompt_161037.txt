I convene a week-long summer institute at which secondary-school history teachers and museum educators work on how to teach episodes of mass atrocity alongside one another, and for two years running the same quarrel has eaten the last afternoon of the week. One camp — call them the comparativists — holds that comparisons of magnitude and of moral gravity among historical atrocities are sometimes within the reach of our evidence and sometimes morally required of us, that a syllabus or a memorial commission which declines ever to rank anything is nevertheless making choices that embed rankings, and that the refusal is precisely what lets a national narrative quietly demote somebody else's dead. The other camp — call them the singularists — holds that comparative verdicts of this kind are either beyond what the archives can support or are themselves a species of injury: a category mistake about suffering, an insult dressed as scholarship, and an instrument whose users, in the cases that actually come up, are nearly always making an apologetic move. I do not want a verdict on which camp is right, and I do not want a diagnosis of why they talk past each other. I want the document they could both actually sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration: three or four pages I can hand out on the first morning.

Concretely: a numbered section of claims both sides can sign — including claims about what the word *worse* is doing, since I suspect much of the heat comes from one side hearing a verdict on the victims' suffering or the perpetrators' wickedness while the other means something closer to *warrants more of a given response from us now* — followed by a disagreements table in which every row is phrased so that the camp it describes would accept that as the wording of its own position. I would also like you to separate four things I think they are running together: how much suffering occurred; how culpable the agents were; how large our present obligations of commemoration, repair, or vigilance are; and how much precautionary attention each case now deserves. If part of the dispute dissolves once those are pulled apart, say which part, and why.

For the cruxes I have four candidate cases, but replace any of them with something better. (i) The downward revision: fresh archival work cuts the best estimate of the death toll of the event the comparativist ranks as worse by something like forty per cent. Does each side's posture toward the comparison shift, and how? I intend this to pry apart an anti-comparativism that rests on the frailty of our evidence from one that rests on the moral meaning of comparing at all. (ii) The gallery: a memorial commission has exactly one unassigned gallery and two candidate atrocities, and declining to allocate is a permitted option. Does allocating express a verdict of comparative gravity, and is there any way of allocating that the singularist can endorse as genuinely non-comparative rather than as a ranking with the lights off? (iii) The third candidate: an atrocity not previously on the syllabus is introduced as an additional comparator, and its arrival changes which of the original two seems worse. Is *worse than* a relation between two items at all, or is it holistically determined by the set on the table? (iv) The prevention case: if one could go back and avert exactly one of two atrocities but not both, does either camp have a non-arbitrary way to choose, and is that choice a comparison in the disputed sense? For each crux, name the argument, case, or finding both camps would accept as bearing on it, and record what each predicts in advance.

Two things about how I want the writing done. Where you cannot produce a formulation that a real comparativist or a real singularist would put their name to, mark the gap in the text — a bracketed note saying the joint wording failed — rather than smoothing it over; a document with three honest gaps is worth more to me than a seamless one I can't trust. And if you think the commission itself is misconceived — if these are not two parties with one disagreement, if the four-way distinction I propose is itself a comparativist Trojan horse, or if one of my four cases is a fake crux that both camps would wave away — say so plainly at the top, and tell me what you would have asked for instead.
