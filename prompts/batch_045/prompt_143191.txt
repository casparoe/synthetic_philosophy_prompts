So — I write novels, about twenty years at it, four published, and I'm deep into a book where almost nothing happens except a woman waking at 4am, scribbling what she thinks she just dreamt, and then having her whole day quietly wrecked by it. Which has dragged me, against my will, into reading about whether dreams are experiences at all.

Here's where I'm stuck. As I understand it there are three positions knocking around. (1) Common sense: of course you have experiences while asleep, they're just experiences, REM and all that, the report is a memory of them. (2) The Wittgenstein-flavoured one, I think Malcolm: the question is confused — "dreaming" isn't the name of an experience you had, the waking report is the only thing there is, and there's no fact of the matter about what went on while you were asleep, no procedure for checking. (3) The one I've seen summarized as the "cassette" picture, I think Dennett: nothing is played back from storage, the report gets composed at or around the moment of waking — but something genuinely happens at composition time, so it isn't pure nothing either. I'm probably garbling who says what, and I honestly don't care about names or dates; please don't spend your answer fixing my attributions, just work with the three positions as I've described them.

What I want: restate all three in the vocabulary I already live in — focalization, free indirect discourse, unreliable narration, diegesis, scene vs. summary, story-present vs. backstory, canon vs. word-of-author vs. fanon, retroactive continuity, drafting and revision, and above all the distinction between the narrating instance and the narrated event, since every dream sequence ever written is by necessity reported after the fact by somebody who just opened their eyes. Two bits of shop talk of my own, since you'll need them: I call a scene "backwritten" when the events are attributed to a character after the fact with no prior draft or notes behind them, and "unbacked" when there is no source text at all — the sentence is the only record that anything occurred, and asking what "really" happened before the sentence existed has no answer inside the fiction. (Related thing I keep hitting in my draft: in my book the dreamer is simultaneously the author, the narrator and the only reader, which is a combination I can't get away with in third person.)

So: give me the honest translation of each of the three positions into that vocabulary, one at a time. Don't make it too smooth — if Malcolm's point isn't just "the narrator is unreliable" or Dennett's isn't just "the scene was written at the last minute," say so. Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about: what translates cleanly, what only fits if you distort it, and what has no counterpart in how a novel handles narration at all — and for the leftovers, whether that's my craft vocabulary being too thin or the philosophers' claim being less clear than it looks.
