I keep going in circles on this and would like someone to lay out the actual structure of the problem for me.

The pitch for side-effect-avoidance, as I understand it, is: we can't write down the full utility function, so instead of stuffing value into the objective you bolt on a penalty for changing things on the way to your goal, and this is meant to buy safety *without* a value specification. But there seem to be two broad ways of filling that in, and I want to know where value sneaks back into each:

(a) change / state-distance measures: pick a baseline (usually the inaction counterfactual — what would have happened had the agent never been switched on) and penalize distance from it.
(b) option-preservation measures: forget world-states, penalize changes in how reachable the principal's goals are, or in how much ability to affect things is floating around.

Conrete case that makes me suspicious of (a): the agent is told to tidy a shared office. Nothing leaves the room, everything is still findable, but every book has moved three feet and someone's whiteboard got wiped. Measured over object positions and surface markings that's an enormous change; measured over what's legible and reachable by the people who use the room it's nearly nothing. Both are just "inaction baseline + a metric," so all the content is in the metric and the baseline looks like a red herring — is that right, or does the baseline do work I'm not seeing?

And the bit I can't see past: offsetting. If the penalty says change is bad, full stop, the agent is paralysed into near-inaction and can't even take a job whose side effects are all good ones. If it says a loss in one respect can be paid for by a gain elsewhere — smash the vase, then do something equally nice for someone else — you already need a common currency, which is most of the value specification the penalty was supposed to let us skip. So: is avoiding side effects just value-specification in a trenchcoat, or is there a version that threads this? Does (b) do any better, and what does it cost — I have a vague worry that an agent fixated on keeping options open will start curbing other people's options, which is pretty much the side effect we were trying to avoid.

Walk me through the office case concretely, tell me where I'm going wrong, and say how confident you are in each main claim plus which single point you're least sure about.
