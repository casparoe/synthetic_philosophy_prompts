I work on a team that attaches probabilities to individual cases — a patient's ten-year risk, a borrower's chance of default — and I am trying to work out what, if anything, settles which reference class is the right one. Here are my verdicts.

1. Dana, 54, ten-year cardiovascular risk. Candidate classes: women aged 50–59 (8%); women aged 50–59 with her LDL, blood pressure and smoking history (14%); women aged 50–59 with those values and her exact genotype (two members, one event, 50%). I say 14%.
2. Dana again, except that the only well-populated narrow class available is people with detectable coronary plaque on a scan (31%), where the plaque is an early stage of the very outcome being predicted. I distrust 31%: it feels less like a prediction of the outcome than a partial measurement of it, and I would not quote it as her risk.
3. A borrower whose narrowest well-populated class is men aged 30–35 in his postcode (11% default) — features with no causal bearing on defaulting. I am happy with 11%.
4. A borrower whose narrowest well-populated class is men aged 30–35 whose surnames begin with a letter in the second half of the alphabet and who were born in an odd-numbered month (9%), a class that emerged from a search over hundreds of candidate features. I reject it, despite its being well populated and statistically robust.
5. A patient for whom two equally narrow, equally well-populated classes conflict: non-smokers with her tumour stage (70% five-year survival) and patients treated at her hospital (52%). The second is a proxy for unmeasured but causally relevant social determinants; the first is causally relevant but omits them. I want to say that if the hospital effect is genuine I should use 52%, but also that I cannot narrow the gap further.
6. The probability that a particular radium nucleus decays in the next hour. Here I think no reference class is needed at all: the physics supplies a single correct number, and no broader or narrower class could change it. I would never say this about a person.
7. The same patient, the same outcome, three uses: setting an insurance premium, where I accept a crude but lawful class such as everyone of her age and sex (8%); deciding whether to operate, where I insist on the narrowest class I trust (14%); and testifying in a criminal trial about whether a DNA profile is the defendant's, where I refuse to give any number at all unless the class is defined only by causally relevant features.

Is there a principle, or a small set of competing principles, that captures these? I suspect cases 3 and 4, and cases 6 and 7, are the pairs I cannot both keep. Please state each candidate precisely enough to apply to a new case, note which of my verdicts motivates each of its clauses, and say plainly which verdicts it cannot accommodate.

Two cases I have not settled: Priya, whose five-year survival for her stage and genotype is 60% in the 2010–2015 cohort and 78% in the 2019–2023 cohort, and who will be treated under current protocols; and Marcus, whose narrowest statistically robust class is defined by an attribute his insurer is legally forbidden to use, where the next-best class would raise rather than lower his premium. What does the account say about each?
