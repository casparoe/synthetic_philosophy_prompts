I am a novelist, and for the past three years I have been working on a long historical novel set in a river valley across the four decades that follow a distant eruption which dims the sky and shortens the growing seasons; my characters are a tenant family, a bailiff, a physician and a priest, and much of the plot turns on whether the community keeps storing grain in common or privatises the granaries. The research has dragged me into a historiographical argument I never meant to enter, and I have ended up holding a position I am no longer sure I can defend: that germs, climates and volcanoes are best treated as historical actors in something like their own right, and that the familiar charge of environmental determinism is mostly a bogeyman kept alive by people who assume that causation without intention cannot be interesting. To keep myself honest I have been sorting non-human causes into three grades in my notebooks: a *trigger*, which precipitates an outcome that was already primed and would have arrived sooner or later anyway; a *lever*, which does not determine the outcome but decides which of several equilibria a fragile system settles into; and a *veto player*, which simply removes options from the menu of what a society can do without thereby deciding what it does do. My sense is that almost every respectable objection to non-human agency is really an objection only to the first grade, and that critics quietly win their case against crude determinism and then assume they have disposed of the other two as well.

I would like you to tell me what would count against this view, in the specific sense of mapping its exposure rather than summarising the debate. Please give me the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and set aside the bare point that volcanoes and bacilli lack intentions and beliefs — I have heard it, and I take it to be worth about one paragraph, not an argument. Some things I would find genuinely persuasive, if you think they obtain: that the "agent" framing makes no prediction a well-specified social-institutional explanation would not already make, so that the vocabulary is doing rhetorical rather than explanatory work; that the view is structurally committed to a selection effect, since the eruption or the outbreak gets named as an actor only retrospectively, once there is an outcome worth explaining, so that the evidence base is filtered in advance; that the interesting work is always done by vulnerability rather than by the shock — by who owned the granaries, not by the ash — which would make the shock an occasion rather than a cause; and that the three grades in my taxonomy are not stable, because a cause that merely removes options can always be redescribed as a background condition, with no principled stopping point. I would also like to know which of these a committed defender of non-human historical agency would themselves concede as counting against them, as opposed to what only their critics allege; which they would dismiss as resting on too demanding a conception of agency, one that would also disqualify institutions, markets or armies; and what sort of evidence would settle the matter rather than merely embarrass one side — for instance, whether a shock of comparable magnitude reliably tracks the same institutional outcome across societies with very different structures, or whether it reliably does not.

Finally, an honest verdict on insulation. I worry that my position can be defended indefinitely by retreating to the claim that "actor" was only ever a heuristic, a way of re-directing attention, not a claim about the world — and I cannot tell whether that retreat is an appropriate modesty for a claim of this kind, on a par with a research programme's willingness to adjust its auxiliary hypotheses, or whether it is the mark of a view that has made itself safe by ceasing to say anything. Please say which you think it is, and what I would have to do to my formulation to give it real risk. Kindly write your answer in American English.
