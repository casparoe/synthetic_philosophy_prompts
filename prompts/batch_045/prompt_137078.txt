I am a novelist. The book I am finishing at present is set largely in the hearing room of an invented statutory body, the Commission on the Common Life, in a jurisdiction closely resembling an English county in the early 1960s, though I would rather it not be identified with any real one. Across the novel the Commission hears eleven petitions. Two of its three members have no philosophical training, one of them is not especially quick, and all three are decent people trying to do an impossible job: an afternoon per petition, one research officer between them, and a duty to publish written reasons for whatever they decide.

Its governing statute, as I have drafted it, authorises the Commission to recommend the restraint of any practice which, being repugnant to the common morality of the county, loosens the fabric of the common life, and which cannot be sufficiently restrained by any means short of the criminal law. I have also given the Commission a standing convention, not a rule, of preferring the least coercive response that will serve: outright criminal prohibition, licensing or regulation, formal public censure by the Commission itself, or nothing at all. I did not design the statute to be a theory of anything. But I have now read enough of the argument between Hart and Devlin to see that my Commission is being asked, over and over, to do exactly what that argument is about, and I would like it to do it properly rather than by instinct.

What I need from you is a procedure my characters could actually run: the questions in order, what would count as a sufficient answer to each, the points at which the procedure stops with a no, and the points at which it hands the matter to a vote because the question is one of values and not of fact. It should be written in the Commission's own idiom — the words of my statute — since my characters have never heard of the debate, and any mapping of the steps onto the two positions as they were actually argued should go in a short appendix that I will read and not use. It should also tell me where a member of Hartian temperament and a member of Devlinian temperament will part company while following the same procedure, because those are the splits my book runs on. Say plainly which findings the research officer can settle and which are irreducibly judgments the members must own.

Please run it on three petitions. The first: a society for the prevention of suicide asks the Commission to make it an offence to publish or sell, to any person whatever, a manual describing reliable methods of ending one's own life. The petitioners concede that most purchasers will never act on it and that no particular death can be traced to it; their real ground is that a county which permits such a book to be sold has abandoned the claim that life is worth living. The second: a body of clergy and laity petition for the repeal of the old offence of blasphemous libel, on the ground that the religious morality it protects is now the morality of a minority; an opposing petition says that repeal would itself be a public declaration that the old morality is dead, and that it is the declaration, not the practice, which dissolves it. The third: the Commission's own review of an existing offence criminalising a consensual sexual practice between adults in private, at which its research officer puts a confidential survey showing that a very large minority of otherwise respectable citizens have engaged in the practice, and that convictions under it have fallen almost entirely on the poor and on one immigrant community.

I have already drafted the chapter for the third petition, in which the Commission votes two to one to retain the offence, the majority reasoning that the county is entitled to legislate for the morality it professes and that widespread private breach is not the same as public toleration. I would much rather be told now than in the proofs that no procedure faithful to that argument can reach the outcome I have written. If it cannot, say so plainly and tell me whether I must rewrite the chapter or abandon the rationale.

Finally: I am working in a cottage with no library, so please give me no reading list and no citations; I could not get the books and a footnote would only slow me down. What I would like instead is your own confidence: how far you trust that the procedure is faithful to the two arguments as they were really made, and which single step you are least sure of — the step where you suspect you may have flattened a distinction, or lent to one side something its author would disown. A step flagged as doubtful I can defend in a note; a step smoothed over is the sort of thing a reviewer writes about.
