Hey — I'm the student rep on my university's Academic Priorities and Budget Committee, and I somehow ended up being the one who has to write the recommendation memo we'll vote on at the start of next month, so I need something I could actually hand to twelve people who are mostly not philosophers. Background: the finance office wants us to approve a package right now — merge or close four 'low-yield' departments and shift about $6M of aid from need-based to merit-based — on the strength of their tuition-revenue model, which everyone calls SEAM-4. It has a genuinely good record: for eight straight years it's predicted net tuition revenue within about 1.5%. Its assumptions are also, by their own admission, false in ways anyone can see: it treats the student body as one representative agent with a constant price elasticity, it assumes enrollment responds only to net price and not at all to program closures, peer effects, or anything applicants hear about us, and it treats departments as independent cost centers with no feedback onto who applies here in the first place. I'm doing a philosophy of science course this term and we just did Milton Friedman's old as-if argument, and I actually feel its pull — unrealistic assumptions are cheap, realism costs tractability and generality, and a model should be judged by its predictions rather than by the psychology it never claimed to get right. My worry is narrower than that, and I can't tell whether I'm onto something or whether I've just argued myself into a corner (my roommate, an econ major, definitely thinks I have): all eight of those good years were years when aid policy and the program portfolio were essentially frozen. The lever we'd be pulling is the lever that never moved during validation. So the assumption 'no one responds to the University's plans' was idle while the model was earning its record and becomes load-bearing the instant we act on it, and we'd be using eight years of error statistics to say something about a kind of move the error statistics contain no information about. I've started calling this the idle-lever problem, and the principle I half-believe is that a false assumption is benign when its falsity doesn't vary across the interventions you're choosing between, and dangerous when the intervention is precisely what makes it false. There's a second twist too: the faculty senate is demanding we publish the department-level yield projections, and I suspect that printing 'these four are low-yield' would itself depress applications to them, so the prediction might eat itself in either direction.

What I want from you: draft the committee's decision memo, two pages max, bottom line up front — approve as proposed, reject SEAM-4 for this purpose, or something conditional — with a concrete time frame, since the budget cycle closes in eight weeks. It has to engage the finance office's case at its strongest rather than sneering at it: they'll say we have nothing better, that deferring is itself a decision with real costs for the departments and students stuck in limbo, and that the more realistic model they built two years ago was slower and did measurably worse on backtests. And I want you to be honest about the best objection to my idle-lever principle, which as far as I can tell is that every model used for policy has this feature, so if I'm right then no model ever licenses an intervention and my principle proves way too much. If there's a version of the principle that survives that objection, say what it is; if there isn't, say so and tell me what we should do instead. Also tell me what to do about the senate's publication demand, since I think that's a separate decision and the committee will probably try to mush it together with the vote. It's fine to tell me I'm wrong — I'd rather be wrong in the memo than find out in March.
