I'm running a 75-minute session for our formal epistemology reading group — about 14 people, mostly grad students with a few advanced undergrads, and they've all seen the definition of K and have heard the phrase "Solomonoff prior" but I dont think anyone has actually felt what it does. No laptops for the participants (I've got a projector, a whiteboard, index cards, and I'd rather not hand out candy or use real money).

What I want is a game they play, not a handout they read: something where they're betting on continuations of a short data stream under a scoring rule that charges them for description length in some tiny toy programming language I hand them, so that by round two they notice they aren't choosing the simplest hypothesis, they're choosing the shortest program in the machine they were handed. And no mini-lecture up front — they should walk into the machine-relativity problem before anyone in the room says the word "invariance".

Here's what I want them to come away seeing. First, that Occam's razor isnt a extra aesthetic principle bolted onto Bayesianism; it drops out of putting a prior over programs. Second — the part I actually care about — that "shortest" is machine-relative, and the additive constant from the invariance theorem is the same order of magnitude as the gap between the hypotheses people really argue about, so at five or six data points your choice of reference machine is doing more work than your evidence is. I was going to seed the data with 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, where doubling and the chord-regions rule (C(n,4) + C(n,2) + 1) both fit and disagree at the next term, and give the two halves of the room instruction sets that make a different one of those cheap — but if you've got a better ambiguous prefix, use it and tell me why it's better.

Third, I want them to hit the fact that the convergence result is a dominance result over computable sources and says nothing about when: maybe a late round where someone in the room is secretly the environment and picks the continuation after seeing everyone's bets.

Give me the rules, the instruction sets, how length gets counted, the payoff scheme, a timeline with minute marks, and a debrief that maps what will actually have happened in the room onto the formal stuff.
