I'm doing a short intro talk for a film-and-philosophy night at our undergrad philosophy society next month, and we're showing The Prestige (the Nolan one, 2006 — the two rival magicians, Angier and Borden). I want to frame it around Parfit's teletransportation cases rather than just talking about rivalry and obsession, but I want the philosophy done properly, not waved at.

The setup as I'd pitch it: the version where the scanner destroys your body on Earth and a replica is rebuilt on Mars; the version where it doesn't destroy you, so there are two of you afterwards; and then Parfit's move that identity isn't what matters in survival — what matters is some kind of psycological continuity with the right sort of cause (I want you to take a view on whether any cause will do or whether it has to be the normal one). Then apply it to three things:

- Angier's machine. Each performance the man who steps in falls through the stage into a tank and drowns, while a copy appears at the back of the theatre. On the Simple View he dies nightly; on the continuity view he survives, and unbranchingly, since the original is destroyed. But is there anything left for the man in the machine to be worried about? Is this really just the Mars case with extra staging, or does the fact that one of the two definately dies make it a different case? And if the copy at the back has all the memories, including the memory of deciding to do the trick, what is the guy on stage supposed to be thinking in the moment before he hits the water?

- A case I made up, because I think the film skips something by never showing it: one night the machine glitches, and the copy appears at the back while the original is still standing on stage. Ten minutes in which both are awake, both walk around, and can see each other; then a stage light comes down and kills the copy. Same end state as a normal night — one survivor, psychologically continuous with the man who entered the machine. Does the overlap change whether Angier survives? Does it change what either of them should rationally care about during those ten minutes? If you want to say branching halves or doubles something, say what it halves or doubles, and say why that matters prudentially rather than just metaphysically.

- Borden and his twin: two bodies, one public life, each living half of it, deliberately splitting the memories so the impersonation holds. Read that as the mirror image of duplication. Is it a clean case where identity obviously isn't what matters and everything is fine, or does the split life show that mere survival isn't enough and that you need enough of it? And if you're tempted to say double survival is as good as ordinary survival, does the two-brothers case give you a reason to doubt that, given each of them only gets half a life?

Then be honest about where the reading breaks down. My worry is that the film isn't really about what matters in survival at all: the machine is a metaphor for what Angier is willing to destroy in himself for the trick, and the ending with all those tanks is making a point about cost and revenge, not about continuity. If you think the philosophy distorts the film, or the film distorts the philosophy, say so, and tell me which two or three scenes I should actually use in a ten-minute intro and which to skip. Don't just use the movie as a handy illustration of a theory — if it pushes back on Parfit, I'd rather hear that. And you can skip explaining the plot back to me; I don't need a tour of the personal identity literature either, I've read the bits everyone's read.
