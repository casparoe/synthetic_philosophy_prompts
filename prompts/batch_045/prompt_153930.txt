My book club watched one of those glossy biopics about a mid-century scientist a few weeks back and my friend who edits documentaries and I have been going back and forth about it ever since. I'm trying to defend the claim that dramatizing a real dead person is a lot more morally loaded than the industry treats it, and I built the chain below — but it ends somewhere I definately can't accept, and every time I stare at it the steps all look fine individually. A couple of bits of jargon are mine, not anyone's: by *narrative capture* I mean the thing where once a dramatization supplies the default image of someone, later evidence gets judged against the film rather than the film against the evidence, and later films cite the earlier film instead of the archive, so a single invented scene ends up stickier than the record. And by the *compression constraint* I mean just the arithmetic: a 100-minute film about a 60-year life is roughly 1:300,000, minute for minute, so it is not a contingent fact about lazy screenwriters that most depicted events will be composites, compressions or outright inventions — it's structural.

Here's the chain:

(1) The only thing about a person that can still go better or worse after they die is how they are regarded; call it reputational residue. There is nothing else left for us to affect.
(2) Death doesn't relax the norm. If it would have wronged someone to spread discreditable falsehoods about them while they were alive, it wrongs them just as much to do it once they're dead.
(3) Because of the compression constraint, a biopic depicts events that didn't happen — invented conversations, merged characters, timelines collapsed. Putting a depiction of an event before an audience puts a claim about the person before that audience.
(4) Audiences form beliefs in proportion to how realistic the film looks and how many people see it; a hit biopic shapes more beliefs about its subject than every scholarly biography of that subject combined, and narrative capture means the false ones don't wash out later.
(5) So the makers of a biopic cause a large number of people to hold false beliefs about a real person.
(6) Causing others to hold false and discreditable beliefs about someone just is what reputational injury consists in.
(7) Therefore the makers of a biopic wrong the real dead — and the better-made and more widely seen the film, the larger the wrong.

That conclusion I can live with, sort of. What I can't live with is where my friend pushed it next. He says: the same goes for every compressed life-narrative, not just biopics — the eulogy that drops the cruelty, the obituary that smooths over the marriage, the historical novel that puts invented words in a real general's mouth, the anecdote a teacher tells about a founder. And: compression isn't optional anywhere, since any account shorter than the life has to select, and selection distorts whether it flatters or damns. So every life-narrative wrongs its subject, and the only narratives that don't are ones nobody consumes — meaning the ideal biopic, on my own view, is one that flops, and the more influential and truth-tracking your film is, the worse you've behaved. He also points out that if I drop the word "discreditable" from (6), my argument says a *flattering* biopic wrongs its subject too, which nobody believes.

Which step do you throw out? I'd rather keep (2) and (4) than (7) if I have to choose, and I don't want the easy way out of saying the dead can't be harmed at all — I think they can. But I also want to know why the step that fails looked fine to me, and what I end up paying: if I give it up, is there anything left of my original claim that biopics are morally loaded in a way the people making them mostly ignore, or does the whole thing collapse into "everything is fine, just make the movie"?
