My friend Jo did a philosophy PhD and now does clinical ethics consults at the hospital, and for about ten years we've been having the same argument about the naturalism vs normativism thing — whether "disease" is a value-free biological notion or is shot through with what we care about. She's maddeningly consistent, or thinks she is, so I want to run a prediction past you. And this isn't academic for me at the moment, see below.

Her verdicts, as best I can remember them:

- cystic fibrosis — disease, obviously
- severe depression — disease, and she gets impatient with people who won't grant it
- high blood pressure with no symptoms at all — disease, because of what it does to you over twenty years
- "pre-diabetes" (blood sugar a bit high, no symptoms) — not a disease, just a risk factor
- osteoarthritis in a 90-year-old — not a disease, just wearing out; the same joint in a 45-year-old — disease
- male pattern baldness — not a disease, even though men pay good money to have it treated
- lactose intolerance — not a disease, and she thinks calling it a "disorder" is silly
- homosexuality — not a disease, and shes scathing about the history here
- my uncle's chronic pain, with every scan coming back clean — a disease, and she won't tolerate any hint that it isn't "real"

Now my case. I'm 34, I had a routine blood panel last month through my new job, and my GP sat me down and told me I have Gilbert syndrome. The way she explained it: my liver conjugates bilirubin more slowly than usual, so my bilirubin runs high; it causes no symptoms worth mentioning (maybe mild jaundice if I fast or get properly sick), it doesn't damage the liver, it doesn't shorten my life, and there's apparently some evidence it's slightly protective against heart disease. But it has a name, I assume a billing code, and my GP wrote "chronic liver condition" on the referral she sent off. I'm filling in a life insurance questionnaire that asks wether I have any disease or disorder of the liver, and I genuinely don't know which box to tick.

So: what will Jo say about Gilbert syndrome? I'm less interested in the bare yes or no than in the rules that fit those nine verdicts about equally well and disagree about mine — the value-free "statistically subnormal function" picture (Boorse's sort of thing, I think) versus the "dysfunction plus harm" one (Wakefield's, I believe?) versus something more openly normativist. Tell me what each predicts about my case, give me your best guess with the reason for preferring it, and tell me which case I should throw at her next to actually separate the rules that are still standing — as far as I know she's never ruled on one of these harmless-dysfunction things.

Two more things. I really want to be able to tick "no" on that form, so tell me if I've loaded my description of Gilbert syndrome in a way that pushes toward the answer I want.
