My reading group (we rotate, last month it was Parfit, this month we finally got around to Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling, the 1843 one under the Johannes de Silentio pseudonym) had a blowup last night and I told them I'd get an outside opinion because Adam and I were clearly not going to settle it. The argument was between Dana and Yusuf, both of whom have read the book properly and neither of whom was strawmanning. Roughly, as close as I can reconstruct:

(1) Dana opens with the base rate point. In the actual world, people who report that God told them to kill a family member are, with overwhelming frequency, psychotic, or lying to get something, or being manipulated. So a disposition to act on private revelation even when the commanded act is a horror is a disposition we should be pretty sure is bad, and a deity who sets up tests that select for it is, from where we're standing, indistinguishable from a deity who selects for murderous credulity. She adds that the structure is Pascal's-mugging-shaped: if unverifiable private evidence plus infinite stakes can override everything else, then nothing else ever constrains you again.

(2) Yusuf says the base rate point is aimed at a book that already swallowed it. Silentio basically grants that from outside you can't tell the knight of faith from an ordinary bourgeois walking home from work, and that's not a slip, it's why the book is called what it's called. And he says the Pascal's mugging framing changes the subject: Abraham isn't running an expected value calculation, he's in a relation. Then a jab that landed: the book's opening section gives four different retellings where Abraham is fully willing, and Silentio says none of them has faith, so bare willingness-to-obey is explicitly not the thing being praised. By his read Dana is objecting to a divine-command-theory Abraham that isn't in the text.

(3) Dana concedes that last bit, and pivots. She introduces what she called the checked Abraham: same setup, except God also says, as a sign, that when Abraham reaches the summit there'll be a ram caught in a thicket there, and there is. Her claim: everyone in the room would then say Abraham was right to go up the mountain, so the real disagreement was never faith versus the ethical, it was evidence versus no evidence, and the book has written two hundred pages about the no-evidence case, which is exactly the case where ordinary judgment screams don't. She also says Kierkegaard can't want the sign, because the sign makes the whole thing publicly checkable and that defuses the story.

(4) Yusuf: that's not a counterexample, that's a different case. With a verified sign Abraham is just someone following a well-confirmed instruction, i.e. a case that can be mediated, and Silentio has a category for that already — it's the tragic hero, like Agamemnon, whose sacrifice the community can understand and mourn. No amount of evidence gets you to a suspension, because evidence is itself part of the universal; the suspension is the claim that the single individual relates to God in a way that isn't captured by anything publicly gradeable. So the checked case only shows that well-confirmed commands are fine, which nobody denies. And Dana's demand that a real normative state be publicly legible is just restating the premise under dispute.

(5) Dana's reply, which is where I thought she scored: if the content of faith is whatever is left after you subtract every publicly checkable consideration, then the teleological suspension of the ethical is a name for acting with the possibility of justification switched off. Then a dilemma. Suppose the command is trivial — break a promise to Isaac, smash something of his. If willingness to comply there is equally faith, the content of the act is irrelevant and what's actually being praised is bare obedience, which Yusuf just said it isn't. If instead the test has to be severe to be a real test, then the story is admiring Abraham for the magnitude of what he was willing to do, which is worse, not better. Pick a horn.

(6) Yusuf takes the severity horn but redescribes it: what matters isn't magnitude-as-obedience but cost. The test has to hit the thing you've staked everything on, otherwise there's nothing to resign; faith is infinite resignation plus getting it back by virtue of the absurd. Then he swings at the framing of Dana's whole objection: she's arguing at the policy level — what rule should we instill, what selection effects does a God who tests this way create — but the suspension can't be universalized into a rule, that's definately the point. A guy who has a standing process for receiving divine commands and carries them out, mostly mild ones, isn't a knight of faith at all; he's got a protocol, and a protocol drags it right back into the universal. So the policy-level objection and the Kierkegaardian claim are aimed past each other.

(7) Dana: that's precisely the problem. If it can't be a rule it can't be aimed at, and deliberately trying to be that person is already the wrong way to be it, so the story can't function as a model for anyone and anyone using it as one is getting something out of it they shouldn't. Plus the knife-down variant: whether we call it faith or filicide depends on whether the angel shows up, which is a fact external to Abraham's state, so on this account the moral status of the act is assigned retroactively by how the story ends, and we'd reject that kind of outcome-dependence anywhere else.

(8) The one thing they agreed on: the concealment. Abraham doesn't tell Sarah, and he tells Isaac that God will provide the lamb. Dana said if anything is indefensible it's that, not the raised knife. Yusuf agreed, and said Silentio more or less admits it can't be ethically defended, and that this is supposed to be the cost of an absolute relation to the absolute rather than a bug. Dana thought that move was a get-out-of-jail-free card and they ran out of time there.

Please referee: go move by move and rate each of the eight from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification each, then say who's more right overall. Also say what both of them are missing — in particular whether there's any version of the suspension that survives the policy-level objection, or whether the policy-level demand just changes the subject, and whether the concealment thing actually is the crux rather than a side issue. I honestly couldn't tell while they were going at it and I've been going back and forth since.
