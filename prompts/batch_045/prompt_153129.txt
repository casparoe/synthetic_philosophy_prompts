I'm running a philosophy-and-film discussion night at the local library next month, and I picked Minority Report (the 2002 Spielberg one) because I want to build the session around the Libet experiments and the neuroscience of free will — the readiness potential, whether the conscious intention comes before or after the brain starts gearing up, that whole mess. I know the rough shape of it but I definately don't have the literature at my fingertips, so I'd like an actual reading of the film against that material, not a general essay about whether free will exists.

As I remember the setup: three precogs see a murder before it happens, Precrime arrests people on the strength of the vision, and it works beautifully for the planned killings but only gives a few minutes of warning for the impulsive ones. That looks like a society organised around something structurally like a readiness potential, except stretched from a fraction of a second to days and attached to a named victim. I want to know how far the analogy can be pushed before it breaks — and I'd rather hear where it breaks than have it flattered.

A few things I'd especially like taken on:

The scene where Anderton finally confronts the man he's supposed to kill. He raises the gun and then dosen't fire, and the man more or less makes it happen anyway. That reads to me like a staged version of the veto — Libet's own escape hatch, the idea that what we have isn't free will so much as free won't. But the prediction still comes true. Does the scene really vindicate the veto, or does it quietly show that a perfectly accurate predictor and a genuine veto can coexist, in a way that ought to make somebody in the debate uncomfortable?

The thing I keep coming back to is the minority report: the divergent future that one precog sometimes sees, which the programme quietly deletes so the precogs keep their reputation for never being wrong. Two threads there. First, if one precog can see a different future, the film isn't committed to a single fixed future at all — so what is it committed to, and what does that map onto in the free will literature? Second, the detail of awkward data being purged to protect the reputation of an effect struck me as uncomfortably close to a criticism I've heard of the readiness potential itself, that it only shows up once you average a lot of trials together and the single-trial variability is effectively discarded. Is that a fair way of putting the criticism, and is the film doing enough for the parallel to land, or am I forcing it?

And there's a worry the experimental literature doesn't seem to touch: in the film the agent never gets the readout, an institution does, and it acts on it before there's anything to act on. Locking people up for a murder they haven't committed treats the prediction as evidence about what sort of person you are rather than as a brain event preceding a movement. Is that a different mistake from the ones Libet's critics usually press, and does the film's prison full of people who were stopped in time say something the papers can't?

Where does the film resist the reading — what does it care about that the philosophical debate doesn't? I want to be able to tell the group what the movie adds that the arguments alone don't, rather than leaving them with the impression that it illustrates a thesis. And if I've got any of the plot details wrong, correct me.
