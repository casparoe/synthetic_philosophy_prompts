Here's the question we set:

People often run two claims together under the label 'presentism'. Call Q-presentism the claim that the questions a historian brings to the past are shaped by the concerns of her own present — that any history is, in that sense, written for the present. Call V-presentism the claim that past agents may properly be morally assessed, blamed or condemned by the standards of the historian's present. Critics of presentism typically reply that we should instead assess historical agents by the standards of their own time. Against this reply, someone argues:

(P1) No historian can step outside the present: every account of the past is written now, with present concepts, present evidence and present interests.
(P2) So the standards a historian brings to the past are always the standards of some present; there is no neutral standpoint from which to apply 'the standards of the time'.
(C) Therefore presentism is unavoidable, and the anti-presentist demand that we judge the past by the standards of its own time is incoherent.

Assess this argument. In doing so, state explicitly whether its conclusion, if it holds, is a conclusion about Q-presentism, about V-presentism, or about both.

And here's the answer:

Q-presentism and V-presentism are different claims, and the argument only threatens the anti-presentist if it gets us V-presentism. Q-presentism looks obviously true and fairly harmless: of course we ask the questions our own time cares about, of course we use present evidence and present concepts. That is just what it is to do history now. The live claim is V-presentism, and the anti-presentist wants to block it by saying that past agents should be judged by the standards of their own time.

I think the argument works, and that it establishes both. The obvious objection is that P1 concerns concepts and evidence rather than moral standards, so it only delivers Q-presentism. But that distinction does not hold up: moral standards just are concepts, and if every concept a historian brings to the past is a present concept, then every moral verdict is a present verdict too. You cannot borrow the past's concepts without translating them into yours, and translation is itself a present act. So the anti-presentist's contrast between 'their standards' and 'our standards' collapses; there is only ever our standards, applied to their case.

It also follows that the anti-presentist has no fallback. If you cannot apply their standards, the only consistent alternative is to stop assessing altogether — describe what happened and withhold the verdict. But withholding a verdict is itself a verdict, since silence about injustice reads as toleration, and anyway no historian can pull it off: source selection, emplotment, even the choice between 'colonised' and 'settled' already evaluates. So historians must assess, they can only assess with present standards, and V-presentism is true.

There is one real point on the anti-presentist's side. 'The standards of their own time' is rarely a single thing, because contemporaries disagree, and the surviving sources are the ones the literate and powerful produced. But this cuts against them: if there is no determinate set of past standards to appeal to, the anti-presentist's rule is unactionable, which is just another way of saying that the choice of standards is always ours.
