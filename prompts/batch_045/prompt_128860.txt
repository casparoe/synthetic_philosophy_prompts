I'm a PhD student in philosophy working mainly on moral status and the metaphysics of consciousness, and I'm trying to turn the third section of a dissertation chapter into a standalone paper for a graduate workshop I'm presenting at in about three weeks. The chapter defends a gradational picture of moral status — the idea that systems can occupy genuine intermediate positions on a scale running from thermostats up through insects, fish, corvids, and us — against people who think there is a sharp cutoff somewhere along that line. My advisor's complaint about the draft is that my "scale" is really three loosely related dimensions that I never explain how to combine, and I've reluctantly come to agree with her, so I'm rewriting the section around a single proposed measure. Below is the new core of the section, written as a self-contained sketch. I'd be very grateful for comments on it, ideally written the way a careful and sympathetic referee would write them: someone who thinks the gradational project is worth pursuing but does not yet believe this particular measure does the work I want it to do.

***

**The measure.** Start from the thought that valence is not merely a signal with a sign attached to it, but a quantity that has to be weighed against other quantities in order to do any work at all. On this picture, for a system to be in a valenced state is for it to have a control parameter that is registered as signed, that can be defeated or overridden by other such parameters, and whose weight in that competition is revisable in light of the system's history. What varies across cases, I want to say, is not whether a system satisfies this condition but how rich the competition is: how many distinct signed pulls can be registered at once, how many of them can actually be traded off against one another within a single decision, and how finely the exchange rates among them can be recalibrated. Call this the system's *arbitration bandwidth*, for want of a better label.

**Thermostats.** A bare thermostat registers a single signed quantity, the deviation from setpoint, and there is nothing for that quantity to compete with; the mapping from error to action is fixed. Its bandwidth is therefore zero — not because it lacks a goal, since it plainly has one in the cybernetic sense, but because there is no economy in which that goal has to be bid for. Now consider the objection I keep getting from everyone I show this to. Bolt a second setpoint onto the thermostat, say a cost-of-energy term, so that it must now weigh staying at temperature against staying within budget. Intuitively the result is still not a subject of anything. My reply is that the resulting trade-off is a fixed-weight trade-off: the exchange rate is fixed at design time and cannot be revised by anything the system subsequently undergoes. A quantity whose weight can never be altered by the system's own history is, I argue, doing the work of a mechanical linkage rather than the work of a valence. So competition by itself is not enough; what matters is competition whose weights are revisable. This is the point where I'd most like to be told that I have quietly changed the subject.

**Insects.** Insects look like the first genuine case. A food-deprived insect will tolerate more risk in order to feed, and the exchange rate between feeding and risk is shifted by the animal's own nutritional state and by what it has recently learned about its environment, which is roughly the revisability I demanded above. But the number of pulls in play at any one time is small, the recalibration is slow and narrow in scope, and there is little evidence of anything that outranks present discomfort in the way an anticipated outcome can outrank present discomfort for us. So: nonzero, and low.

**The apparent cliff.** The objection I take most seriously is that all of this misses something discontinuous — that somewhere between mere aversion and full suffering there is a leap, and that the leap is located wherever a system comes to represent itself as the thing the valence is happening to. I want to deny that the leap is metaphysical. My suggestion is that a self-model is best understood as one more competitor for control, an especially influential one, and that what it changes is the shape of the arbitration rather than whether arbitration exists at all. Self-representation lets an outcome's significance be modulated by how it bears on a longer-lived entity, and it recruits memory and anticipation into the competition; that is a large increase in bandwidth, but a large increase along a scale is not the arrival of a new kind of thing. The sense that there must be a cliff is, I claim, an artifact of the fact that we read the scale from the inside, and only from one end of it.

**The worry I can't settle.** Grant me the measure and I still don't know how to get from a bandwidth score to a moral-status score, because I don't know whether bandwidth scores aggregate across individuals. If they do, then enough insects can outweigh a single bird in a direct trade-off, which I find very hard to accept. If they don't, it is unclear why the number of individuals should be morally irrelevant when the number of states within a single individual plainly isn't. I've been toying with the idea that what aggregates is the total amount of defeat, that is, how much of each system's other concerns get overridden per unit time, but I genuinely cannot tell whether that is a proposal or just a relabeling of the problem.

***

Concretely, I'd appreciate four things. First, tell me whether the revisability condition on weights is doing real work, or whether it collapses into something a defender of the two-setpoint-thermostat objection can simply accept and then re-run the objection against. Second, tell me whether the treatment of the self-model is the weakest link, and if so what the strongest version of the discontinuity objection looks like once you've stripped it of hand-waving. Third, on aggregation: is total defeat per unit time an actual answer, a disguised version of a familiar answer, or incoherent? Fourth, if you had to rank the three or four weakest points in the sketch from most to least likely to sink the paper at the workshop, and say in a sentence each why they land where they do, what would that ranking be? Please don't spend your budget on literature recommendations or on where I should look things up — I'm working offline for the next few days and can't chase references, so I care much more about whether the argument works on its own terms. And if the measure I've described is really just a relabeling of some position that already exists, tell me what that position is and what it implies, in your own words, rather than pointing me toward a paper.
