I am writing a novel set about ninety years from now, and I have written myself into a corner that I would like help getting out of. Its central institution is a standing commission I have been calling the Tribunal of Moral Standing, which assigns every kind of animal — and lately every engineered kind — to one of five tiers. Tier 1 is full standing: the kind may not be killed, confined, or used in research for any benefit that would not also justify the same treatment of an adult human being. Tier 2 requires a weighty justification for killing or confinement and forbids commercial farming. Tier 3 requires that interests be weighed and accommodated where accommodating them is cheap, but permits ordinary use, including food use under welfare rules. Tier 4 prohibits only gratuitous cruelty. Tier 5 is no standing at all: the kind is property, or an ecological asset.

The opinions I have already written reason case by case and never state a rule. Each of them cites neural measurements alongside behavioral evidence, and I deliberately wrote them so that a careful reader could take either to be doing the real work. Now I have to write the hearing on two pending petitions, and I need to know what rule I have committed the Tribunal to, because my narrator is a junior clerk whose entire function in the book is to notice that the Tribunal cannot say what it is measuring.

The precedents, as the record stands:

1. Born human beings of every age and condition, including infants and people with advanced dementia: tier 1.
2. A human patient diagnosed on the record as being in a permanent vegetative state: tier 4, over two dissents holding that membership in the species settles the matter. The majority reasoned that the integrative structures are destroyed, so that there is no longer anything that has a point of view.
3. Great apes: tier 2, the highest any nonhuman kind has reached. Three clerks wrote separately that no capacity the Tribunal has ever named separates them from the humans in tier 1, and that the line falls where it does because of the history of the statute rather than any difference the Tribunal can state.
4. Elephants, bottlenose dolphins, pigs, and dogs: tier 2.
5. Ravens: tier 2, notwithstanding a brain a small fraction of the mass of a dog's. The opinion leans on forebrain neuron counts in the same range as a monkey's and on flexible problem-solving in novel tasks.
6. Sheep: tier 3, although the brain is many times the mass of a raven's.
7. Octopuses: tier 3, with a few hundred million neurons, a large majority of them in the arms rather than the central brain.
8. A small reef fish reported to show mark-test-like behavior: tier 3, by a vote of four to three.
9. Decapod crustaceans: tier 3.
10. Carp and other teleosts: tier 4.
11. Honeybees: tier 5, at roughly a million neurons.
12. A colony of thirty thousand leafcutter ants, petitioning as a single agent: tier 5. The petitioners observed that the colony's aggregated neurons are in the same range as a raven's; the Tribunal answered that a colony is not a nervous system, and that its parts do not stand in the causal relation to one another required to share a point of view.
13. A laboratory-grown human cortical organoid of about two million neurons: tier 5, on the grounds that it has no body, no behavior, and nothing it is like to be it, and in any case falls below the line.

The two petitions I must now decide:

Petition A, which the plot turns on, is brought by the Small: engineered descendants of human beings, twenty-two centimeters tall, with a body mass near a kilogram and a half and a brain of some forty-four grams — less, in absolute mass, than the brain of the sheep in tier 3. The smallness is achieved by smaller neurons, thinner myelin, and shorter axons; the neuron count of the integrative structures is the equal of an unmodified human brain, and the whole thing runs faster per unit volume on a fifth of the energy. The Small have language, cumulative culture, institutions, and funerals. Their petition argues that they are human beings with a modified developmental program; the state argues that they are a new kind, and the statute's word for tier 1 has never been defined. I do not want the scene to turn on a lawyer's point, so please treat the question as the moral one the statute is failing to answer.

Petition B is brought on behalf of the Chorus, a sessile colonial organism of roughly a hundred and eighty thousand zooids, each with a ganglion of a few hundred neurons, amounting to some tens of millions in total, joined by a shared signaling net. Individual zooids learn nothing at all. The colony, however, habituates to a harmless vibration over about three weeks, and the response returns when the vibration is paired with a mild shock; excising a third of the colony does not erase the habit. There is no central ganglion, and the neural tissue is a negligible fraction of the body's mass. Its total neuron count is far below an octopus's and far above a bee's.

What I would like from you:

First, set out the rules that fit the precedents jointly — I can see several candidates in my own material (something like a threshold on neuron count within a single causally integrated system; something like encephalization or size relative to body size; something like behavioral flexibility and learning capacity; something like membership in a kind, with a human carve-out that the vegetative-state case shows to be defeasible) — and say what each of them predicts about the Small and about the Chorus. Assume the Tribunal cannot look anything up: it reasons only from the record as I have given it above.

Second, give me your best guess at what the Tribunal should rule in each case if it wants to be consistent with itself, which precedent is the closest analog in each case, and — where the precedents genuinely underdetermine the answer — an honest account of what principle the Tribunal would be adopting with each of the options open to it.

Third, I need the dissent in each case to be the better argument, because that is the book. Give me the strongest version of each dissent, the one a moral philosopher could actually sign, and tell me what the majority has to pay to answer it.

Fourth, my clerk has one sentence to herself in the final chapter. Draft it: what has brain size — absolute size, neuron count, size relative to the body — been standing in for all this time, and why does a proxy that has been reliable for a century and a half come apart in these two cases?

Fifth, I have one more chapter in which I want the Tribunal forced to say whether the measurement is doing intrinsic work in its reasoning or merely evidential work. The petition I have in the drawer is for an engineered kind with a two-kilogram brain and more integrative neurons than any human being, whose entire behavioral repertoire is innately specified: it navigates, builds, and displays with astonishing precision, but it shows no reversal learning, no habituation, no operant conditioning, a fixed withdrawal response to injury, no response to analgesics, and no sign that anything matters to it. Is that the instrument I want, and what would each of the candidate rules say about it? If a different invented petition would separate the rules more cleanly, tell me what it should be and why it discriminates better.

Please answer in American English.
