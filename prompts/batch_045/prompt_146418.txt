I cover higher education and the human sciences for a national magazine, and for the past two years I've sat, as one of three lay members, on the student affairs committee of my alma mater's board of trustees — an 1,800-student residential college whose honor code is administered by a student honor council and which, by an accident of our governance documents, makes our committee the final appellate body in conduct appeals. This spring we have one I can't stop thinking about. A senior peer writing tutor was assigned, through the college's writing-mentor program, to work with a first-year student over two terms. In one session she recognized a long passage in his draft lifted without attribution from an online source; she confronted him, he admitted it, he rewrote the section, and she said nothing to anyone. Text-matching software later flagged two subsequent papers by the same student, the instructor investigated, and the honor council found the first-year responsible for three violations and found the tutor responsible for failing to discharge the code's non-toleration clause, which obliges any member of the community with knowledge of a violation to report it. She was censured and removed from the mentor program, and she has appealed — she graduates in five weeks, so the censure's practical bite is mostly about the record and the program, not about her degree.

What makes it hard is that the case sits on top of a fight about the code itself. The tutor's argument is not that plagiarism is minor; it's that what she owed the writer came from the relationship the college had constituted and asked her to inhabit, that her role as a mentor was partly defined by candor from him and that she used it — he corrected the work, and he has said in writing that he would never have returned to her, or told her anything again, if she had reported him. The instructor's response is that her silence permitted two further violations and corrupted the record we would have needed to evaluate them. The writing-center director has told us, in effect, that both answers break the program: if mentors are informants, first-generation writers stop using it; if mentors are not, it becomes a place where violations get quietly repaired. A faculty member has drafted an amendment that would excuse a member whose knowledge of a violation arises only inside a relationship the college itself created for the purpose of guidance — mentor, preceptor, academic advisor — provided she requires correction within a stated period, the work is actually remediated, and the breach is not part of a pattern. Meanwhile two of my colleagues on the committee think the appeal is a distraction and the duty is simply the duty, and one wants to go further and rewrite the code around relationships rather than rules. I've been reading about Confucian role ethics for a piece I'm working on, and the idea that obligations attach to the roles one occupies rather than to a detached individual agent has made me doubt our phrasing — but I also worry that my role required it is unfalsifiable, that an exception for college-created relationships lets the institution define away its own enforcement interest, and that whatever we carve out for mentors will have to be carved out for coaches, resident assistants and thesis advisors too.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate to the committee before our meeting next Tuesday. I need a bottom line, stated in the first two sentences: what we should do with the tutor's appeal — uphold, reverse, or remit with instructions — and what we should do with the proposed amendment — adopt, reject, or adopt in some revised form you specify. Then give me the reasoning, organized as findings a future honor council could actually reapply rather than as general reflections: what the content of the mentor's role obligation is and how we are supposed to tell, whether a role the college constituted can carry the college's enforcement interest inside it, what makes a role-based justification more than a license for partiality, and what should happen when two role obligations conflict. Treat the strongest version of the losing position honestly and explicitly — I want the paragraph my colleagues on the other side would say is fair to them, not a straw version — and say plainly what the amendment's exception would cost us in enforceability and consistency across comparable roles. Finally, since the amendment has to reach the faculty at its May meeting if it is to govern next year, put a short timeline at the end. Do not give me citations, reading lists, or a survey of the role-ethics literature; I can find that myself. What I need is a document the committee could vote on.
