I build infrastructure software, and as a side project I maintain a small web application for registering adversarial collaborations. Two people who have been arguing record, before anything is settled: the claims both accept; the exact statements of what they still disagree about, each in wording that its own side would sign; the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one of them; and, for each crux, a case, argument, or measurement both accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down and timestamped. I have no philosophy background. I run the platform, I read every document that goes into it, and I can't tell good metaphysics from confident hand-waving — so the documents have to be self-contained. Please include no citations, reading lists, or pointer to a literature: I can't check them and neither party wants them. Define every technical term you use instead.

My first two users have been arguing for years, on a mailing list, about whether a particular first-order metaphysical disagreement is merely verbal. The disagreement is about whether there are any composite material objects: one side says that, strictly speaking, there are no tables or organisms, only simples arranged table-wise or organism-wise; the other says that there are, and indeed that for any things whatsoever there is something composed of them — an object made of the Eiffel Tower and your left ear. One of my users says this dispute is verbal: the two sides agree about every arrangement of simples there might be and about everything we would observe or do, and differ only in how permissively they use the word exists, so that each is speaking truly in its own idiolect and there is nothing left to be right or wrong about. The other says it is substantive: there is a fact of the matter about which candidate meanings of exists, or which candidate inventories of what there is, match the structure the world has independently of how we talk, and the two sides disagree about that fact. Please write the joint statement these two could sign as the record of their collaboration.

The document should contain:

1. A numbered agreed-claims section. Include what both accept about the first-order dispute: which arrangements of simples, which observations, and which practical or scientific claims are common ground. Include what both accept about what it would take to show a dispute verbal — I suggest, but don't insist on, three tests they should each state in their own terms: whether the contested term can be eliminated so that the disagreement disappears; whether a two-way manual translating everything one side says into the other's vocabulary, preserving inferential and explanatory role, can in principle be given; and what remains if both of those succeed. Include, as its own agreed claim, each side's inventory of the primitive vocabulary its framework needs — plurals, parthood, arranged-thus-wise predicates, grounding, fundamentality, and so on — and note whether an exchange of primitives is a notational difference or a substantive commitment. No agreed claim may be trivial; each should be one a hostile reader from either camp would still sign.

2. A disagreements table, with one row per crux and columns for: the crux; the dissolutionist's formulation of their own position on it; the realist's formulation of theirs; the case, argument, or measurement both accept as bearing on it; the dissolutionist's prediction about how it comes out; the realist's prediction; and, separately, what each side counts as evidence that would move *it*. Where a crux is about words rather than the world, say so in the row.

3. A held-out case, applied using only the criteria stated in section 1, that neither of them has discussed: whether the thing that was down during last quarter's ninety-minute outage — as opposed to the machines, processes, packets, logs, and on-call events, all of which both sides grant — is an object, and whether the claim that the outage lasted ninety minutes has truth-conditions that require it to be. Each side should state what it predicts the other will say about this case, and what it predicts the agreed criteria will deliver, before the two of them actually discuss it.

4. A closing section stating which parts of the original dispute the collaboration concludes to be verbal, which substantive question survives if any, and — if what survives is only a decision about which framework to adopt — whether that decision is truth-evaluable, merely practical, or something the realist can reasonably call substantive anyway.
