You are a professional political philosopher with research expertise in the ethics of war, political obligation, and the morality of political membership. Please think carefully and map out the conceptual space before committing to a line: I want the structure of the argument, not a verdict, and I'd rather be told that a practice is a mess of bolted-together principles than be handed a tidy story. Concretely: first brainstorm the candidate theories, then test each against the details below, then tell me what the practice strictly needs versus what it merely sits well with. I don't want citations or suggestions for further reading — no sources, please; just the argument in roughly 900 to 1,200 words, under headings.

I'm finishing a master's in public law in Helsinki and write an amateur blog on Finnish defence policy, and doing my own service (plus watching friends opt for civilian service instead) got me stuck on a bundle of things that modern states do which I can't fit into one picture. First, the state compels: Finland's Constitution provides for universal male conscription, the standard tours run 165, 255 or 347 days, women may volunteer (as they have been able to since 1995) but are not drafted, and civilian service is fixed at twelve months — longer than the shortest military tour, so that the conscientious objector isn't excused but re-assigned a differently shaped and heavier burden; refusing both routes means prison, commonly about 173 days with no parole. Second, the state forbids: Britain has had a statute since 1870 making it an offence for a British subject to enlist in the armed forces of a foreign state at war with a state Britain is at peace with, and the general pattern of restricting or punishing one's own citizens fighting for someone else is widespread, even where the cause is one the citizen sincerely believes is just and even where the citizen's own state never fires a shot. Third, the state recruits foreigners: France's Foreign Legion enlists non-citizens, lets a legionnaire apply for French nationality after three years' service, or immediately if he is wounded in the line of duty — the provision known as "Français par le sang versé" — and in current wars states openly advertise for foreign volunteers. So the very same act — going to fight, kill and risk death in someone's war — is a crime for the citizen who travels to do it, a route into membership for the foreigner who arrives to do it, and a jail-backed duty for the citizen who would rather not do it at all. My friend in civilian service put it to me like this: if the state really believed this was about defending persons in peril, it wouldn't care which flag you fought under; and if it's really about membership, then the Legion's rule looks like membership earned by spilling blood rather than a duty owed by members — which seems to invert the whole justification.

Two further wrinkles, one real and one hypothetical, that I think should discriminate between the theories. The real one: reserve liability means the state keeps a dormant claim on the conscript's body and labour for decades after the tour ends, triggering it unilaterally and paying sub-market compensation, while employers are legally constrained in how they treat a reservist; and Sweden's nominally compulsory system, gender-neutral on paper since 2010 and reactivated in 2017, in practice calls up only a small fraction of the age cohort from a screened and largely self-selected pool. The hypothetical one: a working group here has floated letting a conscript discharge his obligation by funding a stipend for a trained volunteer — possibly a non-citizen recruited abroad — to take his slot, so that trained headcount stays constant while the personal duty is commuted into money; and a related proposal would attach the obligation to residence rather than citizenship, or express the state's claim as a defence levy payable in service, in cash, or in some mix.

So here is what I'm asking for. Give me a menu of the theories this bundle could be resting on — something like a membership or co-authorship view, a state-as-agent-of-a-collective-self-defence view, a just-cause or cosmopolitan view, a statist view on which neutrality and the control of violence belong to the state, a fair-share or burden-distribution view, maybe a view on which universal liability is a hedge or a deterrent rather than a way of raising headcount — and for each say which limbs of the bundle it licenses, which it condemns as unjustified, and whether it makes the limbs cohere with one another or leaves them in tension. Be explicit about where the practice is underdetermined, i.e. where several incompatible theories would license exactly the same institutions, so that nothing about the surface of the law settles which theory is in play. Then take the commutation proposal and the residence-based proposal as probes: does the existing institution strictly presuppose that the duty is personal, in-kind and non-fungible, or is that merely a contingent fit, and would the proposals preserve or destroy whatever the institution is for — distinguishing, please, the rationale of maintaining trained numbers from the rationale of universal liability as a signal to potential aggressors? Finally, say what would actually have to change in law and in the institution's self-understanding if the implicit theory were replaced by a just-cause view on which only the justice of the war and individual liability matter, or by a pure fair-share view on which service is one burdensome input among others. If you think some limb of the bundle is simply unjustifiable on every theory you can construct, say that plainly rather than smoothing it over.
