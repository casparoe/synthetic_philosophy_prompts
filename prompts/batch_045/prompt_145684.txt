Hey — I need a document, not an essay. Two camps in my reading group have been sniping at each other about Malthus for a while now, and I want the joint statement an actual adversarial collaboration between them would produce.

Camp one, the "dormant mechanism" people: Malthus got the mechanism right — population pressure against a fixed or slowly growing resource base, working through the positive and preventive checks, pulls per-capita income back toward subsistence — and what changed after 1800 is a parameter, not the mechanism. Useful knowledge and energy throughput started growing fast enough to outrun the checks, and nothing guarantees that keeps happening. Camp two, the "escape" people: the central long-run prediction — that gains in per-capita income get eaten by population growth, so that population adjusts to income rather than the other way round — has been falsified. The demographic transition flipped the sign of the relationship between income and fertility, and "right but early" is just a rescue with no expiry date.

What I want in it:

1. A numbered list of claims both sides can sign. Not peace-and-goodwill filler — claims that actually constrain: what each concedes about the pre-industrial record, about what has to be true of something for it to count as a "check" at all, about the difference between a mechanism and a parameter setting, about how much slack a ceteris paribus clause is allowed to have. If some of the fight turns out to be about words ("prediction", "falsified", "superseded", "Malthusian"), say which part is verbal and which isn't.

2. A disagreements table with columns: the crux / the dormant-mechanism side's own wording of its position / the escape side's own wording of its own position / the test or case both accept as bearing on it / what each side predicts. Each formulation should be something that side would actually put its name to, not the version its opponent drew up.

Cruxes I think are in there, though add or replace as you see fit: (a) whether the pre-1800 relationship between real wages and population is a real structural regularity or the artifact of fitting a very flexible family of models to short, noisy series; (b) whether the demographic transition is a change inside the mechanism or a replacement of it — specifically, whether a theory whose core causal story is "higher income, more surviving children, pressure back down toward subsistence" can absorb a population where that gradient has the opposite sign without turning into a different theory; (c) the methodological one: when an auxiliary hypothesis is added after an apparent falsification, what makes it a refinement rather than a rescue.

Two devices I want built into the document, because they're the reason I think this collaboration is worth doing at all:

- The rescue ledger. Every time either side answers an apparent falsification by adding an auxiliary hypothesis, the auxiliary gets logged, and it only counts as earned if it generates at least one prediction that wasn't used in constructing it and that the other side agrees in advance would discriminate between them. Run the ledger over the history of the dispute as each side sees it, and say what comes out in the black and what comes out in the red.
- The suspended-frontier case. A sealed settlement: 2,000 colonists, full modern technical knowledge and capital stock on arrival, a close material cycle with a fixed energy budget, no migration in either direction, and 200 years of annual data on population, output per head, and which check — if any — is doing the adjusting. Each side writes down in advance what it expects those series to look like: rough levels and trend, how volatile, which variable moves first after a shock, whether the adjustment comes through mortality, fertility, or neither. And each side writes down what pattern in that data would make it abandon its own position.

Close with a short section on what, if anything, both agree would actually move the debate in the next hundred years — meaning something someone could in principle observe, not more argument. If you think a crux is one that no observation anyone could make would settle, say so plainly instead of manufacturing a test for it.

You don't need to look anything up, and I'd rather you didn't: if a claim would need a source to stand up, restate it as a commitment one of the two sides is making. Format: numbered agreed claims, then the table, then the ledger, then the case, then the closing section. And please don't adjudicate — I don't want to know who's right, I want the paper both of them could sign.
