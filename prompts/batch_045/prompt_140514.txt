I am writing a novel, and one scene in it has to be philosophically load-bearing rather than decorative, so I would like the argument drafted by someone who can keep both sides honest.

The scene: two adult siblings are clearing out their late grandfather's house in a mill town. He was a schoolteacher who spent his last years on an unfinished manuscript defending Jain anekāntavāda, the doctrine that reality is many-sided and that any assertion holds only from a standpoint, only in some respect, with syādvāda and nayavāda as its logic of conditioned and partial predication. The manuscript's recurring example is a family event: a worker at the mill the grandfather once managed died after a safety guard was removed, and the family and the union have told incompatible stories about it for thirty years. The sister wants the manuscript published. Her brother is a litigator who spends his working life compressing messy events into single findings of fact, and he thinks many-sidedness is a way of evading verdicts.

Write their argument as a scene of roughly nine hundred to twelve hundred words, dialogue only, with the minimum of stage directions. Neither character should be a strawman: each should state the other's position in its strongest form before answering it, each should concede something real by the end, and the disagreement should remain open. The scene should turn on the cruxes rather than circle them: whether conditioned predication is a thesis about truth itself or only about the incompleteness of any single description; whether the doctrine escapes being itself one-sided, or bites its own tail; whether it is more than relativism with better manners; and, above all, whether a view that declines a final verdict can still tell anyone what to do when a verdict is owed. Assume the reader has never encountered the Sanskrit terms, and let every technical move be one the character has a personal reason to make.
