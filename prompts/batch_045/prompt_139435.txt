I sit on the research ethics board of a university hospital, and we have been going in circles for two meetings over a first-in-human protocol that I suspect will become a template for everything that follows it. The sponsor's compound is not an analgesic in the ordinary sense: it is designed to leave the sensory-discriminative dimension of pain — the localisation, the sharpness, the rated intensity — intact while abolishing what they call the unpleasantness, or the affective-motivational dimension, of that same experience. The model is the clinical phenomenon in which brain-injured and post-cingulotomy patients report that they still have the pain but are not troubled by it; the investigators want to produce that dissociation pharmacologically, deliberately, in people who are in real pain. The protocol has two cohorts: an acute arm, patients in the first seventy-two hours after total knee arthroplasty, randomised against our standard opioid-sparing regimen, with the primary endpoint being rated unpleasantness rather than rated intensity and opioid consumption demoted to a secondary endpoint; and a chronic arm, patients with long-standing post-traumatic neuropathic pain in a limb, where the pain no longer carries any useful information about ongoing tissue damage. The sponsor's own framing, stated plainly in the protocol's rationale, is that this is the first clinical test of the abolitionist thesis associated with the hedonistic imperative — that suffering is a technical problem, that the warning function of pain could in principle be preserved by a signal that is not unpleasant, and that the eventual target is the elimination of the distress dimension altogether, in pain and beyond it.

Four objections have been raised in the room, and I don't think they are equally good. The surgical colleagues are worried about diagnostic camouflage: in the acute arm, a patient who feels a wound dehiscing or a compartment filling but doesn't mind it may not report it, and pain is currently one of our monitoring signals. A second group is worried about consent, in a way that is not just procedural: our consent conversation works by getting prospective participants to weigh future burdens, but an agent that removes the aversiveness of a prospective burden may remove the participant's motivation to withdraw, or to attend to any other unwelcome prospect, and the sponsor's own data suggests the effect may not be specific to pain — grief and anticipatory anxiety may be flattened too, which the investigators list as a promising extension and several of us read as a serious autonomy problem. A third worry is measurement: with intensity preserved and unpleasantness abolished, a trial could be reporting genuine selective abolition, or global affective blunting, or retrospective re-rating once the worst is over, or simply demand characteristics in patients who can guess which arm they are in. The fourth worry is the one I find weakest but which has been said out loud most often: the analogy with people born unable to feel pain, who accumulate joint destruction, corneal ulceration and unnoticed injuries, so that abolishing the motivational force of pain is said to be dangerous in itself. That analogy seems to me to misfire for an agent that leaves the signal and removes only its sting, but I would like the memo to say why, or why not. The strongest argument for approval is also the simplest, and it is the one I keep coming back to: if the abolitionist claim is even approximately right, then a board that refuses to test the first credible instrument for it is not being cautious, it is imposing unmeasured suffering on everyone who comes after, and the cost of delay is denominated in pain-years.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate to the board before our 14 November meeting: roughly 900 to 1,200 words, addressed to the board, recommending approve, reject, or approve with conditions, and — if you think the two cohorts come apart — saying so explicitly, with a different recommendation for each and the reason they differ. I need conditions that are enforceable rather than aspirational: on dose ceiling and duration, on who is excluded, on what stopping rule would catch the camouflage problem in the acute arm, on how the protocol should be redesigned so that it can distinguish genuine selective abolition of unpleasantness from blunting, re-rating and demand effects, and on what we should require in the consent process, including whether consent to continued participation needs to be re-obtained from someone whose aversions may have been altered. The losing side should get its full weight: if you recommend rejecting or restricting the acute arm, engage the abolitionist cost-of-delay argument seriously rather than deferring to the precautionary instinct, and if you recommend approval, engage the autonomy worry about an agent that may generalise beyond pain. Please reason from the case as I have described it — no citations, no bibliography, and no discussion of what any regulator or guideline document says; the board will not read it.
