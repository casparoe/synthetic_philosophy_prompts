For the past fourteen months I have been one of five members of the committee that administers my university's emergency hardship fund, and I am drafting a short position paper for the other four. Our situation has a very particular structure, and I would like the paper to turn on it. Applications arrive continuously through the academic year rather than in a single pool; we meet fortnightly; we learn the fund's balance only with a lag of about a week; we may defer an application at most once; and, crucially, the terms of the fund forbid us from ranking applicants against one another — we are allowed only a yes-or-no judgment on whether a case clears our threshold of need. We also never learn what became of the applicants we decline. In practice we have drifted into a satisficing routine: we fix an eligibility-and-need threshold, fund the applications that clear it in the order they reach us until the money runs low, and revise the threshold at the start of each term in light of how fast the pot is draining rather than in light of the merits of the cases we happened to see.

Please argue, for that paper, that this routine is not merely a pragmatic concession to our limited time, money, and information, but the rationally defensible policy — and, more specifically, that our situation is misdescribed, not merely idealized, if it is modeled as constrained optimization: as maximizing expected student welfare subject to a budget on our own deliberation.

The argument will have to do real work against the two objections I expect from the two members who have read a little decision theory:

1. The as-if objection: that any stopping rule we could follow consistently can be represented as maximizing some utility function over some representation of our situation, so satisficing differs from maximizing only in notation, and the honest description of what we are doing is optimizing under informational and computational constraints.

2. The exploitability objection: that a threshold rule with a revisable threshold is path-dependent and dynamically inconsistent — that whoever controls the order in which applications reach us, or who can offer us an arbitrarily small improvement in a case in exchange for a delay, can extract from us exactly the comparisons between applicants the fund forbids — and that a policy vulnerable to this is not rational at all.

Two further wrinkles should be built into the argument rather than set aside. First, call it the invisible remainder: because we never observe outcomes for declined applicants, we cannot tune our threshold by feedback, and the usual story about why a simple heuristic is rational — that the environment rewards it and the agent learns that — is unavailable to us; so if satisficing is rational here, the argument cannot be that we have learned it works. Second, our feedback is biased: we do hear about the cases we fund, and funded cases predictably do better than comparable unfunded ones, so any attempt to calibrate the threshold on observed outcomes would push it in the wrong direction. I would like the paper to use these two facts as a lever: if the rationality of our procedure cannot come from feedback, then it has to be a matter of the fit between the procedure and the structure of the environment, and I want you to say why that is a genuine standard of assessment and not a retreat to whatever works.

One of the members has also proposed a concrete reform: score every application on a fixed scale and, within each fortnight, fund in descending order of score. Argue against that proposal on the basis of the argument above — in particular, say whether scoring solves the exploitability objection or merely relocates it, given that the scores are produced by five different people on different days with no common calibration.

Please keep the argument conceptual rather than empirical: I have no access to a library or to data on how committees like ours actually perform, so I do not want it to rest on findings I could not check or on appeals to authorities. Roughly seven hundred to nine hundred words, in prose suitable for colleagues who are thoughtful but not philosophers.
