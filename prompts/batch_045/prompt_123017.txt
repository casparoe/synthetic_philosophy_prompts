ok so I just had a really long argument with a friend about whether death is bad for you and we got completely stuck, so I want you to carefully compare the two Epicurean arguments for me, because I think the fight we were having was actually about them being treated as one thing when they aren't.

Argument A is the Epicurus one: death is nothing to us, because for as long as we are around death isn't there, and once death is there we aren't around — so there's never a moment where the person and the death coexist, which means death can't be anything to that person, and being scared of it is empty. Argument B is the symmetry argument Lucretius gives: think back to the endless nothing before you were born, notice you feel zero horror about it, and then notice that the nothing after you die is exactly the same sort of nothing, so you should feel the same way about both.

Here's what I want: are these actually the same argument in different clothes, or do they rest on different commitments and fail separately? Which one is really doing the work in the Epicurean therapy, and which one is easier to hold onto if you grant that dying young is worse than dying old?

The thing that made us stuck was a case my friend invented. Suppose you're healthy at 40 and someone freezes you for 300 years and then thaws you out and you live another 40 decent years — she says everyone wants to say you were robbed of those 300 years, they were a loss to you. But if the machine breaks and you never thaw, there's nobody left to be robbed, so it isn't a loss. Same 300 years of nothing either way, and whether it's bad for you depends entirely on whether you happen to get thawed later. She says this hits A hard (the badness seems to float free of any subject existing at the time) but leaves B totally untouched, since B isn't about subjects at a time at all, it's just about two nothings being alike. I said it hits B too: if a sandwiched stretch of nonexistence can be a loss to you, then it can't be the bare fact that it's a stretch of nonexistence that protects you, and B's whole force comes from treating the two nothings as bare nothings.

Who's closer, and why? And if you think B survives, does it survive because of some asymmetry between the before-nothing and the after-nothing, or in spite of there being one?
