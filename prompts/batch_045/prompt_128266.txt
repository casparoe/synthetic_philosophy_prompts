Hey — I go to this modern Stoicism meetup on Sunday afternoons (half the room is startup-founder types who found it through productivity podcasts, half is people who came at it through anxiety stuff) and the line "you only control your own response" comes up constantly. Last week it got used twice in five minutes and I'm pretty sure it was doing two totally different jobs.

First: we'd just lost a council vote on a tenant-protection ordinance we'd spent eight months on, and someone said it as comfort — don't tie your mood or your sense of whether you're a good person to outcomes that were never up to you. I didn't object to that. Then five minutes later the same guy used it to argue we shouldn't spend the next month running a pressure campaign on one specific councilmember, because whether he flips "isn't up to us." That one definately seemed to prove too much: by that standard you'd never do anything whose success depends on another person's decision, which is more or less all of politics.

Can you lay out the menu of precise claims that slogan could be making, from the trivial to the substantive, and tell me which are platitudes, which are actually defensible, and which are false? Here's my rough attempt, tell me where I'm wrong or what I'm missing:

- "control" as guaranteed determination of an outcome vs. "control" as causal influence on it — on the first reading almost nothing is in your control, including your own reactions; on the second almost everything is, including the councilmember's vote
- control over the process (whether you show up, make the call, write the letter) vs. control over the result
- "up to us" meaning you're responsible / blameworthy for it vs. "up to us" meaning it's responsive to your will
- a separate claim again about where it's rational to spend attention and emotional energy

Two things I keep getting stuck on. (1) If your own judgments are supposedly the one thing fully up to you, isn't "control your response" empty as advice? Nobody fails at the thing that's fully up to them, and yet people obviously do fail at it all the time. My guess is the modern version has quietly detached the slogan from the ancient fatalist / providence scaffolding it originally sat inside, and I want to know whether it survives that. (2) Over long enough time horizons you can shape your own reactions — therapy, habits, repeated exposure — so the boundary between what's up to us and what isn't seems to move depending on what timescale you pick, which makes a one-line verdict useless as a decision rule for the campaign question.

Also: which reading would the people in that room think they're expressing, and, most importantly, which reading (if any) actually gets you from the slogan to "so don't run the pressure campaign"? I'd like it as a list I can bring back next week. No reading recs please — I've recieved enough of the newsletters already.
