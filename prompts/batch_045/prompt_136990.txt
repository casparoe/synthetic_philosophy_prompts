I'm a retired systems ecologist—I spent forty years fitting nonlinear population models to messy field data, so I know just enough dynamical-systems theory to be dangerous, and I've started sitting in on a philosophy of history reading group at the public library. Last week we had a shouting match about contingency and chaos in history, and the same old proverb kept getting used as a conversation-stopper: "For want of a naile the shoe is lost, for want of a shoe the horse is lost, for want of a horse the rider is lost." (I know there are longer versions that add the battle and the kingdom; I'm sticking to the three-link version for now, since that's the one on the slide.)

The reading-group leader put it on a slide and used it to argue that history is radially contingent: the fall of a kingdom, or a revolution, or whatever big outcome you like, can hinge on one trivial missing nail, so all those structural explanations—economic decline, state fiscal crisis, class conflict, whatever—are missing the real cause. When I objected that a single missing nail isn't going to flip a battle if the army has a hundred other horses, he replied that I was missing the point, because that is exactly what chaos means, sensitivity to initial conditions. I definately think something is off there, because sensitivity to initial conditions in a chaotic model isn't the same thing as a chain of necessary causes, but I can't get precise about why, and I suspect the proverb is being made to do several different jobs at once.

So here's what I want: can you lay out, as a menu I can bring back to the group, the different precise theses this proverb could be expressing, from the trivial to the radical? For each reading, say whether it's a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false (or at least something the rhyme itself doesn't support). Please use my rough distinction if it helps, but don't just adopt my labels if they're confused—correct me. One reading I've been calling 'serial fragility' is the idea that the proverb depicts a single chain of necessary links with no redundancy, so the final outcome is only as secure as its weakest link, and if any one link fails the outcome does not occur; the kingdom's fall is counterfactually dependent on that nail. The opposite I've been calling 'swamping' (in my old field we'd sometimes say 'equifinality,' but I'll stick to my label): a system with many parallel routes to the same macro-outcome, so one missing nail gets compensated for and the big outcome happens anyway. My hunch is that a chaotic system doesn't automatically give you serial fragility; whether the missing nail matters depends on whether the system is fragility-dominated or swamping-dominated, and probably on the scale you're looking at. But maybe the proverb isn't about necessary conditions at all—maybe it's about each event being sufficient rather than necessary, or about maintenance norms, or about counterfactual dependence versus causal production, or about how small perturbations amplify, or something else I haven't thought of.

Please give me the readings as a list, ordered from the most platitudinous to the most radical. For each one, say (a) what precise claim it makes about causation, counterfactuals, or historical explanation, (b) whether it is trivial, defensibly substantive, or false/misleading, and (c) whether it's the sort of thing a careful historian or philosopher could accept. Then, the part I care most about: tell me which of those readings the reading-group leader would have to be committed to in order for his slide to actually support 'structural explanations are missing the real cause' or 'history is chaos, so big structural causes don't matter.' Which reading would do that work, and does the three-link proverb actually support that reading, or only a much weaker one? If the only reading that supports his conclusion is a radical one and the proverb really only gives you the trivial or intermediate one, say that plainly—I'd like to know exactly how much of his argument is resting on a piece of language rather than a thesis.
