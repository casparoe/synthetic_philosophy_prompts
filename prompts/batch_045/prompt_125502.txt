so my friend and I had a three hour argument last night about whether reading lives of admirable people does anything for you morally. I said yes, she said it's self-flattery with footnotes, and we went back and forth over Plutarch's parallel lives, some saints' lives I've been picking through, and the godawful founder biographies at the airport. About halfway through we both noticed the same weird thing and neither of us had a word for it, so now I'm here.

The thing is this. The writers who most want you to admire and imitate their subject very often put in one unflattering episode. And the effect is not to make the subject look worse. It makes the whole book feel like it's telling the truth. A saint's life will tell you that before his conversion the saint was vain, obsessed with fine vestments, insufferable about his own preaching — and I definately found the conversion more believable for it, and I wanted to be more like him, not less. A life of a general will report that at some banquet he got drunk and humiliated a friend, stick it in the early chapters, and every story after that lands as reported rather than as PR. A founder bio admits he was a cruel manager at twenty five and the rest of the genius stories suddenly read as journalism. Take the flaw out and I trust the book less, not more.

But it's not just any flaw and I can't get the conditions straight. Our tentative version: the flaw has to be one the reader could never imitate (you don't copy the drinking), it has to be over and behind the subject by the time the great deeds start, it has to be the kind of thing the subject would be ashamed of but the reader is invited to find understandable — pride in a young man, temper under provocation — and there's a dose limit, because two or three of them and the portrait starts falling apart instead of firming up. "Worked too hard" and "trusted people too much" obviously don't do the trick, and we couldn't say exactly why.

Three things we reached for and none of them felt right. One, my friend mentioned some psychology finding about spilling coffee — that competent people become more likeable after a blunder. Two, she did a year of law school and says there's a rule that courts will admit things people say against their own interest on the theory that nobody invents those; she couldn't remember what it's called. Three, there's the biographer's own disclaimer, the move where he says up front that he's after character rather than the whole record, so blemishes are part of the job. All three feel nearby but off: the first is about liking someone, the second is about a statement the person made, the third is a promise rather than a cost.

So: does the thing I'm describing have a name, and if not, can you carve it properly rather than just label it? A bare name is no use to me — I want to know what exactly is doing the work, what would count as the same phenomenon and what wouldn't, and where the edges are. Please go through these:

1. "What I'm pointing at" — 120 words: restate the phenomenon in your own sharper terms, so I can tell whether you've got the right thing.
2. "Nearest established concepts" — 350 words: whatever actually fits from psychology, law, rhetoric, or the literature on lives and character. For each, what maps onto what and specifically where it stops matching. The coffee-spill thing, the against-interest thing, and the disclaimer thing at minimum, plus anything better.
3. "Verdict" — 100 words: is there an established name or isn't there. Straight answer.
4. "Term, definition, conditions" — 250 words: if there isn't, coin one, define it, and say what has to be true for a flaw to be one of these. I want a definition I could apply to a passage I haven't read yet.
5. "Boundary cases" — 300 words, take them in order: (a) the general is still drinking when he wins the battle; (b) the flaw is a real betrayal that costs him something in the present and that I'd judge him for; (c) the subject admits the flaw himself in a memoir instead of a biographer reporting it; (d) a hostile biographer includes the exact same flaw to discredit him; (e) the flaw is "he worked too hard." Which side of the line is each on and why.
6. "Closed versus open" — 150 words: during the argument we started calling the certifying ones "closed flaws" — finished, past, unimitable — and the damaging ones "open flaws," and claimed the effect only works with closed ones. Tell me if that's the right axis or if I'm cutting at the wrong joint.
7. "So does the genre teach anything" — 150 words: whatever you conclude, say what it implies for the argument we started with, i.e. whether a life written this way can actually be a model for action or whether the mechanism that makes it convincing is the same one that makes it unusable as a guide.

And don't just hand me a list of names to go read — I'm not near a library for a while and I want the concept, not a syllabus.
