You are a professional philosopher who advises public and charitable bodies: your training is in philosophy of the social science and in normative political philosophy, and you are practised at turning contested, incomplete evidence into a decision that an organisation can actually defend. Think the problem through carefully before you write. Begin with a short, unstructured brainstorming section — bullet points are fine — in which you (a) pull apart the distinct claims that tend to be bundled together under the label "the gender-equality paradox", (b) list the arguments for the programme that would survive even if the strongest version of the empirical claim were true, and (c) list the ways in which whatever you end up recommending could turn out to have been the wrong call. Then draft the document.

I chair the Grants and Programmes Committee of the Callow Foundation, a charity whose governing deed restricts its spending to "widening participation in science and technology education". For eleven years we have funded Interface, a programme for girls aged 14 to 16: Saturday masterclasses led by working engineers, a mentoring scheme, and a paid summer placement with a local employer. It reaches roughly four thousand pupils a year, and the current grant — £2.4 million over three years — ends in March. At our meeting on the twelfth we must decide whether to refund Interface as it stands, refund it with conditions, or wind it down and redeploy the money elsewhere.

One of our trustees, Dr Halloran, has circulated a paper arguing for the third option. She invokes the gender-equality paradox — the cross-national pattern whereby societies that score better on measures of gender equality show *more* gender-typical educational and occupational choices, not fewer — and argues that Interface rests on an unexamined premise: that the remaining sex imbalance in science and engineering reflects barriers we can remove. If the countries that have gone furthest in removing such barriers display the largest imbalances, she says, our programme is either ineffective or, worse, an exercise in overriding the considered preferences of the very girls it is meant to help. Our programme director replies that the pattern is cross-sectional, that countries differ in a hundred ways besides equality, and that the underlying measures are crude.

Three things make the usual exchange harder than that. First, our evaluation team has turned up a within-country result: in the two countries in our data set that moved furthest on the equality measure during the period studied, girls' entry to science subjects moved in the direction *opposite* to the one the cross-sectional correlation predicts. Nobody here knows what to make of this, and the team itself warns that both countries reclassified school subjects midway through the period. Second, Interface's own monitoring data show a split result: participation raises girls' stated interest and their self-assessed ability, and it raises science uptake at sixteen, but in the one cohort we can follow to eighteen the treated group converts to science and engineering degrees at a slightly *lower* rate than a matched comparison group. The difference is not statistically significant, and the director cannot explain it. Third, our secretary has noted that any wind-down or redeployment has to be defensible against the deed's restriction, since it is not obvious that a decision to stop funding girls' science outreach is compatible with it.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate ahead of the meeting. It should run to about 1,100 to 1,400 words, in British English, and it should contain:

1. A one-paragraph bottom line at the top: a concrete recommendation — refund as is, refund with conditions, wind down and redeploy, or some fourth option — with a time frame, and the one or two conditions that matter most to it.
2. A section on what the cross-national evidence can and cannot establish. In particular, state what would have to be true of the countries at the top of the equality rankings for Dr Halloran's inference to go through — about where they sit relative to the ideal rather than merely at the good end of an imperfect range, about what the index actually tracks, and about the status of the preferences it is said to reveal — and say whether we have any reason to believe those things are true.
3. A treatment of the two awkward results, saying how much weight each should bear given the subject reclassification and the non-significant conversion difference, and specifying what the next evaluation would have to establish.
4. A separation of the empirical disagreements from the conceptual and normative ones, including an identification of the points at which Dr Halloran and our programme director are talking past each other.
5. A direct answer to the justice question. Suppose the residual imbalance in the most egalitarian countries largely reflects women's own preferences rather than removable external barriers: what case, if any, remains for spending the foundation's money on Interface? Distinguish at least the foundation's aim as expanding the pool of scientifically trained people from its aim as expanding each girl's options; say whether preferences formed under non-ideal conditions can bear the weight Dr Halloran puts on them; and consider whether the programme could be justified on grounds that do not depend on removing barriers to individual choice at all.
6. The strongest case for the option you are *not* recommending, together with the evidence that would make it decisive.
7. A paragraph on what the foundation should and should not say publicly, whichever way we decide.
8. A paragraph flagging the governing-deed difficulty, clearly marked as something for our secretary and our lawyers rather than settled by you.

Two constraints. Do not give us citations, a bibliography or a reading list: we have no library access, could not check references, and need reasoning we can defend to one another across the table rather than authorities. And do not settle the dispute by stipulation — where the philosophical disagreement is genuine, say so and explain which way you think it should be resolved and why, rather than papering over it. The committee is split roughly down the middle, and two members want to wind the programme down immediately, so the memo has to be usable by people who begin on opposite sides.
