Novelist here, and ive written myself into a hole I dont really have the vocabulary for.

My current book is set in an invented federation — bits of the US, Germany, Switzerland and the EU glued together and renamed — and the climax is a constitutional standoff. A regional premier refuses to authorise a high-voltage transmission line that would carry renewable power out of her region into three others, and she defends herself with the subsidiarity line: the EU treaties say (I think its Article 10 of the Treaty on European Union) that decisions shall be taken as openly and as closely as possible to the citizen, so her region decides. Her opponents point out that the whole purpose of the line is that its benefits and costs spill across borders, that her region happens to be the only place that can physically block it, and that "the people affected" include several million people who will never vote in her region. Everyone in the room is using the same sentence and meaning something different, and I cant tell whether any of them is saying something true. Ive met the same sentence from a Swiss cousin defending cantonal school curricula, from American governors during covid, and from an MEP on the radio who used it to argue both for and against a farm subsidy in the same interview.

So what I want is a menu, not an essay. As far as I can tell the sentence has three moving parts — how you measure closeness, who counts as the people affected, and whether "level" means only the levels that already exist or any jurisdiction you could draw — and different precisifications give very different claims. Please lay out the distinct precise things someone could be asserting with it, from the near-tautological to the wild, and for each: whether its a platitude anyone would accept, a substantive but contestable claim, or something probably false or incoherent; whether it actually hands my premier the veto she wants or quietly hands the argument to the federation; and what would have to be true in my fictional federation for that reading to get off the ground.

The wrinkle that might actually be new: in my invented federation the lower units have something no real one does. Alongside the ordinary territorial rule, any resident can register under another region's legal code for a specific field — family law, education, environmental standards — and that region's rules then follow the person rather than the place theyre standing in. A third of the population has done this. I invented it for plot reasons and only later realised it might gut the slogan, or rescue it, depending on what the slogan means: if "close to the citizen" is about metres and self-rule over a shared place, portable registration looks like it destroys the thing; if its about fit between a person's values and the rules that bind them, portable registration looks like the slogan taken seriously for the first time. I genuinely dont know which.

Give me the menu as a table with these columns:

- reading — a short name for the reading
- precise_claim — the claim spelled out tightly enough that a constitutional court could apply it, including what it says about each of the three moving parts above
- verdict — one of: tautology / platitude / substantive-but-contested / false-or-incoherent
- grid_veto — yes / no / only-if, plus the extra premise my premier would need for it to deliver her conclusion
- portable_jurisdiction — serves-it / defeats-it / orthogonal, with one clause saying why
- stage_direction — the single line of dialogue, detail or tell in the scene that would tip an attentive reader that this is the reading the premier means

Under the table, add two or three sentences on which reading I should put in her mouth if I want her to be neither a fool nor obviously right.
