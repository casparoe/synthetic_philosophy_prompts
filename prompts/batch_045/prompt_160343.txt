I help run a casual puzzle-and-philosophy night at my local library — usually about 12 adults, 90 minutes a session, most of them have done a Mooc or read some pop-sci probability but nobody is a grad student. We did Monty Hall and the two-child thing earlier this year, and last week a friend sent me this Judy Benjamin scenario that I have been chewing on alot ever since. As near as I can tell, a soldier is dropped into a square area cut into four equal quadrants by two lines, one splitting Red from Blue and the other splitting Second Company from Headquarters, and at the start she thinks each quadrant is 1 in 4. Then a radio message comes in saying something like, if she is in the Red half, there is a 3 in 4 chance she is in Second Company territory (or maybe it was the other way round, I might be misremembering). What grabbed me is that people online seem to split into at least two camps: one talks as if you just cross out the Red-and-Headquarters quadrant entirely, so the three remaining quadrants each get 1 in 3, while another ends up with Red-and-Second-Company at 3/8, Red-and-Headquarters at 1/8, and the two Blue quadrants still at 1/4 each. There were words like conditioning and imaging that went right over my head, but the fight is about what that radio message forces you to do to your beliefs.

So: for the next session, could you design a runnable activity or game — some kind of staged betting, trading, or role-play — where the room runs into this disagreement instead of me explaining it. I want about 12 adults, 90 minutes, no real money (points or chips are fine), and participants should come away seeing that the bare story does not tell you which update is right: the choice between treating the message as knocking out a quadrant versus only changing the odds inside the Red half changes what you should be willing to bet or trade, even though nothing about the setup itself changed. Please give me concrete rules, materials (a big 2x2 grid, chips, message cards, whatever), a payoff scheme, a timeline for the 90 minutes, and any roles (a radio operator, a bookmaker, or teams for each update style). They should not hear the labels conditioning or imaging until afterwards; the gap has to show up in what they are willing to do with their chips. One wrinkle I thought of, and would like built in if it does not break things: the two dividing lines are not equally easy to cross. Say the Red/Blue boundary is a mountain range and the Second Company/Headquarters boundary is just a road, so that if probability has to slide out of one quadrant, it should slide to the neighbour that is easiest to get to rather than just spreading evenly. I don't know if that ruins the classic setup or is actually the point, but it felt like it might make the sliding-probability side less arbitrary.

Then I need a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the parts of the Judy Benjamin problem: what the initial 1-in-4 chips stood for, what the radio card did, where the two different betting odds came from, and how the mountain-versus-road wrinkle (if you keep it) changes which quadrant the probability slides into. Be honest about what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — my guesses are that people will argue about what the radio message even meant, or they will think the 3 in 4 applies to the whole grid instead of inside the Red half, or the chips will get confusing — and tell me how to handle each. Also be frank about which feature of the real Judy Benjamin problem the game cannot reproduce and that I will just have to state in the debrief (I assume something about the formal difference between updating on a conditional and updating on a conditional probability, or the need for a prior over what the message could have said, but you will know better than me). If my mountain-and-road idea is a distraction or secretly changes the answer, say so and tell me whether to keep it. Thanks — I am definitly not an academic, I just got nerd-sniped and I want the room to feel the same itch.
