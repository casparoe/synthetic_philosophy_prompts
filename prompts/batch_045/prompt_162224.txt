I recently joined my university's research-integrity committee, and the reproducibility crisis in psychology has stopped being an abstract methodological worry for me: last month we had to decide whether to continue funding a moral-psychology line of work whose headline effect had just failed to replicate in a large non-Western sample. I want you to analyze that larger episode—the reproducibility crisis itself, not merely our local vote—through the lens of the WEIRD critique of folk morality and its implications for moral objectivity.

Assume the broad public picture of the episode, without my needing exact replication rates, dates, or citations. Over roughly the past decade, large-scale replication projects in psychology found that a substantial share of classic social- and moral-psychology effects—surprising, small-sample findings that had often been taught as basic human intuitions—either shrank dramatically or failed to reappear when re-run by independent teams. Among the most cited explanations were small and homogeneous subject pools, researcher flexibility, low statistical power, and publication bias. The field treated this as a credibility crisis and began adopting reforms such as pre-registration, larger samples, and explicit replication attempts.

The lens I have in mind combines two ideas. The first is the WEIRD critique. WEIRD stands for Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic; the critique holds that a great deal of moral psychology has recruited almost entirely from WEIRD undergraduates and then treated the resulting patterns as universal features of human moral cognition. The second is the cross-cultural finding that folk morality varies: the everyday distinctions people draw between right and wrong are not identical everywhere. In many WEIRD settings, folk morality foregrounds individual harm, rights, fairness, and autonomy, while other moral concerns—loyalty to the in-group, respect for authority or hierarchy, and purity or sanctity—may be weaker, differently ordered, or expressed in ways the standard experiments do not capture. The question this raises for moral objectivity is what becomes of the ambition of empirical moral psychology when the supposedly universal moral sense looks like one variable, culture-bound folk morality among others.

Please give me a disciplined, two-directional analysis. First, map the theory onto the episode. Identify the actors: the original investigators, the replication teams, journal editors and reviewers, funders and universities, and the candidate effects themselves as hypotheses under test. For each, say what options they faced and what counted as evidence and payoff. The original investigators could publish novel small-sample effects or invest in slow, cross-cultural replication; journals and funders could reward novelty or robustness; replicators could copy the original protocol exactly or adapt it to new populations. On the WEIRD-plus-folk-morality view, what should each of them have done, given that the original samples may have been measuring a local folk morality rather than the human moral faculty? Be specific about what the theory predicts the actors should have anticipated.

Second, say which features of the reproducibility crisis the lens explains and which it misses or misdescribes. Distinguish carefully between the demographic problem, which is that the samples were WEIRD; the methodological problems of low power, researcher flexibility, and publication bias; and the incentive problem of novelty over replication. Some replication failures are plausibly explained by the WEIRD critique, because the effect was always a local folk-morality artifact. Others are better explained by bad methods or bad incentives, and the critique has no purchase on them. Tell me where the critique is doing real explanatory work, where it is silent, and whether it wrongly predicts variation in cases where moral intuitions actually turn out to be robust across cultures.

Third, turn the case back on the theory and on the objectivity question. Does the reproducibility crisis confirm that moral psychology had been illegitimately extrapolating from one folk morality to universal human nature? To keep the analysis from staying at the level of slogans, introduce and use one refinement: call the move from our participants display moral intuition I to I is a component of the universal human moral faculty the universalist extrapolation, and track what the replication failures do to it. Then distinguish three claims that are easily confused: moral objectivity in the metaethical sense, which is the claim that some moral standards are true or valid independent of anyone's attitudes; psychological universalism, which is the claim that all humans share the same basic moral intuitions; and methodological representativeness, which is the claim that our samples accurately mirror the human population. Say which of these, if any, is actually threatened by the replication failures, and which survive. If the WEIRD critique overpredicts cultural variation, say where; if it underpredicts it, say where.

Finally, I want an honest verdict rather than a forced application. Does the reproducibility crisis vindicate the WEIRD critique as a correct diagnosis of moral psychology, or is it better read as a methodological scandal that leaves the metaethical question of moral objectivity untouched? If the better version of the critique is what I will call a local-first default—the rule that any moral-psychology effect should be treated as a description of the sampled folk morality until it survives cross-cultural replication—what would adopting that default imply for the objectivity debate, and what would it cost the field? Where does the WEIRD critique simply not apply to the crisis, and does a revised or alternative framework—perhaps one that separates demographic representativeness from methodological rigor and from the incentive structure of the field—do better?
