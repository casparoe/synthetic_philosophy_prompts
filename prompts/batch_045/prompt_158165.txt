**Question 3.** In their 1998 paper "The Extended Mind", Andy Clark and David Chalmers argue that cognition can reach beyond skull and skin, illustrating the claim with Otto, who relies on a notebook because of a memory impairment, and Inga, who simply recalls where the museum is. Consider a variant, call the subject Otto*: Otto* uses a cloud-synced note app on his phone instead of a notebook. Some entries he types himself; others the app generates automatically, inferring them from his calendar, location history and search history. An hour later Otto* cannot tell which entries he wrote and which the app produced, and he acts on all of them without checking. The app is as reliably available and as easy to consult as Otto's notebook is for Otto. Does the extended mind thesis, as Clark and Chalmers defend it, give us reason to count the app — including the automatically generated entries — as part of Otto*'s mind? In your answer: (i) state the parity principle in your own words; (ii) say which of the conditions Clark and Chalmers place on genuine cognitive extension the app satisfies and which it fails; (iii) explain what turns on the answer — what follows for the thesis if we say yes, and what if we say no.

**Student's answer.**

[1] The parity principle says that if a process occurring outside the agent plays the same functional role in guiding thought and action as a process occurring inside the agent would, then the two should be treated alike for the purposes of cognitive science: location in the head is in principle irrelevant, and internal and external processes should be treated identically in every respect. The principle works as a methodological check on question-begging — we may not dismiss the external case merely by pointing out that it is external.

[2] Applied to Otto and Inga: Inga recalls the museum's location from biological memory, Otto reads it off his notebook, and the only difference is where the information is stored. Since the notebook plays the same causal role in producing Otto's behaviour as Inga's memory plays in producing hers, the notebook is literally part of Otto's cognitive system, and its entry is one of his beliefs. This is the argument for the thesis, and it is what answers the main objection to it, the coupling–constitution objection: the objection holds that an object's merely causally interacting with an agent cannot make it a proper part of that agent's cognitive system, which assumes that constitution requires something more than causal role. But the parity principle shows that causal role is all there is to go on, since there is no further fact about what counts as cognitive beyond the role a state plays in guiding behaviour. The objection therefore collapses into the very assumption the thesis rejects, that the skin and the skull mark a real boundary.

[3] Otto*'s app satisfies the conditions Clark and Chalmers lay down. The information is constantly and easily available: the phone is on him, synced and searchable, so the availability condition is met as fully as in the original case. They also require that the information be automatically endorsed and reliably produced, and here too the case is straightforward — Otto* treats whatever the app displays as his own without checking it, so the endorsement condition is met, and the app's inferences are reliable.

[4] The remaining worry is the automatically generated entries: Otto* did not write them and cannot tell which are which. But this does not matter. What makes an external resource part of a mind is that it stores information the agent can reliably draw on and that it guides his behaviour in the way memory would; who produced the entry is irrelevant to that. An entry generated from Otto*'s own calendar and location history is at least as reliable as anything he would have typed, and it plays exactly the memory-like role. So the app, including those entries, is part of Otto*'s mind, and Otto* believes what they say — he may even believe things he never explicitly considered.

[5] What turns on this? If we say yes, the thesis survives contact with the way scaffolding actually works now, where devices do cognitive work for us, and the mind turns out to include whatever reliably carries our information; personal identity becomes partly a matter of what is in the cloud. If we say no, we are committed to saying that Otto* does not really know where he is going and remembers nothing when he consults the app — that the episode is not cognitive at all — which nobody should accept. That is why the thesis should be accepted and Otto* is a case of it.
