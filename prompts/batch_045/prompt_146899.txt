A friend of mine who's writing a thesis on this explained Ulysses contracts and constitutional precommitment to me like this: "It's all about binding your future self. Ulysses had himself tied to the mast because he knew that once the Sirens started singing he'd effectively be a different person, with completely different desires, so he was entitled to coerce that later self for its own good. Constitutions are the same thing at the collective level: the founders in their calm moment tie the hands of a later, more impassioned majority, and that's exactly why it's fine for courts to strike down laws that most people want. Entrenchment, supermajority rules, bills of rights — the point of all of them is to stop temporary majorities, so if a majority can just repeal something whenever it likes, the provision isn't really doing any precommitting work. And if a whole generation unanimously wants out of the arrangement, keeping them locked in is plain dead-hand rule, which no serious theorist defends."

I dont trust this as much as he does. For one thing, the "you'll be a different person later" bit seems to be doing a lot of justifying work in the wrong direction — if the later self really is a different person, I'd think that makes coercing them harder to justify, not easier. And he never explains why the founders get to be Ulysses and everyone after them gets to be the guy tied to the mast, given that the people being bound never agreed to anything. But I genuinely can't tell which parts are fine and which are him flattening things.
