I retired six years ago from a lab where I spent most of my career on the early visual system — electrode recordings, receptive fields, that sort of thing — and one of the things that stuck with me from those years is how completely the downstream story ignores whether there was actually anything out there. Stimulate the right bit of cortex, or just shut off the input for long enough, and people report fully textured scenes: a heron at the edge of a pond, a face at the foot of the bed. The signal looks the same either way. So I come to this perception material with a bias: the "common factor" picture feels like plain physiology to me, and I have to work not to let it win by default.

Anyway, the U3A philosophy group I go to on Tuesdays is doing perception next month, and I've been reading around disjunctivism and the argument from illusion. I get the shape of the reply — a shared neural cause needn't be a shared kind of episode, the good case can't be assembled out of materials the bad case also has, and so on — but I don't feel it yet, and I thought a poem might be a better way in than another paper. What I'd like is a pantoum (my granddaughter explained the form to me: quatrains where the second and fourth lines of one stanza come back as the first and third of the next, and the poem closes by repeating its own opening line). Six stanzas or so, nothing fancy in the meter. I want the form to carry the argument: the repeated lines should start out saying something both sides can accept — that no test available from the inside tells the two cases apart — and, having turned up again two stanzas later in different company, should have quietly slid into the much stronger claim that there's no difference in kind. That migration is exactly the inference the disjunctivist says is bogus, and I'd like it to happen in the mouth of the poem rather than be described. Please build the "bad case" out of a genuine illusion, something like a straight stick in water or a coin seen at an angle, not just a full blown hallucination with nothing there at all — I gather the argument from illusion proper starts from the cases where there really is an object being misperceived, and I'd like that to be right. And if possible let the return of the opening line at the end land as the uncomfortable thought that the line was always ambiguous, rather than as a resolution.

The other thing, and this is the bit I haven't seen anyone address, so treat it as my own wrinkle: I want a case where the verdict arrives late. Suppose the episode I'm having is intrinsically just like the hallucination — produced by a cortical glitch, say — but the pond happens to have a heron standing at exactly that spot, and I only find that out the next morning. Or the worse version: I later learn the glitch was triggered by a recording of that very pond fed into an implant, so the match isn't a coincidence at all. Either way, whether what happened to me was a seeing or a mere seeming looks like it's fixed by facts outside the episode, facts that aren't available to me at the time and maybe aren't settled by anything going on in me at all. Does that embarrass the disjunctivist — does the view, once the disjunct floats free of everything the subject has to go on, quietly collapse into the common-factor story plus an externalist footnote — or is it just what a naive realist should have said all along? I'd like one stanza to carry that case plainly, without preaching and without settling it; I'd rather the poem leave me annoyed than comforted. My group is about half retired scientists and half ex-English teachers, most of whom have never heard of sense-data, so it should read as a poem first. But I'd also like it accurate enough that someone who actually knows this literature could point to which repeated line is doing the common-factor work and which is the disjunctivist's denial, and not wince.
