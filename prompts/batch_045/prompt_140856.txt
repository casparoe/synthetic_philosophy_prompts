I am defending a paper at a philosophy of mind and biology workshop next week on whether Aunt Hillary, the ant colony that Hofstadter, in the Ant Fugue dialogue of Gödel, Escher, Bach, invites us to regard as a single agent, can literally have a mind. My thesis is that Aunt Hillary can have a mind even if no individual ant is conscious, because the colony's caste-structured division of labor, chemical signaling, and trail-updating dynamics realize a functionally integrated subject with its own aims and point of view. To block the standard objection that a colony is merely a collection of individuals plus their environment, I introduce a hypothetical variant species whose colony-level working memory is externalized into a slow-growing fungal mat beneath the nest, so that the colony's occurrent states are partly realized outside the ants' bodies. The audience is mostly antireductionist and sympathetic to the idea that mind can extend into the environment, but it also includes two neuroscientists who hold that consciousness requires fast, metabolically expensive neural dynamics, and one metaphysician who denies that any group can have phenomenal consciousness or interests of its own. Prepare my Q&A prep sheet: the seven or eight objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way these participants would put it; for each, give a two-minute reply, a note on where the reply is soft, and a line on what I should concede and what I should hold. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
