I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on contemporary moral philosophy, and I am writing this year's final. Last time I set a question on the aggregation objection to Scanlon's contractualism and got back a pile of papers that did the same three things: restated the formula of reasonable rejectability, recited the familiar case of the technician who is injured so that a large audience can watch a broadcast, and concluded that contractualism cannot add up complaints and therefore cannot make sense of numbers. I want this year's question to make that answer worthless. What I am after is a question that forces students to notice that the individualist restriction — no summing of complaints across different people — is not yet a decision procedure, because before any comparison can be made the framework has to settle two further things: the temporal and institutional grain at which candidate principles are individuated, and the permitted content of an individual's complaint, in particular whether that content may include other-regarding components whose magnitude varies with how many other people are affected. My suspicion is that the aggregation problem does not vanish once these are fixed but rather changes shape, and I want the question to make students say how. Assume they know the framework; I do not want marks available for restating it, and I do not want citations or scholarly apparatus of any kind, since I am testing argument and not reading.

The case I want the question built around is this. Twelve towns share a regional grid, and for twelve weeks of summer the operator must shed load. Two standing policies are available. Under the Rota policy, one town is disconnected for a full week at a time and the towns take turns, so that over the summer each town has exactly one turn; because the week is announced in advance and each town hosts a cold store and a small data centre, a town can plan around it by hiring generators, moving perishable stock and taking the shutdown as its annual maintenance week, so that the cost to a town of its single turn is about five units of burden. Under the Dimming policy, every town loses one randomly timed hour of power every day: an unannounced hour a day cannot be planned around, and the eighty-four interruptions over the summer carry restart and spoilage costs that come to about twelve units per town, or one unit per town per week. So at the grain of a single week the Rota imposes five units on one town and nothing on the other eleven, while Dimming imposes one unit on each of the twelve; but at the grain of the whole summer the Rota imposes five units on each town while Dimming imposes twelve on each. The policy that looks worse for whoever is singled out at any given moment is better for every single town across the period the policy governs. Part (a) of the question should press on what follows: whether this is an aggregation problem at all, whether a fine-grained principle of the form at each occasion of choice, spare the many by minimising the largest individual complaint is a genuine candidate principle that contractualism must take seriously (it is, after all, general), what such a principle would rule out if it were accepted — ordinary turn-taking arrangements, rotas, standing schemes that concentrate a burden on one party at a time in order to spare everyone — and whether the coarse-grained alternative, comparing each town's total burden across the summer, is available to contractualism without violating the individualist restriction at all, since nothing is summed across persons. Part (b) should then remove the temporal escape route and attack from the other side: take the one-off version of the case, with a single week, no future turns and no possibility of equalising burdens over time, so that the choice is between five units for one town and one unit each for twelve; and suppose in addition that the towns are closely linked by long-standing ties, that each town's council and citizens have a sincere and standing concern for their neighbours, and that each town reports a genuine burden of about half a unit of distress at the thought of a sister town sitting dark for a week while everyone else carries on, a distress that grows with the number of towns affected. The question is whether the contractualist may count such other-regarding components, whether doing so lets numbers matter without summing anybody's complaints together, and whether that is a legitimate reading of an individual complaint or merely aggregation smuggled in by another route, given that the very same burden then does argumentative work once for every person who sympathises with it — with the obvious parallel of a thousand people each distressed that one person is permitted to do something harmless. Where the framework leaves a choice open, the question should require students to say which way they would go and to defend it by appeal to the point of the individualist restriction rather than by stipulation.

Please write the question itself in clean, exam-ready prose that I can paste into the paper, with the case and both parts self-contained, then a model answer of roughly 650 words, then a marking scheme worth 25 marks in total, built around five must-hit points with the marks each is worth and a one- or two-line note on how candidates typically get that point wrong. My provisional thought is that the points should be something like: mapping the verdicts at each grain and seeing that no cross-person sum is needed to explain them; treating the fine-grained principle as a real candidate and running the reductio through burden-sharing institutions; explaining why whole-life or whole-policy comparison is intra-personal and therefore outside the restriction, while noting the residue that this manoeuvre only works for recurring, time-shareable burdens among parties with comparable tenure; showing how numbers can enter through the content of each person's complaint and distinguishing that sharply from summing across persons; and giving the strongest objection to that move together with a defended filter or a defended admission that no filter is available. But improve on that structure if you see a better one. Finally, add a short note on the A/B boundary: what an A answer does that a solid B answer, which handles the mechanics correctly, fails to do. The question should be answerable in about an hour, and it should reward taking a defended position over hedging.
