I'm on the group benefits team at a big multinational and we're rewriting the parental leave policy, and I have now stared at the current one for to long to be able to tell whether it's a coherent scheme or just several different theories bolted together. Here's the shape of it:

- the birthing parent gets 26 weeks at 90% of pay, starting whenever they like from two weeks before the birth, and none of it is transferable
- then there's a shared pot of 30 weeks at a flat rate, which either parent can take in up to three separate blocks any time before the child turns three
- 8 of those 30 weeks are earmarked for the second parent and lapse if they aren't used — but only if that parent is also on our payroll; if they work somewhere else, the 8 weeks just fold back into the general pot
- any block shorter than 4 weeks is paid at 70% of the flat rate, and any week taken after the child's first birthday is paid at half the flat rate
- the 90% bit has to be repaid pro rata if you leave within 12 months of coming back
- seperate scheme: we subsidise nursery fees, but only for hours when both parents are working or studying. A parent who is on leave, or working part time, gets nothing for those hours.

What I want to know is what a scheme like this presupposes about what parental leave is *for*. Is the 90% block really a medical and recovery benefit, so that it's sickness leave wearing a different hat? Is the shared pot a right the child has to be cared for by its parents, or a right the parents have, or deferred pay, or a retention device, or a deliberate nudge to rebalance care work inside couples? And which of those does the scheme actually presuppose, as opposed to merely sitting comfortably with?

The bits that puzzle me most: the 90% block is individual and non-transferable, which seems to presuppose something about bodies and recovery, but the clawback only makes sense if the money is conditional on coming back, which is a different kind of justification entirely. The earmarked 8 weeks look like they treat the couple rather than the person as the unit — otherwise why would the weeks evaporate when the second parent happens to work elsewhere? And the childcare subsidy seems to assume the dual-earner family is the normal and desirable case, and that care provided by a parent during working hours is worth precisely nothing, while the leave scheme pays people to provide exactly that.

My hunch is that the fragmentation penalty and the halving after the first birthday are really a "get back to work" mechanism dressed up as flexibility, and that they sit badly with the rest of it — but I'd like you to try to defend them rather than just agree with me. Maybe the whole thing is underdetermined and several theories would generate it.

So: reconstruct the implicit theory, tell me where the scheme is genuinely inconsistent and where it's merely philosophically underdetermined, and tell me concretely what we'd have to change if we rebuilt it on each of the main rival readings — leave as the child's entitlement, leave as deferred compensation, leave as a gender-equality instrument, or the firm trying to be neutral between dual-earner and single-earner families. And if leave is the child's entitlement, does that commit us to extending it to adoptive parents and to carers who aren't employees? Please write in British English (organise, favourite, and so on).
