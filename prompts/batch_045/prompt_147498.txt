I want a case study that runs in both directions: use Kripke's rule-following considerations (the "Kripkenstein" argument in *Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language*) to analyze the International Astronomical Union's 2006 reclassification of Pluto, and then use that episode to test the argument.

Take the background as stipulated rather than as something to verify or contest. Ceres was discovered in 1801 and was listed as a planet for roughly half a century; as more bodies of the same sort turned up, it and its neighbours were reclassified as asteroids. In 2005 an outer-solar-system object, Eris, was found and initially announced as possibly larger than Pluto, alongside a growing catalogue of comparable Kuiper Belt bodies. Faced with the prospect of an open-ended roster, the IAU at its 2006 General Assembly adopted a three-part criterion: a planet orbits the Sun, has enough mass to be pulled into a nearly round shape, and has cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit. Pluto meets the first two and fails the third; a new category, dwarf planet, was created to receive it. An earlier committee proposal that would have kept Pluto and expanded the roster to a dozen-odd bodies was not adopted. Only a few hundred of several thousand IAU members were in the room for the final vote. Dissent never subsided: some planetary scientists rejected the criterion as ill-formed, others argued that dwarf planet should be a subtype of planet rather than a rival to it, and the reclassification became sufficiently notorious to spawn a neologism for being demoted.

What I want is a disciplined mapping, not a general essay on rule-following.

1. Identify the rule-follower. Is the relevant speaker the astronomical community as a collective, the individual astronomer, or the IAU as a designated sub-community with authority to speak for the rest? Say which one Kripke's argument needs at each stage — the skeptic's challenge seems aimed at a single speaker, while the skeptical solution's justification condition advertises the community — and say whether the episode gives any reason to think a community can be the sort of thing that follows a rule at all.

2. State the rule and the novel case precisely. Note that the novelty here is unusual: no single object was unprecedented, but a change in background population changed which similarities looked salient. Say what that does to Kripke's argument, which normally turns on a speaker confronting one new number or one new case.

3. Run the skeptical argument honestly. Produce an explicit nonstandard rule — a "plet" to pair with "planet" — that fits the pre-2006 applications of planet as well as the standard one does and delivers a different verdict on Pluto. If you find that the pre-2006 practice is too rich to admit a plausible competitor, treat that as a finding and say what it shows about how much work the quus-style construction is doing in Kripke's argument. Note also that this community, unlike Kripke's isolated speaker, had an enormous, explicit, socially shared record of applications and an articulate account of how it handled earlier borderline cases (Ceres, the asteroids): does the skeptic's argument survive that abundance, or does it need the speaker to be impoverished?

4. Now the part I care most about, which I think the standard presentations of Kripke handle badly. A codification can stand in at least three relations to the practice it codifies: it can be descriptive (recording a rule that was antecedently in force), legislative (stipulating content for future use), or retroactively constitutive (making it the case that the rule antecedently had that content). Tell me which of these the skeptical solution can make sense of, and which one the IAU was actually doing — and note the tension that the IAU presented itself as *discovering* that Pluto had never been a planet while possessing only the authority to *decide*. My own suspicion is that the skeptical solution collapses the first and third options into each other and so cannot distinguish honest description from disguised legislation; if that's wrong, say why. One complication you should address in answering: the codified third condition is not a property of the body but of the body together with its orbit and its neighbours, so even a purely descriptive intention could not have been reporting a simple intrinsic property that prior usage tracked. Is that a reason to think the definition was a bad description, or a reason to think the antecedent rule was merely silent — and is silence the same thing as indeterminacy?

5. Finally, close the loop. Kripke's skeptical solution is advertized as preserving our ordinary practice while denying that meaning-attributions describe facts. Here a community with full access to its own usage failed to converge, and after the vote the non-convergence persisted for years while textbooks and mnemonics changed anyway. Is that an anomaly for the skeptical solution — does its appeal to community agreement predict convergence where there was none? Or is it a vindication, on the reading that the dispute was always merely verbal? If you take the vindication line, you owe me an account of why the dispute had real institutional stakes, why people were angry, and what the skeptical solution says the disputants were doing when they were arguing. And then the practical question: if the skeptical solution is true, what is the right thing for a body in the IAU's position to do — and does the Ceres precedent count as evidence that the practice already contained a rule for such occasions, or only that it contained a precedent for ad hoc stipulation?

Be explicit about where Kripke's framework is silent rather than forcing it to speak, and where you are extrapolating from it rather than applying it. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble, no restatement of the question, no historiography lesson on the philosophy of language, and keep it under a thousand words.
