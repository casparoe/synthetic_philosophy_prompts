I cover science and ideas for a newspaper, which is somehow how I ended up on the board of the Riverbend Hour: about 200 small businesses and sole traders in my town who keep accounts in hours instead of dollars. You do a job for a member, log it, and spend the hours with anyone else in the network. We keep a cash reserve worth roughly 15% of outstanding balances so members can redeem hours for dollars at ninety cents on the hour, and in practice almost nobody does.

Our director wants to scrap the reserve and the redemption window. In their place: a hard cap on how many hours any one member can sit on, and balances that lose 1% a month after twelve months. Her argument is more or less Bishop Berkeley's. I've been reading his book of questions about the Irish economy from the 1730s — all numbered queries, no answers, written after he took up a bishopric over there — the part where he says gold and silver are not wealth, that a country's real wealth is its land and the industry of its people, and that a note is worth whatever it can actually command, so a bank issuing paper would do Ireland more good than coin sitting in chests. He pushed a national bank on roughly those lines and got nowhere with the Irish parliament. Maybe that's a data point, maybe it isn't.

Our treasurer says the director's plan turns the hour into a gift certificate with extra steps, and that the people who eat it are the ones who can least afford to refuse an hour in the first place. She also points out two live cases: the bakery, which has run up a big credit balance it was counting on converting to dollars for a tax bill, and the plumbing co-op, which just announced it will accept hours at 20% off its listed dollar prices.

I need a one-page decision memo I can circulate before Tuesday's vote. Give me a recommendation in the first two sentences — real, actionable, with a phase-in and a tripwire we could actually check in three months — and then the reasoning, including the strongest version of the treasurer's case rather than a straw version.

Two things I want handled properly, not gestured at. First: does Berkeley's case for credit over coin depend on his immaterialism at all? If nothing material exists, does that give you any reason to think a counter needs no backing — or is the monetary argument independent of the metaphysics, so that a thoroughgoing materialist could sign on to it? Because it matters here: if it's independent, it generalises much further than our director wants, and any token anybody will accept is money, in which case the decay rule is doing all the real work and the Berkeley is decoration. Second: does the argument survive being scaled down from a kingdom with a national bank to 200 members and a spreadsheet? If there's a reason it doesn't, I want to know what it is.

And please say what the plumbing co-op's 20% is — a discount, a shadow exchange rate, or nothing. Don't give me a Berkeley primer; I've read it and the rest of the board hasn't, so write for them. Six hundred words, hard ceiling.
