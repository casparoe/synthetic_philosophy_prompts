im writing a novel and ive hit a scene that i think secretly wants to be a poem rather than prose, so im going to ask for the poem instead.

Setup, briefly: the narrator's father did something genuinely terrible at twenty-two, one afternoon, drunk, and then thirty-eight years of being a careful, kind, quietly religious man who restores antique clocks for a living. His daughter, who's narrating, finally asks him: at that moment, could you have not done it? He says, i dont think i could have — that's what frightens me. She says, then it wasn't you. He says, that's not what im frightened of.

I want the poem she writes afterwards, second person, addressed to him: a Petrarchan sonnet. Octave inside his answer — the causes, the machinery running all the way down, no gap anywhere you could slip a self in; the volta is the moment the question quietly changes from *could you have done otherwise* to *was it yours, were you the kind of man who does that*; and the couplet has to refuse both comforts on offer, hers (that the machinery story sets him free) and his (that if it was all machinery then it wasn't really him). He's a clockmaker, so the mechanism imagery is sitting right their — use it, and then have it break somewhere around the turn; i want the metaphor to fail in the reader's hands rather than get confirmed.

Two places i need you to commit instead of hedging, because i have to decide what this book believes: which of the things people might mean by free will is the one actually worth wanting here, and whether the father has it. And dont let the second question off easy — the poem should know that "was it really yours" collapses back into the same problem as "could you have done otherwise" the moment you ask who made him the man he was, and pick a side anyway. Then give me three or four lines on where the poem cheats: which contested reading it leans on, and the one objection you'd expect from someone who knows this stuff and thinks you've gone soft.
