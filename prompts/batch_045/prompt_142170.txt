I teach a first-year seminar on minds, human and otherwise, and I have decided, possibly against my better judgment, to put Moby-Dick on the syllabus as the one non-philosophical text, on the theory that a novel about a crew arguing over what, if anything, is going on inside a sperm whale is secretly about the hardest case in the distribution question. A student made a point last week that I cannot get out of my head: that Ahab's reading of the whale — deliberate malice, premeditated revenge, a grievance being worked out over years — and the crew's reading of it — a huge dumb animal that strikes by instinct and means nothing by it — are simply the two canonical error-types in the epistemology of other minds, over-attribution and under-attribution, installed in the same ship and set against each other. I think she may be right, and I think she may also be over-reading, and I want a philosopher of mind who also knows nineteenth-century American fiction well to settle it. Before you start writing, spend some time working out which scenes you would actually put in front of eighteen-year-olds; I would rather have a reading that admits where it breaks than a tidy one that quietly cheats, so take the book seriously as a book and not as a container for a thesis.

There is a distinction I want you to use, because I think it is where the novel has something to say that the contemporary argument does not. Call it the difference between the distribution question — which animals have any experience at all — and the character question — what they are conscious of, what their world is made of, whether there is anything it is like to be that whale, whether it has plans, a memory that reaches back, a sense of itself over time, anything that could be called a grievance. My suspicion is that the novel's real contribution is to the second, and that the marker-based approaches people use to settle the first, because they were calibrated on humans and are designed to return a yes-or-no, say almost nothing about the second and quietly help themselves to an answer to it; I would like you to test that suspicion rather than agree with it. Then the epistemic problem proper. Everything we use on each other — inference from similarity of body, behaviour and circumstance — degrades badly when applied to a creature with no hands, no language, a sensory world built on sound and pressure, and a repertoire of behaviour we can only partly read, and I want you to be specific about where the degradation occurs and what, if anything, is left to go on. Two scenes I keep coming back to: the chapter where each crewman in turn reads his own meaning into the gold coin nailed to the mast, and the chapter on the whale's colour, where the argument is about a surface that seems to signify everything and nothing. Does the second of these treat the whale's inscrutability as an epistemic limit on us or as a fact about there being nothing behind it, and does the novel know the difference between those two claims? That question matters more than it sounds, and I want the class to see why.

Two further things, and then you can write. First, Queequeg: the novel spends real effort early on getting us to grant complete interiority to a tattooed cannibal whom Ishmael meets in a strange bed and binds himself to in a mock marriage, and it then declines to do anything comparable for the whale. Is that early thread an argument by analogy — if we can be brought across a difference that large, what is supposed to stop us at this one — or is it instead the novel marking a boundary it has no interest in crossing? I would like you to say which, and to tell me honestly if the honest answer is that the reading fails there and the book simply is not doing what I want it to be doing. Second, a speculative bit I want taken seriously for at least a paragraph: the novel's whales mostly arrive in pods, moving in coordination, and the narrator's accounts of them turning on their pursuers read less like individual animals than like a single thing acting. Is the possibility that the subject of consciousness is not the individual animal at all a live option that belongs on the table when we ask what the distribution of consciousness looks like, or is it a confusion, and if it is a confusion, what kind? Close by saying what the novel adds that the contemporary argument by itself does not, and if you have come round to thinking Moby-Dick is simply the wrong book for this seminar, say so at the very end — but do the reading first.

Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you would write a long and considered email to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, and no exact quotations from the novel, since I need to be able to hand it straight to students who have not all got the same edition — describe the scenes instead. Somewhere between twelve hundred and fifteen hundred words, and where you are being speculative rather than reporting something the text supports, flag it in a phrase.
