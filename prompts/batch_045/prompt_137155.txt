Hey — I need a hand drafting something, and I'm still a bit fried from an argument about it last night. I'm on my university's Dining and Sustainability Advisory Committee (volunteer thing, I got roped in through the grad student union), and we have to vote in three weeks on a proposal from the undergrad sustainability caucus: at the two biggest dining halls, the hot entrée line goes plant-based by default for the whole semester, and if you want the chicken or the beef you add it in the dining app the day before or ask at the station. There's also a pledge wall in the lobby where students sign a board committing to the default, which I think is going to turn out to be the stickiest part. My friend Priya, who's a fourth-year econ PhD and extremely annoying about expected value, spent roughly two hours at the pub telling me the whole thing is moral cosplay. Her core argument is the one philosophers write up as the inefficacy objection: our food service contractor consolidates orders across five campuses and adjusts animal-protein volume in blocks of about 500 pounds, so any given student skipping the chicken lands somewhere inside the rounding band and changes nothing; the chickens were already ordered, already dead, already paid for. When I pushed back she granted that there's some threshold somewhere but said that just relocates the problem — now each meal has maybe a one-in-several-hundred chance of being the one that tips the block, which is basically zero, and if we actually cared we'd stop nudging students and just have the university cut a check to an offset fund instead.

The reason I can't shake it is that our contract has this weird menu-mix clause: if animal-protein orders stay under 12% of meals served for two consecutive semesters, the contractor triggers a renegotiation that changes procurement wholesale — different pricing tier, more plant-based suppliers, the works. So the threshold isn't hypothetical, it's just lumpy and probabilistic, and I genuinely can't tell whether that rescues the argument or just makes the probability problem smaller and weirder. Three more things came up that I want handled rather than ducked. First, leakage: if we make the default plant-based, a chunk of students will just drive to the burger place off Route 9, so our headline reduction number might be fake, and Priya says that's the whole ballgame. Second, our business manager pointed out something that weirdly helps her case — if the university just decides the policy itself, the causal question dissolves entirely, because then it's one institutional act rather than thousands of individually insignificant ones; so maybe the honest version of this is to change the default top-down and stop pretending students are moral agents whose choices matter here. Third, the pledge wall: a bunch of first-years have already signed it, and now some of them are telling their friends that eating meat after signing makes you a free rider on everyone else's restraint, which feels off to me but I can't say exactly why — especially for the students who are on mandatory meal plans, or international students for whom the meat dish is the one that tastes like home, who don't really have an opt-out available.

What I need is the memo itself, not an essay about the philosophy — something I can circulate to the eleven other people on the committee before the meeting, maybe 900 to 1200 words, with a clear bottom line: approve the pilot as written, reject it, or approve with conditions, plus what we'd actually do and by when (I'd like to see a concrete pilot design, one hall versus both, what we measure, and a sunset or review point). The part I care most about is that it takes Priya's strongest version seriously instead of just asserting that individual choices do matter — I want her to be able to read it and feel like the inefficacy point was actually met, even if the recommendation goes against her. Please argue in plain language and don't cite anything; half this committee is facilities staff and a dean, nobody is going to chase down references, and I'm not going to paste a reading list into a campus email. If it helps, assume the vote is genuinely open and I'm genuinely undecided — I'd rather be handed a recommendation I can defend than a summary of the debate.
