Hey — I'm writing a seminar paper on the coherence arguments for expected utility maximization and I've talked myslef into a corner, so I want you to compare two of them for me.

The two I mean: the synchronic sure-loss / Dutch book style argument, where you post fair prices and someone takes both sides of each bet and you lose money no matter what happens; and the diachronic money-pump argument, where you have intransitive or otherwise unstable preferences and someone walks you around a cycle of trades you're each individually willing to make and you end up back where you started minus cash. Everyone writes as if these are two versions of the same point. I'm not convinced, and I want you to say what each one actually needs in order to work, and what each one actually vindicates — probabilism for credence, coherence of preference, expected utility maximization proper, or only some of those.

The worries I have going in: the sure-loss argument seems to need the agent to be willing to bet at her own fair prices on both sides, which is exactly what someone who suspects her own prices are unsettled won't do — and then there's no loss, just unexploited incoherence. The money pump seems to need her to keep turning up for the later trades in the cycle, i.e. some story about whether she plans once and sticks to it or re-decides at each node. So is either argument genuinely about rationality, or are they both just about getting exploited?

Here's the wrinkle I actually care about, and please spend most of your words on it. Call an agent menu-myopic: every choice she will ever face is binary, each one is offered at a different time by a different person in a different framing, and no option ever recurs. Abstractly her preferences cycle — A over B over C over A — but no menu ever contains more than two of them at once, and she never learns that the cycle is there. Is there any coherence argument left that bites on her? Does the synchronic argument do better than the diachronic one against her because it doesn't depend on a sequence, or does it too quietly assume a menu she is never offered? And if nothing bites, is the conclusion that she is not irrational, or that these arguments were never tracking rationality in the first place?

One more detail you should tell me to cut if it's a distraction: make her a monk under a vow of poverty who genuinely does not care about the money. I think that's supposed to show the arguments are merely prudential, but I can't tell whether it's doing real work or whether the same point goes through with any neutral commodity.

Keep it around 600-800 words. I'd also like you to be explicit about your own confidence: which of your claims are safe, which are contested in the literature, and where you're guessing. And push back plainly if I've set something up wrong — if these are really one argument in two costumes, or if menu-myopic turns out to be an incoherent description of an agent, or if the whole framing rests on a mistake, say that instead of politely answering the question I asked.
