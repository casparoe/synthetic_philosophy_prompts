Last night I had a long argument with a friend — she is an anaesthetist at a hospital that has begun trialling one of the bedside consciousness monitors that grew out of integrated information theory, and she spent the whole evening insisting that the interesting part of the theory is not the headline number but the machinery underneath it: that consciousness is supposed to be a matter of a system's intrinsic cause–effect structure rather than of what the system does, that the exclusion postulate crowns exactly one candidate — the maximally irreducible one — as the subject and demotes every proper subset and every overlapping rival to nothing at all, that the quantity and the quality come apart, since Φ is only the amount and the character of the experience is supposed to live in the distinctions and relations you get by unfolding the whole cause–effect structure, and that the theory's own favourite worked contrast is the cerebellum, which has several times as many neurons as the cortex and is supposed to contribute nothing to experience purely because of how it is wired. I pushed back that a quantity defined from the system's own intrinsic perspective cannot, even in principle, be read off from outside the system, and that this is precisely the sort of thing that should never be allowed near a billing code. She conceded the point about billing and then ruined my evening by saying that this was exactly why the whole business was funny. We never really resolved it, and I would like to send her something better than another message.

So: write me a satirical internal memo, about 700 words, from a fictional medical-device company — call it something blandly corporate, with a deadpan motto about measuring experience from the inside — addressed to the hospital ethics committees and risk-management offices that have installed its Φ-meter, and reporting on the problems that have surfaced during rollout. It should be formatted as a numbered set of headings, in the flat, unhurried register of a clinical-engineering document that has been reviewed by lawyers. The comedy has to come from getting the theory right and then taking it seriously in a hospital: because the winner is whichever overlapping complex maximises Φ, the maximal complex in room 4B turns out to include the patient's cortex, the electrode lead and the monitor's own FPGA, so the entity whose experience has to be consented to is a hybrid, and the patient's cortex is merely a proper subset and therefore, strictly, not a subject of experience at all; because regularity can beat usefulness, a lattice of identical simple elements somewhere in the hospital outscores every patient on the ward, which turns a routine generator shutdown into an ethical incident; because the number tracks structure and not function, a firmware refactor that leaves the device's input–output behaviour completely unchanged is nonetheless capable of zeroing or inflating a reading, and the memo's authors are annoyed that anyone would think no functional change is a defence; and because Φ is defined intrinsically, the quantity the device prints can only ever be a heuristic estimate of something no outside observer could in principle check, a fact the regulatory filing handles with a footnote. Give me at least one clause in which the quality of someone's experience comes out wildly wrong — plenty of consciousness, of the wrong kind — and at least one in which a proper subset is told, politely and with reference to the exclusion postulate, that it does not exist.

Two constraints I care about. First, keep it deadpan: the company should never wink, never admit that any of this is absurd, and never break character by appealing to behaviour, function, or what the patient can do — the straight-faced application of the theory to hospital logistics is what should carry the joke, and a reader who knows IIT should recognise that the author has actually understood the commitments being milked rather than inventing a straw version of them (no generic robot-feelings gags, no jokes that would work equally well about any theory of consciousness). Second, the piece should land somewhere: by the end, a reader who has never heard of the theory should have felt the real worry that my friend and I were circling — that a view on which the subject is whichever substrate is maximally irreducible, and the amount is defined from inside that substrate, leaves both the boundaries of the subject and the measurability of the quantity hostage to facts no one outside could settle — without anyone in the document ever stating that worry in so many words.
