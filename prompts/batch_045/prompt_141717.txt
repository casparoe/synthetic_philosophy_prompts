I'm running a 75-minute session of an upper-level decision theory seminar next week, 14 students, poker chips and index cards available, no real money. They've already seen the textbook money-pump argument (the agent who prefers A to B to C to A and gets drained one swap at a time) and most of them think it's a parlor trick that only works on someone who isn't paying attention, so I want a trading game that makes them feel its force instead.

Two structural constraints. First, I don't want to name the three standard responses to a sequential pump — trading myopically each time, reasoning backwards from the end and refusing the swap you'd later regret, or committing to a plan and sticking to it — before my students have lived through one; the rules and payoffs should make them invent one of these on the fly, and half the room should play the exploiter while the other half plays the pumped agent, then we swap roles. Second, I want two wrinkles built into the payoff structure: (1) every swap costs one chip paid to the bank, so an agent whose pairwise gains are small relative to the fee is intransitive but can't actually be drained, and (2) a die roll after each round that ends the game with some fixed probability, so the agent who just refuses everything is forgoing gains that would have been real. Give me the setup, the cards, the trading protocol, the payoffs, a rough timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto parts of the argument — including the uncomfortable conclusion the fee is there to drive home, namely that what the pump really motivates is a cost-relative condition (no cycle profitable at the prevailing spread) rather than plain transitivity. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and which feature of the real dispute the game can't stage at all and I'll just have to say out loud.
