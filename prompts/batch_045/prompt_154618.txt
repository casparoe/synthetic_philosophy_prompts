Exam question

The procreation asymmetry is standardly stated as four claims about the value of a person's existing as against their never existing:

(1) the presence of pain is bad;
(2) the presence of pleasure is good;
(3) the absence of pain is good, even where there is nobody who is thereby benefited;
(4) the absence of pleasure is not bad, unless there is somebody for whom that absence is a deprivation.

(a) Explain why (1)–(4) on their own do not yet yield the conclusion that coming into existence is always a harm, and identify the further premise the argument needs. (5 marks)

(b) Set out the symmetry objection: the complaint that (3) and (4) are unmotivated because no reason has been given for treating the two absences differently. Then give one response available to a defender of the asymmetry. (7 marks)

(c) Now suppose a possible person's life would contain no pain at all, only a modest but positive level of pleasure. Apply (1)–(4) to this case and say whether the argument that coming into existence is always a harm goes through. Be precise about whether the comparison favours existence, favours never existing, or yields no verdict. (8 marks)

Student's answer

The asymmetry holds that pain is bad and its absence good, while pleasure is good but its absence merely not bad. The deprivation clause in (4) is what keeps the view from overreaching: without it one could say of everyone who never exists that she is being deprived of the pleasures she would have enjoyed, so that never existing is a harm to her — which cannot be right, since there is no one there to be harmed.

(a) On their own, (1)–(4) are only a claim about how to value pains and pleasures and their absences. To reach the conclusion that coming into existence is always a harm one needs, in addition, a premise about what human lives are actually like: that every life contains enough pain for the pains to outweigh the pleasures. Without that premise the asymmetry is silent, since in a good life the pleasures might outweigh the pains and then existing would be better than never existing. The missing premise is therefore that every life is on balance bad.

(b) The symmetry objection observes that (3) and (4) differ for no stated reason: if the absence of pain counts as good even where there is no subject to enjoy it, then by parity the absence of pleasure should count as bad even where there is no subject deprived by it. A defender can reply that the asymmetry is not an ad hoc stipulation but is corroborated by our ordinary sense of procreative reasons: we take ourselves to have a reason not to bring into existence a child who would suffer, but no reason to bring into existence a child who would be happy, and that difference in what we owe traces the difference between (3) and (4) exactly.

(c) The argument still goes through in the painless case. Never existing carries the good of the absence of pain, while existing carries only pleasure, and by (4) pleasure does not count as a benefit to the person who has it — the asymmetry discounts pleasures across the board. So there is nothing in the painless life that counts in its favour, whereas the absence of pain on the other side of the comparison counts against it. The painless life is therefore no counterexample: coming into existence remains a harm even there.
