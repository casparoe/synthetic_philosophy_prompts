I have no formal training in philosophy — I write distributed systems for a living — but I have been chewing on Pascal's-mugging-style cases, where someone tells you that unless you hand over five dollars they will, by means you cannot inspect, produce or withhold some astronomically large quantity of suffering, and where multiplying even a minuscule subjective probability by an astronomical payoff seems to tell you to pay. I have ended up holding a position about these cases, and I would like you to tell me what would count against it rather than what counts for it.

The position is what I call the stakes-scaled credibility ceiling. Roughly: the credence I am permitted to assign to a hypothesis is capped by my demonstrated track record at telling true from false claims whose consequences sit in the same order-of-magnitude band as the stakes the hypothesis posits. Nobody has any verified track record at discriminating claims about 10^30 lives, so my ceiling for such claims is very near zero, and the expected value collapses — without my having to place any ceiling on how much I value lives, and without my having to say that probabilities below some fixed epsilon are impossible. The ceiling is meant to be evidence-responsive rather than dogmatic: a genuine, well-audited track record at that magnitude would raise it. That is what I take to distinguish it from the two standard moves I know of, namely putting an upper bound on utility and simply saying that in fact we have overwhelming ordinary empirical reason to disbelieve the mugger.

Tell me what would have to be true for this to be wrong, and rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. In particular I want you to press on the following, which are the places I suspect it is exposed. First, the audited-oracle case: imagine a world with a long, publicly audited record in which a measurable fraction of such claimants did in fact deliver what they promised, so that my track record in the relevant band is no longer empty. If the ceiling then rises, my view looks like it was only ever ordinary Bayesian updating with an unfamiliar prior, and collapses into the second standard move; if it does not rise, it is straightforwardly dogmatic. I want to know whether that dilemma is real or whether there is a third way. Second, the comparison class: a great deal of what I do at work — spending on failure modes with probabilities far below anything anyone has observed, justified by component-level models and accelerated testing rather than by observed system-level failures — involves stakes outside any verified track record too. Does my ceiling condemn all of that, and if not, what is doing the real work? Third, the machinery of bands: where do the boundaries fall, does credence jump discontinuously as claimed stakes change slightly, and what would fix the function's shape other than my desire to get the intuitive answer? Fourth, the many-claimant and sequential versions: a thousand independent muggers, or a million declined muggers after which nothing bad has happened — does refusing all of them follow from my view, and does the ceiling rise or fall as that evidence accumulates?

Say clearly which of these a committed defender would concede as counting against the view and which they would dismiss as misunderstanding it, and give me your own assessment of how exposed the view actually is. Finally, tell me honestly whether the ceiling has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that is a problem for a principle of this kind as opposed to a mere heuristic — I would find it most persuasive if you could show me a case where the rule, applied consistently, gives advice I would reject in my own engineering work. Please write in continuous prose, the way you would write an email: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. I do not want citations, papers, or reading lists — I can look those up myself, and I am after the argument rather than the literature.
