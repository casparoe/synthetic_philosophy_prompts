Hi — I'm the registrar at a regional museum, and I sit on the three-person committee that gets asked identity questions: is the thing in our store the same object as the one on an 1902 loan receipt, does a repatriation claim cover this carving or only the copy that was made after the original burned, does the insurance policy pay for "the object" when something like ninety per cent of its material was replaced in a 1970s restoration. We get maybe twenty of these a year, the director signs off on a one-page note, and the other two members are a curator and a lawyer with no philosophy at all and about an hour per case, so I need a procedure rather than a theory, and it has to be usable by people who have never heard the words I'm about to use: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision back to us because the issue is really about what we care about rather than about what is true.

The wrinkle I keep hitting is that these aren't obviously the same question — same-for-the-purposes-of-paying-a-claim, same-for-the-purposes-of-giving-it-back, and same-for-the-purposes-of-the-catalogue number seem to come apart, and I suspect that's not just lawyers being slippery — so tell me whether the procedure should answer one question with a purpose built into it, or several questions in sequence, and what goes wrong if we pretend there is just one. Please also build in the cases that break tidy rules: an object destroyed and remade from its own material, an object that survives while all of its material is replaced bit by bit, one thing that gets cut into two, and two fragments joined into something we now treat as one.

Run it on three of ours: a marble torso smashed in transit and reassembled from the original pieces with new arms and a new base; a ceremonial mask whose original was destroyed in a fire in 1961 and replaced by a copy the community has used in ceremony ever since, with fragments of the original turning up in a private collection last year; and a bronze that was melted down and recast from the same metal in the nineteenth century. I'd guess at least one of these comes out in a way I'll hate, so please don't soften it — say which one and why the procedure forces that. Also tell me what would change your mind about the order of the questions, and where you think the whole thing is most likely to go wrong when my colleagues actually try to use it. And write it as continuous prose, no headings, bullets, bold or tables, because it's going into an email to the director and she'll just forward it as is.
