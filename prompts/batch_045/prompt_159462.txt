The question (one of four; students had a week, 800-word limit):

Question 3. "Multiple realizability is the key premise: it refutes the identity theory, and it establishes functionalism." Discuss. A good answer will do most of the following: (i) state the multiple realizability argument against the type-identity theory, distinguishing type-identity from token-identity and saying which one the argument targets and why; (ii) distinguish the multiple realizability thesis from the far stronger thesis sometimes called universal realizability — that given a suitably chosen machine table, almost any physical system, a wall or a beer can, realizes any functional state — and say whether the multiple realizability argument requires or merely invites that stronger thesis; (iii) distinguish role functionalism from filler (realizer) functionalism, and say what, if anything, the argument shows about how to choose between them; (iv) consider whether the type-identity theorist can escape by identifying a mental state with the disjunction of its realizers, and give the strongest objection to that move; (v) assess the two qualia-based objections associated with Block — the inverted-spectrum argument and the absent-qualia argument — and say whether, if they work, they refute functionalism as such or only some version of it.

Student's answer:

Functionalism is best introduced as a response to a difficulty for the identity theory. The identity theory comes in two strengths: type-identity, on which each mental type is identical with some physical type (pain = C-fibre firing, say), and token-identity, on which each mental event is identical with some physical event, without any claim that the types line up. The multiple realizability (MR) argument, associated with Putnam and developed later by Fodor, is an argument against the first of these only. If pain can be realized by a wide variety of structurally very different physical states — in a human, in an octopus, in a silicon robot — then there is no single physical type with which pain is coextensive, and type-identity is false. Token-identity is untouched: every individual pain is still some physical event or other. So MR refutes one version of the identity theory and leaves another standing. It is worth noting, though, that MR is itself a claim about what is physically possible, so the type-identity theorist has room to resist it empirically, and her natural fallback is to relativize the identity claims to a species or population: pain is C-fibre firing in humans, something else again in Martians.

MR should be distinguished from the much stronger claim that a mental state could be realized by anything — that with a suitably chosen machine table, a wall or a beer can instantiates the functional organization of a mind. Call this universal realizability (UR). The MR argument does not require UR: it needs only that one mental type has more than one possible physical realizer, not that every physical system realizes every mental state. But UR is a live worry for the functionalist all the same, and she owes us some account of what constrains the class of admissible realizers. A purely abstract characterization of the role looks as though it can be satisfied by systems that intuitively have no mental life at all, so the theory needs a principle that separates the cases.

The second half of the quotation is where the real work is. MR shows that pain is not identical with any single physical kind, and from this it follows that the functionalist must be a role functionalist: pain is the second-order property of having some state or other that occupies the pain role. The alternative, filler or realizer functionalism, on which pain is whatever physical state occupies the role, is simply the type-identity theory in a new vocabulary, and it falls with it.

Nor does the identity theorist escape MR by disjoining. Suppose pain is identified with P1 ∨ P2 ∨ ... ∨ Pn. The problem is not that disjunctions are not real properties; it is that disjunctive predicates are not projectible. They support no counterfactuals and figure in no laws, and the causal powers of a heterogeneous disjunction are at best the intersection of the causal powers of the disjuncts, which is too thin to do the explaining psychology actually does.

The most serious objection to functionalism is the qualitative one. Block's inverted-spectrum argument runs as follows. Take the entire population of China, organized by radio so that the nation as a whole instantiates the same machine table as you do. The nation is functionally identical to you, and yet it plainly has no mind: nobody thinks that wiring a billion people together in that way brings a new subject of experience into existence. So there can be a system functionally indistinguishable from a human being that has no qualitative states at all, and no functional characterization is sufficient for consciousness. If that is right, functionalism is false: it is committed to a sufficiency claim it cannot make good.

I conclude that MR does real work against type-identity, and that it also pushes us towards role functionalism; but role functionalism in turn is exposed to the qualia objection, and there is no obvious way to meet it.
