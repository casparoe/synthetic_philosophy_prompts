I am a novelist, and for the past two terms I have been auditing — not enrolled, and increasingly out of my depth — a graduate seminar in formal epistemology at the university where I teach one module a year on adaptation. The reason I went is that the narrator of the book I am currently drafting is a paintings conservator, and a good deal of her inner life consists of revising her opinions on the basis of things she has only half taken in; I wanted her reasoning to be defensible rather than merely atmospheric. The seminar has now pushed me into writing down my own position on learning from uncertain evidence, and I would like it audited before I put any of it into her mouth, since I have a suspicion that I am trading on something I cannot have.

Roughly where I have landed: (1) credences should be probabilities at each time, and there are genuine diachronic norms — an agent may not simply re-form her opinions wholesale; (2) the correct rule for a shift on a partition whose members are not made certain is Jeffrey conditionalisation, with the rigidity of the conditional probabilities on the partition doing all the work; (3) it should make no normative difference in which temporal order two such shifts are taken into account — a rule that delivers a different final state depending on the path taken looks to me like a defective rule; (4) the case that did most to convince me of (2) is one I invented for the novel and then came to believe in, though I never ran the numbers on it until this week, and when I did they came out lower than I expected: my conservator is working on a fresco under sodium-vapour lighting, useless for colour. She starts at 0.5 on the drapery being ochre rather than raw sienna; the light is such that she would be about 0.95 likely to be struck by the patch as ochre if it is ochre and about 0.25 if it is sienna, so she begins at 0.6 that it looks ochre. What moves her over the next hour is not the light but the pigment and brushwork in the surrounding passages — evidence bearing on ochre directly, which taken on its own would put her at about 0.86. Since how she expects the patch to look depends on what she takes it to be, that same reasoning also carries her credence that it looks ochre up to about 0.85. The rule, as I read it, takes 0.85 as the input, holds P(ochre | looks ochre) and P(ochre | does not look ochre) fixed at their old values of roughly 0.79 and 0.06, and returns about 0.68, which is not the verdict I want. I cannot tell whether the trouble is with the case, with (2), or with my reading of the rule; (5) I also hold that it matters, normatively, whether a change in credence is produced by evidence or merely caused — by a drug, a mood, a bump on the head — and that no rule of updating applies to the latter; and (6) I have no wish to give up the claim that when evidence does arrive with certainty, plain conditionalisation on it is mandatory.

What I would like is the audit rather than a tutorial: go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look it, ranked by severity rather than all flagged equally, and for each real tension say precisely what would have to be given up and what that would cost me — including whether the cheapest repair is to restrict the rule, abandon one of my commitments, or reconceive the case. Please also flag any tension I have not noticed. Answer in British English, in no more than 450 words, as a ranked list with one line on what must give for each item, then a closing line naming the pairs that dissolve on inspection; no preamble, and no bibliography or reading suggestions.
