I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background, and my team has been going in circles about "tool AI" vs "agent AI" for months — half of us think it's a real engineering distinction, half think it's marketing. I wrote down some criteria in a design doc, but they're mine, not from anywhere, and I can't tell weather they're the right ones:

1. Authorization granularity: you authorize a tool per call; you authorize an agent per objective, once, and then it acts on its own.
2. Closed action space: with a tool you can enumerate up front the things it's allowed to do.
3. Reversibility: undoing a tool's mistake is cheaper than the mistake; an agent can do things you can't walk back.
4. Objective-holder: an agent pursues a goal it wasn't handed this turn; a tool doesn't have one.
5. Online updating: acting changes what the system will do later, so you're no longer the one setting its behavior.

Take Microsoft's Tay and run it through these. As I remember it: launched on Twitter in March 2016 as a chatty bot that learned from its interactions, pulled within about 16 hours after ~96,000 tweets of racist and sexist output, a lot of it via a "repeat after me" capability, and Microsoft's explanation was a "coordinated attack by a subset of people" that "exploited a vulnerability in Tay." Later replaced by another bot called Zo. If I have a detail wrong, say so once and move on — but flag any conclusion that hangs on a fact you're not sure about.

I know the takeaway everyone repeats (don't let a bot learn from Twitter trolls, and "Tay was just a mirror"). Skip it. What I want is the mapping: which of my five criteria actually separate the two categories here, which collapse or contradict each other, and which one does the real work. Then run it the other direction — what Tay shows about the distinction itself. Is tool-vs-agent a property of the model, or of the deployment and who holds the kill switch? Does Microsoft's "vulnerability" framing quietly assume one answer? And be honest where the distinction is silent: Tay had no long-horizon planning, no physical effects, near-zero stakes, so maybe it's a terrible probe for this and I should be looking at something else — if so say what episode would actually test it.
