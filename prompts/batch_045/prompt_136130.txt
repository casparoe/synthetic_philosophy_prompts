I'm a retired physiologist — most of my working life was spent on instrument calibration, oddly enough, measuring things that didn't particularly want to be measured — and in the last couple of years I've drifted into philosophy through a reading group at the local library. We're working through Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments and I've got a bee in my bonnet that the others think is a misreading. As I understand him, the basic mechanism is projective rather than genuinely other-directed: since we have no direct experience of what anyone else feels, the only way we can form any idea of it is by imagining what we ourselves would feel in their situation — we put ourselves in their place, but what comes back is a reading of our own makeup, not theirs. Smith even notices something like this at the level of the body itself: people of delicate fibres report an itching or unease in the corresponding part of their own bodies at the sight of a beggar's sores, while men of the most robust make feel a soreness in their own eyes when they look at sore ones. That variance is exactly what a calibration man sees — the instrument's own construction leaking into the reading — and it's what got me going.

The impartial spectator, on my reading, is meant to cancel one specific source of error: the fact that you're the person affected, the one who gains or loses or has been insulted. It's detached and well-informed, but nothing in the set-up suggests it is differently *constituted* than we are. So my worry, which I've started calling the shared-constitution problem, is that it can strip out positional bias but not constitutional bias — it can't correct for the fact that we're creatures who feel things at just this intensity, for just this duration, and in just this register. Two cases I keep chewing on. First: someone born without the ability to feel pain, who after a nasty injury isn't distressed at all — every spectator runs the case through their own nervous system, gets agonising pain, and delivers the verdict that great distress is called for, which is simply the wrong answer for that person. Second: someone whose temperament runs hot, for whom a level of resentment or grief the rest of us would call excessive is in fact the ordinary, proportionate response of their constitution, and who gets told by the spectator to tone it down. The spectator over-reads in one case and under-reads in the other, and can't tell the two failures apart, because the one thing it can't do is take up a constitution other than our own.

Please argue for the stronger version of this, i.e. my position: that the impartial spectator is constitutionally parochial in just this way, that the parochiality is not something Smith could repair by making the spectator better informed, more impartial, or more idealised, because it's inherited from the projective mechanism of sympathy that the spectator is built out of — and, as the pay-off, that Smith therefore shouldn't be read as an ancestor of ideal-observer theories at all, since his spectator isn't approximating a standpoint-independent moral fact but cancelling one very particular kind of noise. I'd like a real argument rather than a summary of what Smith thought, and I'd be especially obliged if you'd take on the two replies my reading group keeps making: (a) that Smith never claims the spectator detects what the other person actually feels, only what it is proper for anyone in that situation to feel, so the misfit in my cases isn't a cost but a consequence of what the theory is about; and (b) that a properly "well-informed" spectator would simply know about the congenital condition or the hot temperament and adjust. Say, too, if you think the whole thing rests on a misreading — I'd rather be told. Please write in British English, in plain prose (technical terms are fine, but don't assume I know the secondary literature, and there's no need for references or reading lists — I've no library access worth the name), and take the two cases as definately mine rather than Smith's.
