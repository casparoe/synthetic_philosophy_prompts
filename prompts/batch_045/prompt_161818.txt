I run a small reading group with a few friends from my decision-theory course, and we have been working through paradoxes of expected utility; last week we got stuck on the Pasadena paradox, where the expected value of the game is not infinite but undefined. We meet above a coffee shop, and for our next session I would like to close with a short poem we can read aloud in about a minute, something that makes the structure of the problem do poetic work rather than just using the paradox as a decorative title.

Please write a villanelle in the voice of someone who has just tried to price the Pasadena game and cannot. Use the standard setup: a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads; if the first head arrives on toss n, the payoff is (-1)^{n-1} * 2^n / n dollars, and because the probability of that outcome is 1/2^n, the expected-contribution series is 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ... , the alternating harmonic series, which in the natural order of tosses sums to ln 2. The two refrains of the villanelle should be the two rival totals that the same series of contributions yields under two different orderings. The first refrain should express the natural-order sum: taking the payoffs in the order the tosses arrive—win on the first toss, loss on the second, win on the third, and so on—gives the alternating harmonic series and hence ln 2. The second refrain should express a rearranged sum: taking the very same contributions two positive ones for every negative one—1 + 1/3 - 1/2 + 1/5 + 1/7 - 1/4 + ... —converges to 3/2 ln 2. Let the refrains take turns exactly as the villanelle form requires, so that the reader feels the two orderings passing each other; in the final quatrain let the two refrains stand as the last two lines, side by side and unreconciled. Let the two new lines of that final quatrain say exactly what has to go: not the coin, not the payoffs, and not the series, but the inference from a conditionally convergent series to a well-defined expected value, since the positive contributions (1 + 1/3 + 1/5 + ...) and the negative contributions (1/2 + 1/4 + 1/6 + ...) each diverge, so the expectation is undefined and every ordering is merely a rearrangement. Someone who knows the paradox should be able to tell which refrain is which and why they end up side by side; someone who does not should still get a poem about a wager, a coin, and a sum that will not sit still.

Please make the philosophy accurate enough that a decision theorist would not wince, and let the verse bear the weight rather than leaning on prose explanation. Begin your reply with the poem itself, with no preface, no title, and no commentary before or after it: start with the first line of the villanelle and then give the poem, exactly as the form dictates. Do not name any authors or papers.
