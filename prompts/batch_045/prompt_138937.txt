I'm an undergraduate in a seminar on historical reasoning in international politics, and I'm revising a paper on why named historical precedents — "a Munich," "another Vietnam," "a Thucydides trap" — mislead. I've been trying to split the failure into two stages, and I want a number on which stage does most of the damage.

Some terminology so the question is precise. Call the first stage case-selection: the crisis gets coded as one precedent rather than another, or as no precedent at all. Call the second stage mechanism-selection: once a label is in play, it silently stipulates which causal mechanism is operative — in the Munich case, that concessions feed an appetite and raise the adversary's expectations of further concessions; in the Vietnam case, that intervention triggers domestic consolidation around the adversary's leadership and generates sunk-cost dynamics on the intervening side; in the rising-power case, that the challenge is driven by the shift in relative capabilities rather than by anything either side chooses to do. My draft argues that the second stage is where most of the epistemic damage occurs, and that this is why the remedy we keep being offered in the seminar — widen the menu, consider more precedents, appoint a devil's advocate for the road-not-taken analogy — fails: it treats case-selection as the bottleneck when the bottleneck is downstream of it.

The proposition I want a credence on:

P: When a named precedent is invoked in a crisis deliberation, the distortion of decision-makers' expectations about the adversary runs primarily through mechanism-selection rather than through case-selection. Consequently, holding the mechanism fixed while swapping the precedent moves the group's expectations less than holding the precedent fixed while substituting a different mechanism.

I'll say what counts as resolution. Suppose we could run both variations on a group that has already settled on something like "this is Munich, so concessions will be met with escalated demands." In variation one, keep the demand-feeding mechanism but re-describe the case using a different precedent, or no precedent at all; in variation two, keep the Munich description but substitute a different mechanism, such as the domestic-consolidation one. Score how far each variation shifts the group's expectations about the adversary's next move. P is affirmed if the second variation moves things more, in most such cases.

My credence is 0.6. A friend in the seminar, who thinks the whole problem is that the live menu of precedents is tiny and politically loaded, puts it at 0.85: on her view, case-selection is downstream of whichever precedent is rhetorically available, and talk of mechanisms is mostly post-hoc dressing for a conclusion already reached. I'd like your number, and more usefully the decomposition behind it.

Please decompose along roughly these lines, adding terms if you think I've left something out:

- whether mechanism-selection is even a separable stage in real deliberations — i.e., whether groups actually settle on a mechanism, however implicitly, rather than jumping straight from label to prescription;
- conditional on its being separable, how much of the predictive work the mechanism does relative to the case-description;
- whether my two variations could come apart cleanly enough to be scored at all, or whether the test collapses because "the mechanism" and "the precedent" cannot be varied independently — on most accounts the precedent just is the package of mechanism plus case;
- whether the small-menu and political-availability story my friend prefers is a genuine competitor to mine or really a story about why a particular mechanism gets selected;
- whether the whole framing is measuring the wrong thing because precedents in deliberation are doing legitimation and coalition-coordination work — giving a ministry or an alliance a shared focal point — rather than predictive work, in which case "epistemic distortion" is the wrong metric and P may be undefinable rather than false.
