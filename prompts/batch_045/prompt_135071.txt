I work in applied machine learning — I'm fluent in the usual vocabulary (hypothesis classes, empirical risk minimisation, VC dimension and other capacity measures, no-free-lunch results, priors over hypotheses, covariate and concept shift, out-of-distribution generalisation, shortcut learning and the invariant-prediction literature) — and I've been attending a small reading group with some philosophers of science where we got badly stuck on Goodman's new riddle of induction. As I understood it: define a predicate so that a thing observed before some future time is grue just in case it is green, and a thing observed after that time is grue just in case it is blue. Every emerald examined so far has been green and therefore grue, so the evidence appears to confirm "all emeralds are green" and "all emeralds are grue" equally well, yet the two generalisations come apart after the critical time. One side of the room kept saying the answer is that green is projectible and grue is not, because green is better entrenched in our language; I kept saying that this sounds exactly like a prior over hypotheses, and then we spent an hour going in circles about whose vocabulary was doing the begging. I'd like to settle it by making the translation explicit rather than by arguing about it. (I'm writing this on a long flight with no connectivity, so please don't give me references, page numbers, or reading suggestions — I can't check them, and I'd rather have the argument than the apparatus.)

So: reconstruct the new riddle inside my vocabulary as faithfully as you can, and treat the translation itself as the deliverable. What kind of object is grue — a hypothesis in the original class, a hypothesis in a widened class that takes the time index as an extra input, a feature-map or representation choice, or something my vocabulary has no slot for? What does "the evidence confirms both equally" correspond to: underdetermination by a finite sample, the inability of ERM to separate hypotheses with identical training loss, a no-free-lunch statement, or something stronger? What would "projectible" have to mean — a restriction on the hypothesis class, an inductive bias, a prior, a property of the representation, a regularity assumption about the data-generating process? And what work is "entrenchment" actually doing, in terms of those same options?

Then two variants I made up, which I think are where the real content is, and which I want handled in the same terms. First, grue-by-provenance: let the disjunction switch not on calendar time but on which instrument, lab, or site produced the observation, so that the predicate means green as measured on apparatus A and blue as measured on apparatus B. That looks to me identical in structure to a spurious correlation or shortcut feature, of the sort the invariant-prediction literature treats by demanding a predictor that is stable across environments. Second, grue-by-index: let the switch depend on the position of the observation in the sample itself (the first n observations versus the rest), which looks to me like a hypothesis that has leaked the train/test split. If those two diagnoses are right, then the original riddle is the case where the "environments" have collapsed into one and the split has not been handed to us, and my question is what, if anything, is left of Goodman's problem once it is put that way: does the machine-learning framing quietly assume a fixed set of environments or a test distribution that does exactly the work entrenchment was supposed to do, and is it therefore answering an easier question than the one Goodman asked?

Finally, the residue report, which I care about most. Say which claims translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all. In particular: is the difference between green and grue best located inside a single predicting agent, as a prior or bias, or is it essentially a fact about a community — a convention, or a coordination equilibrium among agents who must share predicates in order to pool predictions — and if the latter, does my framework have any way to say that? Is there a reading on which there is genuinely no fact of the matter about which predicate is projectible, so that the machine-learning version with its held-out distribution is simply changing the subject? And tell me honestly whether my instinct that entrenchment is just a prior is correct, or whether it is a translation that quietly imports an assumption and thereby conceals the thing the philosophers thought was hard.
