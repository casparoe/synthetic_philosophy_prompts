I am writing a novel, and there is a stretch of technical argument in it that I would like to get right rather than merely plausible-sounding.

The setting is a movement-disorders clinic that fits patients with an implanted stimulator carrying a mechanistic model of that patient's own gait-initiation circuit — the device holds an estimate of which components are constitutively involved in producing a step, checks that estimate continuously against what the patient's body actually does, and raises what the engineers call a drift flag when the model's predictions go bad. The novel turns on a disagreement about what a drift flag means. My engineer argues that the flagged episodes are the same mechanism running under shifted parameters: the disease has moved the gains, not the wiring. My neurologist argues that the patient has recruited a different mechanism — a compensatory route that yields a superficially similar step by a different path. A third character, a philosopher hired as a consultant, proposes to settle the matter by intervening: deliver a small electrical probe to a candidate component and see whether the behaviour changes. If it changes, the component is part of the mechanism; if it does not, it is not, and one of the two clinicians has a different mechanism on their hands. I would like that consultant's reasoning to be something a real philosopher of science could say in good faith.

As background I have been reading a science writer's account of the mechanistic philosophy of science, which summarizes the position in the following terms:

"A mechanism, on this view, is nothing but a collection of parts doing things, arranged so that their doings together produce some change: ion channels opening and closing are a mechanism for the action potential. What makes something the same mechanism is the job it does, not what it is made of — the very same mechanism can be built out of quite different hardware in different species, and one and the same piece of hardware can be several different mechanisms depending on which output you happen to be tracking. This is where mechanistic explanation gets into trouble. If mechanism identity floats free of the parts, then two laboratories studying 'the same' mechanism may be studying different things entirely, and there is no fact of the matter about whether a system that has begun to behave differently is the old mechanism malfunctioning or a new mechanism quietly taking over. The interventionist criterion is what settles such questions: wiggle a candidate part and watch the output. If the output wiggles, the part belongs to the mechanism; if it doesn't, it doesn't, and what you have in front of you is a different mechanism."

I cannot tell how much of this to put in my consultant's mouth. I would like the no-fact-of-the-matter claim to be true, because the novel is better if the engineer and the neurologist are arguing about something their evidence cannot settle — but I do not want to build three chapters on a misreading, and I suspect the last two sentences are doing less work than the essay implies.

Would you go through the passage more or less sentence by sentence and tell me what someone who works on mechanisms in the philosophy of science would accept as accurate, what they would call a simplification, and what they would reject outright? In particular: is mechanism identity really fixed by the phenomenon rather than by the components and their organization, or is that one position among several? Does the wiggle test really decide whether two episodes involve the same mechanism, or is it a test for something narrower — and if narrower, what exactly? Is the question of malfunction versus recruitment genuinely unsettleable, or merely hard? And if the intervention my consultant proposes cannot bear the weight I have put on it, what could a character honestly offer — what further evidence, or what argument about levels, normal conditions, or robustness — that would genuinely favour one reading over the other? A line or two of dialogue for each of the three characters that someone in the field could read without wincing would be very welcome.

One further request about the answer itself: please keep clear, throughout, which of your claims are settled in the literature and which are live disputes, and do not present a contested position as the consensus. The novel depends on reasonable people disagreeing, so I need to know where the disagreement actually lies rather than being told that one side has won it.
