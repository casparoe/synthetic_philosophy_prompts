Please argue for the following position about the principle of procreative beneficence — the view that, when prospective parents can choose among the possible children they might have, they morally ought to select the one whose life is expected to go best. The position is that the principle is self-undermining once universalized, in roughly the way an arms race is.

Develop the argument in three steps. First, argue that much of the expected-welfare gain that makes the principle attractive comes from traits whose value is at least partly positional — height, cognitive ability, competitive temperament, resistance to locally prevalent disease — so that what a selected child gains depends on how others turn out. Second, argue that if nearly all parents select, the resulting population-level shift cancels the comparative advantage that justified each individual selection, while each parent's reason to keep selecting remains as strong as ever, since a parent who unilaterally opts out foreseeably disadvantages their own child. Third, rebut the strongest available reply: that the principle should be restricted to non-positional components of welfare, such as freedom from suffering or longevity. Argue that this restriction either collapses the principle into the uncontroversial duty to avoid serious disease, forfeiting whatever made it distinctive, or else smuggles positional goods back in under another description.

Conclude by stating precisely what a defender of procreative beneficence would have to give up if the argument succeeds.
