At a growth seminar yesterday someone killed a criticism of Malthusian reasoning with "Malthus was right, he was just early." The context: a paper arguing that higher yields and the fall in fertility that comes with rising income only pushed the constraint outward, so long-run limits reasoning is still fine, and we shouldn't treat the non-appearance of mass famine as a refutation of the mechanism. I want to take the line apart instead of nodding along. I'm a PhD student in economic history and I keep hitting versions of this move and never know what exactly is being claimed.

So: what are the distinct precise things "right but early" could mean here? I can see three rough families myself — the trend is real and still running but its timescale was misjudged by a couple of centuries; the mechanism is real but dormant until trigger conditions that simply haven't been met yet; the model got the structure and the sign right but the parameters wrong, so the implied timing was off — but I want a proper menu with verdicts: which reading is close to trivial, which is substantive and defensible, which is empty or collapses into "wrong," and which one people probably mean when they say it. Two sub-questions I care about: is "right" doing part-whole work (right about diminishing returns to labour on a fixed resource base, not about the whole predictive package)? And is "right" secretly a counterfactual claim — the model would have predicted well had the escape not happened — which sounds like it makes the original prediction untestable by construction?

The bit I actually need is a test. My idea is that the respectable version has to come with a dating clause: a specification, checkable independently of the outcome it's meant to predict, of the conditions under which the check bites. Is that the right test, how does each reading fare against it, and which readings would genuinely support my colleague's use of the line (i.e., license not updating much on two centuries of non-famine)? One wrinkle: on the reverse-causal story, where population pressure itself induces the innovation that lifts the ceiling — Boserup-flavoured, necessity as the mother of invention — does "early" even make sense, or does that story change the mechanism rather than delay it?
