For the last four years I have belonged to a small reading group of retired scientists — geologists, a chemist, an ecologist, two physicians — that works through philosophical topics with more enthusiasm than discipline. Our most recent meeting, on whether the way things actually went was in some sense going to happen anyway, went badly, and not because anyone was being dense. Everyone kept using the one phrase, 'path dependence', to mean at least four different things, and every time someone offered an example the discussion slid into an argument about whether the example was a good example instead of about what was in dispute. So what I want is something that classifies the disagreement before evaluating it: a decision tree (or, if that shape will not hold, a taxonomy in which every branch is labelled with the question that splits it from its siblings) that starts a person at 'I think the past matters here' and walks them to a named position — built, as far as possible, from the logical space first and the list of names second.

Three distinctions matter to me, and I would like the tree organised around them rather than around a roster of -isms. First: whether history is being said to shape the *route* or the *destination*. A process can be exquisitely sensitive to where it started and still wash out into the same end state; in my own field we were taught a word, 'chreod', for a developmental course a system can be nudged onto and then cannot leave, and I suspect a good deal of what gets called lock-in is really that, and a good deal of the rest is not. Second: whether the complaint is about inefficiency at all, and if so worse *for whom* and *relative to what is still reachable* — whether the alternatives are genuinely available or only nominally so, and whether the locked-in arrangement has since been made better by everything built around it. Third: whether a rerun from roughly the same starting conditions would converge on the same outcome, which I take to be a different claim from either of the first two, and one that people smuggle in as if it followed from them. There is also a distinction I have not seen anyone draw and that I would like a node for: whether the path shapes only *which* option gets selected from a fixed menu, or whether it shapes the *menu itself* — the available alternatives shrinking, mutating, or new ones being created as the process goes along. Please use these cases, distributed across the branches rather than bolted on at the end: the standard typewriter/computer keyboard layout (I gather the two readings of that story are themselves a battleground, so let the tree say which reading leads where), the railway gauge or the side of the road one drives on, a lake or reef that flips into an alternative stable state and will not flip back at the original nutrient level, and the 'replay the tape of life' case. Six branch questions at most, eight to ten leaves. Every node must be phrased as a question a person can answer without knowing where it leads — nothing as coarse as 'does history matter?'. Every leaf should carry the name of the position where a name exists, 'unnamed' where it does not, and one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment. Where a combination of answers is available but seems to have nobody in it, make it an empty leaf and tell me why you think it is empty: incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unoccupied.

I am working from an armchair and cannot chase references, so please do not give me a reading list, a bibliography, or citations — I want the structure, not the literature. In this order, under these headings: 'Orientation' (one short paragraph on what the instrument is for), 'The tree' (the main item, 600–800 words; each node with its question, its branches, and one line on what turns on the answer), 'Leaves' (about 400 words: name, one-line commitment, one line on the awkward consequence), 'Unoccupied combinations' (about 250 words, with your diagnosis for each), 'Where the instrument fails' (about 250 words: pairs of positions the tree cannot separate, and why), and 'Five questions for the group' (the questions themselves, no commentary).
