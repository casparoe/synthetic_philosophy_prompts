I'm a novelist, and I run a week-long residential workshop for about fourteen fiction writers, mostly literary with a few working in genre. I have one 90-minute slot and nothing but index cards, envelopes, and a timer. I want an activity that makes them feel, in their own practice rather than as a thesis, the old logical puzzle about whether a claim about tomorrow's sea battle is already either true or false — specifically the way it bites when you are the one who gets to decide what tomorrow holds.

The angle I want to exploit is something I've started calling "back-shadowing": in fiction, a line written early — a scrap of dialogue, a stray image, a throwaway prediction — can be made true or false by a chapter you write three months later, so at the moment of writing there is apparently nothing in the world that settles it, and yet once the draft is closed it seems to have been true all along. I want the group to collide with that themselves. Give me the setup, the roles, the rules, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments onto the structure of the puzzle. Have them work on scenes from their own works-in-progress so the stakes are small but not fake. No lecture up front, and they shouldn't hear the standard name for the problem until the debrief. Please keep it from sliding into a free-will seminar: what I want them to hit is that the trouble concerns the truth of the sentence, not causation and not who knows what.

Also tell me what will predictably go the first time I run it — I'd guess writers will make the prediction self-fulfilling or self-defeating to dodge the issue, and will psychologize it into a character's fear — and how to handle each. Be frank about which part of the puzzle the activity cannot stage and that I will have to supply in my own words.

Finally: mark where you're extrapolating, and if the analogy between back-shadowing and the sea battle is broken in some way that would leave the group with a false picture, say that plainly rather than building a graceful session around it.
