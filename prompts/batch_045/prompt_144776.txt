Our Plutarch group has its last session tomorow and I'd like to close it with a poem rather than a summary, because honestly the thing we keep circling isn't a thesis and I cant see how to say it in prose.

Some background, in case it's useful: we've spent the semester on the Parallel Lives, and the argument that won't settle is whether reading a life is supposed to change you or only inform you. The prologue to Alexander says outright that he's writing lives, not histories, and that a jest or a stray remark shows a man's character better than the most famous siege or the bloodiest battle; in the Lucullus he uses the simile of a painter drawing a beautiful face that has a flaw in it — neither leave it out nor belabour it — and says the lapses are to be read as a shortfall of some particular virtue rather than as the working of vice. Most of the pairs close with a formal comparison of the two men. All of that we agree on. What we don't agree on is whether any of it is a training programme or just the kind of thing you wrote in a prologue.

So: a sestina. Six sestets plus a three-line envoi, end-words rotating properly. The words are LIVES, HISTORY, FACE, JEST, LACK, PAIR — your choice of order, but each should be doing a job: LIVES and HISTORY are his own distinction; JEST is the small thing that's supposed to carry the whole character; FACE is the painter's simile; LACK is the blemish he admits to and then files under virtue; PAIR is the comparison at the end. The reason I want a sestina and not just some free-verse thing is that the form forces you back to the same six words, each time in a new position, which is what reading for character is supposed to be like: you return to the same life and find something different because you have changed. Make that visible — the last time FACE turns up it shouldn't mean what it meant the first time.

Two more things. First: one of our group, Marta, has been reading a chapter each evening and checking her day against it, more or less as an examen. She says it works on her. She also says she's started remembering the day in the shape of the chapter, tidying it toward the life she just read. I've been calling that the emulator's regress — to learn from a life you already need enough character to tell a virtue from its flattering portrait, but the lives are supposed to be what gives you that. Give it its moment, and give Marta her due; it isn't a stupid practice. Second: don't let the poem tip into either a sermon about imitation or a shrug about how it's all constructed anyway. Our sessions ended with everyone half-convinced by both, and I'd rather it ended there.

Then a short note after the poem, plain prose, a couple of paragraphs like you were writing me an email: no headings, no bullets, no bold, no tables, and nothing markdown-y around the poem either — no italic title, no footnotes, no stage directions, just the lines. In the note I want to know where the poem leans on a reading that people who actually work on this argue about, rather than one they accept, and where it takes liberties with Plutarch. Please keep the settled stuff separate from the contested stuff: what the prologues plainly say and what the collection plainly does is one matter; whether the Lives are really a programme of moral formation, how much of the anecdotal material is Plutarch's own embroidery or him copying a source he never checked, and whether his Middle-Platonic commitments give him any answer to the emulator's regress or whether he simply never notices it, are all live questions. Don't write as if your take on those is what everyone thinks.
