Background, stated in full so that nothing here depends on outside knowledge. Futarchy is Robin Hanson's proposal for government by prediction market: elected representatives do not vote on policies directly, but instead agree on a quantitative measure of national welfare; for each candidate policy, a conditional prediction market then estimates what that measure would be expected to read if the policy were adopted versus if it were not; and the policy with the higher market-expected welfare is the one enacted. The idea is often summarized by a slogan that doubles as the title of the paper that introduced it: vote on values, but bet on beliefs.

Where I met it and what work it was being made to do. I am in a small online working group that advises a mid-sized decentralized organization on its governance; we are considering replacing part of our token-voting with conditional prediction markets, and last week we walked through the futarchy scheme. I raised an objection, and one member — an engineer who has read around the idea a fair bit — replied with the slogan as though it closed the matter: "That's exactly the point: vote on values, bet on beliefs." I am convinced the reply was too quick, but I cannot say precisely why. My objection had three parts, which I will name so we can refer to them:

(1) The denomination problem. A value vote cannot stop at "we value welfare." It must name an observable quantity whose future reading settles the market — a number, measurable by a specified date, by a procedure that cannot itself be gamed or relitigated. Welfare is not such a quantity. So either the vote settles on a crude proxy (output, life expectancy, retention, some index), in which case a great deal of contested value judgment has silently migrated into the thing that was supposed to be the pure-belief half of the scheme, or it defers to some committee that interprets the measure when the market resolves, in which case discretionary politics reappears at exactly the point where it decides everything.

(2) Horizon truncation. Markets must resolve, and the consequences of policy do not. Whatever metric is chosen is read at a resolution date; the market therefore prices the metric's reading at that date, not welfare over the affected lifetimes, and a policy that buys a good reading by borrowing from later is priced as a success.

(3) The reflexivity loop. In the scheme as I understand it, the value vote is held in the shadow of expectations about what the markets will say, and the choice of metric substantially determines which policies win. Choosing a metric is therefore itself a wager on how the world works and on who benefits — and voters who anticipate this will propose and vote for metrics favorable to the policies they already favor. Values and beliefs appear to be fixed together, in a loop, rather than in the clean sequence the slogan suggests.
