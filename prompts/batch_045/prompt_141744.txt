I sit on the novel-entity review committee at my university — we look at anything a lab wants to build that isn't a standard model organism, so engineered cell lines, stripped-down synthetic genomes, cell-free systems, self-assembling junk the bioengineers dream up. We meet every couple of weeks, eight to twelve proposals a term, and one of the boxes we have to tick is whether the thing is a living organism, because that decides whether it needs the full containment permit and the lawyer's sign-off or can just be handled on the open bench. Our charter never defines living organism, so we've been doing it case by case and it's become embarrassing: last month we ruled that a bacterium with a heavily reduced synthetic genome was alive and that a self-replicating RNA in a cell-free extract was not, and I don't think any of us could explain why those two came out differently. I took one philosophy course years ago and have this vague memory of a metabolism-versus-replication split and something about autopoiesis, but I don't trust my recollection and I can't tell which of those ideas is actually load-bearing and which is decoration. So: I don't want a survey of definitions or anything you'd have to look up — no reading list, no citations, just your own reasoning. What I want is a decision procedure I can photocopy and hand to people: an ordered list of questions to run through in a meeting, what counts as a good-enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to throw up its hands and say this is a values call rather than a question of fact, because our biosafety officer signs off on whatever we write and he will not accept a judgement dressed up as a test.

Two constraints from our setting make this less tidy than the textbook version. First, we're only permitted to reason from what we can actually observe in a thirty-day window with the assays we have: we can't certify a capacity for Darwinian evolution or survival across centuries, only what happened while we watched, so the procedure has to say how to use short-run observations as evidence for long-run capacities without quietly pretending the proxy is the thing its supposed to be measuring. Second, the committee is two engineers, an ethicist, a microbiologist and me, and nobody except maybe the ethicist has any philosophy training, so any distinction you keep has to be statable in plain language — but please don't flatten out the distinctions that are doing real work, I'd rather keep an awkward step than lose it. One of our engineers also keeps insisting that any definition requiring reproduction rules out sterile individuals (a mule, a worker ant, a cell line that can't divide), and I genuinely can't tell whether that's a decisive objection or a debating trick, so the procedure needs a place for it.

Please run the finished procedure on five of our actual cases: the self-replicating RNA in cell-free extract (copies itself if we keep feeding it nucleotides, no membrane, no metabolism of its own); an aggregate of frog cells that swims around, heals itself when cut, and eventually dies out without ever reproducing; a freeze-dried bacterial stock sitting in our minus-eighty freezer — I have a feeling a decent procedure will rule that one not alive, and I'd definately hate that, because then a chunk of how we store things has no principled basis, so tell me straight if that's where it lands; a transmissible cancer cell line that gets passaged between animals and evolves as it goes; and a self-replicating 3D-printed machine that assembles copies of itself from parts we supply, which the engineers submitted half as a joke and half to make a point. Where the procedure can't separate two of these, say so rather than forcing a verdict. Then tell me where the whole thing will misfire — the kind of submission it will handle badly — and for each part of what you give me, say how confident you are in it and single out the one step you're least sure about.
