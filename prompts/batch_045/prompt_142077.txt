I'm in a theory of computation course this term (second-year CS) and the way we handle the Church–Turing thesis keeps bugging me. Me and my roommate, who's a physics major, went back and forth about it for like two hours after a guest lecture on analog computing and got nowhere, so I figured I'd ask people who think about this stuff properly.

Here's what I keep noticing about how the subject is actually done, as opposed to how it's introduced. First: the course defines "computable" by picking Turing machines, then shows that a handful of other setups — lambda calculus, register machines, whatever — pick out exactly the same class, and presents that convergence as *evidence* for the thesis, while also saying flat out that the thesis can't be proved, because the informal side ("whatever a human or a machine can do by following a rule") isn't a mathematical object you can prove things about. Second: every time someone floats a machine that would outrun a Turing machine, the reaction is set-aside rather than refutation. The two examples we kept circling: a gadget that does its first step in a second, its second in half a second, its third in a quarter, so it gets through infinitely many steps in under a minute; and a device that measures some continuous physical quantity to infinite precision and reads the answer off an infinite decimal expansion. Both get waved off as "not a physically realisable process" — but my roommate's point is that physics idealises like that all the time (point masses, frictionless planes, infinitely extended fields, real-valued quantities generally), so the move can't just be "it's unphysical, therefore no". Third: our complexity unit helped itself to "efficient = polynomial time on a Turing machine" plus the assumption that any sane physical computing device is at worst polynomially slower, and the crypto notes then lean on exactly that when they say some protocol is safe because breaking it would take too long. But it's also treated as common knowledge that quantum algorithms really do put pressure on that efficiency claim, and that it could have turned out false — whereas the basic thesis gets treated as not the kind of thing that could turn out false at all.

So what does that whole bundle of practices presuppose about what sort of claim the Church–Turing thesis is? Is the implicit view that it's an empirical claim about the physical world — in which case why is all the evidence for it mathematical, i.e. the agreement of several formalisms, which are all just mathematics and so tell you nothing about matter? Or that it's a definition, or an analysis of what we mean by "following a rule" or "effective procedure" — in which case why does anyone bother marshalling the convergence argument, and what would a hypercomputer even be a counterexample *to*? Or something in between? My roommate's guess, which I have no way to evaluate, is that it works like the definition of a physical quantity — temperature, energy, that sort of thing — where the meaning gets fixed by the role the term plays in a whole network of claims, so it's neither provable nor straightforwardly falsifiable, but also not arbitrary, and can come apart from any one of the claims that pin it down. Is that what the practice is quietly committed to, or is it too strong a story, or does the practice really only need something much weaker and just happen to sit comfortably with several different stories at once? I'd also like to know where exactly the practice is genuinely underdetermined rather than committed. And whatever you think it presupposes: what would concretely have to change in how computer science is done — how uncomputability results get stated, how hypercomputer proposals are received, how "efficient" gets defined, whether cryptographers should be more nervous — if we swapped that story for one of the rivals? Oh, and is the asymmetry I mentioned actually defensible, the base thesis being treated as untouchable while the efficiency version is treated as an open empirical question, or is that just historical accident?
