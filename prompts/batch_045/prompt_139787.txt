I'm helping to spend a smallish health budget — real money, a real vote — and our committee keeps splitting between programmes that buy a modest, well-measured improvement in how a lot of people feel and programmes that prevent or treat a handful of conditions (trigeminal neuralgia, cluster headache, that sort of thing) where patients describe the pain as unbearable. Before I write the short paper I owe everyone, I want the spec rather than the theory: what would a view about the moral weight of suffering as against happiness have to get right to be good enough to actually move money with?

The family of views I keep half-convincing myself of is what I'll call lexical threshold negative utilitarianism, and I'll define it so we don't talk past each other: put pleasures and pains on a single intensity scale; below some threshold T of suffering the two trade off against each other in the ordinary aggregative way; at or above T suffering takes priority in the strong sense that no quantity of sub-threshold goods — not any number of people with mildly nicer lives — compensates for a single instance of it, which is the intuition behind the dust-specks-versus-torture case people always wheel out. What I want from you is the desiderata list such a view has to meet, each with the case or consideration behind it, and each marked non-negotiable or camp-preference. Here are the ones I already think are on it, which I want you to organise, sharpen, and tell me which are bogus: where T sits and why that isn't just gerrymandered to save the cases I like; the cliff problem, given that intensity looks vague, so a sorites across a long series of neighbouring cases either forces a sharp line somewhere arbitrary or breaks transitivity of better-than; what the unit of the threshold is — a single episode, a whole life, a person's lifetime distribution, a population; whether the trigger is really intensity or something like duration, unpredictability, or the subject's own retrospective attitude (the marathon, childbirth, the ascetic who recieves it as meaningful); which pain is even being counted when lived-through pain, remembered pain and anticipated pain come apart, which from what I've read they do, since remembered intensity seems to track the peak and the end rather than the total; and what to do when intensity can't be read off behaviour and the exposures are enormous but uncertain, as with fish or insects.

I also want my own pet idea stress-tested rather than indulged: that pleasure has a compressed top end — the very best moments don't differ in intensity nearly as much as the very worst ones do — so maybe the lexical structure is really a derived fact about the shapes of the two distributions and the threshold is an artefact the theory should dispense with entirely. If it helps to pin the requirements down, here is the case the committee actually argued about: one patient with pain at nine-tenths of T lasting forty years, one patient with pain at eleven-tenths of T lasting ninety seconds, and money for only one of them. Tell me which of the requirements are just prejudices of one side, since I can drop anything that only matters if you already want the view to be elegant; tell me which single requirement does the most work in knocking out candidate views; and if some subset of the requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, say so and give me the argument, because if the list is itself a result I'd rather find out now — my guess is that lexical priority plus a vague threshold plus a genuine ordering is the unstable triple, but I want to see it done properly. You can rate the obvious candidates against the list in a line each if that helps, but keep the weight on the criteria.
