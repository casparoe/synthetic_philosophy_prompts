Hey — I run a small fortnightly reading group, six to eight people, mostly philosophers plus two cognitive-science grad students, and we're spending one 90-minute session on illusionism about consciousness. I want a decision tree handout, not a paper — something I can draw on the whiteboard as we go.

Main constraint: none of the branch questions may use the words qualia, phenomenal, illusion, eliminativism, representationalism, or any other -ism. The whole point is that someone should be able to fill the thing in before they know what anybody calls their view, so every node has to be a question about a case or a claim, phrased in plain words. Save the labels for the leaves.

Build it from logical space, not from a list of names: give me 8–9 branch questions first (yes/no or three-way), say which combinations of answers are actually available, and only then drop the real views into the cells. 10–12 leaves, each with a one-sentence note on its most awkward commitment.

Distinctions I definately want as branch nodes:

Where the misrepresentation is supposed to happen. I think people conflate this constantly. Either the perceptual systems themselves deliver qualities — the redness is painted into the delivered scene and that's where the error is — or perception delivers something thinner and the error is entirely downstream, in the self-monitoring/reporting system that announces that one is thereby aware of a quality. I've been calling these 'paint-first' and 'press-release-first' but use better names if you have them. Every illusionist leaf should say which one it is.

Whether the seeming is something the subject genuinely undergoes, or just something the subject is disposed to judge and say. Call it 'warm' vs 'cold'. A view that says there is literally nothing it is like to be under the impression that there is something it is like should not be able to sit in the same cell as one that says the impression itself is a real state with a character of its own, and I want the tree to force that choice rather than let people mumble.

Whether the story is meant to cover dreams, imagery, and background bodily sensation, or only perceptual experience in the task-guided, action-guiding cases.

Whether the target is the properties themselves or our concept of them / our way of talking — somebody can think the properties are exactly as advertised and still think our concept is a mess, and somebody can think the properties are non-existent and still want to keep the discourse.

And what the view owes us on the meta-problem: whatever it says, it has to explain why nearly everyone, when asked, insists on the qualities.

Three cases must appear as nodes, please: a vivid dream of a red room, no stimulus, nothing to do with action; a long-term meditation practitioner who after years of training reports that her experience no longer seems to have any qualitative character at all while her colour discrimination is unchanged; and someone who mid-task, when asked, says they weren't experiencing anything whatsoever, then on being shown the stimulus insists of course they saw it the whole while.

Leaves that have to be findable: Chalmers-flavoured anti-materialist realism; Russellian monism; the mysterian option; a first-order representationalist view that keeps the properties but has an error theory about introspection; a higher-order or self-representational view; the attention-schema style view; standard Frankish-style illusionism, split by where the error is; the Dennett-ish view where there isn't even really an illusion; full eliminativism; fictionalism or instrumentalism about the talk; and the quietist who thinks the whole dispute is verbal. 'Unnamed' is fine for anything you can't place.

Two or three unoccupied cells with a sentence each on why nobody is there — incoherent, just unattractive, or merely unexplored. One I especially want diagnosed: the person who says the seeming itself has real qualitative character but that there are no qualitative properties. Is that cell available in the tree or does it collapse?

And flag the pairs your tree can't separate. I suspect the attention-schema view and Frankish-style illusionism collapse, and maybe the Dennett-ish view and eliminativism — tell me if any further question splits them or if they're genuinely the same leaf.

One page, boxes and arrows, no bibliography and no citations: half the room has read different things and I want it to work for someone who's read none of it.
