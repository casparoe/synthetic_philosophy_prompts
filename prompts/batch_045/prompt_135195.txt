Bit of an odd one. I'm a backend engineer (no philosophy past one freshman survey course I mostly slept through) and for about a year now I've been helping a friend build a forecasting app on the side: people assign probabilities to yes/no questions — will some chip ship by Q3, will a given country's central bank cut rates, whatever — and when the question resolves we score them by squared distance, lower is better: put 70% on something that happens and you lose 0.09, put 70% on something that doesn't and you lose 0.49, and someone who parks at 50% on everything loses 0.25 per question no matter what. I'm told this is the standard "Brier" thing. The part I actually want help with is that we bolted three things on top of it and I can't tell if they're all one philosophy or three fighting philosophies. (1) A "conviction" tweak: if at least a third of your open forecasts sit outside the 40–60 band, your losses get multiplied by 0.95, because the product team didn't want people farming a decent score by shrugging at everything. (2) An "update credit": every time you revise a forecast in the direction of the eventual outcome you get 0.02 knocked off, capped at 0.10 per question, because we wanted to reward changing your mind when evidence comes in — so someone who went 30 → 60 → 90 on something that happened beats someone who said 90 on day one and never touched it. (3) For conjunctive questions we split them up: you forecast A, B and C separately, the app multiplies them and treats the product as your forecast for "A and B and C", and the leaderboard ranks you by your average loss across sub-questions with every question weighted the same, so a hundred easy trivia items count a hundred times as much as one genuinely hard one.

A friend who did a philosophy minor looked at this, muttered something like "accuracy first" and "proper scoring rule" and then rolled her eyes at parts (1) and (2), and I couldn't tell which parts she was endorsing. So: what does each bolt-on presuppose about what a credence is and what it's for? I'd like it as a menu I can take back to the team — for each piece, the implicit view of belief/credence/rationality it only really makes sense under, versus views it merely fits fine with, and whether the pieces are even consistent with each other (does crediting the *path* of revision, or rewarding distance-from-50%, commit you to something accuracy-at-a-time can't say?). Please cover (1) and (2) for sure and the others as you think matters. Also: does any of this presuppose that the number someone types is their real felt confidence, as opposed to just their best move given the scoring, and does the answer change? Don't just hand me the textbook label and stop — I want the reconstruction, and I want to know where our scheme is underdetermined, i.e. where two quite different theories of belief would both happily license what we built. And if you think one of these bolt-ons is straightforwardly a bad idea on its own terms, say so and say why. End with one last line beginning "Verdict:" containing a single short phrase (under fifteen words) saying what the scoring scheme as a whole is best read as.
