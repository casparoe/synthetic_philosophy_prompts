Hey — quick one, and it's a slightly weird ask. I'm putting together the end-of-term packet for my grad seminar on archives, power and the politics of evidence, and I want to close with something that makes the point by being funny instead of being another thirty pages of brooding prose about the state and the record. So here's what I need from you.

Write a mock government white paper, about 700 words, from something like the Bureau of Archival Reparation — invent the office, invent a director, invent the letterhead. The setting is a country that has decided, officially, that the great injustice of its past is that most of the people who lived in it were never recorded: no deeds, no letters, no depositions, no trial transcripts, just a line in somebody else's ledger. The Bureau's mandate is to repair that absence.

Here's the twist I want the whole thing built on, and it's not one you'll have seen before, so think it through rather than reaching for a stock take on "who gets to be a historical actor." The Bureau has concluded that the cheapest way to repair an archival silence is to manufacture the missing records. Not forgeries in the crude sense — call them counterfactual acquisitions: documents generated by a model trained on the records that did survive, calibrated to what the paper calls the Anticipated Absence Ratio, its metric for how many documents a person of a given station, trade and region would have produced if the machinery of record-keeping had ever bothered with them. These files are then accessioned, assigned a provenance, shelved in original order, and opened to researchers, with a small flag in the finding aid — buried on page forty — marking them as retrospective accessions. The Bureau's defence, made entirely straight-faced, is that no one is harmed: the genuine records of the powerful are untouched, the gaps are now populated, and historians finally get the representative sample they've been demanding for years.

I want the comedy to come from the archival reasoning being right, not from general bureacracy-is-absurd stuff. Get appraisal, provenance, original order, permanent value, deaccessioning, sampling, the fact that preservation is itself a selection, and the fact that a finding aid is an argument about what matters. The target is the assumption that being in the archive is the same thing as mattering — that visibility just is recognition, and that a gap in the record is a debt you discharge by filling it in. Don't let anyone say the word "reductio"; by the end a reader should have worked out for themselves that the Bureau has solved the political problem by wrecking the epistemic one, since future historians will now cite the fabrications, and the unrecorded will have been recorded twice over — once as an absence, once as a guess. And I dont want it stated outright, but there's a real question sitting under it: is getting into the archive a way of acquiring power, or a way of acquiring exposure? The people who turn up most often in the record are the people the state was watching.

Ground rules: deadpan throughout, no winking, no "ironically," the Bureau believes every syllable. The single place you can break character is a short sidebar, 80 words max, in the voice of a junior archivist who has started to notice something — and even there keep it understated, no speechifying. Nothing that would require a library to check: no citations, no footnotes, no bibliography, no real authors or real works named anywhere in the piece, invented sources only. You can gesture at something like the Haitian Revolution if you want a concrete example of a silence, but don't hang the piece on it.

Send it as plain text, numbered sections, with the sidebar marked off somehow. Thanks!
