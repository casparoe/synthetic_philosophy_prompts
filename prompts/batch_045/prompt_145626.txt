I'm a science journalist — I cover neuroscience and AI adjacent stuff — and somehow that got me one of the two public-member seats on the national research council's Emerging Technologies panel. Next meeting is in nine days and I've been volunteered to draft the decision memo on one application, which is probably a mistake but here we are. I need something I can circulate to the other six members by Monday: a real recommendation they can amend or vote down, with the reasoning, and with a fair hearing for the people I expect to lose the argument.

The application: a group wants four years of funding to build a large-scale emulation of a mouse thalamocortical loop. The thing they're insistent about is that this isn't input-output mimicry. Their claim is that they can preserve the fine-grained counterfactual structure — for any unit, what downstream activity would have been had this unit been silent or fired differently — and they've got validation results on recorded data from about forty mice that are already covered under an approved protocol. No new animals. The experimental design has three conditions:

- the "live" run, where the network is causally connected internally in the usual way;
- a "ghost" condition, where the exact same trajectory of activity is imposed on the same network from outside, so the internal units still light up in the same sequence but the internal causal links aren't doing any work — the counterfactual dependencies are gone;
- a "cascade" condition, where two hundred instantiations start from a single initialization, differ only by numerical noise, and diverge from there, each run at about two hundred times real time, so forty minutes of simulated activity goes by in maybe twelve seconds of wall clock.

The lab is split about what any of this means. The senior PI's position, stated fairly bluntly in the application, is that it's software and the moral ledger is entirely about the forty mice. The postdoc who designed the thing got a rider inserted proposing that the live condition be treated as a possibly sentient preparation, with a pause rule — if a run enters a learned activity pattern the lab has been calling stress-like, operators pause and either perturb or terminate — and a cap on concurrent instantiations. There's also a corporate co-funder, an animal-testing-alternatives company, that wants to license the platform and market it as a welfare-free replacement for animal work. Its engineers would be the ones actually applying any pause rule, and it has asked the lab to issue a public statement that the system has no plausible sentience. I don't need to tell you which way that incentive points.

The jurisdiction mess: our rules say any protocol with more than a negligible chance of using a sentient preparation goes to the animal care committee, but the animal care committee has already told us in writing that it has no remit over software and will return anything we send it. So the gap is ours to fill.

What I want from the memo, roughly 1200 words max:

1. A concrete bottom line — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with a staged structure and actual dates, not "further discussion is needed." If it's conditional, say what the gate is before they're allowed to touch the cascade condition.
2. Which of the three conditions, if any, we should treat as possibly containing a subject, and why. My own instinct is that the ghost condition is where the actual argument is, but I can't get the rest of the panel to even see why it's a different question from the live one.
3. How to count, since our whole ethics framework is built on counting. Two hundred near-identical instantiations that diverge only by noise: is that one preparation or two hundred? Does it matter that they've been running at two hundred times speed — does the moral weight, if there is any, track the number of internal state transitions or the elapsed time, and what do we say to the applicants who point out that on one reading a slow run is worse and on another it's the same?
4. Who gets to make the sentience call. I don't think it can be the lab and it definately can't be the partner, but I need a proposal that survives the objection that we're inventing a new review layer out of nothing.
5. The strongest version of the position I expect to lose. Which is: the moral weight here is in the forty mice and the millions of actual animals in actual labs; declaring a simulation a possible patient hands a ready-made talking point to model-welfare people, invites bad-faith pressure on every future AI grant, and commits a committee with no expertise to policing software; a pause rule applied to a bodiless network with no brainstem and no arousal system is theatre, and theatre that will end up in the partner's marketing copy. I want that argued properly, not gestured at, because two of my colleagues believe it.

And two practical things. No citations, no suggested reading, no "the literature is divided" — I can't follow up on them and the panel won't read them, so give me the reasoning itself. And flag anywhere you're assuming a fact I haven't given you, since I may end up writing some version of this up and I'd rather not find out in print that I invented something.
