I teach a graduate seminar on the later Wittgenstein, and a colleague in cognitive science has asked me to comment on a chapter she is contributing to an interdisciplinary volume on concepts — psychologists and philosophers writing in turn about what a concept is. She has roughly a page for Wittgenstein, no footnotes, and an audience that will never open the Investigations. Her draft says this:

Wittgenstein's central claim is that meaning is use — a word means nothing more than what speakers in fact do with it, so there is no private mental definition that usage has to answer to. His argument for this is the private language argument: a language whose words named only the speaker's own sensations would be impossible, because the speaker could never tell a correct application from an incorrect one, and where there is no such distinction there is no rule being followed at all. Applied to concepts, this gives the family-resemblance theory. Most of our concepts have no necessary and sufficient conditions; the things we call by one name are linked only by overlapping similarities, in a chain in which each member resembles the next though nothing is shared by all. Membership therefore comes in degrees — a robin is a better example of a bird than a penguin — and for borderline cases there is no fact of the matter beyond how typical the case seems. Since nothing more fixes a concept's application than our practice does, the only question left to ask about a category is whether it is useful. That is why psychology gave up the search for definitions in favour of measuring typicality, and why diagnostic kinds like those in the DSM should be understood as family-resemblance categories, about which there is nothing further to be right or wrong about than their clinical usefulness.

She is a careful scholar and this is better than most capsule accounts I am sent, but I think at least two or three of its moves are carrying weight they cannot carry, and I would rather send her a precise list than a vague complaint. My own suspicion is that the characterization of family resemblance is not Wittgenstein's at all but prototype theory read back into him — the neighbour-to-neighbour chain, and above all the graded-membership claim — and that the private language argument is being made to underwrite a thesis about indeterminacy that it does not support. But I have not followed the secondary literature closely in years, and I do not want to insist on a reading that specialists now regard as outdated.

What I need is an audit I can turn into two paragraphs of comment by the end of the week.

First, take the passage more or less sentence by sentence and tell me what a Wittgenstein specialist would accept as a fair compression, what is a defensible simplification for her purposes, and what is a misrepresentation. For each misrepresentation, name the conflation precisely: is she collapsing a remark about the grammar of our concepts into an empirical thesis about mental representation, or sliding from "there is no essence to be found" to "there is no fact of the matter," or from "no criterion of correctness" to "no correctness," or from use to actual usage statistics?

Second, is the chain reading of family resemblance Wittgenstein's? My own recollection is that the relevant image is one of similarities overlapping and criss-crossing rather than a series in which each item resembles its neighbour, and that the force of the remarks is methodological — a refusal to let a philosophical picture of what a concept must be dictate what we find — rather than a claim about how categories are structured in the mind. Correct me if I have this backwards, and tell me what in the text resists her version.

Third, there is an objection I have never known how to size up, and I want to know whether it is fair to raise against a one-page compression or merely pedantic. Anything resembles anything else in countless respects, and some chain of similarities can be run between any two objects whatever; so unless something constrains which similarities are the relevant ones, "linked by overlapping similarities" seems to predict that every collection of things whatsoever forms a family. Is there a Wittgensteinian answer to that, or is the constraint supposed to come from the practice rather than from the formula?

Fourth, and most important for me: does the closing inference to the DSM survive if the premises are corrected? Even granting that a concept has no necessary and sufficient conditions and that its point lies in our practice, I do not see how it follows that the only question about a diagnostic category is whether it is useful, or that there is nothing to be right or wrong about beyond clinical utility. Tell me whether the inference fails, and if it fails, whether it fails because of a mistake she makes or because the corrected Wittgenstein simply has no bearing on the issue.

Finally, two cases I use in my seminar, which I would like her account to be able to handle. The first is a term fixed by an explicit written rule — say the definition of a conflict of interest in our university's faculty handbook, which is stipulative and relatively sharp. The second is a cluster concept of the sort she has in mind, say punishment or narrative. How would a corrected version of her account treat the first kind of case, and is the account as she has stated it even compatible with such cases existing, or does it commit her to saying that the handbook definition is illusory?

What I will send her is a short list of what to delete, what to qualify with a clause, and what to leave alone. So give me that list, in the order of how much damage each item does to the argument she goes on to build.
