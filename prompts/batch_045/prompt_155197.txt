Bit of a niche one, and I'd rather you pick a side and argue it properly than give me both sides and sit on the fence.

Setup: someone's preferences over three holiday cottages cycle — they'd rather have Alder than Birch, Birch than Cedar, and Cedar than Alder — and each of those pairwise preferences is worth about £50 to them. An estate agent who's worked this out can pump them: starting from Alder, offer them Cedar for £40, then Birch for another £40, then Alder again for another £40, and round again. Every single trade is one they prefer at the time, and they end each lap back where they started, £120 down.

Now the variant I keep chewing on; call it the batched pump. Same cycle, except the agent has to present the whole lap up front as a single package: sign here, hand over £120 over the next hour, finish up with the cottage you already had. Any cyclic agent refuses that. The pump only turns when the offers arrive one at a time and the agent takes each on its own.

So argue this: the money pump argument against intransitive preferences is circular. All its force comes from insisting that a rational agent takes offers one at a time, when evaluating the temporally extended sequence as a single object is more or less what diachronic rationality is. Any view that can even state the pump already hands the agent the machinery to refuse it — backward induction, or settling on a plan and sticking to it — so the argument can't then be used to establish the assumption it secretly depends on.

Two things you can't skate past. First, the obvious one: the agent who refuses the first trade is stuck holding Alder whilst preferring Cedar, and stuck again when Cedar is re-offered, and so on indefinitely — they've swapped being fleeced for being frozen out of an improvement they'd rather have. That objection is definately the one I'd press hardest, so argue that it's a cost of having a predator in the neighbourhood rather than a defect in the preferences, ideally by analogy with some way of exploiting a perfectly transitive agent. Second: does anything you've said change if the estate agent only charges a penny a trade rather than £40, so that sitting tight costs you more per lap than going round once does?

Give me 600–800 words, British spelling, and don't bother with citations or a reading list — I want the argument itself, not who thought of it first.
