Our hospital ethics committee is rewriting its guidance on end-of-life decisions, and I've been asked to put my own position in writing first, before reading anyone else's, so the group can take shots at it. Roughly where I've landed:

(1) Killing and letting die aren't different in kind; the doing/allowing distinction does no moral work of its own. Where it seems to matter that's derivative — it's evidence about what the agent was after, it tracks whether the patient consented or had a right violated, or it has good institutional effects like trust in doctors and clear legal lines.

(2) Roles create real positive duties (parent and child, doctor and patient, lifeguard and swimmer), so letting someone die can be every bit as wrong as killing them, and it's the duty doing the work, not the omission.

(3) In the clinic I still want to say there's a genuine difference between taking a patient off a ventilator at their request and giving them a lethal injection at the same request, holding fixed that they're competent, dying, and dead within minutes either way. And I want to keep saying it in the awkward variant where we sedate them to unconsciousness first and then stop feeding and hydrating them — formally an omission, but it feels definately like the injection.

(4) I also think large doses of opioids to control suffering are fine even when the doctor foresees they'll shorten life, while a drug given for the purpose of ending life isn't, and I lean on intending vs. merely foreseeing to explain the difference.

(5) And I think it's much worse when my own doctor fails to save me than when a stranger does, which looks like smuggling the distinction back in.

Does this hold together? Which pairs actually pull against each other and how badly, which ones only look like they do, and for each real conflict what exactly has to give? Rank them by severity instead of flagging everything, and tell me what single change would fix the most.
