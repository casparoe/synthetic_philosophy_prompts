I write fiction — three novels, and I teach an occasional workshop — and there's a thing I keep running into in revision that I don't have a word for. I'd like one, if there is one, but honestly what I want more than a word is the shape of it.

The basic move first, so you see what I'm not asking about. When a scene goes dead, the fix is nearly always to cut the sentence where the character sizes up her situation and put in the body instead. Out goes "she understood that he'd meant it as an insult"; in goes her hand going back to the deadbolt a second time, the way she can't get her breath all the way down. Everybody says this. Show don't tell, on the nose, whatever. Two other things I keep noticing are not that.

One: the body outlives the thought. My character finds out at breakfast that the threat was a prank, a dumb joke, nobody was ever in the parking garage. She believes it completely — ask her and she'll say she's safe. At lunch she's still walking to her car with her keys between her knuckles. Nobody in workshop ever flags this as a inconsistency, nobody says wait, she knows better now. And they don't read the belief as the truth and the keys as a leftover habit; they read the keys as the more honest of the two. The fear and the judgment come apart and the fear wins the room.

Two, and this is the one I actually care about: order. Take a character who believes the dog is harmless and whose hand still goes to the latch. Written one way — "She knew the dog was harmless. Her hand went to the latch anyway" — readers come back describing the fear as an afterimage, already over, and a little sentimental, the way you'd describe something that's finished. Reverse the two sentences — "Her hand went to the latch. She knew the dog was harmless" — and readers describe her as frightened right now, and they keep describing her that way after they've read the second sentence. Same two facts, same character, same paragraph, opposite verdict on whether the fear is still going on. And it works in first person too, where she's the one doing the reporting and has full access to what she believes.

A writer friend said the first one "recalcitrant emotion," she may have the word slightly wrong, and I definately don't think it covers the second one, because nothing in my case is about a belief she failed to update — she updates fine, on page, out loud — it's the sequence of the sentences that decides whether the thing reads as present or past. So:

1. Does the second thing have a name of its own, in philosophy of emotion or anywhere else that deals with how emotions get attributed? Or is it the first thing in a different costume, and I'm mistaking one mechanism for two?
2. Whether or not there's a name, where does it stop? Does it still work in second person, in past perfect, across a chapter break, when the two sentences are split between two characters, when the feeling sentence is narration and the belief sentence is dialogue?
3. Which of the big accounts of what an emotion is — a kind of judgment, a perceptual experience of value, or a felt bodily episode with a cause — actually predicts the order effect? I want to know whether this is evidence about emotion or only evidence about sentence-level reading, because if it's the second I've been drawing conclusions about my characters for years off something that's really just about prose.

Don't hand me a label and call it done, and please no reading lists or citations — I can't get at journals and I don't want to chase references. What I want is the carving: what exactly this is, what it isn't, which of the nearest candidates genuinely fits and why the others only look like they do.

Also: tell me how confident you are in each of the claims you make, and single out the one you're least sure of. And American English, plainly written, if you wouldn't mind.
