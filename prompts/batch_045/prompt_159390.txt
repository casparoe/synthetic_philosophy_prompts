I volunteer one Saturday a month at a tiny local history archive, mostly helping box up correspondence, and I've got a running disagreement with my brother (he does stats for a living and thinks I'm overthinking all of this) about how much a clue should move you when you didn't observe the clue cleanly. Concretely: there's one letter in the box and the question is whether it's in the vicar's hand or his clerk's — call the hypothesis V, and say I start at 0.5. I've been reading about the update rule where you don't condition on what you saw, you only directly move your probabilities over a partition of the things you might have observed, and then hold fixed how likely the hypothesis is conditional on each cell, so that your new confidence in V is just P(V | looks like V) × (new probability you assign to "looks like V") plus P(V | doesn't) × (new probability you assign to "doesn't"). I mostly like that rule, but I keep hitting cases where it seems to give the right number for the wrong reason, or where two cases that look identical to the rule feel completely different to me. My verdicts:

1. Bad lamp, quick glance, I go from 0.5 to 0.8 on "the script looks like V's". I feel I should land around 0.7 on V — worth something, but not face value, because the light is bad.
2. My confidence that the script looks like V's goes from 0.4 to 0.8 in two different ways: (i) I squint harder under the same bad lamp, or (ii) the finder tells me the box came from the vicar's own study, which is itself a clue about authorship. Same shift in the same cell, but I feel (ii) should push me further than (i), because the provenance is a second clue and not just a better look.
3. Next morning, same lamp, same photo: "looks like V" goes 0.8 → 0.9. My gut: belief in V should barely budge. But if a genuinely independent second photo — different angle, different camera — pushes me to 0.9 on "looks like V" all by itself, my gut: that should move me a lot.
4. The intern took the photo. If she photographs every tenth letter, versus if she only photographs letters she already suspects are V's: my gut is that the second should leave me less confident than the first even though the photo is pixel-identical — but this is the one I'm shakiest on and my brother thinks I have it backwards.
5. A colleague emails that the conservator said the ink is probably iron-gall. I'm 0.7 sure she's reporting him accurately and only 0.6 sure he was talking about *this* letter and not another one in the box. My gut: those are two different kinds of doubt — "the signal got garbled in transmission" versus "this may not be about my letter at all" — and they should do different work, but the rule as I've stated it seems to squash them together into one probability for "the report says iron-gall".
6. I find out the lamp was even worse than I thought, bulb on its way out. My gut: I should slide back toward 0.5 even though nothing new about the letter happened. And relatedly, I feel two people who get the same batch of clues in different orders ought to end up in the same place, but I also feel each clue should be weighed against what I knew when it arrived.

My working principle is something like: the rule is fine, you just have to impose the fixed-conditionals bit on a partition fine enough that *the reason your confidence moved* is itself one of the cells, rather than on the coarse "looks like V / doesn't" partition. But I genuinely can't tell whether that handles all six or is just a slogan, and I suspect it does nothing at all for 6. So: is there one principle that gets all of these, or as many as possible? Please state it precisely, say which clause is motivated by which case, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it forces me to give up (especially 4). Then tell me what it predicts for the case I keep going back and forth on: I look again in proper daylight and now I'm 0.8 confident the script looks like the *clerk's* — should I land roughly back at 0.5, or somewhere else entirely? Numbers are welcome, I'm happy to do arithmetic. Please don't just point me at a book or a paper, I have no library access out here and I want the argument, not the references.
