I'm in a graduate seminar on game theory and rational choice, and we have just started on the program-game framework for the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma: each player submits a program, the referee hands each program the other's source, each outputs C or D, and payoffs satisfy T > R > P > S with 2R > T + S. I built the following argument for a variant we were asked to think about, and it seems to prove too much. I would be grateful for a diagnosis of where it breaks.

The variant: the referee does not hand over sources verbatim. It draws a seed at random from a finite, publicly known family of behavior-preserving recompilations, applies the corresponding recompilation to each source, hands each program the scrambled version of the other, and never reveals the seed.

(1) Whatever I submit fixes both my own move and the opponent's move, since the opponent's output is computed from the source it is shown, and that source is determined by my submission. So in choosing a program I am choosing an outcome pair, not merely a move.
(2) I should therefore evaluate candidate submissions by the payoff of the outcome pair each one produces, treating the opponent's response as a consequence of my choice.
(3) The opponent's response depends on my submission only through the features of it that the opponent's program inspects; and I can hold those features fixed while varying only the part of my program that produces my own output.
(4) For any fixed opponent response, defecting pays strictly more than cooperating: T > R and P > S.
(5) By (3) and (4), for every submission there is a variant that induces the very same response from the opponent but defects instead, and that variant is strictly better. So no cooperating submission can be optimal.
(6) So mutual cooperation is impossible in the scrambled variant.
(7) But nothing in (1) through (5) mentions the scrambling, so the same reasoning rules out mutual cooperation in the ordinary variant, where each program sees the other's source verbatim.
(8) And that is false: in the ordinary variant, both players submitting cooperate iff the opponent's source is byte-identical to mine yields mutual cooperation, and neither can profit by deviating, since any program that defects is no longer byte-identical and so meets defection.

I would most like to keep (1), (2) and (4); I suspect (3), but I cannot say precisely what is wrong with it. Please tell me which step to reject, why that step looked fine, and what each of the alternatives would cost me. I also want to know whether (6) is true even if the argument for it fails: can mutual cooperation be reconstructed under scrambling by making the recognition condition behavioral rather than syntactic — comparing input-output behavior instead of source strings, with each program obtaining its own behavior by quining and simulating — or does the regress of mutual simulation block that? If it is blocked, is that a genuine impossibility or only a limit of the programs I can think of? Finally, say how confident you are in your diagnosis and name the single claim in your answer that you are least sure of.
