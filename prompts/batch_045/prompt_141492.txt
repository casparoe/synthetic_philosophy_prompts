Our philosophy of biology reading group spent two evenings on Gould and Lewontin's spandrels paper, and the argument that ended the second evening was about whether the word "adaptation" can be made to do precise work once the Panglossian habit of dividing an organism into traits and telling a selective story for each one is given up. Nobody in the room defended that habit; we could not agree on what the positive account should look like instead. Since then I have been going through cases and writing down my own gut verdicts, and I would like help turning them into a principle — or, if the pattern will not hold, help deciding which verdicts to drop. Here they are. Male nipples are not an adaptation, only a byproduct of a developmental program shared with females. A segregation-distorter allele that disables competing sperm, cuts the male's own fertility roughly in half, and doubles its transmission is an adaptation — for its own transmission, at the level of the gene rather than the organism. An invented case, to keep us honest: a beetle lineage whose males bear backward-pointing spines on the intromittent organ, where spinier males sire more offspring when females remate but each female they mate with lays fewer eggs over her life; I want to call the spines an adaptation, but only once I have said for whom. The panda's enlarged radial sesamoid is an adaptation for stripping bamboo, and the mammalian middle-ear ossicles are an adaptation for hearing, even though both began as something else — a wrist bone and a jaw joint. Feathers, though, are not an adaptation for flight; that is the textbook case of exaptation, and I believe it, even though feathers were later reshaped for flying. The floppy ears and piebald coats of foxes bred for tameness are not adaptations; they rode along with something else that was selected. The human taste for sugar and fat is still an adaptation for seeking calories, even though it now shortens our lives. Another invented case: a moth population encounters a new pesticide roughly thirty generations ago, a pre-existing detoxification enzyme turns out to have a weak side activity against it, and individuals with slightly more of that side activity survive and leave more offspring — I want to say this is on its way to being an adaptation but is not one yet, and I have no idea where the line falls. Finally, Gould's allometric argument about the size of the Irish elk's antlers, together with a made-up analogue in which crest height across thirty species of a skink radiation scales with body length at a slope near 1.9 while taller-crested males win more contests: I am inclined to say that having antlers and having crests are adaptations, but that the size of the antlers and the height of the crests are not. My tentative rule is that a trait is an adaptation for doing F in a lineage only if heritable variation in the trait produced differences in reproductive success because of F rather than merely in connection with F, and only if doing F was not just a physical or developmental side effect of something else that selection acted on — while whether F still raises fitness today is irrelevant. A friend adds that we need a fourth category, a "retro-fit": a trait that began as a spandrel and was later partly rebuilt by selection for a use it did not originally have. I cannot tell whether that earns its keep or is exaptation with extra steps.

Please give me one account, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case you have not seen, with a note after each clause naming the case that forces that clause to be there. Then apply it to the two cases I keep going back and forth on: the human chin, which I sometimes think is a spandrel left over from the shortening of the face and sometimes think is an adaptation for resisting mechanical strain in chewing and speech, and the beetle spines, where my verdict seems to shift depending on whether I ask about the male, the female, or the breeding pair. Give me at least one further case — one I have not listed — on which the account predicts something I would not have predicted. Tell me plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, which of them you would overturn and why, whether "retro-fit" is a genuine category or a redundancy, and whether the independent evolution of the camera eye in cephalopods and vertebrates should count as evidence for adaptation at all or only as evidence against a constraint hypothesis. If you think the right conclusion is that "adaptation" should be abandoned rather than defined, argue for that instead of hedging. Commit to a position and give your reasons rather than laying out the options and leaving me to choose, and mark for each clause whether you would defend it on its merits or are only proposing it to rescue one of my verdicts. Do not spend any words on citations or suggestions for further reading: I have library access, and what I lack is the reasoning rather than the references.
