Posting this here because I've been going back and forth on it for about a week and I keep loosing the thread.

Setup first so we don't talk past each other. REU = risk-weighted expected utility, Buchak's rank-dependent proposal. Given a prospect, order the outcomes worst to best, utilities u1 ≤ … ≤ un with probabilities p1 … pn. Then

REU = u1 + Σ_{i≥2} r(p_i + … + p_n) · (u_i − u_{i−1})

where r: [0,1] → [0,1] is increasing, r(0) = 0, r(1) = 1. EU is the special case r(p) = p; risk aversion is r(p) < p. It's rank-dependent: the weight on each little step up in utility is the transformed probability that you do at least that well. So unlike EU the value of a prospect isn't just its mean — the shape of the distribution matters, and it matters globally rather than locally.

Now the argument. Mine mostly, though someone on the discord sharpened step 5. A trusted experimenter offers you 10,000 independent bets, each a fair coin flip, +$11 on heads, −$10 on tails. Assume utility is linear in dollars over the relevant range (or just: the payoffs are denominated in utils) — I don't want the answer to be "concavity." The coins are verifiably fair, no ambiguity, no transaction costs, no counterparty risk. Two framings:

Package: one decision, accept or decline all 10,000, results revealed at the end.
Sequence: the bets arrive one at a time, you decide each as it comes, you can stop anytime, and you cannot bind your future self — except that at t=0 you can buy a commitment device that forces acceptance of all of them, which is its own weirdness and I'll come back to it.

(1) A rational evaluation of an option can depend only on the probability distribution that option induces over final outcomes. Anything else — the names of the options, the order you consider them in, whether the risk shows up as "one big risk" or "many small ones" — is framing, and rationality isn't allowed to be frame-dependent. This is just act-consequentialism applied to decision problems, and it's the premise I'm least willing to give up.

(2) Package and Sequence induce the same distribution over final outcomes. Accept-everything is roughly normal with mean +$5,000 and sd ≈ $1,050; the chance you end up down overall is about one in a million, and even the 1-in-1000 worst case is still around +$1,750. Same outcomes, same probabilities, same everything. So by (1) the two framings must get the same verdict.

(3) Everybody takes the Package. EU does. And so does REU for any r short of straight maximin: even r(p) = p^100 dumps essentially all the weight into the bottom percentile of the distribution, and the bottom percentile here is still a gain of about $2,500.

(4) In the Sequence, REU with a moderately risk-averse r declines each bet as it arrives: one bet is worth −10 + r(0.5)·21, which is negative for anything like r(0.5) ≈ 0.25. And that's the whole selling point of REU — declining a single 50/50 +11/−10 is the paradigm Allais-ish preference the theory was built to vindicate, so this isn't some corner case.

(5) So REU says: take the Package, decline every part of the Sequence. But by (1) and (2) the Package *is* the Sequence — identical distributions over final outcomes — so there's nothing left for the difference in verdict to be a response to. Which means REU isn't just wrong, it's making the same mistake as someone who prefers $5 to $10 because it's Tuesday. And notice this isn't special to REU: the argument kills every non-separable rule, so the only coherent risk attitudes are ones you can shove into the utility function, i.e. we're back to EU.

(6) Here's why I think it proves too much, and this is the part I can't shake. Your whole life is a long sequence of gambles, so by the same "look at the long run" logic the aggregate is a near-sure gain and you should be risk-neutral in each one. That would make insurance irrational — you pay premiums your whole life and the insurer's profit is not a coincidence — it would make "don't bet the farm" irrational whenever EV is positive, and it would make declining a 1-in-1000 chance of death for $10,000 irrational. Separately, (1) as I stated it also seems to rule out caring about *when* you find out how things went: you'd have to be indifferent between the results being revealed one bet at a time and all at the end, and I have a real preference there that doesn't feel like a framing effect to me.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd rather keep "declining a single 50/50 +11/−10 bet is fine" and "insurance is sometimes rational" than keep "REU is incoherent," so if I have to pay, tell me what I'm paying and whether I'm also paying it somewhere I haven't looked yet. Three things I specifically want addressed:

(a) If the answer is (2), does REU just become unfalsifiable? Is there any case where it predicts something EU doesn't, other than "it depends on how the agent chose to carve up her decisions"?
(b) Is the commitment device load-bearing or a distraction? A sophisticated REU agent at t=0 seems to buy it, which makes the "declines each bet" in (4) look like it only holds given a particular way of applying the theory — but I can't tell if that's the real answer or an ad hoc rescue.
(c) Does anything turn on whether a "decision" is something given to the agent by the world or something the agent chooses? I go back and forth on this hourly and I don't trust myself on it anymore.
