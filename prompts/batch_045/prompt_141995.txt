hi — I'm a philosophy phd student, and thursday I'm presenting a chapter of my dissertation to the cultural evolution seminar here. It's an interdisciplinary room: two evolutionary anthropologists who work on costly punishment and cooperation in small-scale societies, three or so behavioral economists who run coordination and public-goods games in the lab, one political theorist, and a handfull of grad students who've read a bit of Lewis's Convention and nothing else in social ontology. Nobody there does philosophy of language. I get 25 minutes and then maybe 30 of questions.

The chapter argues something like this. A lot of norms get modelled as cooperation problems that need enforcement to survive — punishment, reputation, internalized guilt. But a decent-sized subclass of them are closer to pure coordination problems: everybody does better if we all do the same thing, and which thing it is doesn't matter much. Lewis's machinery does the work — a regularity held up by mutual expectations plus each person preferring to conform given that others conform, with the particular equilibrium selected by salience and precedent rather than by its content being better. Then my own addition, which I'm calling focal-point laundering: once the equilibrium is up and running, people produce reasons for it, the reasons circulate, and the norm's arbitrariness stops being visible from inside. It now presents itself as resting on reasons, which is a bad description of what's actually holding it up. The practical upshot I'm defending is that for this subclass, enforcement-based accounts overpredict fragility and put the lever for change in the wrong place — arguing against the reasons won't move the norm, and what does move it is relocating the focal point: a new precedent, a visible coordinated defection with a ready alternative.

What I need is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defence of the thesis. Give me roughly eight objections I'll actually get from this room, not the ones I'd get from philosophers, ordered by how much damage they'd do if I handled them badly. Phrase each one the way that person would actually say it — I want to hear the anthropologists talking about costly punishment and what the ethnography looks like, the economists asking what this adds to equilibrium selection, the political theorist on who had the authority to set the precedent in the first place. For each: a two-minute reply, and be straight with me about where the reply is soft. Also: what should I concede in the first two minutes, before anyone asks anything, so I don't spend the whole session looking like I'm defending something stronger than I am? And which single reply has to land for the chapter to be worth anything?

One more thing. I already know the objection I'm dreading: that focal-point laundering is unfalsifiable, that whenever a norm shifts after a public argument I'll say the argument was really a salience shift, and whenever it doesn't shift I'll say the argument failed to be one. I don't know how to answer it and I'm hoping nobody raises it. Please raise it anyway and tell me what the least bad response is.
