I run a small weekend reading group — mostly people from the same rationalist-adjacent corner of the internet I have been reading since the old group-blog days — and this month we worked through Moritz Schlick's short essay On the Meaning of Life. As I read him, the negative half of it is a regress argument: you cannot answer the question of life's point by pointing to something outside life, whether that is God, the progress of humanity, posterity, or an afterlife, because that demotes living to the status of a mere instrument and then the very same question reappears one step up about the further end. The positive half is that value has to be locatable in the living itself, and Schlick's model for how that can be so is play — an activity whose point is not detachable from the doing of it, where you are not trading the hours for a result — which he associates with youth, meaning a stance rather than an age, and which he thinks adult life, organized as it is around outcomes, steadily squeezes out. Two members of the group stayed behind afterward and got into an argument about whether any of that actually survives contact with cases, and I could not referee it.

Mira opened by saying that Schlick's standard, whatever its official form, cashes out as absorption, which gets the shape of meaning wrong. Her example was a man who spends forty years designing and refining an intricate card game that no one else will ever play: by Schlick's own lights he looks like the paradigm of a meaningful life, since nothing about what he does is a means to anything, yet intuitively we want to say he has done something with his life only in a thin, sad sense. And absorption cannot screen content: someone who is wholeheartedly absorbed in cruelty — a torturer who delights in his craft, or in a milder register someone who ruins other people's projects purely for the pleasure of ruining them — is also living for the sake of the activity. So, she said, meaning has to require that what you are absorbed in be worth something, and no account built only out of the internal structure of an activity can say that. Doug replied that this misreads the dialectic: Schlick's regress is a negative result, showing that no external end can confer value on a life, and play is introduced as the proof that value without an external end is possible at all, not as a definition to be applied case by case; read that way, Schlick is not in the business of ranking lives, he is dissolving the demand for a cosmic scorecard. Mira said that will not do, because Schlick plainly does rank lives — that is what all the talk of youth and of adult life losing its point amounts to — and in any case the internal/external distinction cannot carry the load: his own paradigm cases, games and climbing and making music, are saturated with aims and outcomes, with winning and summiting and finishing, so he cannot simultaneously say that play has no external end and hold play up as the model. Doug then reformulated: the test, he said, is counterfactual rather than structural — an activity carries its point in itself when you would still have reason to go on even if the outcome never arrived, whereas the researcher who labors for a result that will only land after her death would have no reason left if it were guaranteed never to land; that is what play has and labor lacks, and it does not require play to be goalless. Mira pressed the cost: on that reading, the parent, the organizer, the physician — the very people we treat as paradigms of meaningfulness — would say that if the child died, the institution was never built, the patients never benefited, the years were thrown away. If Schlick has to call those lives meaningless, his view is far more revisionary than he himself seems to want, given that he also wants to say that an older person can keep the youthful stance. Doug then split the difference: he said meaning is not really a scalar quantity for Schlick, that the essay is closer to therapy for a standing anxiety than to a metric, so it is not in the business of telling us that hobbies beat parenting — and that the cruelty objection is unfair in any case, since Schlick's ethics is not a subjectivism, and a life that destroys other people's capacity for play is self-undermining on his own terms, as his 1908 book on the wisdom of life already suggests. Mira said that is a bait-and-switch: if the cruelty case is excluded by a moral side-constraint brought in from outside, then meaning and morality are independent and Schlick's positive claim degenerates into the advice to cultivate hobbies; and if play is instead what grounds value, the constraint cannot be doing the work he has just assigned to it. Neither would move, and they asked me to settle it, which I could not.

So please do it for me. Answer as a professional philosopher who works on early analytic philosophy and on the contemporary literature on meaning in life would: careful with distinctions, intolerant of hand-waving, explicit about which claims you are attributing to Schlick and which are your own reconstruction. Before you adjudicate, spend a couple of paragraphs brainstorming — reconstruct Schlick's argument as charitably as you can, note what a play-based account would need in order to answer each of the two objections, and only then go move by move. Then rate each of the seven moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, justifying each rating in a sentence or two, and say who is more right overall. I am especially interested in three things: whether the regress argument really establishes that the terminus of the chain must fall inside a life, rather than merely that some things must be valued for their own sake somewhere; whether Schlick is best read as offering a criterion of how meaningful a life is, or as dissolving the question; and what both of them are missing. My own nagging suspicion is that the whole exchange presupposes a life of secure leisure and health, and that a view on which meaning requires that your activity not be a means is going to have something uncomfortable to say about anyone who has to work for a living — but I have not been able to turn that into an argument, so if it is a real problem, show me what it does to the position, and if it is not, say why not.
