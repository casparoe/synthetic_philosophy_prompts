I teach the fifth meeting of a cross-listed final-year/MA seminar called "Markets and Knowledge": 22 students, roughly half economics and half philosophy, one 75-minute slot, a room with movable chairs and a whiteboard, no laptops and no phones. University rules forbid cash stakes, so anything at risk has to be points toward something small — a box of chocolates, or two percent of the course grade; tell me which of the two your design needs and why.

Where the students are: they have an intuitive, half-articulated Hayekian enthusiasm — markets as machines that pool dispersed knowledge, prices as a kind of social cognition — and they have an informal grip on common knowledge from an earlier session, but nothing formal. I do not want a lecture at the front of the room, and I do not want the phrases "no-trade theorem", "adverse selection", "common prior", or "Hayek" to be uttered before the debrief.

Design me a single runnable activity — a trading game with rules, materials and payoffs — that makes them run into the following four things before anyone names them.

1. Under a shared prior, the other side's willingness to trade is itself evidence against the informational advantage you took yourself to have: the "I wouldn't join a club that would have me" structure. I want a round in which willingness to trade is made *public* through a deliberately coarse communication protocol and trade collapses rather than opens up.
2. The result is a constraint on what trade can *mean*, not a prediction that trade won't happen. Once a non-informational motive is in the room — someone has a use for the asset, a hedging need, a liquidity shock, or simply a different prior — trade comes back, and the interesting question becomes what the observed trade was evidence of.
3. A price formed under that kind of asymmetric information is not "the market's estimate of value." It is an expectation conditional on someone's being willing to take the other side, so it carries a winner's-curse correction built in; "the market says p" and "the total evidence supports p" can come apart, and I want students able to state the direction of the gap from having watched it happen.
4. All of this is bought with a common prior that I intend to rig visibly, by drawing signals from a physical urn in front of them. That is precisely the assumption a Hayekian should refuse to grant, since the epistemic case for markets is arguably about agents who do not share a prior and do not yet know what the candidate hypotheses are. The session should end with that tension live rather than settled.

Concretely, please give me:

- **Materials and setup.** Exact, constructible numbers: the asset's value, the signal structure with its conditional probabilities, how many traders, and how I build the decks or urns out of index cards or envelopes. I have one teaching assistant who can play a scripted role, and I am willing to be a die-driven counterparty if the design needs one.
- **Three or four rounds**, each differing from the last by exactly one mechanism change. At least one round must make willingness to trade public under the coarse protocol (say: three permitted sentences, and nothing else); at least one must introduce the non-informational motive via a "user" or hedger role with a private use value; at least one must offer an optional purchase of a second signal at a posted cost before trading, so that students can feel information acquisition become unrewarded.
- **A payoff scheme with no money.** I would like you to consider scoring each student on a strictly proper scoring rule applied to a stated probability rather than on realized value, so that risk aversion and small-sample luck don't drown the epistemic point — but if some round works better with realized payoffs, say which and why.
- **A minute-by-minute timeline** for the 75 minutes, including setup and teardown, with the mechanism changes marked.
- **A debrief**, ideally as a table mapping specific moments in the session to specific claims, plus the two or three questions I should put to the room to open it. Include where the Hayekian objection is supposed to enter and what I should say if nobody raises it.
- **What will predictably go wrong.** I expect at least: the prediction being about expectations rather than about every individual pair, so twenty-two people won't visibly behave like the theorem; one or two students refusing to trade on principle; one or two treating it as a puzzle to be gamed rather than a market; someone blurting their actual signal during the communication round; and half the room drawing the wrong moral from who happened to end up with the most points. Give me the handling for each.
- **What the activity cannot reproduce and I must supply in lecture.** I take it this includes the common-prior assumption itself, the fixed-point or iterated character of the argument (the game can only stage one or two rounds of higher-order reasoning), risk neutrality, zero transaction costs, and the distinction between "no speculative trade" and "no gains from trade at all." Tell me if there is anything else on that list.

Please don't spend the answer on bibliography, citations, or named theorems and their filiations — I have all of that. I need the thing I can run on Thursday.
