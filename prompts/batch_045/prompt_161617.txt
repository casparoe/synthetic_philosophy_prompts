I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've been going to a weekly reading group with a few friends where we've been working through experimental philosophy — the stuff about how the answers people give to standard thought experiments move around depending on where the subjects are from. In my own work I tend to think of a philosophical theory as a specification and of thought-experiment intuitions as unit tests: the golden outputs you check a candidate implementation against. What the cross-cultural literature seems to show is that those golden outputs are environment-dependent — run the same case in front of a different population and the expected value changes. I tried to write out carefully what that implies, and I ended up somewhere I can't accept but can't find the way out of.

(1) If whether people intuit that p varies systematically with the cultural background of the people asked, then that intuition carries no evidential weight about whether p: a response that flips with a feature of the respondent that has nothing to do with the case is a response tracking the respondent, not the world. (2) In a number of well-known cases — Gettier-style cases, the reference cases where a name is associated with a description that in fact picks out the wrong man, brain-in-a-vat-style cases — the share of people giving the textbook answer does shift with the cultural composition of the sample. (3) So intuitions elicited by philosophical thought experiments are not evidence about what those thought experiments were designed to test. (4) The undermining in (1) cannot be confined to the particular cases that happened to be surveyed: what (2) establishes is that the intuition-generating faculty is unreliable here, and an unreliable source does not become reliable in the cases where you didn't happen to run the study. (5) The faculty that delivers the textbook verdict in a Gettier case is the same faculty that delivers the verdict that a valid argument cannot take you from true premises to a false conclusion, that if p then q together with p licenses q, and that torturing a child for amusement is wrong. (6) So those verdicts are not evidence either — and not just in philosophy: anything resting on untutored judgment loses its data, including the linguist asking whether a sentence is grammatical, the mathematician asking whether a step in a proof is obviously correct, and even perception, whose verdicts also shift across populations in the standard illusion cases. (7) But nobody believes that, including the people running the studies, who rely constantly on their subjects' comprehension of the vignette, on the subjects' answers to comprehension questions, and on their own judgments about what the cases show; and a method with no track record at all would never have produced the convergence that made one answer the textbook one. (8) Therefore (1) is false: systematic cultural variation in an intuition is not by itself enough to strip it of evidential force.

Each step looks fine to me on its own, and I would rather give up almost anything than accept (6), but I also don't want to go back to just ignoring the cross-cultural results. My reading group has split four ways and I can't adjudicate: one person says the flaw is in (2), that the variation is an artifact of translation and of how subjects construe the vignette, so there is no real variation in intuition at all; one says it's in (5), that philosophical intuitions were always meant to be considered judgments rather than snap reactions, and the surveys only measure the snap reactions; one says it's in (4), that unreliability in one class of cases is simply no evidence about another class; and one says the whole thing is a scope problem in (1), because subjects who answer differently may not be intuiting the same proposition at all. I'd like to hold on to the spirit of (2) and (3) — that the variation is real and tells us something — and I'd rather pay almost any price elsewhere than accept (6).

Which step should I actually reject? I'd like you to say exactly what goes wrong in the step you reject, to explain why it looks innocuous enough that I walked straight past it, and then to price the alternatives: for each of the other places one might intervene, what would I have to accept if I intervened there instead, and is there any version of the argument that survives with only (1) weakened rather than dropped? Translating into terms I understand better would help: is the right diagnosis that my unit tests are flaky, that the harness is measuring something other than the module under test, that I'm running the same test in two different environments and treating it as one test, or that I've written a suite that can only ever pass on a machine I am not running?
