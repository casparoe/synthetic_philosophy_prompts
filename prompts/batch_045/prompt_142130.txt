I am counsel to the currency commission of my country, which is six months away from redenominating the national currency — we are dropping three zeros, so that 1,000 old units become 1 new unit — and I have been asked to draft the continuity-of-obligations provisions. This has stopped being an academic matter for me: I personally hold a twenty-year fixed mortgage on a warehouse and a twenty-year annuity from a former employer, and I have been arguing with my colleagues about whether the number in a contract or the purchasing power behind it is the thing the law is protecting.

For context, here is the scheme as it stands, and the feature of it that puzzles me.

First, the three core rules. (1) The nominal-value rule: a debt expressed as a number of units is discharged by paying that number of units, whatever has happened to prices in the interim; courts will not top up a debt because the unit has lost purchasing power, and will not reduce it because the unit has gained. (2) Legal tender: a creditor must accept the state's currency at its face value in discharge of a debt, and the state's specification of what counts as a unit is conclusive in court. (3) Continuity on redenomination: every existing contract continues in force and every number in it is simply divided by a thousand; no party may claim that the change of unit has altered the substance of the obligation.

Second, the exceptions and asymmetries, which are where my puzzlement comes from. The state itself issues inflation-linked bonds; tax thresholds, public pensions and civil-service salaries are adjusted annually to a published consumer price index; and the new statute creates, for statistical purposes only, a reference basket — an official unit defined as a fixed basket of staple foodstuffs and fuel, which the central bank will publish monthly and which no one may use to denominate a payment. Meanwhile the draft invalidates private clauses that peg a debt to a foreign currency or to a named commodity unless the clause instead refers to the official index. And the centrepiece of my own drafting, which I am no longer sure I can defend: an equitable reopening clause, available only on the debtor's application, only where the obligation runs longer than five years, and only where the official index has moved by more than forty per cent since the contract was made, under which a court may re-express the outstanding number in the new unit so as to restore, in the court's words in my draft, the real position the parties intended.

Third, the philosophical material I have been reading to get my bearings. In his discussion of justice and exchange (the fifth book of the Nicomachean Ethics, as it is usually arranged), Aristotle treats money as something that comes into being to solve a problem: goods are not directly comparable, exchange between people who need different things requires that they be made comparable, and money is introduced as a common measure that does this and thereby also makes deferred exchange possible, since one can accept it now in the confidence of being able to get what one needs later. Crucially, he says the measure is what it is by convention and not by nature — his word for coin is visibly derived from his word for law or convention — so that the community can, by agreement, change it or make it worthless. But he also concedes that money is not a perfectly stable measure: its own value shifts, as the value of other things does, even if it tends to shift less. And he holds that money exists for the sake of exchange rather than as an end in itself, that treating its unlimited accumulation as the point of economic activity confuses a means with an end, and that breeding money from money by interest is the most unnatural form of that confusion, since money's job is to facilitate exchange, not to reproduce. In the background of all this is his claim that justice in exchange is a matter of proportional equality, with what binds the community together — need, or demand — as the real thing being measured, and money as its stand-in.

So: if Aristotle is right that money is a conventional measure whose point is to secure proportional equality in exchange, does the nominal-value rule look like the legal expression of that view, or like its betrayal? A convention can be redefined, which is exactly what makes dropping three zeros legally trivial; but a measure that drifts is failing at the one job it was invented to do, and a rule that treats the number as sacred while the state quietly indexes its own receipts and promises looks like it is exploiting the conventionality of money while refusing the responsibility that comes with being a measure. I would like that tension reconstructed properly, and I would like to know how much of my scheme is actually forced by a defensible theory of money and how much is an accident of drafting that several incompatible theories would equally permit.
