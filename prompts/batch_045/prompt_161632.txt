I have spent fifteen years in trusts and estates practice, and I keep running into a metaphysical thesis that I can neither accept nor dismiss: origin essentialism, the claim that an object's origin is essential to it — that the wooden table at which I am sitting could not have been made from a wholly different block of wood, and that I could not have come from a different sperm and egg, not even from gametes that would have produced someone qualitatively indistinguishable from me.

I would like that thesis, and the arguments that surround it, restated in the vocabulary I actually work in. I am fluent in it, so do not explain it to me: specific gifts as against general and residuary ones; ademption by extinction, and the question a court asks when a bequeathed object has been altered — whether what remains at death is still the thing the will named; the construction of gifts to children or issue, including to a child conceived after the testator's death; the statutory fiction under which an adopted child is treated as if born to the adopters; the way the law of assisted reproduction divides genetic, gestational and intended parentage and then reassigns the pieces; and equitable tracing, where a beneficiary's claim follows value through the substitution of one asset for another.

Please translate, one at a time:

(a) the artifact claim itself, together with the reply that imagining a table made from other wood but possessed of this one's observable properties is not imagining this table;

(b) the corresponding claim about persons and their gametes;

(c) the hard case that monozygotic twinning poses, where the zygote splits after fertilisation and there are then two candidates for the individual I am: on the essentialist view, what is the origin, and could I have been the other one;

(d) the variant raised by embryo selection in assisted reproduction, where several viable embryos exist, a clinician chooses among them, and the unselected ones are never brought to term; and

(e) the ordinary case, in which the sperm that fertilises the egg is settled by chance among a vast number of genetically distinct candidates, so that the winning gamete looks arbitrary.

For each, say what carries into my vocabulary without strain, what survives only if you quietly alter what the claim is doing, and what cannot be said in that vocabulary at all. Then give me the residue report I actually want: whether what fails to translate is missing because my vocabulary is instrumental — it stipulates identity for a purpose, severs origin by decree, and treats every counterfactual as causal and evidential rather than as a question about what this very thing could have been — or because the metaphysical thesis has never had to specify how fine-grained an origin is, and so has never been tested by a decision procedure that forces it to be exact.

My suspicion, which I would like tested rather than confirmed, is that the law's willingness to substitute, sever or backdate origin shows that origin essentialism is a stipulation about how to use the word this rather than a discovery about objects. If that suspicion is wrong, I want to know what in the essentialist arguments I am misreading, and what it costs to hold the thesis while conceding that no institution could ever be required to apply it. I am not asking for authorities or further reading.
