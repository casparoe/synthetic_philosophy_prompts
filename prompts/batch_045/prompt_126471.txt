I'm finishing the framing chapter of my dissertation in conservation biology (I work on landscape-scale habitat fragmentation in a mixed agricultural-forest mosaic), and our lab has started a joint reading group with the philosophy department on welfare-informed conservation. I keep getting stuck in the same place: nearly everything I read proposes or quietly assumes some way of totalling up the welfare contained in a landscape and then argues about the sign of the total, but nobody says what a measure of that total would have to do in order to be the right one. So before I read any more of it, I want the specification.

What I need is a list of requirements on any account of the net welfare of an ecosystem, where the account is answerable to one particular job: ranking alternative land-use trajectories for a defined area — say this 400-hectare block of secondary forest on the edge of a reserve, where the live options are strict protection, clearance for housing, conversion to intensive agriculture, or abandonment to regenerate — by the welfare those trajectories can be expected to contain. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and say whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of one camp. Please cover at least the following, and add or substitute where you think I have the logical space wrong: the difference between the welfare standing stock of a landscape at a time and its welfare throughput, meaning the welfare churned through per unit time by births and deaths (in most taxa most offspring die young, so a cross-sectional sample of the animals alive now, weighted towards survivors, may badly misrepresent the flow — contrast a high-fecundity passerine population with low adult survival against a similar-biomass ungulate population with the opposite life history); the decomposition of any effect of habitat loss into an extensive margin (fewer welfare-bearing lives) and an intensive margin (changed welfare per remaining life through density dependence, resource competition, crowding in fragments, edge effects), and whether those margins may be traded off against each other or must be reported separately; whether the verdict must be the same for two trajectories with identical population dynamics reached by different means — contraception or culling versus bulldozing, fire, or poisoning — or whether sensitivity to method is itself a mark of a good account; how the theory handles the replacement community, since cleared land does not stay empty and ends up holding synanthropic generalists or, if farmed, domesticated animals whose lives may be net positive; the temporal extent of the comparison, including regeneration after abandonment, whether discounting is permissible, and whether the sign of the total may be fixed by centuries that no available model says anything about; the denominator problem, that throughput looks dominated by small-bodied invertebrates, and whether restricting scope to plausibly sentient taxa is principled or special pleading; and the tension between welfarist purity and tractability, since the only data anyone actually has are area, habitat type, species composition, rough densities and demographic rates, and proxies like species richness or naturalness are not welfare — must an account be computable from such data, or may it be a standard of correctness we cannot apply? I also want to know how an account is supposed to behave if the welfare throughput of most wild habitat turns out to be negative: whether resistance to something like an extinction implication is a genuine desideratum or a prejudice, and likewise for the demand that the account deliver expected-value rankings rather than precautionary verdicts under thick uncertainty. Flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — I suspect tractability and purity are one such pair, and additivity over time together with immunity to domination by the far future another — and tell me which single requirement does the most work in ruling candidates out.

End with a compact table, no prose beyond a line per row, scoring four positions against your list: that wild lives are on balance positive so protection is welfare-promoting; that wild lives are suffering-dominated so habitat loss tends to reduce suffering; that wildness or naturalness carries value the welfare ledger does not capture; and that our uncertainty is too deep for any net verdict, so conservation should stick to its usual priorities. Attach names to these only where you are confident of them.

Two last things. Where you are least confident that a requirement is genuine rather than a camp preference, say so in a word; and close with two sentences on which requirement you would abandon first if forced, and what empirical finding — about density dependence, invertebrate sentience, or anything else — would most change the list. Keep the whole thing under 900 words including the table; the criteria matter to me far more than the scorecard, so do not pad it.
