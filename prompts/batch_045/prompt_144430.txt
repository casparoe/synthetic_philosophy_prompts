You are a moral philosopher who works on the theory of punishment — desert, proportionality, standing, the expressive function of sanction — and also on social ontology and the metaphysics of persons over time. Work through this carefully rather than quickly, and before you settle on a view, list the candidate theories you might reach for and say what each would predict about the features I describe. No citations or reading lists are needed; reason from the structure of the practice itself.

The practice I want you to reconstruct is one I keep running into from the inside, and I cannot get the philosophical shape of it into focus. I sat on the programme committee of a mid-sized academic society when an online campaign demanded that one of our invited speakers be disinvited for things he had written years earlier. What struck me was not the outcome but the machinery, or rather the absence of it. Nobody had to decide: there was no rule about which conduct disqualified a person, no threshold, no body with authority to rule on it, no procedure for evidence, and in particular no schedule of proportionality — the severity of the response was a function of how much attention the case attracted, not of what the person had actually done. There was also no sentence: nothing to serve, no point of completion, and reinstatement happened, if it happened at all, by the case fading from attention rather than by anything being discharged. The same pattern shows up in journals retracting papers, publishers dropping authors, employers acting on decade-old posts, festivals withdrawing invitations, and professional societies like mine — and in every one of them the participants characteristically deny that they are punishing anyone, describing what they do as withholding a platform, exercising their own speech, or holding someone accountable; yet the vocabulary they reach for is saturated with desert: consequences, should have known better, unrepentant, insufficiently sorry. Meanwhile the institutions involved usually have formal procedures with proportionality, representation and appeal, and for these cases decline to use them.

Here is my own guess, which I want you to test rather than humour. It seems to me that what gets imposed is better described as a change of status than as a hardship: the person is moved out of the category of people we platform, and that is why our proportionality intuitions misfire and why there is no natural end point, since statuses do not expire but must be re-conferred. If that is right, the practice is a form of punishment whose participants lack the vocabulary to admit it, and it lacks every piece of apparatus — an authority, a tariff, an end date, a route back — that we normally insist on before letting anyone punish. It also seems to presuppose two things at once that I cannot reconcile: that the person who wrote the thing twenty years ago is the same person in the relevant sense, so that desert carries across the gap, and that the person can now change, since the demand for a satisfactory apology is the loudest part of the whole ritual — even though there is nobody with standing to accept an apology and no act of acceptance that would end anything. And the slogan that no one is owed a platform seems to me to cut both ways: if giving someone a platform is a morally loaded act, so is withdrawing one, and I cannot see why the first generates duties while the second generates only permissions.

So: reconstruct the implicit theory. What view of punishment, of standing, or of what a person is does this bundle of features only make sense under? Which rival views does it quietly rule out? Be careful to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and tell me where it is underdetermined — where several quite different theories would license exactly this behaviour, which might explain why participants can never agree about whether a given case succeeded. If the participants were persuaded that this is punishment after all, what would have to change in the practice — publish rules, name decision-makers, hear evidence of intent, set durations, build a route back — and would making those changes destroy whatever the practice is for? Finally, tell me where my status-change diagnosis is wrong, and whether the absence of an end date is a defect of the practice or, given what it is actually doing, the only coherent option available to it.
