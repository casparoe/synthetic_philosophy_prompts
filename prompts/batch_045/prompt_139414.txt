I've been reading a chapter in one of those popular "big questions" ethics books (not naming it) about doing more than morality asks, and I have no philosophy background so I can't tell how much of it to trust. Roughly, and mostly in its own words, it says:

"Some actions are supererogatory — they go beyond the call of duty. Throwing yourself on a grenade, giving away half your income, running into a burning building. Doing them makes you a hero; not doing them isn't wrong and nobody can blame you. Morality has a required part and a forbidden part, and supererogation is just the bit sticking out past the required part: an optional bonus zone where you're free to add extra good if you feel like it. Genuinely optional, though — not giving to charity is morally on a par with having tea instead of coffee. This is also where utilitarianism falls apart, because it says you always have to produce as much good as you can, so for a utilitarian everything good is mandatory and sainthood is just the minimum. And there's a deeper problem: if the heroic act is the best thing you could do, and you always have most reason to do the best thing, then it's required after all, and the whole category collapses back into ordinary duty."

My brother-in-law took two philosophy classes in college and says the "its just like tea" bit in particular is a strawman, but he won't explain why. So: claim by claim, what would someone who actually works on this accept, what's a fair simplification for a general reader, and whats just wrong? Is the stuff about utilitarianism a real problem for that view or a caricature of it, and is the last bit a knockdown argument or one of those things people are still arguing about? The chapter also says in passing that you can't deserve any credit at all for merely doing your duty — is that right?

Three cases I came up with myself that the book doesn't touch and that I definately can't fit into its picture:

1. The cost slider. Same rescue: version A costs me nothing, I just press a button; version B costs me a job interview that would have landed me a 50k job. B feels clearly heroic, A feels like I'd be a jerk not to. If "supererogatory" just tracks how expensive the act is for me, where does the line get drawn — or is it only a sliding scale where we all agreed to be lenient past a certain point? And does the free version become a real duty, or stay optional?

2. My friend is a lifeguard. Same dive, same risk, but on shift she says it's just her job, and on her day off the same thing is heroic. Does a job description really create a moral duty out of nothing, or is the real difference about what she agreed to, what other people can demand of her, or what she's owed for it?

3. The mixed ones. The rescue that also gets you a promotion. The kidney you give your brother. Does getting something out of it disqualify an act from being supererogatory, or is the category about cost and risk rather than about why you did it?

One more: the book says there's no mirror image, no such thing as something bad but not forbidden. That suprised me, because petty selfishness seems exactly like that to me.

And finally: how much should this make me distrust the rest of the book, and is there something big the chapter left out that changes the picture?
