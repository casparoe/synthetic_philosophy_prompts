ok so my reading group has spent like two semesters on epiphenomenalism and the paradox of phenomenal judgment and I'm fairly sure we've been arguing past each other the whole time. We want to turn it into a small workshop proposal, and for that I need the document the two sides would actually produce if they'd done this properly: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration.

Side A is an epiphenomenalist: phenomenal properties are causally inert with respect to everything behavioural and cognitive; my saying "that looks red to me" or "this hurts" is fully produced by non-phenomenal machinery, and the fact that the experience is sitting right there doing nothing is supposed to be fine. Side B thinks the whole view dies of the paradox: if the experience does nothing, then my judgment that I'm having it isn't caused by it, and then it's unexplained how that judgment manages to be about the experience, or worth trusting, or how any of us ever acquired these words in the first place. B takes that as a reductio rather than as homework for A.

What I want is a numbered agreed-claims section first — including a wording of the paradox itself that both sides can sign, and an agreed statement of what would count as solving it rather than dissolving it — and then a disagreements table. For the table: one row per crux, with the crux stated neutrally, A's own preferred formulation of their position, B's, and then for each crux a concrete case or argument both would accept as bearing on it, plus what each side predicts about that case. (Predictions written down in advance is the whole point — I don't want anyone retrodicting.)

Some things I've been pushing in the group and want dealt with, and you should feel free to say which of them are confused:

- Split the report into three separable claims: what causes the report token, what makes it about the experience, and what makes it authoritative or justified. Have each side say which of the three epiphenomenalism actually threatens. My bet is that most of the argument is really about whether you can have the second without the first.
- A is not a single position. There's the "the judgment is partly constituted by the experience, so no causation needed" reply, the "primitive acquaintance / the introspective act just is a non-causal relation to the experience" reply, and the "common cause plus a reliable neural read-out" reply. Say whether the choice among these changes anything downstream, because I suspect A's answers to the cruxes differ depending on which one they're running.
- The latency ladder. Instantaneous report, report after 200 ms, after an hour from a memory trace, after ten years, and the deferred-vocabulary case where you have the experience at five and only learn the word for it at thirty. Ask where, if anywhere, the read-out story stops being available, and what each side predicts about the top rungs.
- The cross-wired read-out. A subject whose report mechanism reliably indicates the experience but is wired so that it could in principle be inverted, so that the subject would say "red" while undergoing green. Can A distinguish the veridical from the inverted case without letting the experience do any causal work, and does B think the distinction is even coherent on A's terms?
- The artifact case: a machine whose reports about its own internal states are produced by a monitor module that exerts no influence on the states it reports on. Does the paradox transfer, and does anyone think that case tells us something the biological one doesn't? I don't want this to eat the document, but I do want a verdict on whether it's a genuine extra test or just the same argument in a costume.
- Flag which parts of the dispute you think are merely verbal — I definately suspect "caused by" versus "in virtue of" versus "tracks" is doing more work than either side admits — and which parts are real and would stay real under any rewording.
- For each side, what they'd count as evidence or argument against their own view. If nothing would move one of them, say so plainly instead of inventing something diplomatic.

Keep it armchair: no citations, no reading lists, no bibliography, no "see so-and-so 1996" — the whole point is that the arguments have to stand on their own. Format the doc as the numbered agreed claims then the table, and end with one last line, \answer{...}, containing in ten words or fewer the single crux both camps would agree the whole thing hinges on.
