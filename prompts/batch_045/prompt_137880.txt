i'm the safety manager at a mid sized metro system and i personally run every incident review we do — 40 to 60 a year, mostly minor, a couple with injuries, and one with a fatality three years back. our method is the five whys: keep asking why until you hit something you can act on. it keeps breaking in three ways.

one, we stop way too early at whatever nobody in the room challenges — the operator didn't follow the procedure, the part failed, that's the vendor's spec. everyone nods, we write it down, done. i suspect part of this is that the regulator wants us to name a single root cause per incident, so the report format is itself pushing us to stop somewhere.

two, we go in circles. alarms were muted because of alarm fatigue; there's alarm fatigue because alarms fire constantly; alarms fire constantly because thresholds are set conservatively; thresholds are conservative because of the 2019 incident, whose only action item was "mute the alarms." three facts just propping each other up.

three, definately the opposite problem: some chains never bottom out at all. every answer opens two new questions and the two hours is gone.

i've read a little and it looks like philosophy has this exact problem under the name of the regress of reasons, and three incompatible answers about when a chain of justification is allowed to stop: foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism. i need that turned into something my team can actually run — non-philosophers, two hour meetings — and the output still has to be a report a state regulator will accept, so "we don't know" isn't an option by itself.

so give me the procedure. the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it stops, where it says keep going, and where it has to say "this is a judgment call, a named person decides and signs." keep whichever distinctions from those three theories actually change what we'd write in the report and drop the ones that don't, and be honest about where the procedure will misfire on us.

one specific thing to rule on: an engineer here keeps pushing a rule that a reason only counts if whoever's asserting it can actually state it, in writing, on the spot — not if it's merely the kind of reason that could be given by somebody with time to go look it up. she says that's what seperates a real long chain from a fake one. is that a coherence test, a foundationalist test, or its own thing, and does it belong in the procedure?

then run it on three cases. (1) overspeed into a work zone: the chain stopped at "driver exceeded authorized speed," and the union says that's premature because the schedule gives four minutes for a six minute run and the only way to make it is to carry speed into the zone. (2) an intermittent signal failure where the account hangs together beautifully — maintainence backlog, understaffing, budget cuts, the reorg, all mutually supporting, no loose ends — where i want to know whether the procedure tells us to stop there, because my guess is it does and i don't like that answer. (3) a near miss where nothing bottoms out and we're at minute 105.

answer in American English please.
