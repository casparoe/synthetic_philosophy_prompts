I'm writing a chapter on the ethics of taxing inherited wealth, and the argument I keep circling around rests on a picture I no longer trust: equal opportunity as a footrace in which everyone should begin at the same starting line, with large bequests figured as runners being carried out past that line before the gun, so that confiscatory inheritance taxation is simply the enforcement of the start. My supervisor wants me to swap it for what she calls the relay picture, on which a lineage is a single team running one race over many legs and the bequest is the baton pass; she thinks this reframing defuses the case for heavy taxation, since a team that hands on what it has gained is not cheating. My worry is that the relay picture does not answer the objection but only relocates it to the question of whether the family line is the right unit of account.

Audit both pictures. Give me the correspondence table for each — what plays the role of the runner, the starting line, the starting gun, the prize, the handicap, the team — and say what argumentative work each mapping is doing. Then give me the disanalogies, and tell me which of them actually bear on the taxation case. Three things seem to me to be hidden by the footrace frame, and I want your verdict on how much damage they do: first, that there is no single starting gun, since advantages like schooling, networks and capital are converted into positions continuously and through several different allocation mechanisms at once, so there is no moment at which a fair start could be measured; second, that upbringing shapes not merely where a runner starts but which finishes she wants, so the content of a fair start cannot be specified without deciding whose preferences count — the child's actual ones or the ones she would have had under some other rearing; third, that a race is competitive and positional, whereas much of what inherited wealth buys — insurance against catastrophe, the ability to refuse an exploitative job, the capacity to found anything at all — is not advantage relative to anyone else. Finally: does the relay picture dissolve the argument for heavy inheritance taxation or merely restate it at the level of the lineage, can either picture get you as far as a rate or as far as a principled distinction between taxing the receipt and taxing the transmission, and if both pictures fail, what should replace them?
