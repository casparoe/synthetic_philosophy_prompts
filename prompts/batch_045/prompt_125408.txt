Next month I've been roped into doing a session for our university art museum's docents — about twelve of them, mostly retired teachers with a couple of retired engineers mixed in, no philosophy at all, but twenty-plus years of handling objects, and they will absolutely drag me off into technique and attribution if I let them. 75 minutes in the print study room, so I can put things on a table but I cant do slides. The goal: by the end they should be able to handle a visitor who says 'if you can't tell them apart, what are you actually paying for?' without either sneering at the visitor or caving and saying 'nothing, it's just snobbery.'

Don't write the session for me — tell me how to build it.

What I want is the dependency structure. My instinct is that if I open with a painting they'll think the whole thing is about pictures, when the distinction I'm actually after is the one between arts where the history of production is constitutive of the work and arts where it isn't (Goodman's autographic/allographic thing, though I'm not assigning it). But I'm not sure that's the right place to start with this crowd, or whether with them it's better to start from the thing they already know how to do — attribution — and then show that attribution settles a question the value question doesn't reduce to. Say what has to be on the table before the problem bites, and why.

The carrying example: I need one case to run the whole hour. It should be something they can put on the table or at least hold in mind as an object; it must not have any perceptual 'tell' of fakeness, because the second that's available they'll spend the session hunting for the flaw instead of thinking about the value question; and it shouldn't be a mechanical reproduction of an existing work, or copyright and copying talk will swallow everything. Tell me what your example is, what it has and what it deliberately lacks, and what the variations are that I can dial up if I need to.

Here's the scaffolding I've been using, which I'd like you to work with (and push back on). I seperate three things: origin value — it matters who made it; career value — it matters what's happened to it since, who owned it, what it survived; and exposure value, which is my own addition — the value a thing gets from having been found out, the way a known forgery testifies to the limits of connoisseurship at a time in a way no original ever can, so the value ordering isn't monotonic and the detected fake can beat the undetected one in one currency while losing badly in another. Is that third one real, or is it just career value in disguise? And where in the hour does it belong?

Two more things I know are coming. One of them will bring up van Meegeren inside five minutes — the Dutch painter who faked Vermeers, sold one to Göring, and then had to prove at his trial that they were fakes in order to dodge a treason charge — and I want to know whether that's a gift or a trap, and if it's a gift, where in the sequence I let it in. And at some point someone will ask about the workshop replica: the thing made in the master's studio, with his consent, largely by assistants. I think that middle case is what stops them collapsing everything into a binary, but I don't know whether to use it to open the gap or to close it.

Misconceptions: give me the ones you'd predict in the order you'd predict them, and the exact point in the sequence to head each off. My guesses: they'll assume the difference must show up in the object somehow; they'll think 'it's all about money' is itself a philosophical verdict rather than a place to start; and they'll assume better science — pigment analysis, dendrochronology — dissolves the question rather than just relocating it.

Finally: the check. Give me one question to put to them at the end, plus what an answer that's actually got it looks like versus an answer that's just polite, and tell me what I should openly refuse to settle at this level and say out loud that I'm refusing.
