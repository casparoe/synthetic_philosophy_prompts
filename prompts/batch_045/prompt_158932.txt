I sit on a volunteer board that reviews applications for early release, and almost everything we do turns on why the applicant acted as she did: whether she now grasps the harm, whether she has stopped making excuses, whether the same pressure would produce the same act again, whether her account of her own reasons has changed. The trial that sent her there ran on what looks like the opposite principle. As I read the transcripts, the prosecution had to prove nothing about her ultimate reasons, and the jury was directed that a good motive is no defence and a bad motive no aggravation — the person who kills to end suffering commits murder, the person who steals to feed her children commits theft.

Yet the system is not consistent about this, and I would like the implicit theory reconstructed rather than defended or attacked. It cares intensely about whether she meant the result, foresaw it, or acted in order to bring it about; burglary requires an ulterior purpose; duress and self-defence are available only if the threat or the attack was what actually moved her, so that someone who would have acted anyway gets nothing; an honest and reasonable mistake about the facts generally excuses, while a mistake about the law generally does not; penalty-enhancement statutes add years precisely for reasons of bigotry; and in general no one may claim payment for doing what one was already legally bound to do, even as the same system pays bounties to informants and honours those who did more than was required of them.

So: what view of blameworthiness — or of the worth of an action, or of credit — does the trial-side doctrine presuppose? Does it presuppose that the moral standing of what someone does is fixed by the act itself and by what was foreseen, and not at all by why? Or can a view on which the reasons a person acts from are central explain the doctrine some other way — as caution about evidence, or as a limit on what the state may coercively inquire into? Please keep separate three questions I keep running together: whether the act was wrong, whether the agent is blameworthy, and whether the agent deserves credit. Tell me which of them the doctrine is actually about, and whether the trial and my board are answering different ones.

One refinement I have been unable to shake. It seems to me that what matters may not be which reason the agent happened to have, but whether the right-making reason was load-bearing: would she have done the same thing if that reason had been absent? Consider a lifeguard who rescues a rival only in order to beat him later; a cashier watched by a camera who would have given correct change anyway; and someone who does the right thing while sincerely believing it to be wrong. Does the doctrine's implicit theory have any resources for these, or does it simply refuse the question?

I do not want a recommendation about any case, and I do not need citations or readings. I want the implicit theory stated plainly, the rival views it quietly rules out identified, the places where the practice is underdetermined and several theories would license it marked as such, and an account of what would have to change in courtroom practice — and on my board — if we replaced the implicit view with one on which the reasons a person acts from are what matter.
