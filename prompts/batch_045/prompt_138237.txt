I'm a novelist and something's been happening with my last two books that I can't quite get a handle on. I'd like a word for it, if there is one.

In the 2017 one there's a character — a solicitor, mid-thirties, outwardly completely fine, holds down a job, never shouts at anyone — who can't get through a day without running through every possible version of a conversation before she actually has it, and who feels physically sick if someone changes plans on her the same morning. I made the whole thing up to drive the plot. I wasn't copying it out of any manual. In the book her sister has a name for it, "the flat panic", just a bit of dialogue.

Readers started writing to me. Not "I enjoyed it" — "that's me", hundreds of them. Then a councellor friend said three of her clients in a single year had turned up describing themselves as having flat panic and asking for help with it. And last year someone at a festival told me they'd gone to their GP describing exactly that pattern and come out with an anxiety diagnosis, and that ever since they'd been noticing it in everyone. So the thing I invented out of my own head is now a way people walk into a clinic and describe themselves — and this is the bit that gets me — the clinicians seem to be seeing more of it, and what they mean when they reach for the diagnosis seems to have shifted a bit, to include the version from my book. As if the category moved to meet the people turning up, and the people turned up because the category was there.

Second one, quicker. A friend's YA novel has a kid who leaves every social event in stages: says goodbye, comes back in, says goodbye again, hovers by the door for forty minutes. My friend called it "tapering". Now there are teenagers calling themselves tapers, and teachers using it, and a couple of them describe the leaving differently than they did five years ago — more deliberate, more of a thing you announce.

It's not a self-fulfilling prophecy, I don't think. Nobody was told they had anything. And it isn't what I think gets called labelling either, where some authority sticks a name on you; these came in sideways, out of paperbacks, and people took them up on purpose and seemed relieved to. But it's not just "a word caught on" either, because what changed isn't only what people call themselves. It's what they notice, what they do, and apparently what the professionals end up treating.

Is there an established concept for this — the loop where a way of describing people changes the people, who then change the description, so the category and the thing it picks out keep drifting along together? And where the description can come from anywhere, not just from the people with the clipboard? I know there's stuff in philosophy and in psychiatry somewhere in this neighbourhood; what I want to know is exactly which bit mine is, which bits it isn't, and where the edges are. Does it still count if the label came from fiction rather than from a clinic? Does it stop being the same thing if the description was actually accurate all along, if I'd stumbled onto something real? Does the loop break if the person knows perfectly well they got the word out of a novel? And what about the cases where a word spreads and nobody changes at all, or where people change and the word never follows?

I'm on a panel in the spring about fiction and diagnosis and I'd like to say something better than "dunno, culture's weird". A name would be a start, but names aren't the point: I want to know what this is and what it isn't. If there's no term for it, coin me one, define it, and tell me where it stops.
