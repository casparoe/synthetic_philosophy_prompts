I am getting married in June, and my partner and I are writing our own vows. A friend of ours, who happens to work in philosophy, remarked over dinner — meaning it as a joke — that our vows have exactly the structure of Gregory Kavka's toxin puzzle: the good thing (the marriage, the ceremony, the gifts, the filing status) is handed over the moment we stand up and say the words, while the thing we thereby say we intend — to remain together, forsaking all others, until one of us dies — is something that at every later moment at which it could matter we would have no reason to do, if we no longer wanted to. The remark has since spoiled several of my evenings, and I would like to put it back into circulation by handing it out at the rehearsal dinner.

What I want is a satirical document of about 650 words, formatted as a mock county-clerk form — a "Declaration of Intention to Marry" — issued together with the clerk's official scoring rubric, a short FAQ, and one testimonial from a satisfied applicant. The premise is that the clerk's office is legally required to certify that a marriage intention is genuine, and that it has tried to satisfy this requirement by offering three certification routes, each of which is one of the live accounts of what intending is:

(1) the doxastic route, on which intending requires believing that you will do the thing, so the applicant must state their own calibrated credence and "I do" becomes a forecast — the honest applicant fails, and the only applicants who pass are the overconfident and the self-deceived;
(2) the planning route, on which an intention must be stable and means-end coherent, so the applicant must document costly, non-refundable means already taken — and the rubric should note, without apparent irony, that the more means one has taken, the harder it is to tell whether one is acting from the intention or from the sunk costs;
(3) the "at-will" route, on which one may simply adopt the intention in order to receive what is being offered for having it, which is the toxin case exactly, and which the FAQ should describe, deadpan, as the premium tier.

The humor has to come from the philosophy being right rather than from name-dropping: no mention of Kavka, of the toxin puzzle, or of academic debates inside the document itself — it should read as though this form has always existed. Three further bits I would like worked in: "counterfactual-sincerity scoring," in which the examiner tests whether the applicant would still do the thing in the nearest world where the benefit has already been conferred, i.e. the morning after the wedding; a recurring-filing rider requiring the declaration to be resubmitted each anniversary, where the case gets worse every year because the couple now knows they did not act on it the year before; and a rider permitting applicants who cannot form the intention to be certified instead for intending to form it, which the manual acknowledges can be repeated indefinitely.

The piece should end as a reductio: the rubric should quietly make clear that the form can be completed only by applicants to whom it does not apply, and that the office nonetheless certifies nearly everyone, because what it actually checks is only that the words were said aloud. Please keep it in American English, keep the tone deadpan and bureaucratic, and never let it explain or undercut the joke; and please do not resolve the puzzle — the reader should be left having felt why the certification is impossible. Three of our guests are philosophers and should find the satire fair; everyone else should still be able to tell what is being made fun of. No footnotes or citations.
