I'm doing a feature for a science magazine on the people who think 'qualia' is a perfectly respectable posit and the people who think the whole thing ought to be quined — the term is so loaded that each side keeps hearing the other as denying something obvious, and I want the box that runs alongside the piece to be the document they'd actually both sign.

So: draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a qualia realist (the sort who thinks there's a further fact about what it's like, over and above every functional, discriminative and reportable fact, and that the conceivability arguments show it) and someone who thinks the posit is empty and that all we ever really have is what subjects say and do. Brutal compression: exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, no preamble, no sign-off, no 'in conclusion'. No citations, no ones paper cited by name, no reading list — I don't want a lit review, I want the compressed thing.

What has to be in those five lines, as best you can fit it:

- a claim both can sign about what the fight is *not* about (that there's something it's like to see red, that experience matters) and what it *is* about: whether the standard four-property package — intrinsic, non-representational, ineffable, privately and directly apprehensible — describes anything that could be different while every functional, discriminative and verbal-report fact stays fixed;
- each side's own view in wording that side would accept, not a version the other would find easy to knock down;
- the crux, plus an honest note about which part is merely verbal — my hunch is that a lot rides on whether 'quale' has to come as that four-property package deal at all, and on whether a state that no subject could ever in principle notice, remember, or act on is still a state with a determinate nature or just the signature of a posit that has come loose from every criterion we have;
- and the bit I actually care about: a case both would agree bears on the crux, with each side's prediction written down in advance. The one I keep coming back to is a slow-motion version of the inversion thought experiment. We very gradually rotate a subject's colour experience over a year, recalibrating at every session so her discriminations, vocabulary use and memories always match baseline perfectly — she never notices a thing. At the end we tell her exactly what was done and offer to rotate her back, at the price of erasing everything she learned that year. Both sides can accept the setup; so what does each predict she says and does once she's told, what would each count as evidence *against* their own view, and is there anything at all here that would move both of them?

Don't force a resolution — if the honest upshot is that they can sign four lines and have to part on the fifth, say so in that fifth line and leave it there.
