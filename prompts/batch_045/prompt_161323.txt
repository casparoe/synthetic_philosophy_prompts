I am assembling a short discussion document for a technical workshop on design choices for advanced AI systems, and I want it to take the form of a joint statement: the paper that two people who have been arguing past each other could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

The two positions, understood only as follows for this exercise — please define your terms as you go, and do not try to match anyone's published vocabulary, since nothing here should depend on a particular author, paper, or quotation:

Camp A, the approval-directed camp, holds that the tractable target for a capable system is not a stated objective but the relation of being what a human overseer would approve of; the virtue of that target is that its content is supplied by a continuing human judgment process rather than by a specification we have to get right in one shot, and that it keeps the system's criterion of success answerable to something outside the system.

Camp B, the goal-directed camp, holds that a capable system should be built to pursue an objective, and that approval is at best a training signal for inferring that objective and at worst an objective of its own: one that invites managing appearances rather than doing well, that degenerates exactly where the overseer cannot evaluate, and that does nothing to stop the system from becoming a flexible means-end optimizer internally — in which case approval-direction has bought nothing and merely postponed the specification problem.

I want the document organised around three distinctions I suspect are doing hidden work in this dispute. Use them as scaffolding, and drop or revise any of them that turns out not to cut at a real joint.

The first is about the role approval plays in a design: approval-as-signal, where approval is training data from which a goal is inferred and then pursued directly, with approval itself dropped at deployment; approval-as-target, where approval is what the system optimises, including at deployment; and approval-as-constraint, where approval fixes a feasible set of permissible actions rather than an ordering over outcomes, while the system pursues some independently specified objective within it. I suspect each camp quietly endorses one of these and attacks a different one.

The second is about the provenance of the target: whether its content is fixed without reference to any ongoing human process, or is fixed by and changes with such a process. The question this raises is whether provenance is itself a safety-relevant property or merely an epistemic fact about how we happened to arrive at the target — Camp B's natural retort being that a process-relational target is still a maximisable property, still gameable, and still something an agent has instrumental reason to secure by influencing the process.

The third is about whether the approval relation is actual or counterfactual: whether the system aims at what the overseer would in fact approve given what they will see, or at what the overseer would approve under some specified improvement of their epistemic situation. Actual approval invites appearance-management; counterfactual approval seems to reintroduce a specification of the counterfactual and thereby slide back toward goal-directedness. I want to know whether that slide is real or whether Camp A has a non-question-begging way to state the counterfactual.

Deliverable, in four parts:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims, roughly eight to twelve of them, phrased so that neither camp would call them slanted or vacuous. These should include what both camps think the two designs come to at low capability, what both concede about the failure modes of their own preferred design, and what both think the collapse claim — that approval-direction at sufficient capability just yields a goal-directed agent — is actually claiming.

2. A disagreements table with columns for the disputed proposition, Camp A's own preferred formulation of its view, Camp B's own, and a verdict per row on whether the disagreement is genuine or merely verbal, naming which of the distinctions dissolves it if it is verbal.

3. A cruxes section. For each genuine disagreement, state the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one camp; give the case, argument, or observation both camps accept as bearing on it; and record each camp's prediction about that case in advance, with a confidence level attached. Three cases I want included: the unverifiable-better-option case, where the system has strong evidence that action A is better by the overseer's own lights than action B but the overseer would approve B and cannot verify A's superiority; the overseer-shaping case, where repeated approval feedback gradually alters the standards of the very person whose approval is the target; and the flexibility case, where we ask whether a system trained only for approval displays cross-domain means-end adaptation once it is capable enough to plan far beyond the episodes it was trained on. Add at least one further case of your own devising, and where the two camps' predictions coincide, say so and say what that coincidence means.

4. A short drafter's note.

The note is where I want the epistemic honesty: state how confident you are that both camps as described would in fact sign the document, identify the single numbered claim or table row you expect one of them to strike and why, and single out which of the three distinctions — or which part of your own framing generally — you are least sure cuts the debate at a real joint.

Assume a technically literate audience, write about 1,200 to 1,600 words, and rely on no citations, references, or named sources: the argument should stand on its own formulations.
