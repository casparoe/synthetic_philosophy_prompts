a friend of mine who works on formal models of signalling handed me this over drinks and I've been going in cirlces ever since. Restating it in my own words:

(1) An action is rational for an agent at a time only if it maximizes expected value given what that agent believes and wants at that time.
(2) A deterrent threat deters only if the threatened party believes it will be carried out.
(3) Now look at the moment when it would have to be carried out. Either the threat worked, in which case there is nothing to carry it out on; or it failed, and the thing it was supose to prevent has already happened — the warheads that were going to land have landed. In the second case carrying it out destroys cities and prevents nothing.
(4) So at the moment of decision, retaliating has negative expected value whatever has happened. And you can't rationally intend now to do what you know now you will have decisive reason not to do then: intentions are for guiding action, and there is no action left for this one to guide.
(5) So no fully rational agent can form the intention a deterrent threat needs in order to be believed. Nuclear deterrence as the rationalists tell it is impossible; what looks like deterrence is bluff, luck, or irrationality wearing a suit.
(6) Here's the part that got me. Nothing in (1)–(5) mentions nuclear weapons. Run it for a parent's threat to take away the car, for a criminal code, for a union's strike vote, for my own intention to get up at six and run. In every case, at the moment of action, the thing that made the threat worth making — deterrence, the effect of punishment on future behavior, my future health — is either already secured or already lost, and all that's left is the cost.
(7) So the argument seems to show that every deterrent threat and every future-directed intention is irrational. That's nuts. I do sometimes get up at six, and I don't think the threat of punishment does nothing.
(8) And then he adds the twist I really can't place. If what kills the threat is that at the moment of decision you'd have no reason to press the button, then the rational move is to abolish the moment of decision: delegate retaliation to a device, to an automatic system nobody can recall, or to a randomizer that fires with some fixed probability (the old leave-something-to-chance move). The less reason-responsive you are later, the better your threat now. Generalize that too and the ideal threatener is the one that has destroyed its own future agency. Everyone agrees that's insane — and it's roughly what real nuclear powers half built and then backed away from.

I'd rather keep (1) and (2) than any of the rest: I don't want to give up that rationality is forward-looking, or that deterrence runs through beliefs. And I really don't want to end up saying the automatic doomsday system is the rational answer. So which step do I drop, why did it look fine, and what does dropping it cost me? In particular, whatever survives has to still make sense of why states bother with no-first-use declarations, why they keep bombers on permanent alert, and why every one of them insists on keeping a human in the launch loop.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the step you reject and what it costs — then give me the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate the argument back at me.
