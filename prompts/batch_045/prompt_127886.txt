Last night I had one of those arguments that starts at the kitchen table and ends with both people restating their position more loudly: my friend and I both accept that in a series of ten thousand colour chips running imperceptibly from clear red to clear orange, chip 1 is red, chip 10,000 is not, and no observer can locate the change — but we draw completely different conclusions from that. She thinks the appearance of vagueness is exhausted by our ignorance of a boundary that is really there; I think the boundary is missing and that this is a fact about our language rather than about our evidence. We then got stuck on a case I invented to press her, and I have not been able to formulate her reply to my satisfaction: suppose the chips are machine-printed and the machine's own log recorded that the cut-off point was fixed by a hardware random number generator, then the log was deleted and no physical trace of the chosen number survives anywhere in the world. Intuitively there is some chip that is the last red one, nobody can ever know which, and there is no interesting higher-order question about whether the cut-off itself is sharp. My friend thinks this settles everything; I suspect the case merely builds a sharp boundary into the setup by stipulating an artificial predicate, and so is not a sorites at all. I want something better than our stalemate, and I would rather have an instrument than an essay.

Could you build me a decision tree for a small reading group (six to eight leaves, one internal question per node) that sorts a person from those three shared data points — chip 1 is red, chip 10,000 is not, nobody can tell where the change is — to a named position on vagueness? Required leaves: epistemicism; supervaluationism; the paraconsistent mirror image of supervaluationism, in which borderline cases are true on some precisifications and false on others and so both true and false; degree-theoretic or fuzzy treatments; contextualist treatments that let the standards shift with conversational context; and the view that denies there is any fact of the matter about the predicate's extension even at the level of admissible sharpenings. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from this list: first set out the two or three independent axes that actually do the work (whether the predicate's extension has a sharp boundary, whether classical logic and bivalence survive, whether the indeterminacy is located in our linguistic practice or in the world, how higher-order vagueness is handled), show which combinations of answers are available, and only then place the real views in that space, naming any unoccupied cell and telling me for each whether it is empty because it is incoherent, because it is merely unattractive, or because nobody has bothered with it. Where two leaves cannot be separated by any question you can honestly ask, say so explicitly rather than inventing a distinction.

Two constraints and a few ingredients. First: the questions themselves should be concrete cases, not self-classification, and they should avoid the words vague, borderline, truth value and bivalence in their wording — nobody should be able to tell from a question which leaf it leads to, and nobody should be sorted by which label they like. Second: no citations, no suggested reading, no footnotes; I want the argument, not the literature. The cases I would like used, in addition to the chip series: the machine-printed series with the deleted log described above; a statutory term such as "reasonably safe" or "resident" whose intended application was fixed by a committee whose minutes were then lost, so that there is a stipulation but no access to it; and a case of two equally well-informed speakers who agree on every observable chip yet disagree about a chip in the middle, where the question is whether either of them has made a mistake and what kind. If you think one of my three cases is rigged or begs the question against one leaf, keep it anyway and say so at that node — that is exactly the kind of thing I want flagged. Each leaf should also carry one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and I would like you to identify which single node in the finished tree is doing most of the real work separating epistemicism from its rivals.
