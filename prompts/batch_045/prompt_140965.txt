I teach a final-year option here at Marchmont on interpretation and authority, and a good deal of the term is spent on the textualism-versus-purposivism dispute as it is conducted about statutes: what it even means to take the words on their own, whether a collective body can have an intention worth calling one, how a purpose can be described at different levels of generality so that almost any text can be made to serve almost any outcome. What prompts me to write is that I now have to decide one of these questions myself. I sit on the Standing Committee on Outside Professional Activities as the Senate's academic member, and we have a case to rule on at the end of the month.

Ordinance 31 provides that no member of the academic staff shall, without the prior consent of the Committee, receive any remuneration in connection with the teaching of any person who is a registered student of the University. It was introduced in 1937 after a scandal about private coaching colleges that sold candidates preparation for Marchmont's own examinations, and the Council minutes of the day speak of stopping staff from profiting by teaching their own students towards their own papers. The case concerns a colleague in Economics who built, in his own time and largely at his own expense, a short online course in statistics for managers, open to anyone at a fee; of some four thousand people who have enrolled, eleven happen to be registered students of Marchmont, none of them in his department and none of them reading anything the course touches. He declared it on his annual return, and the Committee's administrator told him by email that it fell outside the Ordinance. An anonymous complaint has now arrived, and we must rule. Three things divide us. First, the words, taken on their own and with no purpose in view, are plainly satisfied: he took money for teaching people who are registered students — and the same reading would seem to catch the royalties several colleagues draw from textbooks bought by our own students, which nobody believes the Ordinance is about. Second, the Council debated an amendment in 1971 that would have confined the Ordinance to teaching in preparation for any examination of the University; it was defeated by fourteen votes to eleven, and we cannot agree whether that shows the Council deliberately chose the wider words or shows nothing much at all. Third, over dinner after our last meeting two of us who wanted the same outcome disagreed completely about the reason: one held that the 1937 words should be read as having the applications they would then have been expected to have, the other that they should be read as speaking to circumstances the drafters could not have envisaged; and a third of us doubted whether that choice is a question about what the Ordinance means at all, as opposed to a question about what an administrative body like ours ought to be doing. Several members also think that whatever we write will be treated by the administrator as binding guidance in future cases, and that this is a reason to say as little as the case requires; I am not persuaded.

I have to circulate a draft decision paper before the meeting. Please work as someone who has both drafted institutional regulations and taught the philosophy of law at a high level, and think carefully rather than quickly: before you begin drafting, spend a little time listing the readings of the Ordinance that are genuinely available and, for each, what else it would drag in — textbooks, open courses, paid external examining, and so on — and only then commit to one. The paper should be something the Committee could actually vote on, not a survey of the jurisprudence: it should state a concrete course of action with a time frame, it should say plainly whether the Ordinance was breached and what is to follow from that, and it should set out the strongest available argument for the reading it does not adopt, since that is the argument I expect to be pressed hardest in the room. Please do not cite cases, statutes or scholarly works — I have all of that to hand, and what I need from you is the reasoning rather than the references. Use British English throughout, and organise the paper under the following headings, in this order: "Recommendation" (around 150 words, leading with the bottom line and any dates); "The readings available, and what else each of them catches" (around 300 words); "Why I recommend the reading I do" (around 300 words); "The strongest argument for the other reading" (around 250 words); "How much, if anything, this decides for future cases" (around 150 words); and finally "How confident I am, and the point I am least sure of" (around 120 words), in which you should say how confident you are in the recommendation overall and identify the single step in the argument you would most expect a good objector to go for.
