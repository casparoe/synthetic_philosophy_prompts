The question:

For the last four weeks we have worked with a toy model of karma as a theory of moral desert. Call it the Ledger Model. It has five commitments. (1) Karmic fruit is produced by a natural, impersonal causal law; no deity, judge or administrator assigns anyone their suffering. (2) Only intentional action generates fruit: it is the volition (cetana) that does the work, so genuinely accidental harms generate none. (3) The severity of the fruit is fixed by the moral quality and strength of the volition, not by the size of the downstream consequences. (4) Fruit may mature in a later life, and there is no reset between lives: the ledger carries over. (5) Because the ledger carries over, every episode of suffering an agent undergoes is the maturation of some earlier intentional action of that same agent-stream. We also discussed two objections. The arbitrariness objection: the distribution of suffering looks morally arbitrary, and karma is advertised as removing the arbitrariness by showing that suffering is deserved, but the model seems only to push the arbitrariness back a step, since we can now ask why the agent performed the earlier action in the first place. The no-self problem: the tradition that gives us the model also denies a persisting self, and it is unclear what is left to carry a ledger across lives once the self goes. Your task, in 600 to 900 words: take a stand on whether the Ledger Model can underwrite a genuine claim of desert, and defend it against whichever of the two objections you take to be the more serious. You may revise the model, but only if you say precisely what you are changing and why.

The student's answer:

The Ledger Model can underwrite a genuine claim of desert, but only if we are careful about what desert comes to once the judge is removed. On commitment (1) no one assigns the suffering; nothing is inflicted on anyone for a reason. The fruit simply arrives. That means the model is not in the business of retribution at all. Retributivism, as we read it, is the claim that it is intrinsically good that a wrongdoer suffer and that some agent may therefore inflict suffering on them. With no inflicter, there is no retribution, and so the right way to read commitment (5) is as a causal-explanatory thesis: suffering is fully explained by prior volition. Once we see that, the arbitrariness objection more or less dissolves. The objection says the distribution of suffering looks arbitrary and that karma is supposed to take the arbitrariness away. But on the causal reading we do not need the distribution to be morally intelligible, only nomically intelligible, and commitment (3) supplies that: the severity of whatever happens is a strict function of the quality and strength of the earlier volition. So suffering is never undeserved, and it is never arbitrary either — it is the exact causal upshot of what the agent willed, and the agent did the willing. I take the no-self problem to be the more serious of the two, but it only bites if you take the no-self view in its strongest form. If there is literally nothing that persists across lives, then there is no ledger to keep. But the version we read in week three accepts causal continuity without a persisting self, and a causally continuous stream is enough to carry a ledger: what matters for desert is that the same stream that willed later undergoes, not that there is a soul doing both. So commitment (4) is safe, and the objection fails. One consequence is worth drawing out, because I think it is a real cost of the model rather than a virtue. Given that (4) says there is no reset and the ledger carries, a stream that has been going for a very long time must be carrying considerably more undischarged debt than one that began recently, and so the model predicts that the longest-lived streams will on average be the worst off. That is counterintuitive, and I cannot remove it without giving up (4), which the model needs. A final point in the model's favour: because the fruit resembles the seed — commitment (3) says that what you get back matches in kind what you did — an agent can in principle work out what she did by attending to what she is undergoing, which makes karmic desert epistemically accessible in a way that the deserts of ordinary retributivism are not. That is a genuine advantage over the views we read in the first three weeks, and it is why I think the model survives both objections once (5) is read causally rather than retributively. I would revise the model in one respect only: volitions performed under coercion should accrete less fruit, because as it stands the model looks like it punishes people for what they were forced to do.
