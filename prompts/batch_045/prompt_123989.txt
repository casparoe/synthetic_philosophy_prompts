I left school at eighteen, have spent the last two years reading about philosophy of mind on my own, and I run a small discussion group that meets monthly in the back room of a pub. Next month's session is mine to lead, and I want to spend it on the argument that goes roughly like this: every physical happening has a complete physical cause; what I decide and want is not the same thing as a physical happening; a happening that already has a complete cause cannot have a second, independent one as well; so nothing ever happens because of what someone decided or wanted. I have ninety minutes, a whiteboard, and about eight people who are sharp but have read no philosophy — a retired engineer, a software developer, a nurse, two teachers.

Please do not write the session for me and do not just explain the argument; I want a plan for how I should build it up.

What I need from you is the architecture of the explanation. First, the order the pieces have to arrive in and what depends on what: which premise has to be genuinely conceded before the rest can bite at all, and which one, if I state it too early, will just sound like a stipulation I have smuggled in. I suspect I have to get them to feel the completeness of the physical story before I mention anything mental, but I'd like you to be more precise than that.

Second, the carrying example. I have been working with someone deciding to raise an arm to hail a taxi. Tell me what such an example has to have — a physical story that can plausibly be complete, a mental description that isn't just a redescription of the behaviour, no tempting third factor — and what it has to lack. In particular, should the mental cause be spread over time, a standing intention that survives a delay, rather than a single momentary urge? What does each choice buy and cost me?

Third, the misconceptions, in the order they will actually reach me, and the exact point in the sequence at which each should be defused: that the conclusion is that minds are illusions; that two causes can obviously both be real, as when two people push a stalled car; that introspection settles it, since I simply know I raised my arm because I wanted a taxi; and that we can just say the mental happening *is* a physical happening and go home. The software developer will certainly say that this is only the old programme-and-hardware point, and that a high-level description does real work without adding any energy. I need to know whether she is an ally or a trap, and where she belongs.

I also have an escape hatch of my own that I want you to test. It seems to me that if we count the effect thickly — not merely the arm's rising but the arm's rising *out of a desire to hail a taxi* — then the physical story does not obviously cover that effect, and there is something left for the mental cause to do. Is this a real reply, or does it onlypush the bump under the carpet? And if it is real, does it belong early, as something to head off, or late, as the live disagreement I should end on? One member may also propose that the mind does not push but *selects* among the outcomes physics leaves open; tell me whether that is a version of my thick-effect move or something I should openly decline to take up.

Finally: one question I can ask in the last five minutes that will show me whether someone has grasped the structure of the argument rather than merely been alarmed by it, and an explicit list of what I should refuse to go into at this level and say so out loud — I'm assuming free will, consciousness, and whether physics is really closed are all on that list, but tell me what else. Please write the plan in British English.
