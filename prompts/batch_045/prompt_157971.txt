So there's this guy in my city who hosts the local LessWrong-adjacent meetup — let's call him Doug — and for about a year now I've been quietly keeping a list of his verdicts on every decision-theory puzzle I can throw at him, because I genuinely can't reverse-engineer what he's running. He's read the Sequences more than once, rolls his eyes at causal decision theory, and talks about Hofstadter-style superrationality, but half the time he sounds like a straight evidential decision theorist and half the time he sounds like he's doing something closer to timeless/FDT-style reasoning, and he won't say which. His track record so far: one-shot prisoner's dilemma against an exact psychological duplicate of himself, no communication, both know the setup — cooperate; same, except the duplicate has been told it's "the copy" and he's "the original" — still cooperate; same, except the duplicate's last week of memories were overwritten with noise — cooperate, and he said the difference doesn't matter; same, except the duplicate's payoffs are 10% larger than his — cooperate; Newcomb's problem where the predictor is explicitly described as running a full simulation of him — one box; Newcomb where the predictor is a black box that's been right 99 times out of 100 and definately does not simulate him — he refused to commit and said he really doesn't know; a hitchhiker-type case where the driver reads faces and has never been wrong — pay; PD against a randomly picked stranger with no correlation whatsoever — defect; and voting in a national election — he votes, and says it's the same principle as the duplicate cases. The only time I got anything like a slogan out of him, he said the thing that matters is whether his deciding and the other guy's deciding are the same event happening in two places, and he got visibly annoyed when I said that sounds like plain evidential decision theory.

The case I want to spring on him at the next meetup is one where I think similarity, correlation, and simulation all come apart. Two agents, built independently by teams on opposite sides of the world, who have never been in contact, who are not psychologically similar in any interesting way, neither of whom simulates or predicts the other, and neither of whom causally influences the other — but there's a fact about the universe's initial conditions (work with me here; stipulate something superrdeterministic if you need a mechanism) that guarantees any two agents facing structurally identical decision problems make the same choice. So his cooperating and the other agent's cooperating are perfectly correlated, and that correlation is the only thing linking them: no shared algorithm, no shared reasoning, no causal arrow either way. I want to know whether Doug cooperates or defects, what rule he's implicitly running, and — this is the part I actually care about — what other rules fit his whole track record just as well and what each of them predicts here, since I don't think his verdicts so far pin anything down. My own candidate for a follow-up case that might separate the surviving rules is a PD against an agent running completely different code that is provably extensionally equivalent to his, same input-output function, different implementation; I can't tell whether that actually separates a "is it like me" rule from a "does its output depend on my algorithm's output" rule, or whether it collapses back into the same answer. Also tell me whether your prediction flips if the correlation in the new case is 90% instead of certain, because I suspect that's where the rules really diverge. Please answer in American English, and spare me the "here's what's actually rational" lecture — I want the read on Doug, not the object-level truth.
