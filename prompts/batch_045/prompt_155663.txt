hey — I'm writing up where I've landed on the Fermi paradox so a few people can take potshots at it before I post it, and I'd rather find the holes now than in the comments. Roughly my position, in no particular order:

1. The silence is a real puzzle, not something to shrug off. Billions of years of head start, plenty of planets, no reason to think we're first — a galaxy with anyone else in it should look settled, or at the very least partly reorganised for industrial purposes by now, and it doesn't. That stands in need of an explanation.

2. The best explanation is a late filter. Civilisations get to roughly our stage and then stop — they stall, or they destroy themselves — and this happens more or less always. I don't think the filter is behind us.

3. I don't buy the "they're here but we can't see them" family of stories: zoo, quarantine, they've gone virtual, they're asleep waiting for the universe to cool down a bit. Every one of them predicts its own undetectability, which is exactly why I'm suspicious of all of them.

4. The timescales aren't close. Once you've got self-replicating probes, or even just serious interstellar capability, filling a galaxy — or at least lighting enough of it up to be visible from across it — takes a blink next to the age of the galaxy. So "they exist but left no trace" isn't available to me.

5. I'm a mediocrity person in general. I don't want to assume our epoch, our species or our planet is special unless the evidence forces it.

6. I think we shouldn't be deliberately broadcasting — METI, active SETI, whatever you want to call it. The downside is catastrophic and the upside is next to nothing.

7. I think the practical upshot is that we should treat this as a fire alarm and put real resources into surviving whatever the filter turns out to be; existential-risk work is what the paradox calls for. And I think the reason we might be the exception is that we're the first civilisation to see the warning coming — nobody else knew it was there.

8. Finding microbial life on Europa or Enceladus would be the most encouraging discovery I can imagine, and I think it should make us more hopeful about our own future.

9. I also think the "we've barely looked" point is basically right: the slice of the search space SETI has actually covered is tiny, and I don't want to rest anything on non-detection.

I've got a suspicion that not all of this fits together but I genuinely can't tell where the problem is, so please audit it rather than lecture me. Which pairs actually pull against each other, ranked by how bad the tension is rather than all listed as equally serious; for each, what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would cost me; and please flag anything that only looks like a tension but really isn't, since I'd rather not cut something I don't have to. If there's a single revision that cleans up the most, say which one. British English please, and no citations or further reading — I'm writing this offline and I'll fill in names myself.
