I'm teaching a first-year seminar on skepticism this term, and my students respond to the brain-in-a-vat hypothesis in a way I've come to think of as the "engineering reflex": they grant that we could not tell from the inside, and then immediately start designing experiments — check whether the physics is globally consistent, look for rendering artifacts, count how many times the same stranger walks past the window — as though skepticism were an empirical hypothesis that a sufficiently well-funded team could finally rule out. I would rather hand them something that makes the point by indirection than argue with them for a fourth week, so I'm asking for satire rather than a handout.

Write an Onion-style news article — dateline, headline, and roughly 450 to 550 words of body — reporting that a large interdisciplinary team has announced conclusive proof that we are not brains in a vat. Set it at an invented institute (say, the Institute for Applied Veridicality), which has just completed a five-year, eight-figure "Externality Probe": subjects press a button, the laboratory server logs the press, the log is read back to them on a screen, and reported agreement between the log and the subjects' own memory stands at one hundred percent; a second arm of the study, in which a control group was told over breakfast that they were in fact envatted, produced no statistically significant change in anyone's confidence; a pre-registered replication, run by the same subjects, reproduced the finding; the peer reviewers were enrolled in the study; and the grant, which is renewable, required a reassuring result. Give me at least two pieces of quoted speech: the project director, upbeat and technical, and a rival — a skeptical philosopher at another university, or a member of the team's own funded red team — whose objection is quoted in a way that lets it land even while the article's voice smoothly dismisses it.

The constraints are what make this worth writing, so let me be exact about them. Every joke should come from the epistemology being right, not from the scientists being fools: the evidence is gathered through the very channel under suspicion; the vat was stipulated at the outset to be able to fake any test whatever, so every result was always available to it; the result is a Moorean gesture in a lab coat; and, if the piece is to be fair rather than cheap, the researchers should also have noticed — and announced as a selling point — the Putnam-style wrinkle that the sentence they are so pleased to have verified could not be true, or could not mean quite what it seems to mean, if it were false, together with the standard comeback to that wrinkle, which a well-briefed critic should get to make. I also want one character to notice, without the article noticing, that the envatted subjects' beliefs are perfectly reliable with respect to their own environment, so that the reliabilists in my seminar have something to chew on. Stay deadpan the whole way through: nobody says that the proof proves nothing, nobody breaks character or winks at the reader, the target is the practice of manufacturing reassurance and not the people who want it, and the closing line should let an attentive reader feel the trap snap shut without anyone's naming it. My students should be able to read it having read none of the literature and still get the point; I should be able to read it without wincing.
