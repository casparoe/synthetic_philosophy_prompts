hey — I've been trying to get my view on Ulysses contracts straight, the personal kind and the constitutional kind, because I'm presenting it to a reading group next month and I'd rather find the holes myself than have Dave find them. Roughly where I've landed:

(1) Self-binding is only justified when the later choice gets made under some identifiable impairment — craving, panic, mania, acute pain, whatever — and not merely because the later self wants something different. Being earlier gets you nothing by itself.

(2) So yes to: locked dispensing for someone in opioid addiction, a mandatory delay before cashing out a retirement account, handing your passwords to a friend before a known manic episode, court-enforced psychiatric holds. Though I'm genuinely unsure whether the last one belongs on this list or whether it's doing something completely different from the others.

(3) No to covenant marriage — the thing where a couple opts in at the time of marriage to a regime where getting out later is much harder. No to advance directives you can't revoke. And no to waiting periods the state imposes on everyone regardless of whether they chose them, the way a legislature makes everyone wait some number of days before certain purchases.

(4) But on the constitutional side I'm for real entrenchment: supermajority amendment rules, a court with genuine strike-down power, an independent central bank, an insulated civil service. And I want to justify it the same way — the demos ties itself to the mast against its own future populist episodes.

(5) I think the dead hand objection is basically a dud, and the usual replies to it (the people persist across generations, "we the people" is a trans-temporal actor, the constituent power is always available) are enough to kill it.

(6) Also: I think any constitution has to be genuinely re-authorizable by the living — some real mechanism of refounding — and that a founding document written when most of the population couldn't vote has a legitimacy problem that goes beyond what the document says. I can't tell how much weight this should carry against (4).

(7) The thing I actually lose sleep over I've been calling the ratchet: every act of precommitment changes who does the next precommitting, so a long enough chain of small self-bindings can walk you somewhere that no self anywhere along the chain would have chosen. I think that, not the dead hand, is the real objection to (4), and I hardly ever see it made. Related, what I call the mast problem: you can only tie yourself to something, and for a demos the thing you'd tie to is the very population you're trying to bind, so the picture loops back on itself.

So: does any of this hang together? Go through the pairs, rank the tensions by how bad they actually are rather than listing everything as equally broken, and for each one say which commitment would have to move and what that would cost me. Also tell me which apparent conflicts aren't real — I can see that (2) and (3) look flatly inconsistent, and that the state-enforced stuff in (2) looks like it contradicts my no-state-imposed-binding line in (3), but I suspect at least one of those dissolves and I can't tell which. If something has to go I'd definately rather it were a case verdict than a principle, but that's temperament, not an argument.
