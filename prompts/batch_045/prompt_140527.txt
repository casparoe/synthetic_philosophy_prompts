I'm defending the third chapter of my dissertation in two weeks — twenty-five minutes of talk, then a committee that gets as long as it wants — and my problem is that I've only rehearsed in front of my lab, where everyone already agrees with me. The chapter defends the claim that avoiding side effects can be made operational with almost no specification of value: instead of a utility function over outcomes, you need a fallback policy (what would have happened had the agent never been switched on), the set of states that policy could still have reached from the branch point, a short priced menu of restoration operations — move a rigid object a meter, restart a stopped process, replace a consumed unit of a resource, each with a unit price — and a single scalar budget the user sets. What I call the restoration cost of a state is the total price of the cheapest sequence of those operations that would get the world from that state back into the reachable set, with two clauses I'm proud of: the restorer may not use any affordance that exists only because of the agent's action, and the agent's own actuators are not on the menu. The proposal is to constrain expected restoration cost to stay under the budget. My positive argument is that this distinguishes changes that are cheap to undo from changes that are not, without the designer ever having to say which end states are good — and that both a raw state-distance penalty and a hand-tuned per-feature penalty collapse in opposite directions, the first by punishing any change at all and the second by needing the very value specification I'm trying to avoid. My negative result, which I present second, is that any penalty depending only on the final state and bounded above must either render some task infeasible or permit an arbitrarily damaging side effect in some environment.

The committee is four people, and I want the questions in their mouths rather than in mine. There is a senior roboticist who thinks the whole research program is reward shaping with extra ceremony and that the honest answer is better data and better world models; he will want to know how restoration cost is to be estimated from a learned dynamics model in a high-dimensional observation space, and why the agent won't simply act to make its own cleanup look cheap. There is a formal-methods person who will go straight for the negative result, restate it more precisely than I stated it, and ask whether anything survives when the state is only partially observed or when the branch point is chosen differently. There is a decision theorist from the business school who thinks in counterfactuals and price systems and who will say that a priced menu of operations plus a budget is a utility function with the serial numbers filed off, that prices inherit every index-number problem cost-benefit analysis ever had, and that my counterfactual depends on when the agent is imagined to have refrained. And there is a philosopher of value, hostile to the framing of the whole field, who will press the point that side effect is a normative word, that the choice of state variables is where all the value went, and that a measure which gives zero impact to doing nothing has simply built a preference for the status quo into a formula that claims to be value-free.

What I need is a prep sheet, not a defense of my view. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get, ordered by how much damage they'd do rather than by my chapter's structure, each one phrased the way that particular person would phrase it, with a two-minute reply I can give without notes and an honest note on where the reply is soft — including, for the record, that the step in my negative result where I move from boundedness to something like continuity over a discrete state space is where I'm least confident and would rather not be examined. Tell me what I should concede in the first two minutes, before anyone has to ask for it, since conceding early is cheap and being dragged is not. Tell me the one reply that has to land for the chapter to be worth defending at all. And give me the question I'm hoping nobody asks — I have a suspicion it involves the fact that restoring to some reachable state rather than to the state that would actually have occurred lets an agent satisfy the constraint by moving the world to a worse reachable one, or by cheaply removing the thing that would have registered the difference — and tell me what I should say if someone does ask it.
