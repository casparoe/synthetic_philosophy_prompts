I'm putting the finishing touches on a chapter about objective chance and rational credence, and before I hand it to a reading group I'd like a consistency check from someone who doesn't already know where I think the trouble is. Here is the package, more or less as I would defend it.

(1) The Principal Principle: my credence in A, conditional on the hypothesis that the objective chance of A at t is x together with whatever admissible evidence I have, ought to be x. And I take the history of particular fact up to t, in full detail, to be admissible.

(2) Chances do not require indeterminism. A world can be deterministic and still contain non-trivial chances — think of a gas whose evolution is fixed by the micro-dynamics but whose chance of spreading into the far corner of the room comes from a statistical postulate. But I also hold that in such a world knowledge of the exact total state of the world at t has to count as inadmissible: if it were admissible, the future would be certain given what I know and the chances would drop out of the picture, and I don't want to say they drop out.

(3) Chances supervene on the whole mosaic, future included. What the chance of this atom's decaying tomorrow is depends partly on how the rest of the world's history goes, tomorrow and after; chances are what fall out of which overall description of the total history best balances simplicity against informativeness.

(4) The only inadmissible evidence is evidence about whether A itself occurs, direct or indirect. In particular, evidence about what the chances are at times other than t is admissible, and has to be — otherwise the Principle goes idle in exactly the statistical cases where I most need it.

(5) I'm attracted to a functionalist gloss on all this: what makes a quantity a chance is largely its occupying the role the Principal Principle describes, so a quantity that systematically misled anyone who deferred to it would not deserve the name.

(6) Call a process frequency-locked when a finite run of tosses is governed by a law saying that at every toss in the run, the objective chance of heads equals the proportion of tosses in the entire run that land heads. Take a run of exactly one thousand tosses in which exactly seven hundred land heads, so the chance of heads at every toss is 0.7 — and notice that it stays 0.7 however the run has gone so far, because the proportion is fixed by the run as a whole. You are standing at toss 500. You know the law, you know the chance of heads now is 0.7, and you have the record: the first 499 tosses produced 400 heads. It follows that exactly 300 of the remaining 501 tosses are heads, which makes heads on toss 500 roughly a sixty per cent prospect. My verdict: set your credence at 0.7 anyway. The arithmetic that gets you to sixty per cent is precisely the sort of reasoning that deference to chance is supposed to cut off, and a chance you have to second-guess with bookkeeping is not doing its job.

(7) Two worries I can't bring into focus. First: I'm a Bayesian about updating and I think you should always use all of your evidence, and deference to chance looks like a licence to ignore part of it. Second: making chances relative to a time and to an evidence situation looks as though it makes them something less than fully objective.

What I want is an audit. Go through (1) through (7) and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only look that way, ranked by severity rather than flagged uniformly; for each real tension, state precisely what would have to be given up and what the cheapest repair would cost me elsewhere in the package; and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Tell me which of my two worries in (7) survives inspection. Then run whatever package you recommend against the frequency-locked case and say whether my verdict in (6) survives it — and if you think the case itself is defective, that the law I describe could not be a genuine chance law, or that it collapses chances into frequencies, say so plainly and explain why, since that would move the problem rather than solve it.

Please argue for one resolution rather than surveying the options: I'd rather be told which commitment you would drop and why than be handed a menu. And write in American English.
