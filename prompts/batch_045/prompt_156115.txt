So: you're a philosopher of mind who works on extended and embodied cognition, and I want you to read the film through the extended mind thesis. Before you start writing, brainstorm for a bit — sketch the scenes you might use, list the strongest objections to your own reading, and only then commit to it. Where I'm coming from, so we're on the same page. The thesis (the Otto-with-a-notebook style view, parity principle and all) says that when an external resource plays the right functional role and meets the usual conditions — reliably available, easy to consult, automatically endorsed, and in some versions consciously endorsed earlier on — it's literally part of the agent's mind rather than a tool coupled to it. The standard pushback is that coupling isn't constitution: the notebook isn't conscious, doesn't have its content in the way a belief does, isn't governed by the same dynamics, so calling it a belief is a category mistake dressed up as a discovery.

Leonard's tattoos and his annotated Polaroids look like a textbook case, and I definately don't want a piece that just says "his notebook is his memory, thesis confirmed". Here are the three things I keep going around on, and I want you to take them seriously and tell me which ones actually land.

First, call it the flicker problem. Leonard's beliefs seem to blink in and out of existence depending on which Polaroid happens to be in his pocket that hour — lose the photo, and the "fact" is gone; find it again, and it's back. Beliefs aren't supposed to be that hostage to a contingent bit of the environment. Is that a reductio of extending the mind that far outward, or should the externalist just bite the bullet and say yes, some beliefs really are that fragile, and our ordinary picture of belief as stable is an idealization about people with working memory?

Second, call it re-inscription. Leonard doesn't only consult his notes, he re-authors them: every time he meets a Polaroid he writes a new caption, and the caption is shaped by what he wants to be true in that hour — the Natalie captions, the "don't believe his lies" business, whatever Teddy tells him to write down. So the store isn't a fixed record being read; it's a record being continuously rewritten by a system with no memory of having written the previous version. The standard conditions seem built for stores that are stable and that the agent merely reads. Does that loop break the "trust" condition outright, or does it just make Leonard an unusually revisionary agent — like a diary you keep rewriting so that your past self always agrees with you?

Third, call it the manipulation symmetry. If the photo in Leonard's pocket is part of his mind, then Teddy writing on it, or Natalie staging the scene with the pen and the picture, isn't someone hiding evidence from a person — it's a direct intervention in another person's mental states, closer to implanting a belief or gaslighting than to lying. And the film's horror seems to run on exactly that: what's done to Leonard feels like something worse than deception. So is the movie quietly giving the externalist an argument — the horror of what Teddy does only has the shape it has if the notes are part of Leonard's mind — or is it exploiting the thesis's ugliest consequence in a way that should make us suspicious of the thesis?
