I'm closing a graduate reading group on collective decision-making, and the last session is on the discursive dilemma. I want to hand out a poem rather than a summary, and I'd like the form to carry the argument: a sestina — six sestets plus the three-line envoi — set not in a courtroom but in the instance my group built for itself. A three-person faculty search committee must decide whether to make an offer, and its own stated rule is that an offer requires both that the candidate is excellent and that the field is one the department is hiring in. Every member votes sincerely and every member's three votes are perfectly coherent; the incoherence appears only in the tally, which says yes to excellence, yes to the field, and no to the offer — a combination nobody at the table holds.

The poem needs to get four things right without lecturing: that no one is being strategic, confused, or insincere; that the two natural escapes — read off the premises and derive the verdict, or simply count the votes on the verdict — give opposite answers; that the premise route buys consistency by letting the group's verdict depend on more than the votes on the verdict; and that what is left on the menu is worse: let one person decide, or restrict which combinations of views may be put to a vote at all. Let the six end-words between them carry the moving parts — the two premises, the verdict, the rule that binds them, the counting, and the view that belongs to no one — so that each sestet feels like the same six considerations reshuffled and no nearer a decision. Where strict repetition of an end-word would force a line into nonsense, bend it and tell me where. Give the last word to the envoi: all six words in three lines, no verdict pronounced, no moral about committees.
