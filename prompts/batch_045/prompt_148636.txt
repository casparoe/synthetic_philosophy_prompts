OK so this came up at work and I've been going in circles on it. We're writing a funding recommendation and the rule we've been told to apply is basically cost-per-QALY: one year in full health = 1 unit, death = 0, intermediate health states get a number between 0 and 1 from those standard-gamble / time-trade-off questionnaires, you add up the QALYs across everybody, and a QALY is a QALY no matter whose it is — no bonus for being severely ill, no penalty for being rich, no discount for being 85. (NICE in the UK is the famous version, with a fixed pound figure per QALY as the ceiling, plus special higher allowances for end-of-life drugs.) Meanwhile in transport and environmental regulation the equivalent agencies do cost-benefit off willingness-to-pay: how much money people say they'd pay for a small cut in their own risk. That obviously weights a rich person's safety more heavily, and everyone in health policy thinks importing that into treatment decisions would be monstrous. So we add up utility across people in one place and refuse to let money do the comparing in the other, and I can't tell whether that's a deep consistency or just turf.

Here's the bit I actually want help with. A colleague who did some philosophy says the whole exercise is meaningless bcause utility functions are only determined up to positive affine transformation — so there's no fact of the matter about whether my mild chronic back pain is worse than your bad migraine, and adding across heads is incoherent. But that argument seems to kill every cost-benefit analysis ever done, and we plainly do make these judgements all the time, so "meaningless" can't be the right verdict? Separately, I had a fix in mind: anchor each person's scale to their own range — best possible life = 1, worst possible life = 0 — and then the units are comparable and you're done. Someone said that's a known move with known problems, but wouldn't tell me what they were.

So: what does the cost-per-QALY practice actually presuppose? I want the implicit theory reconstructed, and I want to know which parts are strictly required and which it merely sits well with. As far as I can tell it needs at least: that wellbeing (or health-related quality of life) comes in comparable cardinal degrees; that there's a common zero and a common unit across people; that gains to different people just add; that a QALY gained by someone starting from a terrible baseline is worth the same as one gained by someone starting from a mildly annoying baseline; and that distribution doesn't itself matter. Which of those is load-bearing for the practice, which is an optional extra the practice could drop without collapsing, and are the end-of-life/severity allowances and the refusal to use willingness-to-pay consistent with the rest of it or do they bolt on a different theory? Then: what would concretely have to change in the practice if we replaced that implicit theory with (a) a view where we only ever have partial or ordinal comparability between people, (b) a view where the badly off get strictly more weight per unit of benefit, or (c) the "impartial spectator who doesn't know which person they'll turn out to be" style of justification?

End the whole thing with a single machine-readable line: \answer{...} containing one short phrase naming the commitment you think is genuinely indispensable to the practice — or the single word "none" if you think it can be rebuilt on purely ordinal / Pareto-style grounds.
