Hi — I've got to give a fifteen-minute framing talk at a one-day workshop on brain emulation and neuromorphic computing that I'm helping organise in the spring, and I want to do it on what von Neumann would have made of the last fifteen years. I know that sort of question usually gets done badly, as an excuse for the speaker's own opinions, so I want it tied tightly to what he actually argued.

Here's my probably-shaky memory of his position in the little book that came out of the Silliman lectures in 1958 — correcting me where I've got it wrong is probably the most useful thing you can do for me:

- the nervous system is digital at the level of the single neuron, but not straightforwardly so, there's a genuine analogue component, so it's a hybrid rather than either clean case;
- the components are slow and low-precision compared with the valves he was used to, and the brain buys reliability out of parallelism and redundancy — many sloppy channels rather than a few accurate ones;
- the brain isn't running anything like formal logic or mathematics, those are a late cultural invention, and whatever its native code is it's nearer to statistics;
- a great deal of what looks like learning is actually inherited, a big chunk of memory is there at birth as an initial specification of the wiring and of how to work it.

Plus, from the automata side of his work, the idea that there's a threshold of complication below which making things more complicated just degenerates and above which complexity can take off.

The three things I want his reaction to:

1. Large language models. Enormous networks, weights starting from random noise, trained on text at industrial scale, using very high-precision arithmetic on extremely reliable hardware. No inherited structure at all. Given the points above, would he read scaling laws as confirmation of his threshold idea, or would he say the analogy breaks because there's no description-plus-copier here, nothing is inherited and no acquired complication accumulates, so scaling isn't evolution in any sense he'd recognise? Would he expect a wall, or a take-off?
2. Neuromorphic hardware and the wetware people — low-precision in-memory computing, spiking chips, and labs growing cultured neurons on electrode arrays to do computation. Which of these would he think actually cashes out his reliability-from-unreliable-parts point, and would he think biological hardware is a dead end for engineering?
3. Connectomics and whole-brain emulation. We now have complete wiring diagrams for small animal brains and there are serious projects aiming to simulate a mammalian one. Given the inherited-memory point, would he think a wiring diagram is the interesting object, or that it leaves out most of what matters?

A couple more things, please. Which of the three would he find most confirming and most disappointing, and why. Which of his own claims would he have to give up if he saw modern neuroscience — I'm thinking of the all-or-none neuron versus graded dendritic computation and neuromodulation, which feel like they'd push him further towards the analogue pole. And what would he have wanted to measure, if you dropped him into a lab tomorrow.

On confidence, since I'm going to say this out loud to a room of people who know the field: mark clearly which bits are him, on the record, which are extrapolation from commitments he definitely held, and which are you inventing a plausible opinion for him. Give me a rough confidence on each of the three verdicts. I'm after the argument, not references. And if you think the whole exercise is a bad idea — because he died in 1957 with the manuscript unfinished, or because those brain views were speculative even by his standards, or because I've got him wrong in a way that makes the question unanswerable — say that plainly instead of writing me a polite fictional interview. I'd rather scrap the talk and talk about something else.
