I am finishing a novel whose narrator is David Hume, conveyed by a device I shall spare you into the present day, and I cannot write one long scene until I am confident about what he would actually say in it. I would be grateful for your help with that.

The scene: Hume is drawn into an argument with a physician-ethicist who runs an assisted-dying assessment service in a jurisdiction where assistance is lawful only for terminal illness. She is not a villain and not a scold. She is humane, evidence-minded, and guided by three working assumptions I take to be standard in her field: that a wish to die is, in almost every case, a symptom of a treatable condition rather than a considered judgement; that suicidal states are typically transient, which is why restricting access to means, and in the last resort detaining people without their consent, saves lives; and that the only suffering a decent society may lawfully answer with death is suffering that is already terminal.

The case she puts to him is this. A woman of fifty-four has a progressive, painful, non-terminal condition. Over two years she has made her arrangements calmly and in writing, has refused a treatment that would extend her life at the cost of the capacities she says make it hers, and has been assessed as competent under every test the service applies. She has been refused. The physician asks Hume, seriously and without condescension: on your own principles, is this refusal defensible, and if not, where exactly does it fail?

What I need is an account of Hume's answer worked out in enough detail to become dialogue. Please work under the following conditions.

First, keep him inside his own machinery. Do not convert him into a rights-based autonomy theorist: no dignity, no self-ownership, no my life, my choice. His resources are the three duties his essay on suicide examines — to God, to society, and to oneself — his treatment of utility and agreeableness as the sources of merit, the standpoint of general sympathy, his distinction between the calm and the violent passions, and his argument that if suicide were a rebellion against the order of nature then so is the diverting of a river. Show me which of these carry the weight in this case and which buckle.

Second, take her three assumptions one at a time. The claim that a death-wish is ipso facto a symptom is where I expect his calm/violent-passions distinction to do real work, and I want to see how far it takes him before it runs out. The transience claim is empirical, and he will ask for evidence and for the rules by which it was gathered — but I also suspect a tension he should notice: a philosopher who held the self to be nothing but a bundle of perceptions in rapid succession cannot comfortably say that your future self will thank you. Tell me whether he sees this and how he escapes it, or whether he does not escape it. The terminal-illness line is where I most expect him to be alien rather than prescient, since his own argument permits that a person who is a burden to others may do well to remove himself. Does he hold to that when the burden is carried by a welfare state rather than a family? And what does he say to the objection, which my physician will certainly raise, that talk of burdens cheapens the lives of people who are in fact dependent on others?

Third, be honest about the limits. Where his apparatus has nothing to say, or where anything he says would have to be borrowed from us, say so plainly and stop. I would rather write a Hume who falls silent than one who anticipates disability-rights critique or evidence-based psychiatry.

Fourth, two smaller matters. Give me his manner: dry, sociable, ironic, contemptuous of what he would take to be a new priesthood of medicine, yet neither a nihilist nor a man who has not considered how much a failing body can be asked to bear — remember that he wrote the few pages of autobiography printed after his death in the knowledge that he was dying, and that he withdrew his essay on suicide from publication and left its appearance to others. And flag, for my benefit as the novelist, the two or three places where it would be easiest for me to cheat by making him sound like a twenty-first-century liberal, and why each would be false to him.

I would like 900 to 1,200 words, in continuous prose rather than lists, since I need to hear the argument argued. Give me no citations, no reading lists and no quotations from the secondary literature: I have Hume's own texts to hand and no use for anyone writing about them. Refer to the essay simply as his essay on suicide; titles and dates are of no interest to me. Write as though he were in the room with her.
