You are a professional philosopher who specialises in epistemology and in Wittgenstein's late work, and who has taught graduate seminars on On Certainty often enough to know where students get stuck. Please think carefully about this, and begin by brainstorming the logical space of the debate before you settle on any structure or naming anything.

I convene a graduate reading group, and I want to open our first session with a diagnostic: a "which hinge epistemology do you actually hold?" instrument that sorts participants by their verdicts on cases rather than by the labels they would apply to themselves. So no item may ask directly whether hinge propositions are true, whether they are beliefs, whether they can be known, or whether Wittgenstein was a relativist; every item must be a concrete case with two or three answer options, phrased so that a student who has read the text once could answer it without knowing which position the answer leads to. I have some cases I want used: the gesture of holding up one's hands and claiming knowledge of it; a chemist working in the 1780s whose entire practice of explanation presupposes that a burning body gives off something it contained; an anthropologist who, after some years in the field, finds herself treating her informants' dream reports as a source of information about events she did not witness; a missionary and a diviner who cannot get any argument off the ground with each other at all; a patient in a psychotic episode who says his hands are not his and who has to be fed; a community in which, over three generations, everyone who treated some proposition as bedrock has died and whose grandchildren treat its negation as bedrock without anyone ever having argued for the change; and a speaker who asserts that the earth existed long before he was born, then concedes he has no evidence he could produce, and still refuses to take the words back. Add four or five cases of your own to those, for a dozen or so in total, choosing ones that force apart positions my list may leave together. Then give a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto positions.

Before you name any positions, I want you to set out the questions that actually do the separating, and the grid of answer-combinations they permit. I would suggest at least these four: whether a hinge can be assessed as correct or incorrect at all, and if so from where (from within the practice it makes possible, from some independent standpoint, or from nowhere); what a change in a community's hinges over time is — a revision of a belief, a replacement of one framework by another, or nothing that should be described in either way; what is supposed to be the source of a hinge's authority, if anything — the way the world is, our shared practices, our animal nature, or no source at all; and whether standing in the hinge relation has any characteristic phenomenology or is visible only in what we say and do. Lay out the cells that grid generates, and only then locate the real positions in them: the epistemic or entitlement-style reading, the knowledge-first or disjunctivist reading, the naturalistic reading on which hinges are certainties of animal behaviour rather than anything propositional, the grammatical or quietist reading, the therapeutic reading on which the whole set of questions is to be dissolved rather than answered, and whatever pluralist or relativist reading you think belongs. For each unoccupied cell, say in a sentence whether nobody is there because the combination is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it has merely gone undeveloped.

Two things I care about especially. First: last term our group kept collapsing two stances into one — the quietist stance that there is simply no question of assessing hinge propositions, and the pluralist stance that there are several systems, each as much in order as the next. Build the instrument so that a participant's pattern of answers separates those two, or, if you think no case can separate them, say so plainly and explain what the difference could then consist in. Second: end with a short note flagging any pair of leaves your instrument provably cannot distinguish, together with a sentence on what further question would be needed to split them. Please write in British English, assume the reader knows the text but no secondary literature, and do not supply any bibliography, reading list or citations — I want the structure and the cases, not sources.
