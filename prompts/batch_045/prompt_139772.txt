I teach a fourth-year seminar on naturalism and its discontents, and I wrote the final exam myself. One question asked students to work out what Quine's general position commits him to about moral values — a topic he wrote almost nothing about directly, so the answer has to be built out of his commitments about meaning, evidence, ontology and the status of philosophy. The assigned readings were only the two dogmas essay, *Word and Object*, *Ontological Relativity*, and *Epistemology Naturalized*; the exam was closed-book, and grading should rest on those, not on secondary literature. Two course devices matter here. First, all term we separated three questions students tend to run together: the semantics question (do moral sentences have cognitive content?), the inventory question (must a regimented total theory quantify over moral properties?), and the authority question (could a true moral judgment give anyone a reason to act?). Second, we drilled a small procedure I called the regimentation test: take a moral sentence, regiment it into first-order notation with its quantifiers explicit, list what its bound variables must range over, and then ask whether those entities are already in the inventory of our best science. Below is the question as it was printed, followed verbatim by one student's answer. My teaching assistants' marks on this answer are currently spread across almost the whole scale, so before I finalize the rubric and the grade boundaries I want an independent read from someone who has not seen my own view of it, which I am deliberately withholding.

**Question 3 (30 marks; suggested length 500 words).** Quine wrote almost nothing about ethics. Explain what, if anything, his general philosophical position commits him to about moral values, and be explicit where an apparent commitment is really just a plausible extension of his views by you. Address all three of: (a) whether his naturalism — reality to be identified and described from within science, with no prior philosophical tribunal — forces the conclusion that moral judgments are not truth-apt; (b) what his holism about evidence implies about whether and how moral judgments can be revised; (c) whether the inscrutability of reference, or ontological relativity, yields a relativism about moral values. Apply the regimentation test from seminar to one moral sentence of your choosing. Keep the semantics question, the inventory question and the authority question separate; collapsing them is the most common way to go wrong here.

**Student answer (Candidate 17).** Quine's naturalism is methodological rather than metaphysical. Reality is to be identified and described from within science; there is no prior philosophical tribunal standing outside science from which to certify or correct it, and Quine accepts the circularity of using scientific standards to vindicate science. So the complaint that moral values must go because *good* and *ought* appear nowhere in the vocabulary of physics rests on a misunderstanding: Quine uses *science* broadly, taking in psychology, economics, sociology and history, and he treats science as continuous with common sense rather than as a substitute for it. Physics is his paradigm case, not the whole inventory.

That settles nothing about the semantics question, however. What Quine rejected was the analytic-synthetic distinction; he retained the empiricist criterion by which a sentence has cognitive content only if experience can bear on it. Moral sentences predict no observation — no stimulation of the sense organs would confirm or disconfirm that stealing is wrong — so for Quine they lack cognitive content, and the surface grammar of moral argument conceals a clash of attitudes. Moral judgments are therefore not truth-apt.

Holism does not rescue them. Statements face the tribunal of experience as a corporate body rather than one at a time, so no moral sentence is confirmed or disconfirmed in isolation; and since any statement can be held true come what may if enough is adjusted elsewhere in the system, no moral sentence is immune either. Revisability is a property of the fabric as a whole, and holism gives us no reason to single out moral judgments as specially fragile or specially protected.

Ontological relativity then delivers the relativism one expects. Reference is inscrutable: the field linguist's word for a rabbit can be rendered as *rabbit*, as *undetached rabbit part*, or as *rabbit-stage*, with nothing in the evidence to choose among them. In the same way the reference of a moral predicate is indeterminate, so *torture is wrong* is true relative to one manual of translation and false relative to another, and no manual is more correct than any other. This is Quine's version of the familiar thought that moral judgments hold only relative to a conceptual scheme.

The regimentation test confirms it. A theory is committed to whatever its bound variables must range over; when we regiment our moral discourse, its variables range only over persons, acts and consequences. No moral property is among the things we must quantify over, so there are no moral properties; and if there is nothing for a moral predicate to stand for, moral sentences say nothing at all. The inventory question thus settles the semantics question, and the authority question never arises, since nothing that lacks content could give anyone a reason to act.
