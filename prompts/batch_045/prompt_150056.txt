I'm a senior counsellor at an outpatient addiction clinic and I chair the weekly conference where we decide what to do about what clients tell us about their own use — "I can stop whenever I want", "the test must be wrong", "I only drink when things are bad and things are fine now". About a dozen a week, ten or fifteen minutes each, junior staff with no philosophy background have to be able to run it, and anything that changes a treatment plan gets signed off by the clinical lead. So I want a decision procedure, not an essay on denial.

The problem is we use "in denial" for about six different states and it matters which one it is: the client knows and is lying to us; the client knows and simply can't act on it (ordinary weakness of will, nothing wrong with their beliefs); the client has arranged not to find out — skipping the blood draw, getting their partner to pick up the results and not tell them; the client genuinely never had the information; and the client is filling a gap (blackouts, fuzzy memory) with a story that isn't especially motivated by anything. I want steps that separate those and say what follows: when we push, when we drop it and move on, when we go and get more evidence, and when we stop and hand it to the lead because what's left is a values call about how hard to lean on someone.

Two constraints from what I've read about self-deception, which I want baked in rather than footnoted: it doesn't have to be something the person decided at some point, so no hunting for the moment they chose to fool themselves — look at what's sustaining the not-knowing right now; and once we've decided we can't take someone's word about their own state, we can't turn around and use their word as evidence about it. There's also a case I don't know what to do with: two of our groups have converged on a shared line ("none of us really has a problem, the clinic exaggerates") that nobody seems to hold on their own. Treat that as a case the procedure has to handle, not a footnote.

Give me the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure just stops with a no, and where it stops because the question is values and not facts. Then run it on two real cases: (1) a client with a positive screen who insists the lab mixed up the samples and has now asked his brother, who he lives with, to pick up his results so he never has to open the portal; (2) a client who says "I can stop any time, I've done it before" — and has in fact stopped for three months twice, whose use is down a lot but not zero, and who the junior staff all want to call denial. That second one, please give me the answer I won't like even if it means we drop the denial framing. And tell me straight what the procedure can't do.

No preamble: lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning.
