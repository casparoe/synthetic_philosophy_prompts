me and my housemate had a proper three-hour argument last night about whether thinking is basically just analogy-making, and this morning he sent me a link to some blog post with a message saying "this is the whole book in one paragraph so you can concede now". The book is Hofstadter and Sander's Surfaces and Essences. I read it and it feels maybe 80% right, but I think two of the sentences are smuggling in more than the view actually earns and I cant put my finger on which. Here's the paragraph (his blog post, not the book):

"Most of us think of analogy as a fancy rhetorical device that clever people reach for. Hofstadter and Sander argue it's the exact opposite: analogy-making is the mechanism behind every act of thought. Recognising the letter d in twelve different fonts is analogy. Understanding a sentence is analogy. A toddler pointing at a poodle and saying doggie is doing analogy. On this view a concept isn't a definition but a bundle of remembered instances, and to understand something new is to retrieve the stored case most similar to it — categorization just is analogical retrieval. What makes us smarter than a calculator is that our concepts are fluid: a situation can be recategorized, and one concept can slide into a neighbouring one, which is what they call slippage, and slippage is where creativity comes from. The upshot is that concepts really do have essences — deep, stable cores that survive when you strip away the surface — so the book is partly a defence of essences against people who think categories are just conventions. And since analogy is all there is, there's no room in the mind for anything like a rule: grammar, logical inference, arithmetic procedures, all of it is analogy wearing a disguise."

What I want to know is what someone who actually holds this view would accept here and where the popular version is cheating. I have a suspicion about the sentence where concepts become bundles of remembered instances and categorization becomes finding the most similar one — that reads to me like it quietly turns their view into plain similarity-matching, or just exemplar theory with a new name, which I'd have thought they'd reject. And the essences bit: I thought essences were supposed to be fluid and context-dependent in that book, not deep stable cores, but he says I'm being pedantic about one word.

There's a reason I care beyond winning the argument. He's using the paragraph to claim that large language models literally think, on the grounds that all they do is match new text against a huge store of old text, and if analogy is the whole of cognition then that's the same thing. I said the book would predict the opposite: something with no fluid recategorization and no slippage would be all surface and no essence. Whichever of us is right seems to turn on getting the paragraph right, so tell me which claim in it is actually doing the work, which one a defender of the view would object to hardest, and whether his LLM conclusion survives once the dodgy sentences are fixed.
