I sit on the standing revision committee for our national diagnostic manual, and our terms of reference require us to weigh what Hacking called looping effects: a category can change the people it is applied to, who then change the category, so we have also to weigh whether we are recording a kind or making one. Our recent record: we added persistent bereavement disorder, over objections that grief was being medicalised, noting that it is bounded by an identifiable precipitating event, that two neighbouring manuals had used it for eight years with prevalence no longer climbing, that bereaved associations asked for it, and that without a code no treatment was reimbursed; we refused problematic news monitoring despite reported impairment, on the ground that the behaviour is near-universal in milder form, has no marker and no treatment of proven benefit, and that even a research listing would recruit the worried well; we added compulsive gaming although it is equally near-universal in milder form and equally markerless, saying that a decade of clinics and self-identification had already formed the kind and that we were recording it, not inaugurating it; and we added a severe premenstrual syndrome code after a decade of campaigning by patient organisations, over objections that it medicalises a normal cycle, while in the same revision we dropped the separate high-functioning autism code despite a petition from thousands of adults diagnosed under it, on the ground that attachment to a label is no reason to keep a category whose boundaries do not hold. We have also cited Hacking's account of fugue in the 1890s as our cautionary case.

The proposal now in front of us is persistent sensory intolerance: clinically significant distress or avoidance triggered by ordinary sounds, textures or lights, lasting more than six months and not better explained by autism or an anxiety disorder. Our dossier says referrals have tripled in five years, that most self-referral now follows short-form video and symptom-checker sites, that clinicians report patients arriving with the draft criteria in their referral letters, that there is no biomarker and only two small uncontrolled trials of a sensory-integration protocol, that a large organised online community already uses the label, has its own vocabulary, and is lobbying for workplace and school noise accommodations — which in this jurisdiction can already be obtained through a general disability route without a diagnostic code — that an industry-funded sham-controlled trial will recruit only if a billable code exists, and that several neurodivergent advocacy organisations oppose inclusion as pathologising ordinary experience in people they say existing codes already cover.

What should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves — full inclusion, research listing, or rejection — and which of our precedents is the closest analog? Which rule have we in fact been running: record kinds that already exist rather than make them, wait until a kind has settled before coding it, decide by what the code unlocks downstream, or defer to the wishes of those who would be classified? Show where those rules come apart on this case rather than where they agree, state what principle each of the three available decisions would commit us to, and name the one further piece of evidence or the one further proposal that would best tell the rules apart — bearing in mind that the stability evidence our earlier decisions relied on came from other manuals or from settled lay practice, and that here the evidence may only be available after we have acted.
