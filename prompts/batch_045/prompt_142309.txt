In the graduate reading group I convene on the later Wittgenstein, one participant — a cognitive scientist who sits in and is unsympathetic to the whole enterprise — has been pressing the family analogy against the text rather than for it, and the rest of us are stuck.

The passage under discussion is the one where Wittgenstein asks what it is that all games have in common and answers, in effect, that we do not find one property shared by every case but a tangled net of similarities overlapping and criss-crossing, a similarity of one feature here and of another there, and that he proposes to call these family resemblances. Our visitor's objection is that a family is precisely not like that. What holds a family together, he says, is descent: a real, hidden, unobserved bond, in virtue of which the visible features do not merely co-occur but are to be expected to co-occur. The noses and the chins run together because of shared genes, and had you the genes you would have exactly the necessary and sufficient condition that Wittgenstein says is not there. So he offers a dilemma. Either family resemblance is a promissory note for an essence — anti-essentialism deferred rather than argued — or it abandons the hidden bond, and then it can say nothing at all about why the features cluster, why this nose keeps company with that chin. On either horn, he concludes, the analogy fails exactly where it was supposed to do its work, and we are entitled to go back to looking for whatever plays the explanatory role the genes play: a structural or innate account of what a concept is. He adds two corollaries. First, the image is not even self-exemplifying: belonging to a family has a hard criterion of membership, descent, so the source of the analogy is not itself a case of what it is used to illustrate, whereas a game presumably is. Second, the honest version of the point is available elsewhere anyway — on a descent-with-modification picture, a concept is like a biological species, where contingent historical descent together with whatever mechanism keeps the co-occurring properties homeostatic gives you a stable cluster without giving you a definition, which is, he says, an essence of a merely historical and contingent kind.

I want an audit rather than a verdict.

First, set the correspondence out explicitly: in the comparison as he is using it, what plays the role of the instances of the concept, of the shared features, of the missing common property, of the extension of the concept over time, of a speaker's competence in applying it, of correction and error, and of the mistake he takes Wittgenstein to be making.

Second, sort the disanalogies. Some will be inert for the use he makes of the analogy and some will be load-bearing; I want to know which are which and why. In particular: does it matter that a family is bounded by a criterion Wittgenstein wants to deny to concepts, that family membership is a biological fact rather than a status conferred by a practice, that a family has a determinate history and a determinate membership count while a family-resemblance concept is supposed to have open and negotiable boundaries, and that resemblance in a family is explained by a common cause while resemblance among games is not explained at all? And I want your judgement on one that cuts the other way: in a family everyone is equally a member while resembling the family face to very different degrees, which seems to give the analogy exactly the resources to accommodate the familiar datum that some instances of a concept are better examples than others. Is that a feature the analogy legitimately has, or does it smuggle in a notion of graded membership that the passage was not committed to and that Wittgenstein would have refused?

Third, say whether his conclusion survives. Distinguish, before you answer, at least three claims the analogy might have been doing work for: the negative descriptive claim that no single feature is common to all games; the claim about practice that we can use a concept correctly without being able to state its definition; and the methodological claim that the search for essences is a confusion in need of dissolution rather than a problem in need of a solution. Tell me which of these his dilemma actually touches, whether touching one of them is enough for the conclusion he wants, and what he is committed to on the horn he does not take.

Fourth, tell me whether there is a comparison that does the work better, and what each candidate costs. I have three in mind: a rope or thread whose strength comes from the overlapping of many short fibres rather than from any fibre running the whole length; a path worn across a field by repeated crossings in slightly different places; and an old city that has grown without a plan, with tangled quarters in the middle, later additions, and suburbs laid out on a grid — the comparison of language to an ancient city that I remember from elsewhere in the same book. For each, say what it preserves and what it loses, whether it escapes the clustering objection or merely relocates it, and whether it leaves room for the fact that belonging to a concept is not just a matter of resembling, since it is something we can get wrong, be corrected about, contest and revise.

Finally: does any of this reach the neighbouring analogy, the comparison of speaking a language to playing a game? A game has rules that could in principle be written down, a point, an outcome, and players who can lose; a language-game has no scoreboard, and on Wittgenstein's own account its rules are not the sort of thing that could be specified in advance for every case. If the objection transfers, show me how; if the two analogies stand or fall independently, tell me which one is carrying more of the real argument and why.
