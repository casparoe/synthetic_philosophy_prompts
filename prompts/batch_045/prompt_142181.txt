I convene a fortnightly graduate reading group on decision theory and rational agency — fourteen participants, mostly philosophy and theory-CS doctoral students with two economists, all of whom know the prisoner's dilemma and can state what a Nash equilibrium is. I have one ninety-minute session, no budget, no screens beyond the room's projector, and no appetite for a lecture.

What I want them to run into, rather than be told, is the move from choosing an action to submitting something that chooses on your behalf, and the way that dissolves the dominance argument for defection in a one-shot dilemma — and then, before they can settle into satisfied mutual cooperation, the discovery that what they achieved rested on something much thinner than mutual recognition.

My provisional design is three short rounds plus an optional fourth, with sealed submissions and random re-pairing each round: an anonymous baseline dilemma; then a round in which each person submits a program written in a tiny pseudo-code that may inspect the other's source at a small cost in points; then a round in which a meaning-preserving rewriter (renaming local variables, reordering independent clauses, reformatting) is applied, privately and at random, to one side of each pairing before its source is inspected — so that the obvious "cooperate if and only if the other source is character-for-character the same as mine" quietly stops working. The optional fourth round replaces inspection with a sandbox: you may run the opponent's program against a probe program of your own choosing and observe only its output.

What I need from you:

1. The pseudo-code itself: the smallest set of primitives — including some way for a program to refer to its own source and to the opponent's, and a conditional on their syntactic or behavioural relation — with the hard constraint that I can adjudicate any pair by hand, on paper, in under a minute. Include a worked adjudication of a pair in which both sides inspect, and a rule for malformed or non-halting submissions.
2. A payoff scheme, with the inspection cost calibrated so that neither blind cooperation nor free verification is trivially right, and a trivial stake — chocolate, not money.
3. The exact handout text and submission protocol, including how to stop out-of-band coordination and pre-agreed conventions, which would make the whole thing collapse into a handshake.
4. A debrief that maps specific moments onto specific claims: the invention of the identity check; the appearance of the bully program that cooperates only with its mirror image alongside the program that cooperates unconditionally, and the realisation that the equilibrium set contains the bad as well as the good; the recompiler round; and, if we get there, the point at which a program that cooperates with any probe becomes indistinguishable, from the outside, from one that cooperates only with cooperators.
5. An honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce: the self-reference is stipulated rather than derived, the existence of a fixed point is assumed rather than proved, and the set-up is not the same thing as acausal or superrational reasoning. Tell me which of these the facilitator must simply supply in words, and how to do that without it landing as a lecture.
6. What will predictably go wrong: formatting mismatches, someone taking exploitation personally, the recompiler round reading as a rigged gotcha, and the clock.

Finally, be explicit about where you are least confident that this design lands the point it is meant to land, which contested reading of the program-game result the design takes sides on, and what I should watch for during the session that would tell me to drop or swap a round. Participants should not hear the theoretical label until the debrief.
