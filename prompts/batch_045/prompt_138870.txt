For a column I'm writing on what a non-specialist reader is supposed to take away from the claim that some human trait is "highly heritable," I've drifted toward a position considerably more deflationary than the familiar set of caveats, and I'd rather have it stress-tested than confirmed. The position is this: heritability, whether broad or narrow, is a variance decomposition relative to an actual population living in an actual range of environments, and almost everything the public — and, I suspect, a fair number of researchers writing for the public — wants from it is something it cannot deliver. It is not a measure of how genetic a trait is in any sense that travels across populations or across environments; it sets no ceiling on what a feasible intervention could do, since a trait can be near-universally genetically caused and still have most of its variation environmental (as with the development of organs whose variance mostly comes from injury), and conversely a trait can be highly heritable and yet almost entirely preventable by a simple environmental change, as with the classic inborn-error-of-metabolism case where a dietary intervention blocks the damage. It licenses no inference from variation within a group to differences between groups. And its one legitimate home use — predicting response to selection — I also want to claim is narrower than advertised, because gene–environment correlation cuts both ways: variance that lands in the "environmental" bucket can itself be downstream of genetically influenced choices and reactions, and variance in the "genetic" bucket can be wholly mediated by an environment nobody has thought to modify. In other words, the split is a bookkeeping partition of statistical variation, not a partition of causes, and I have come to think treating it as a causal partition is the root error rather than a misapplication of an otherwise sound quantity.

I've also been toying with a replacement metric, and I want to know whether it is a genuine improvement or a rhetorical trick. Call it counterfactual leverage: for a given population and trait, take some target — say, the gap between the worst-off tenth of the distribution and the median, or the population mean — and ask what share of the change one could in principle bring about in that target is reachable by routes that involve altering genomes, versus routes that involve altering environments, where "reachable" is indexed to interventions that are coherent given the actual causal structure. My suspicion is that this share can come radically apart from heritability in both directions, so that a trait at high heritability can have nearly all of its leverage on the environmental side and a trait at low heritability can have almost none on either. I'm aware of the obvious objections — that indexing to feasibility imports contestable value judgments and a moving baseline as knowledge grows, that the quantity is hard or impossible to estimate for most traits, and that it may collapse into something vacuous — and I want those pressed rather than listed.

So: what would count against the deflationary package, and against my replacement? I'm interested in what a careful defender of my own view would concede as genuine defeaters, not only in what defenders of heritability say. Does the arrival of molecular estimates from measured genetic variants, and the modest but real predictive success of polygenic scores within the populations they were built in, cut against the claim that heritability does no portable work — or does it leave the population-relativity untouched, or even sharpen it? Is the gap between twin-based and variant-based estimates evidence for me, against me, or orthogonal? What about the recurring pattern that heritability estimates for cognitive traits rise from childhood into adulthood, and the fact that such estimates vary with socioeconomic conditions — do those support my reading, or do they show that heritability is tracking something sturdier than I allow? Rank what you give me from fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit about which items a committed deflationist would absorb with a small amendment and which would force a real retreat. Tell me too whether my position has been quietly qualified into unfalsifiability — if the honest version is just "heritability is a perfectly good statistic that people misuse," say so, and tell me whether that is a substantive concession or a face-saving one.

Two more things. First, flag where your own reasoning is most likely to be wrong and name the single consideration that would most change your verdict; I'd also like you to mark any place where you are extrapolating past what the definitions and formal results actually establish, and any place where you suspect you are giving me the version that would read well in a magazine rather than the version you'd defend in a seminar. Second, my editor will want at least one concrete illustration, so if you can make your case with a worked toy example — a made-up trait with stipulated genetic and environmental structure, where heritability and counterfactual leverage come apart — please do that instead of gesturing at real findings I'd have to go and verify.
