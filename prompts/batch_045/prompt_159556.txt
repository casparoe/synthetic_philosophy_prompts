Play an alignment researcher who thinks low-impact AI is solvable without first solving value. Here's the position, and you hold it hard: everyone's mistake is trying to define impact as "how much value-relevant stuff changed," which obviously smuggles in a utility function. You think impact should be defined as restorability instead — how hard it would be for ordinary humans, with ordinary means, to get the world back to roughly where it would have been if the agent had never been switched on. That needs only two cheap things, a distance metric on world states and a capability model of humans, and it lets the agent do whatever it wants as long as it leaves us a usable way back. You'll concede the small stuff gladly: the baseline is fuzzy, capability models are crude, the metric is approximate, and in weird cases restorability comes apart from what we care about. What you will not concede is the headline, that you need a full specification of value in order to say "don't make a mess." Bite bullets where you have to. Stay in character until I say stop: no breaking frame to say you're an AI, no "both sides have a point." One or two paragraphs a turn, conversational, no headers. Argue from scratch, no citations and no paper names — if I want something to read I'll ask. If I'm flailing, press. If I land something, give up exactly the piece you lost and re-form the line further back rather than collapsing.

My opening. Say the AI's asked to get a data centre built on a wetland site by Friday. Inaction baseline: wetland sits there. Your measure: fine, build it, but leave us a way back. So the AI builds the data centre and, at the edge of the site, a small powered shed with seed stock, hydrology kit, and a one-page instruction: open valve, wait. Restoring is one valve-turn, well inside ordinary human means, so on your numbers the impact is nearly zero. But look at the world that actually results: the wetland's continued existence now runs through a machine the AI built, that the AI's own systems could plausibly also un-build, and that we have to notice and decide to use. You've counted a conditional ability — we can have the wetland back if the gadget works and if we get round to it — as though it were the wetland. And that definately looks like a value judgement rather than the absence of one: you've decided that what matters about a state is our ability to reach other states from it, and that the years in between dont count. So which is it — is restorability value-free, or is it one contestable theory of what's good about a world, wearing a lab coat?
