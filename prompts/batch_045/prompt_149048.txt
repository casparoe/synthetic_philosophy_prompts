I spent most of last evening arguing with a friend about accuracy-first epistemology, and I have come to think that the entire dispute turned on an analogy that neither of us stopped to examine. I would like that analogy audited.

The framework, as I understand it: degrees of belief, or credences, are numbers between 0 and 1, and they are to be assessed the way acts are assessed, by how well they do against a standard of success. The standard is accuracy, understood as closeness to the truth: full credence in a truth and zero credence in a falsehood is the best case, and everything else falls short by degrees. The central argument is a dominance argument. For any incoherent, non-probabilistic assignment of credences, there is a probabilistic assignment that is closer to the truth in every possible world, so incoherence is accuracy-dominated and coherence is a requirement of rationality. The engine is a scoring rule, a way of measuring how far a credence lies from the truth value of its proposition, constrained by strict propriety: relative to any probability distribution over the worlds, expected inaccuracy is uniquely minimized by setting your credences equal to those probabilities. Probabilism is then supposed to follow no matter which strictly proper rule you pick, which is what is meant by calling the vindication non-pragmatic.

My friend works in decision theory and used all of this against me. His claim is that accuracy-first epistemology is just decision theory with the labels changed, and that once you write the mapping out it stops doing the work it advertises. The mapping he wrote on the board: agent to believer, available act to candidate credence function, state of the world to possible world, consequence to distance from the truth, utility to accuracy, expected utility to expected accuracy, dominance to accuracy dominance, maximising expected utility to maximising expected accuracy. He drew two conclusions from it. First, the accuracy measure occupies exactly the slot that a utility function occupies, and utility functions are interest-relative objects read off an agent's preferences rather than discovered; strict propriety is at best a coherence constraint on a measure, not a way of selecting one among the many admissible ones, so the supposed objectivity of the epistemic good is an artefact of the labelling, and the non-pragmatic vindication is pragmatism with the terminology swapped. Second, the dominance apparatus can at most deliver coherence; it says nothing about what your credence should be given your evidence, so it cannot deliver the evidential norms a real epistemology needs, such as updating by conditioning or matching known chances. To get those you need expected-accuracy arguments, and those take the expectation with respect to the agent's own credences, which means a credence function is being scored by its own lights.

He added three claims I could not evaluate. One: it is a known phenomenon that two different admissible scoring rules can disagree about which of two imperfect credence functions is more accurate in expectation, so the choice of rule does real normative work the moment you leave the dominance argument behind. Two: if you restrict the scoring to the propositions about which you actually have evidence, which he called evidential filtering, then incoherent credence functions can come out undominated and probabilism no longer follows. Three: the expected-accuracy move is self-certifying, since any coherent credence function minimises expected inaccuracy by its own lights, so the framework can only ever tell you to keep the credences you already have, whereas in decision theory the probabilities you average over are an input and never the thing being chosen.

I want to keep the claim that accuracy is an objective epistemic good and that the vindication of coherence is genuinely non-pragmatic. I am more willing to give up the claim that the framework yields a complete epistemology, including evidential norms, than to give up objectivity. But I suspect the analogy may be carrying his conclusions rather than supporting them, and possibly carrying mine too.

So: give me the correspondence table explicitly, including the slots that cannot be filled, and say what each empty slot costs. What plays the role of control over the act, given that belief is not voluntary? What plays the role of preferences? Is there any analogue of an outcome depending causally on the act, or of trading utility across outcomes and across persons, or of imprecise utilities and permissive ties? Which of these disanalogies bear on the two conclusions he drew, and which are harmless? Does the analogy undercut his position as well as mine, given that decision theory also needs norms that are not read off preferences? Are his three additional claims correct, and does evidential filtering threaten probabilism or merely restate the point that dominance arguments are about coherence? And if the action analogy is the wrong one, what would replace it: statistical decision theory and loss functions for forecasters, calibration and sharpness, measurement with error, or something else?
