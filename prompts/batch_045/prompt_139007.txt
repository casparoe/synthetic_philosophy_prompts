I'm putting together a one-page statement of where I stand on the equality-of-what question. It's for a reading group — we each circulate our view in advance and the rest of the session is spent trying to break it — but I also do some volunteer work with a disability benefits advocacy organisation, and I'd like whatever I end up with to be usable there and not merely seminar-proof. Reading it back, I think my commitments may not fit together, and I'd rather find that out before Thursday than during it. So: audit, please.

Here is the set, roughly in the order I would defend it. (a) The currency of egalitarian justice is welfare, understood as how well a person's life actually goes for them — something like experienced well-being, or the satisfaction of the concerns the person actually has. Resources and capabilities are at most means, and I reject the capabilities view as a statement of the currency: I don't think how good someone's life is consists in what they are able to do or be, and a person with a narrow but deeply satisfying life is not worse off just for the narrowness. I'm also unmoved by the thought that welfare can't be compared across people; I think the comparisons are rough but good enough. (b) We owe people nothing for expensive tastes. Someone who has deliberately cultivated a liking for rare single-malt whisky, or who insists on living in the most expensive quarter of the city, and who then reports needing twice my income to be as well off as me, has no claim on me. I'd say the same about costly religious observance, though less confidently, and I'm not sure where to put my Deaf colleague who wants a sign-language interpreter at work — compensation or expensive preference? (c) I don't trust self-reports. People adapt to chronic pain, to confinement, to low expectations, and the familiar figures of the contented housebound carer and the cheerful person with a serious impairment seem to me to be exactly the cases where reported satisfaction is misleading. So institutions can't simply ask people how well off they are and distribute accordingly. (d) Where someone's body or mind converts resources into well-being inefficiently — a disability, a chronic illness, a metabolic disorder requiring a diet costing three times an ordinary one — justice requires compensation, and it requires it even where the person, by any measure I'd accept from (c), says they are perfectly happy; my friend with the metabolic condition is in excellent health and reports being very well, and I still think the extra cost should be met. (e) I'm drawn to a publicity and administrability condition: allocations should be made under rules citizens can see and check, officials shouldn't be in the business of assessing how happy or fulfilled anyone is, and I'm happy for them to use observable proxies — whether someone can work, get around, take part in social life. (f) I'm a luck egalitarian about the pattern: brute luck generates claims, option luck doesn't, and I hold that throughout, not only in (b) and (d). But here is a verdict I'm attached to and can't square: someone constitutionally unable to enjoy much of anything — no illness, no impairment, nothing clinical, just an unlucky hedonic set point that nobody chose and nothing treats — strikes me as having no justice claim to extra resources, and I feel no pull to say otherwise.

What I want is a ranking, not a list. Go through the set and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are, and how severe each real tension is, with what would have to give in each case and what giving it up would cost me. Some things I'm specifically unsure about, though please don't limit yourself to them: (a) and (e) seem to me perfectly compatible — that's a distinction between what we're ultimately trying to equalise and what institutions can be told to track — but two people have told me they can't both be true, and I'd like to know who is right. I also suspect (c) is doing more work than I've admitted: it looks like a free-standing methodological worry, but I think it may be what lets me keep (d) while holding (a), and I'm not sure it can bear that weight without turning into a nonsubjective theory of welfare that hollows out (a). Then there's (b): most expensive tastes aren't cultivated the way my whisky case is, they're absorbed from upbringing and class, and if (c) is right that desires are shaped by forces nobody chooses, I don't know how many tastes are chosen in the sense (b) needs — so (b) may either collapse into (d) or need a different rationale entirely. And I'd like a verdict on the melancholic person in (f): does my own package commit me to a claim there after all, is there a difference I'm missing between having no claim and there being nothing anyone could do, or does that case show that a welfare currency generates claims that can't be discharged in anything the state can hand out? Finally, is there one revision that cleans up the most, or is the honest answer that I should give up (a) and admit to being a resourcist or a capabilities theorist with welfare-shaped intuitions?
