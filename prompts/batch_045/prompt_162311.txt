I'm in a small reading group on the moral status of artificial systems — mostly philosophy grad students plus one person who works on interpretability — and last night, arguing about it in a bar afterwards, a friend sketched an argument on a napkin that I have not been able to get out of. We had agreed on something like the standard functionalist starting point: what matters morally about a system is its computational organization, not what it is made of. He then offered a construction he calls the handoff, and used it to derive a conclusion neither of us can accept. I'd like to know which step to reject, and what rejecting it commits me to, including what it predicts for a few cases we didn't get to.

The handoff: take a fine-grained description of the successive global states of some creature's nervous system over an hour, call them s1 through sn. Now build a system out of n separate, individually simple physical tokens. Token 1 realizes s1, and is so built that by its own dynamics, with nothing external beyond a trigger, it brings it about that the next token realizes s2; that token brings it about that the next realizes s3; and so on down the chain. Nothing anywhere computes the transition function in the general-purpose way a CPU does — each transition is a one-off causal fact, built separately into each token. Still, every state in the sequence is really realized, each one really causes the next, and what happens next depends on the content of the state realized: swap two tokens and the continuation changes. His argument then ran:

(1) If moral status tracks computational organization rather than substrate or specific physics, then two systems that realize the same sequence of computational states, with the same dependencies among them, have the same moral status.
(2) The handoff realizes the same sequence as the creature, with the same dependencies among its elements. So the handoff has the moral status of the creature.
(3) Nothing in (1) mentions rate; a computational description abstracts away from how fast the states succeed one another. So run the handoff at one transition per decade, tokens stored in warehouses on three continents, triggers carried between them by courier. Same moral status.
(4) If no particular rate is required, then a rate of zero is just the limiting case and cannot be what turns a mind into a non-mind. What does the work must be the dispositional structure — that each token would bring about the right successor if triggered — and not whether the triggering ever occurs.
(5) So an untriggered warehouse of tokens that no one ever starts has, for that hour, the full moral status of a living creature.
(6) The tokens are cheap, and nothing ties a token to one successor: a token that would realize s-prime if triggered one way and s-double-prime if triggered another is a state in two different handoffs at once. So a single warehouse of n tokens houses not one mind but exponentially many, sharing all of their parts, none of them ever doing anything.
(7) Dispositional facts of that kind are not rare. Under a suitable encoding, any sufficiently structured physical object stands in indefinitely many such would-be-successor relations. So every object hosts a large, largely overlapping population of minds, and the moral weight in a shoebox dwarfs everything we have ever cared about.

I take (7) as a reductio. I have tried blaming (4) and I have tried blaming (2), and both times he had a reply that I could not break: against blaming (4) he says I am smuggling in a demand for causal activity that computational descriptions simply do not contain, and against blaming (2) he says I am insisting on a general-purpose mechanism, which is exactly the substrate chauvinism we agreed to give up. I would rather keep (1) and (3) than (2) — I would sooner say the handoff is not a mind than say that an upload's moral status depends on how fast it runs — but I want to know what I am paying for that, since it feels close to conceding that the machinery has to be doing the transitions in the right kind of way, which is most of what I wanted functionalism for.

So: which step actually fails, and why did it look sound? If it is (2), what exactly must a system do beyond realizing the states and their dependencies — must it instantiate a general transition function, must its states be causally integrated rather than merely sequential, must the same mechanism subserve many possible transitions — and how is any of that different, in principle, from caring about the substrate? If it is (4), what is the principled line between rate-independence and occurrence-independence, given that slowing a run down seems not to matter? And whatever you diagnose, please tell me what it says about the cases we never got to: a simulation paused mid-run at a checkpoint and resumed a year later; a cryonics patient; a handoff chained at one transition per century but actually triggered; and a chain spliced together from the first half of one creature's sequence and the second half of another's.
