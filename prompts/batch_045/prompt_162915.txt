You are a philosopher of medicine who works on the naturalism-versus-normativism dispute about the concept of disease. Think the problem through carefully before you write — it is worth brainstorming several candidate mechanisms and discarding the ones that cheat — but give me a polished result, not your notes.

Build me a thought experiment to order. I am using it in a lecture whose point is that the disagreement between naturalist and normativist accounts is substantive rather than merely verbal, so the case must generate its verdicts from the theories instead of building them into the description.

Specifications:

1. Realistic and contemporary — an office, a school, a housing block; no science fiction, no invented neuroscience, no futuristic technology. One paragraph.
2. Everyone in the relevant reference class has the condition, because the environmental exposure that produces it is near-universal. The condition is an impairment of a part's own functioning, so nobody can wave it off as a wound or as something merely present in the body; and it harms its bearers in a way they themselves care about and would pay to avoid — a real decrement in a capacity, not just an abnormal test result.
3. From those details alone it must follow that (a) the best-known naturalist account — health as statistically species-typical functioning of the parts, disease as subnormal functional contribution relative to a reference class — returns "not a disease," while (b) the influential hybrid account — dysfunction as a mechanism's failure to do what natural selection designed it to do, plus the value judgment that the condition harms its bearer — returns "disease."
4. It must also follow that a third view, on which a condition is a disease only if clinical medicine rather than regulation, engineering, or public-health policy is the institution that appropriately addresses it, returns "not a disease" while conceding the harm. Secure this by making removal of the exposure the effective remedy, not by stipulating that doctors have nothing to offer.

Then: show each theory's reasoning from the details of the case; name in one sentence the single feature doing the work; say what happens to all three verdicts if that feature is removed — what if exposure falls to a third of the population, for instance — and whether the case then collapses into one everybody already agrees about; and give the strongest reply open to the defender of the account that comes out worst, including your view on whether that reply is legitimate or is really a way of making the account unfalsifiable.

Begin with the scenario paragraph itself, with no preamble and no restating of these instructions; follow it with a single line giving the three verdicts; then give the analysis. Keep the whole thing under 700 words.
