So a friend sent me a chapter from some pop-philosophy book on experimental philosophy, and im not sure how much of it to trust. The bit on the Knobe effect goes roughly like this:

"Knobe's studies show that our judgments about intentional action are really moral judgments in disguise. Participants read about a company chairman who's told a new program will raise profits and, depending on the version, help or harm the environment. He says he doesn't care about the environment at all, he only wants profit, and the program goes ahead. When the side effect is bad, people say he harmed the environment intentionally; when it's good, they say he didn't help it intentionally. His knowledge and foresight are identical in both versions, so the only thing doing the work is disapproval: people read him as having aimed at the harm, though what they're really doing is just expressing blame. Knobe's conclusion is that 'he did it intentionally' means nothing more than 'he's to blame for it', so there's no descriptive question left to argue about — and the effect has since been found for causation judgments too, which shows it isn't specific to intentionality. The moral for philosophy is that armchair intuitions are just moral reactions wearing a descriptive costume."

My gut says the "nothing more than blame" part is too strong, but I can't say why, or which of the other claims are actually fine. Could you go through it and tell me what someone working on this would accept, what's a fair simplification, and what they'd call wrong — and whether that final moral survives once you fix the mistakes?
