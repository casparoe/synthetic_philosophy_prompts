Hi — I'm on the attributions panel at a decent-sized museum (paintings and sculpture, mostly European, plus a modern bit) and we've got a case next month that I think exposes us. Our verdicts so far, for whatever there worth: a Roman marble figure that's a copy of a lost Greek bronze — we labelled it 'Roman copy after a Greek original' and refused the big name; a huge 17th-century workshop canvas where the master painted the heads and hands and the shop did the draperies, finishing them after he died — 'workshop of', no bare 'by'; a set of enormous shiny sculptures by a very famous living artist who has never physically touched any of them (they're built by technicians from his computer files and he's happy to call them his) — 'by'; a signed readymade from the 1910s, a factory-made object he simply chose, whose original got smashed and was replaced in the 1960s with a new example of the same model that he authorised — we accepted the replacement as the work itself; a print made after the photographer's death from his own negative, by a printer following written instructions he'd left — 'by', with the print date in brackets; and a painting we now know was done start to finish by a forger in the 1920s, which has been on our walls for eighty years and is genuinely lovely — we refused the attribution but kept it up under a new label saying 'imitator of'.

Now the new one. A sculptor who died in 2019 worked with a fabricator and had shown a piece only in plaster, but her studio notes (in her hand, undated) specify a bronze edition of six, the foundry and the patina. She had one bronze pulled from those moulds in 1998 and signed it off; that bronze was destroyed in a warehouse fire in 2005. The foundation she set up has now had a new bronze cast from the same moulds and wants to give it to us as 'by [her], cast 2024'. The valuation on the gift form is roughly fifteen times what it would be if we call it 'after' — which its not supposed to matter to us but, you know, it does.

What I want is exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no intro and no conclusion. 1) The two or three rules about what an attribution actually asserts that fit all six of those earlier verdicts — I can think of maybe 'the artist's hand', 'the artist said so', and something about the label being a claim with money riding on it, but you'll do it better. 2) What each of those rules says about the 2024 bronze, including whether it matters that she signed off a cast from these very moulds in 1998. 3) Your best guess at what we'll actually decide, and why that rule rather than the others. 4) The closest analogue among the six precedents, and whether it's really close. 5) The one further case we'd have to rule on that would best tell the surviving rules apart. If you think we've allready been inconsistent somewhere, say where — that can slot into whichever point it belongs in.
