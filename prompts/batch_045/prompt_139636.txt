I was at a small workshop on practical rationality a few weeks ago, and after dinner two of the participants got into an argument that I have not managed to settle in my own mind. The question they were circling was whether hyperbolic discounting explains weakness of will or merely redescribes it. I'd be grateful for a careful adjudication rather than an opinion. I know the relevant literature well enough, so please argue from the structure of the problem: no citations, and no attributing these views to named authors.

Both of them accepted the following set-up. An agent faces a choice between a smaller-sooner reward and a larger-later one. Under exponential discounting the ratio of the two discounted values does not depend on when the choice is evaluated, so a preference between them cannot reverse merely with the passage of time. Under hyperbolic discounting, where value is A/(1+kD), postponing both rewards by the same interval raises the relative weight of the earlier one, and so an agent who prefers the larger-later option when both are distant can come to prefer the smaller-sooner one as the pair draws close.

(1) Owen opened by saying that this is the whole attraction of the account: it produces a preference reversal with no change in the agent's information, no change in her beliefs about the options, and no change in the options themselves. Only her temporal position changes. That, he said, is exactly the structure of an akratic episode — the agent does not learn anything new between resolving not to eat the cake and eating it; what changes is where she stands relative to it. On this view, he continued, appeal to a special faculty of willpower, or to a distinctively practical failure of rationality, is otiose. What we have is an agent with a certain discount structure moving through an environment that keeps presenting the temptation.

(2) Mira replied with what she called the vanishing problem. If the curves genuinely cross, then at the moment of action the agent really does prefer the smaller-sooner option, and she is doing what she most wants. Nothing akratic is left. More generally, she said, hyperbolic discounting is a theory of preference, so it can only explain the action by making the action the one the agent prefers — but the puzzle of akrasia is precisely that the action comes apart from what the agent takes to be best. So the account faces a dilemma: either the ranking does not flip, in which case the model does not predict the action, or it flips, in which case the model predicts the action by dissolving the phenomenon. She added that the model also seems to target the wrong temporal structure. The classic case is synchronic: at the very moment of acting, the agent still judges the alternative better and will sometimes say so while reaching for the cake.

(3) Owen answered that Mira was identifying the preference that governs choice with the ranking the agent endorses, and that a sophisticated agent can hold the two apart. She can see the flip coming, announce that she is about to do the worse thing, feel it happen, and do it anyway. Far from being a case the model cannot reach, that is the phenomenon. He then argued that the synchronic/diachronic contrast is overstated, and offered a case of his own. Hold the delay fixed: in one condition the cake is on the counter in front of her, visible and smelling, available in twenty minutes; in the other it is sealed in a box in the basement, also available in twenty minutes. A pure function of delay predicts no difference between the conditions, but plausibly the reversal occurs in the first and not the second. So, he said, we should read the discount function as discounting in a general psychological distance, of which temporal delay is one dimension along with spatial, sensory, attentional and counterfactual distance, and we should understand akrasia as proximity-sensitivity of motivational strength rather than as anything specifically temporal.

(4) Mira said the generalization costs more than it buys. First, the predictive cost: the empirical content of hyperbolic discounting lies in the determinate crossing points it predicts from delay alone, and in its divergence from exponential discounting. If any salience variable can be recruited post hoc to move the crossing point, then no pattern of reversal and no failure of reversal can refute the model, and we have bought coverage of the clear-eyed case by selling the thing that made the hypothesis worth testing. Second, she said the generalization is not even stable in sign. For burdens as opposed to rewards, proximity seems to push the other way in the cases that matter: a dreaded task can become harder to initiate the nearer it looms, and people report deferring it precisely when it is most present to them, even though the costs of deferring are also looming. So rewards and burdens would need different proximity functions, tuned after the fact. Third, and to her mind decisive, there is the authority datum. An agent who expects to reverse does not treat her later preference as merely another one of her preferences. She walks the long way home past the bakery, she asks a friend to hold her to it, she leaves the cake in the basement. If the flip were simply a change in what she prefers, all of that would be mysterious: the later preference is hers too. What her behaviour shows is that she takes the earlier ranking to have a standing authority that the later reversal does not have, and a theory stated entirely in terms of preferences has nowhere to put that.

(5) Owen accepted the third point but denied that it tells against him. That, he said, is exactly what a sophisticated agent should be expected to do, and there is a standard apparatus for it: the earlier self acts first and so has strategic leverage over the later one, using pre-commitment, removal of temptation and the rest. A strategic advantage is all that is needed; the earlier self need not have normative authority. As for the regret that accompanies the action, he said it is not the return of the earlier preference competing with the flipped one, but a second-order desire not to be moved in this way, which is perfectly compatible with the first-order flip.

(6) Mira closed by conceding that the strategic apparatus handles some cases and insisting that it misses the target in others. Take two agents with the same discount function facing the same delayed pair, one of whom succumbs and one of whom does not, and suppose what distinguishes them is not any preference but how attention is deployed in the minutes before the choice. Then the shape of the discount function is at most a background condition determining whether there is a temptation to be resisted; it is not the mechanism of the failure, and it does no work in explaining why this agent resisted and that one did not. And the deeper point, she said, is structural: a hyperbolic discounter is at every moment maximizing her current preference function, so no state of an agent of that kind can represent a standing intention that constrains her later choice. Whatever gives the earlier ranking its authority is not a temporal discount structure. The model is not false, she said; it is about a different subject.

Please go through the exchange move by move. For each of the six moves, say whether it is clearly right, partly right, or clearly wrong, and give your reason in a sentence or two, distinguishing where possible between a point that is correct and a conclusion that does not follow from it. Then say who has the better of the argument overall, and why. Finally, tell me what both of them are missing: in particular, whether this is one disagreement or several run together, whether the causal question (what produces the action) and the normative question (what makes it a failure of agency rather than mere imprudence) are being kept apart, and whether there is a formulation of the original question on which both of them turn out to be right.
