I need a Mary's-room variant built to order, for a graduate seminar whose guiding question is whether the disagreement between the two main physicalist replies to the knowledge argument is a substantive one or merely verbal. The two replies I have in mind, stated in the form I want you to work with:

- The ability hypothesis: what Mary gains on release is not knowledge-that at all but a bundle of abilities — to imagine a red experience, to recognize one as it occurs, and to recall one. Because the gain is a gain of abilities rather than of true belief, there is no new proposition for the physicalist to find room for, and the knowledge argument never gets a first premise.
- The phenomenal-concept strategy: what Mary gains is a new concept, one that picks out a perfectly ordinary physical state under a new mode of presentation. With that concept in hand she can think and know a proposition she could not think before — roughly, an identity linking the physical state she already knew everything about with the experience she now conceives under this concept — so there is genuine new propositional knowledge without any new property.

Both replies are typically presented as accounting for the same intuition, namely that Mary learns something when she leaves the room. My seminar question is whether they are really two accounts of one thing or two different claims that could come apart. To answer that, I need a case where they do come apart.

The design brief: construct a case in which the two theories give different verdicts about whether a subject knows what it is like to see red, with the difference falling out of the setup rather than being written into it. The lever I want you to use is a dissociation between possessing and deploying the phenomenal concept — forming beliefs with it, reasoning with it, drawing on what it encodes — and having the three canonical abilities (imagining, recognizing, recalling). You may run the dissociation in either direction if you can make it work; if you think only one direction is coherent, take that one and explain in a sentence why the other is not.

Constraints on the case:

- One paragraph of scenario, no more than about two hundred words.
- Keep the science fiction to what the original setup already grants: a black-and-white room, a monitor, complete physical information. Ordinary neuropsychology is fine. No uploading, no neural download of a concept, no pill or device that confers knowledge or ability by fiat, no simulator that predicts Mary's responses for her.
- Whatever difference or deficit you introduce must be specified functionally — what the subject can now do and cannot now do — and not by naming a mechanism; and it must not rest on contested empirical claims about how some named clinical condition actually behaves, since you cannot look anything up here and I do not want the case to stand or fall on an empirical guess.
- Do not make Mary's physical information incomplete, do not give her the experience earlier, and do not deflate the intuition that at the moment of release she learns something. Both replies grant that intuition; a case that quietly removes it has changed the subject rather than tested them.
- The verdicts must be derived, not stipulated. State what each theory takes knowing what it is like to consist in, and then show why the case does or does not satisfy that account. If you find yourself writing that on the ability hypothesis she does not know simply because you specified that she lacks the abilities, the design has failed: whether the loss of those abilities amounts to a loss of the knowledge is exactly the point at issue.

After the scenario I want five things:

1. The verdict of each theory, with the derivation: what the theory says the knowledge consists in, and why this case does or does not supply it.
2. An honest note on which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens if that feature is removed or reversed — in particular, whether removing it collapses the case back into an ordinary Mary's-room scenario with the usual verdicts, or into no test at all.
3. Where the third main reply lands — the one on which Mary comes to be acquainted with a property that is not physical — and whether your case therefore also does independent work against physicalism or leaves that question where it was.
4. The strongest attack available to whichever theory comes out worse, stated in its best form rather than as a straw man, followed by your reply. I expect the live moves to be something like these: that a phenomenal concept severed from every recognitional and imaginative disposition is not a phenomenal concept at all, so the strategy never applied; and that whatever survives in your case is itself a species of ability, so the ability hypothesis was never threatened. Meet whichever of these your case actually invites.
5. A short closing verdict on the question the seminar cares about: does a case of this shape show the two replies to be making different and testable claims, or does the attempt to build it reveal the dispute to be a quarrel about what to count as knowledge? If you conclude that no case can separate them without stipulation, say that plainly and give the argument — a negative result is an acceptable answer, but only with the reasoning attached.

No citations, no reading suggestions, no appeal to what any particular author said; the case and the argument should carry the whole thing.
