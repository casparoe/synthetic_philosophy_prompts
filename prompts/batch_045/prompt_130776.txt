For a piece I'm writing on the standing fight over evolutionary psychology, I want you to draft the joint statement that a committed adaptationist researcher in human evolutionary psychology and a committed skeptic — the one who thinks the field mostly produces untestable just-so stories — could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

To keep it from collapsing into slogans, structure it around a distinction I want made explicit and honored throughout. Any adaptationist hypothesis actually bundles three separable claims: a *specialisation* claim, that the mind contains mechanisms tuned to a narrow class of inputs rather than a general-purpose learning device; a *provenance* claim, that the class of inputs the mechanism is tuned to corresponds to a recurrent problem in ancestral environments; and a *design* claim, that selection for solving that problem, rather than developmental plasticity, cultural transmission, or the mechanism being a byproduct of something else selected, is what best explains the mechanism's structure. Assume both parties accept that a hypothesis can be right on one and wrong on another.

Then give me: the claims both can sign — including about what the spandrels-style byproduct objection does and does not establish, about how much the field's evidence base really supports given how narrow its human samples are, and about what would count as a mechanism being innate as opposed to merely reliably acquired; the residual disagreements in wording each side would accept for its own view; and the cruxes. I have candidates for the cruxes and want your judgment on them. One is the *credit asymmetry*: confirmed predictions get claimed, failed ones get absorbed by a mismatch epicycle, so would a pre-registered registry of ancestral predictions, scored against hold-out populations the hypothesist did not design around and against novel modern environments where an ancestral mechanism should misfire in specifiable ways, actually settle anything — and what does each side predict would happen? Another is what I call the *reliability gap*: if a mechanism is produced by canalised development triggered by input that is reliably present in any normal human environment, is the skeptic's plasticity alternative a genuine rival or just a redescription of the same adaptation — and if it is a redescription, where did the empirical content go? Flag any crux you think is merely verbal, in particular any place where the dispute is only about whether *adaptation* means selection history or present engineering fit.

Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table with each side's recorded predictions. I don't need citations or a reading list — I need formulations that are neither vacuous nor slanted, since this is going to be the spine of the article.
