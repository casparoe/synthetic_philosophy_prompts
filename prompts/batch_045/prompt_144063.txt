Got into a long argument with a friend last night about Chesterton's fence and it turned into an argument about Shirley Jackson's The Lottery instead, which we both read in high school and rember very differently.

His take: the story is basically a refutation of Burkean deference. A practice can roll on for generations with alot of nothing behind it, everybody quietly assuming somebody else checked, and the villagers' entire case is just that it has always been done — so the fence principle is inertia wearing a philosophy costume. I took the opposite side: they've lost whatever reason the thing had and the upshot is that they stone a neighbour once a year, which looks like the fence principle vindicated rather than refuted. Then he said something I couldn't answer: Chesterton's fence only says don't tear the thing down until you understand it, it doesn't tell you what to do once you've established that nobody does understand it, and in the story nobody ever finds out.

Can you read the story through both ideas properly? Things I want settled rather than gestured at:

- the epistemic version of the fence (your ignorance of the function means you aren't entitled to remove it) versus Burke's heavier claim that a long-standing practice carries accumulated tacit wisdom that no individual's reason can reconstruct. I don't think those are the same claim and I think the story treats them differently.
- the gap between no one can say why we do this and there is no why. I want a distinction between a practice that survived because it was quietly doing some work nobody can name, and one that survived because dropping it is everyone's second choice, or because it's convenient for whoever runs it. The story hands us evidence both ways: the lottery is still nominally for a good harvest, but all that's really left is a scrap of an old rhyme and a battered box, and other villages have already given it up without anything obviously going wrong. Which way should that cut?
- where the reading breaks down. Tessie only objects once it's her family — does that undermine her objection under either theory, or is it irrelevant? And is it a different story if the lottery did work, everybody knew it worked, and the victim was still picked at random?
- whether Old Man Warner is meant as the Burkean voice or as a strawman of one. I kept flip-flopping.

Don't go look anything up, and I don't want quotes — I remember the story fine. What I actually want out of it: is the story evidence for the fence principle or against it, and does it show something neither of us managed to put into words, namely that the force of the principle is about who eats the cost of being wrong — the person getting stoned, not the people theorising — so it's doing moral work and not just epistemic work?
