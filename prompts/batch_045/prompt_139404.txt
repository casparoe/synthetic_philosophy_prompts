I moderate a fairly big survival/crafting server — the kind where people sink months into building one thing — which means I'm the one who ends up deciding whether to roll something back after a dispute. I make these calls on gut and my gut calls don't obviously line up with each other. I'd like to know what principle, if any, is behind them, and which ones I should stop trusting.

Here are the rulings roughly as I actually make them:

1. A player spends about three months of evenings farming a rare drop. Another player talks them into a trade and swaps in a lookalike item at the last second. I roll it back and I ban. That feels like theft and I don't think I'm being sentimental about it.

2. Two players fight in the area where the server rules say open season, and the loser drops a couple hours worth of gear. I do nothing. That's the game, they both opted in.

3. Same as 2, except afterwards the winner goes and messages the loser's friends to laugh at them, and the loser quits the server. Now I feel like something extra happened that I should respond to, and I genuinely can't tell whether to file it as the same kind of thing as 1 or as something else entirely.

4. Someone builds an enormous cathedral on a private creative plot nobody else can enter. Another player finds an exploit, gets in, and levels it — and records it and posts the video. I feel distinctly worse about the version where they recorded it than the version where they didn't. That seems backwards, since the cathedral is equally gone either way, so maybe I'm reacting to something other than the loss.

5. Someone tells me their account was hacked and their stuff deleted. They hadn't logged in for over a year and never came back, so far as I can tell they never found out. I still think something bad happened. But I can't say who is worse off, and it feels weaker than 1 in a way I can't articulate.

6. There's a duping bug and an item that almost nobody had becomes worthless overnight because everyone has it. Nothing was taken from anybody. I still feel the person who originally farmed it got done an injury, and I don't know what to call it if it isn't theft.

7. Stealing in-game currency that can be sold for real money feels more like actual theft to me than stealing the same amount of currency that can't be traded out. But I don't want to conclude that the untradeable case isn't theft, so I don't know what to do with this feeling.

8. A player buys a cosmetic with real money and a year later the devs give it away free. I think he has no complaint whatsoever, even though I think 1 is serious. Maybe the difference is that in 1 a person did it and here nobody did?

9. A player kills an NPC questgiver and the whole server is locked out of that questline for a week. I treat that as real interference, closer to 1 than to nothing, even though the NPC isn't anyone and respawns anyway. I'm more confident about this than I am about 5, which strikes me as odd.

10. I read once about an old text-only online world where a player ran something that made other players' characters carry out sexual actions in a public room — no physical contact anywhere, everyone anonymous. That one I'm confident about: serious harm, one of the clearest items on my list. But I also think a player who is upset because someone mocked their character's haircut has not been harmed. My confidence in those two is miles apart and I want to know what exactly separates them.

The rule of thumb I keep half-reaching for is: a virtual thing is real when it's scarce under the rules, somebody had to work for it, and other people can see it. But that seems to predict 4 should be the worst thing on the list and 9 should be nothing, and it gives me no grip at all on 6 or 5.

So: is there one principle that gets these, or a small set of candidates? State it tightly enough that I could actually read it out in a ruling, tell me which clause is doing the work in which case, and please say which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and wants me to drop — I'd rather give up a gut call than keep a rule with four special exemptions.

And tell me what it predicts on cases I haven't had yet: a single-player permadeath run that no one will ever see; a world built from a seed that will never be generated again; a companion character in a game someone has talked to for two hundred hours that a patch deletes; and whether it should matter if the world is text-only versus full VR with haptics. My head says it shouldn't matter and my chest says it obviously does.

Please don't just tell me virtual property is real property or that it's all just numbers in a database. I've heard both, and neither one helps me make a call at 11pm on a Tuesday.
