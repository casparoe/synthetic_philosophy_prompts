I am working on the heterogeneity problem for hedonism and have stopped trying to read my way out of it. I want to start instead from my own verdicts on cases, because I suspect the dispute is partly a dispute about which cases count. Before the cases, one distinction I want respected: the problem has a *differentia* reading (what feature, if any, is shared by all and only pleasures) and a *common scale* reading (whether pleasures, however various, are addable and comparable on a single scale well enough to be maximized). I care about both, and I want you to say which one you are answering at each point.

My own tentative answer to the differentia question: an experience is pleasant just in case, at the time of having it, nothing in the subject is oriented toward that experience's stopping — where this is neither a matter of the subject's wanting it to continue (wanting can be absent, as with the popcorn below), nor of its feeling good in any detectable way (that can be absent too, as with the flow case), but a matter of there being no phenomenal *awayness* in the experience itself. Call it the non-aversion condition. I offer it as a hypothesis to be broken, not defended.

My verdicts. I say *pleasure* where I mean the experience belongs on the positive side of the ledger, and I flag separately where I doubt it improves welfare.

1. Cold water after a long run in heat: pleasure, but minor.
2. The absorbed flow of a close-fought tennis match, during which I felt nothing good at all and only later reported having enjoyed it: pleasure, and importantly so.
3. Scratching a persistent itch: not pleasure, but relief — even though, at the time, nothing in me wanted it to stop.
4. Eating a whole bowl of popcorn while absorbed in a book, never noticing: I am inclined to say there was pleasure there, and equally inclined to say there was not, and I cannot settle it.
5. A flicker of delight at a rival's failure: pleasure, but I want to credit it with little or no welfare value.
6. Eating food painfully hot with chilli, or watching a film that genuinely frightens me: the episode is one I would repeat and count as a positive; the burning and the fear themselves are not pleasant; and no part could be removed without destroying what I was after.
7. A quiet Sunday contentment with almost no distinguishable phenomenal character: positive, though I can locate no feeling in it.
8. The familiar clinical pattern in which a person pursues a stimulus tirelessly while reporting no enjoyment of it: not pleasure, whatever the behavior suggests.
9. A drunken evening I thoroughly enjoyed and would rather had never happened: pleasure at the time; I do not know whether it improved my welfare.
10. The satisfaction of finally filing a tax return I never cared about: I go back and forth between pleasure and mere removal of a discomfort. The same for the state of a very simple agent — an animal, or a machine with something like a better-or-worse sensory register but no desires and no concept of its own states.

What I want from you:

State, in one sentence precise enough to be applied without charity, the principle you think best captures the pattern in 1–10, and name explicitly which of my verdicts it cannot keep. Then test my non-aversion condition against the same list: tell me what it gets wrong, whether the cheapest repair (a part-whole clause? an index to the episode rather than to the momentary experience? a counterfactual test?) costs less than abandoning it, and what the repaired version predicts for case 10.

Set out, as rivals, the best felt-quality or valence account and the best attitudinal or desire-based account you can construct, and say exactly where each fails against my list — not in general, but at this or that numbered case. If you think the honest answer is that nothing non-disjunctive unifies the class, say so plainly, and then tell me whether a family-resemblance or cluster account is still a form of hedonism or a capitulation to a pluralist list of welfare goods, and what specifically is lost in the capitulation.

Two further things. First, comparability: does your preferred account yield a total ordering of pleasures by amount, or only local comparability within families of similar pleasures, and does the difference matter for the maximizing use hedonism makes of its own currency? Second: which of my ten verdicts do you take to be the least reliable, on grounds internal to my own list rather than to your theory — and which single case, if I changed my mind about it, would do most to change what the correct principle is?
