I write literary fiction. My third novel is in its final revision, and it turns on a betrayal. My difficulty is not structural but moral: my own judgments about how much blame a character deserves, and my readers' judgments, keep shifting with things those characters did not choose, and the last chapter has to take a stand. I would like a principle that either licenses or forbids those shifts.

Here are my verdicts, given as a novelist gives them — on the page, with the scene fixed.

1. Two characters drive home drunk from the same party, at the same speed, down the same road. One arrives safely. The other strikes a child who runs out between parked cars. I blame the second substantially more, and so does every reader who has seen the draft. When I stipulate in the text that their carelessness and their indifference were exactly alike, the gap narrows and then stubbornly persists.

2. Another character, born suspicious and quick to take offence, destroys a friendship. I do not treat her temperament as mitigating. She no more chose it than the second driver chose the child, and yet only one of these feels like luck to me.

3. A man who would certainly have betrayed his brother under interrogation is never arrested. My early readers refuse to blame him at all. I blame him a little, on the strength of what the narrative shows of his dispositions.

4. I drafted the same betrayal twice: once as a scene, in real time, and once summarised in retrospect by another character. The action, the motive, and the consequence were identical. Readers blamed the betrayer far more in the scenic version. I take this to be illegitimate, but I cannot make myself stop feeling it, and I have to decide which draft to keep.

Cases 1 and 2 are the pair I cannot reconcile, and I would rather be told which to give up than be given a principle that silently accommodates both.

The principle I have been working from, such as it is: blame tracks the quality of will an action manifests; facts outside the agent's control matter only as evidence of that will, and drop out once the evidence is held fixed. Case 1 survives the stipulation, so this principle fails it. Case 5 is worse for it, since the rendering changes no evidence at all.

The case I go back and forth on is the one that makes the whole exercise feel like a trap: every one of these characters is determined, in the most literal sense, by me. I wrote the temperament, the child, the road, the arrest that never came. If absence of control excuses, then no fictional character is ever blameworthy and my book is a cheat; if it does not, then the control condition is doing no work anywhere. So I need to know which way the principle jumps here, and whether the fact that readers blame invented agents while knowing them to be invented tells us anything about what blame is for.

What I want, in under 700 words: one sentence stating the principle you think survives; then a table with a row per clause — the clause, the case of mine that motivates it, the verdict of mine it overturns, and its prediction for one case I have not listed — including a row for the determined-character case; then two or three sentences on which of my verdicts you would throw out and what it costs me as a novelist to throw it out. No footnotes, and nothing that depends on my having read the literature; a friend at university gave me the labels resultant and constitutive luck for cases 1 and 2, and that is the extent of my vocabulary here.
