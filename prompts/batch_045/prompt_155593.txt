My friend and I — both of us have read a fair amount of this literature — have now had the same argument about luck egalitarianism four or five times, and it never moves. The responsibility-sensitive side says justice consists in undoing disadvantage that a person could not reasonably have avoided; the relational critics say this turns justice into an audit of the badly-off person's conduct and demeans them by treating them as victims to be compensated rather than as equals to be respected; and when someone raises the levelling-down complaint one side treats it as decisive and the other as a technical curiosity. My friend's hypothesis is that the obstacle is metaphysical: if nothing anyone does is genuinely up to them, then the brute-luck/option-luck distinction cannot be drawn at all, and the view collapses either into strict outcome equality or into nothing, so nothing can be settled until free will is settled. I think that is at best part of the story, and suspect the live disagreements lie elsewhere — whether equality is to be promoted as an end or honoured as a constraint on how we treat people, which cases get to count as data (the reckless driver, the person with expensive tastes, someone whom nobody wants to employ), or whether could have avoided is being used as a moral notion or merely a predictive one. Can you diagnose what sort of impasse this actually is — verbal, substantive, two different questions under one label, or a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work — and, on the basis of that diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress, or make the case that none should be expected? Please answer in British English.
