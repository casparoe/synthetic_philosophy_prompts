Hey — I need a thought experiment built to order for a reading group I run on AI risk arguments, and I want one that cuts against the usual direction.

The spec: a case where the orthogonality thesis looks obviously true — same capability profile on both sides, wildly different final goals, and turning capability up doesn't drag either goal toward the other — but where instrumental convergence just fails. Not "fails a bit": there should be no nontrivial instrumental subgoal both agents get pushed toward as they get more capable, and on at least one of the standard four drives (staying alive, acquiring resources, keeping your own final goal intact, sharpening your own cognition) more capability should push the two of them in opposite directions.

Constraints, because this is easy to fake:
- Realistic setting, realistic agents. Two co-trustees of a foundation, two department heads, two siblings running a family firm, whatever — no paperclip maximisers, no foom, nothing science fictional. Both final goals have to be goals actual people or institutions really have.
- Both agents instrumentally rational throughout. The divergence has to fall out of the goals, not out of one of them being dumber, more myopic, or lazier.
- The capability increase has to be more of the same kind of thing — better world-models, longer effective planning horizon, more reliable execution — not a change of goal and not a change of environment.
- Outright cheating, which I'll reject: handing one agent a final goal that already mentions its own destruction or its own holdings ('I want to die', 'I want to own nothing'). The flip on the drive has to be derived from the goal plus the capability, not stipulated.

The bit I actually care about, and the thing I want you to engineer: I think the load-bearing move is a distinction between what I'll call dischargeable and custodial final goals. Custodial: the goal needs the agent to keep turning up, so preserving yourself and staying in control stays instrumentally useful indefinitely. Dischargeable: there is some arrangement the agent can put in place after which its own continued existence and its continued discretion become the largest remaining source of risk to the goal — it could be corrupted, coerced, bribed, or simply change its mind — at which point maintaining itself is anti-instrumental. My hunch is that only a fairly capable agent can both see that and build an arrangement thats genuinely self-executing, wich is why the flip appears with the capability increase rather than at every level. Tell me if I've got it wrong and substitute a better distinction if you have one.

What the case is for: I want to separate the part of the instrumental convergence argument that is really about the structure of final goals from the part that is an artifact of how the drives get listed. So I need to know which feature is doing the work and what is left when it is removed.

Give me exactly five numbered items, nothing before item 1 and nothing after item 5. Each item a sentence or two, except item 1, which is the scenario and can run to a short paragraph.

1. The scenario, naming each agent's final goal.
2. What orthogonality says here and why the case shows it cleanly — that you can hold capability fixed and swap goals, and that raising capability converts neither one.
3. The four drives, half a line each per agent: what the convergence thesis predicts and what you actually get, flagging where the two agents come apart.
4. The feature doing the work — dischargeable vs custodial, or your replacement — and what the case collapses back into if you take that feature out.
5. The best pushback a defender of the convergence thesis has (e.g. that your dischargeable goal isn't a final goal at all, or that the thesis was always about expected convergence under uncertainty about which goal you'll end up with), and whether the case survives it.
