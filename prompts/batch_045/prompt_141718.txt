hey — I take the minutes (and get a vote when someone's on holiday) for the deployment review committee at a mid-size AI lab. We sign off on internal and external rollouts of agentic models. Our written remit says we're supposed to assume the model may be misaligned and ask what still holds if it is; in practice we've been feeling our way along case by case, and I want to know what we've actually been doing.

Our verdicts so far:

1. Approved: coding agent, write access only to a sandbox, every action diffed, diffs read by a small trusted monitor model, hard cap on files touched per hour.
2. Rejected: agent holding real production credentials. Safety case was basically RLHF, the model's own written explanation of why it would behave, and red-teaming that told the model it was a harmless exercise.
3. Approved: draft-only assistant — writes patches and emails, nothing executes without a human click. Someone objected it could draft a phishing email; we said the human is the control.
4. Rejected: agent that could send email to external addresses on its own, even though its code execution was sandboxed tighter than in 1. Sending is irreversible and leaves what we can audit.
5. Approved with conditions: data-analysis agent with decoy credentials sitting in its working directory; any read triggers instant revocation and human review. Conditions: decoys refreshed weekly, kill-switch drill monthly.
6. Approved narrowly: a weak trusted monitor that only catches about 40% of planted sabotage in drills — allowed because a miss costs a rollback, not a disaster.
7. Rejected: a self-critique loop where the same model grades its own actions, as the primary measure.
8. Approved: a pilot where the decisive argument was that the agent got handed plausible opportunities to copy a small internal dataset out and never took them, in an environment we argued looked like production from the model's point of view.

Two things we keep saying loosely: "does the load-bearing part of this safety case need the model to cooperate with us?", and "if the worst case happens on Tuesday, can we still trace it and undo it on Friday" — we've started calling the second one the audit half-life.

New proposal. An agent that opens PRs against a shared internal library about 200 engineers depend on. It can't merge; CI runs on everything; network egress is allowlisted to the package registry and the repo host. Three detection channels: (a) the same weak trusted monitor from 6, reading each diff; (b) a second monitor reading the agent's chain-of-thought for scheming or for intent to slip something past review; (c) the agent is told in its system prompt that it's monitored and gets a channel to flag its own output as suspicious, fast-track human review, no penalty for using it. Monthly honeypot: a decoy credentials file and a decoy "deploy straight to prod" script.

The failure mode we actually care about isn't exfiltration, it's subtle sabotage: a change that reads fine to a human reviewer and to the weak monitor but quietly weakens a validation check, with consequences that surface months later in code nobody re-audits. In our own drills the model can definately produce exactly that when asked. The only channel that would plausibly catch it is (b) — which is a channel the model itself produces, and which a model that wanted to hide intent has every reason to degrade. (There's also an argument we can't settle: is telling the model it's monitored a deterrent, or just training it to hide better?)

So: what do we do? I care less about the thumbs up/down than about the rule we seem to be running, the other rules that fit all eight verdicts just as well, and where those rules come apart on this one. Give me your best guess either way and why, say which of our precedents is the real analog, and if our precedents genuinely underdetermine it, tell us what principle we'd each be committing to with each option. Then name the one further case or ablation that would actually tell the rules apart — I've got candidates (switch off channel b; let it merge; an agent whose reasoning isn't legible to us at all; something irreversible but with a spotless tripwire record) but I can't tell which of them is doing real discriminating work and which just re-runs the same argument.

And please say how confident you are in the call, and single out the one thing you're least sure of — the prediction, the rule you think we're running, or some factual claim about what these systems can actually do.
