Science-and-ideas journalist here, mid-draft on a piece about the "your individual footprint" framing. Last week I moderated a panel on food and emissions, and when someone in the audience asked whether any of us should actually stop buying factory-farmed meat, a woman behind her cut in with "there's no ethical consumption under capitalism" — and the room basically accepted it as checkmate, which feels like a lot of work for one sentence to do.

So, for a sidebar: give me the menu of precise claims that line could be making, from trivial to radical. I can allready see a few — the near-trivial one that every long supply chain has some exploitation baked into it somewhere; a causal one, that my single purchase doesn't change how much gets produced, because of thresholds and inelastic demand; something about structurally caused harms not being fixable by shopping; and maybe a stronger one about responsiblity being the wrong category for consumer choices altogether. For each, tell me whether it's trivial, substantive and defensible, or just false — and the bit I actually need: which of them, if any, would really get her the conclusion that it doesn't matter what she buys. One of the panelists kept insisting the crux is the gap between making a difference to an outcome, having some expected difference, and merely participating in a practice that does harm. Is that the load-bearing distinction here, or a distraction?
