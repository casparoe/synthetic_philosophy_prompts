I'm a junior and the copy chief of my high school newspaper, which means I'm the one who has to defend the style rulings our editorial board has made about gendered language, and I'd like your help with a decision we have to make at our next meeting.

Some background on why I'm stuck. Last fall my English teacher handed out a satirical essay — she said it was by Douglas Hofstadter, and if I remember right it takes a familiar complaint about person-words and swaps the sexes, so that the gender we normally treat as the plain, unmarked default suddenly shows up as the marked, slightly ridiculous one. What stuck with me was the test it hands you: take a word that is supposed to be generic, swap the gender, and see whether the result sounds like a joke or means something different. Our board has quietly been using something like that test for two years, but I cannot tell whether it is the test we are actually running or just a habit of speech, and the case in front of us turns on exactly that.

Here is our record, as I have reconstructed it from the minutes:

We banned chairman in favor of chair, even after the teacher who currently holds the post told us she prefers the traditional word. We banned freshman in favor of first-year, and the minute records the reason: the flipped form sounds like a joke, and that is the tell. We banned mankind in favor of people or humans, and manpower in favor of workforce. We kept human, humanity, woman, manual, and manufacture, on the grounds that those are not the man that means a male person. We allowed actress in one profile because the student described herself that way, though our default is otherwise actor. We kept freshman in the official title of the district's Freshman Handbook when we referred to the publication, and we kept mankind inside a verbatim quotation from a 1969 district report, while allowing neither in our own prose. And last April we ran a humor piece in the April Fools' issue that used several of the banned words, under a heading that said SATIRE and with an italicized note from the editors at the top; we allowed it after a long argument.

The new case: three juniors, all girls, have proposed a monthly feature in which a reporter stops students in the hallway and asks them one question. They want to call it Man on the Street. They say the title is deliberately funny — a wink at how often they have been the only girls in a room — that readers will see their names and photographs, so nobody will think a boy is doing the interviewing, and that Person on the Street sounds like a form at the motor vehicle office. Two complications. First, the feature is meant to outlive its creators: if it works, next year's staff will rotate through it, and there is no guarantee the byline will be a girl's or that anyone will still know the title was a joke. Second, our policy has never been applied to a section title before; we have only ever ruled on prose, on official names, and on quotations.

What I want from you is this. Name the two or three rules that fit those eight rulings about equally well — I can half-see a rule about not using male-marked generics in our own voice and a rule about not making a reader feel that something is not for people like them, but I want them stated sharply enough that they disagree somewhere. Say what each predicts about Man on the Street and why. Tell me what we should decide if the point is to be consistent with ourselves, which of our precedents is the closest analog, and which single detail makes it closest. If our record genuinely underdetermines the case, tell me plainly what principle we would be adopting with each choice, in language the board could actually vote on. And give me one further hypothetical case — a different proposed title, or the same title in a different situation — that would separate the rules that currently agree, so that I can put it to the board as the test.

Please do not just tell me what you would do if you ran the paper; I want to know what our own track record commits us to, and where it runs out.
