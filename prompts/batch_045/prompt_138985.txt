hey — I run a fortnightly reading group on disagreement and forecasting, about a dozen people: half econ/phil PhD students, half working programmers who got into this through prediction markets and the usual rationalist blogs. We keep having the same argument in different vocabulary, so I want to force everyone (me included) to say *which link in the chain* they think breaks.

So: write me a decision tree, as a flowchart in words, that starts from one commitment everybody in the room shares — here are two people, honest, reasonably smart, both of whom have seen roughly the same evidence and have swapped views a few times, and they still assign very different probabilities to the same event; that happens constantly — and ends at a leaf that says what is actually going on in that case. Someone should be able to walk a real disagreement from their own life through it in ten minutes, so keep it to about ten branch questions and eight to eleven leaves. I'm not after a list of views with labels attached; I want questions that sort you by what you're willing to say about cases.

The result I'm reasoning about is the agreement theorem: if two agents start from a common prior, each conditions on their own private information, and their current credences in some event become common knowledge between them, those credences have to be equal. I'll state it roughly like that on the handout, so don't worry about matching anybody's formalism — partitional information structures or whatever — just work from the three commitments: same prior at the start, rationality as conditioning, and common knowledge of the two numbers at the end.

Rules for the questions:

- Nobody should be able to tell from a question which leaf it leads to. No "do you accept the common prior assumption?"; ask about a case instead and let the answer do the work.
- Build the tree from the logical space first — the questions and the combinations of answers they allow — then put the actual positions into the boxes that are occupied, and tell me which boxes are empty and why: incoherent, unattractive, or just unclaimed.
- Where two leaves look the same from the outside and the tree can't really separate them, flag that instead of pretending a branch question splits them.

Cases the tree has to handle (use them as the material for branch questions, don't just park them in an appendix):

1. two radiologists, same images, same training, one says 70 percent malignant and the other 25
2. two macroeconomists who have read each other's papers and still disagree about whether a rate rise causes a recession within a year
3. a case where one side has a possibility the other hadn't even put on the table — before 2001 basically nobody in the group had "someone flies planes into a skyscraper" in their hypothesis space
4. a vague event: "the rollout will go badly", where nobody can say what would count as going badly
5. both parties have done the maths themselves on the back of an envelope, got different answers, and neither has the compute to do it properly
6. they agree on the number but one says "I wouldn't bet my house on that and you apparently would" — same first-order credence, different confidence in the credence
7. they disagree about whether they're disagreeing: A says "we don't really disagree, we just weight things differently", B insists they do
8. one of them has forgotten where she got her evidence, only that she trusts it

Leaves I want to be reachable, in whatever order the tree naturally puts them: the priors were never shared and that's fine, priors are just subjective; the theorem is right but the common-knowledge condition almost never actually obtains, so read it as a convergence claim about idealized back-and-forth; the two aren't working over the same space of possibilities at all, so there's nothing for the theorem to bite on; it's a verbal or vagueness-driven dispute rather than a factual one; it's bounded rationality, i.e. error rather than rationality; the beliefs aren't single precise numbers to begin with, so there's no posterior to compare; and one leaf for "this was never a factual disagreement, the theorem is out of scope". Add whatever else the space demands.

Also, plz: for the different-possibility-space leaf, split it on whether the missing possibility could in principle be handed over, and say whether the theorem comes back once both sides have the richer space — I suspect that sub-branch is doing more work than people admit. And tell me what the tree does with a *nearly* common prior: priors that differ by a hair, or that agree on everything we can actually test. Is "close enough" a leaf, or a fudge?

For each leaf give me: a name in plain english (no person's name, no paper title), one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and a one-line note on whether anyone in that room would actually own it. Then a short "what this tree can't do" section. No reading list, no citations, dont name-drop papers — I want the structure, not the literature.
