I teach a graduate seminar on the ethics and epistemology of strategic deterrence, and I am finishing a chapter on brinkmanship. One of my doctoral students has handed me an argument I cannot refute to my own satisfaction and whose conclusion I cannot accept. Colleagues disagree about where it breaks: one blames the second premise, another the fourth. I would like your diagnosis, and I would like it to do more than point at a premise.

The argument, as she writes it:

(1) A threat is credible if and only if, at the moment at which it would have to be executed, the threatener would still have sufficient reason to execute it, given everything that is then true. Call this the ex post rationality constraint.

(2) Suppose an adversary has carried out a nuclear first strike. Everything the retaliatory threat was meant to prevent has by then already happened. Executing it can only add the further destruction that the adversary's surviving forces would inflict on one's own society. At the moment of decision, retaliation is additional cost with no offsetting benefit.

(3) Therefore no retaliatory nuclear threat satisfies the ex post rationality constraint, and no such threat is credible. (From 1 and 2.)

(4) A threat deters only if the party threatened believes it will be executed. A rational adversary who accepts (3) assigns the threat no probability at all.

(5) Therefore nuclear deterrence cannot deter. Grant me, for the sake of argument, that it has in fact deterred: that the long absence of great-power war is partly owed to nuclear threats. Then something in (1)–(4) must be false.

(6) The reasoning is entirely general. It applies to any threat whose execution costs the threatener something and whose entire point was to secure compliance in advance: a threatened prison sentence, a union's strike threat, a parent's threatened withdrawal of a privilege. If (1)–(4) hold, none of these can deter either. That is absurd.

Two complications of my own, which the argument must survive.

The first is a distinction I draw in the chapter between postures by reference to who holds the retaliatory decision at the moment. In self-keyed retaliation, a human being must authorize each launch and can refuse. In what I call auto-retaliation, the decision is delegated to a system that fires on detection of a first strike, cannot be recalled, and is not anyone's decision at the time. Auto-retaliation looks like the one posture to which (1) simply does not apply, since there is no moment of decision at all. Yet it produces a result I find hard to accept: the more completely a state removes the human from the loop, the less able it is to make any threat conditional, to accept a surrender, or to keep a crisis limited — so a posture of perfect credibility appears to forfeit part of the very influence it was adopted to secure. I call this the credibility ceiling, and I want to know whether it is a real phenomenon or an artifact of how I have set the problem up.

The second is a case she has not addressed. Suppose the first strike is not disarming: the adversary destroys most of one's forces but not all, so that there is a continuation of the war in which both sides retain weapons, further exchanges remain possible, and a postwar settlement is still to be determined. She replies that (2) still holds, because any benefit of retaliation in that continuation belongs to a different, later threat rather than to the threat that was supposed to do the deterring. I am not convinced the two can be separated that cleanly.

Two constraints on the answer. I would rather keep (4) and the historical concession in (5) than (1). And I do not want a solution that rests on leaders being irrational, passionate, or unpredictable in ways that make them unmodellable: I know the standard moves — reputation, commitment devices, the rationality of irrationality, leaving something to chance — and I want them priced rather than merely named.

Please answer in the following sections, in this order:

1. Diagnosis — roughly 200 words: which step fails, and precisely what the failure consists in (an equivocation, a scope or quantifier shift, a modal slide, a step valid only on a reading the premises do not support).

2. Why it looked sound — roughly 150 words: what makes the faulty step compelling, and what the argument is implicitly helping itself to.

3. Ledger of repairs — a table: one row per premise (1)–(4), with your verdict (keep, reject, or repair), the repaired formulation if any, and in one sentence what accepting that repair costs elsewhere in your view of threats, punishment, or rational choice.

4. The two stress cases — roughly 300 words: run your diagnosis against auto-retaliation and against the non-disarming first strike with its continuation. Does either case change the verdict, and is the credibility ceiling a real constraint or my own confusion?

5. Bottom line — roughly 120 words: what, if anything, the episode shows about the difference between deterring an adversary and being willing to act once deterrence has failed.
