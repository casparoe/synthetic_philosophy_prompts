I convene a fortnightly seminar for graduate students in philosophy of mind and cognitive neuroscience, and two of the regulars have been at a standstill since October. Nadia defends something like classical role-functionalism: mental kinds are causal roles within a system's broader functional organisation, and the multiple-realizability argument — that one and the same mental kind can be filled by wildly different physical kinds — is the master argument for that view and against any neural identity theory, because it shows the mental kind is not the physical kind. Tobias defends what he calls realizationism: realization is a constitutive part–whole relation in which a realizer's own causal powers ground the powers of the larger system, so what a state is mentally depends not only on the role it occupies but on what it is made of; and he thinks the multiple-realizability argument is at once empirically overstated (once you hold the taxonomy of the compared systems fixed, the apparent multiplicity shrinks) and metaphysically inert (real multiple realizability, if there is any, is equally compatible with his view, so it cannot be evidence for hers). Their dispute has become one of those arguments where each side has a fully general way of reinterpreting whatever the other says. I have persuaded them to close the term with an adversarial collaboration, and I have agreed to chair it.

What I want from you is the document itself, and it should not lean on the literature: no citations, no reading lists, no ascriptions of views to named authors or papers. Argue everything from scratch, as if the room had no internet, because the point is for the seminar to see whether the multiple-realizability argument has any probative force left once it is stated carefully. Draft four parts. (1) A numbered section of agreed claims — at least ten, each substantive enough that a partisan would feel the pinch of signing it, with at least two claims Nadia would be reluctant to accept and at least two Tobias would be reluctant to accept; include among them an agreed characterization of what would count as a case of multiple realizability worth arguing about. (2) A disagreements table with four columns: the issue; the formulation Nadia would accept for her own position; the formulation Tobias would accept for his; and a note on why that disagreement is not merely verbal. (3) Three cruxes, i.e. sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them. (4) A short closing audit stating which parts of the dispute you take to be verbal, if any, why, and what would settle whether they are.

To keep both of them honest I am stipulating the evidence-generating setup they have already jointly accepted, and I want the cruxes built around it rather than around the usual stock examples. Call it the graft/rewire protocol. A graft intervention replaces the neural state type that normally fills role R in a living subject with a structurally different state type drawn from another lineage, wired so that it receives the same inputs from and sends the same outputs to the rest of the system, while differing in everything that channel does not screen off: internal structure, metabolic cost, temperature sensitivity, drug response, noise profile. A rewire intervention instead holds the realizer fixed and alters its wider embedding, changing its upstream and downstream partners while preserving its causal profile inside the local channel. Assume both are performable and leave the subject conscious and able to report, and assume that rewire interventions sometimes yield a subject whose reports and discriminations we would classify under a different mental kind although the realizer is unchanged — the one-realizer-many-roles flip side of multiple realizability.

My candidate cruxes, which you should use or replace: (a) grain — how finely the role must be specified, given that a fine grain threatens to make cross-system multiple realizability vanish while a coarse grain threatens to let intuitively non-mental realizers in, such as a graft assembled from an entire organ, from a population of trained workers, or from a market; (b) the taxonomic bridge — what entitles us to call the role in two differently embodied, differently environed systems the same role, given that roles are specified over inputs and outputs and the two systems may share no input/output taxonomy; (c) inheritance — whether the realizer's own powers must constitutively ground the role's causal powers or whether merely occupying the role suffices, and in particular whether Nadia can state that requirement without conceding Tobias's view and whether Tobias can still explain why multiple-realizability evidence seemed to matter in the first place. For each crux give a concrete case or result under the graft/rewire protocol that both would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance, concretely enough that a null result or a surprise actually costs somebody something: no crux where both sides can absorb any outcome. Finally, two constraints on the dialectic. Nadia may not solve the grain problem by stipulating that the correct grain is whatever grain the actual neural realizer happens to have, and Tobias may not solve the bridge problem by denying that cross-species mental ascriptions are ever true. Where someone has to bite a bullet, say which bullet it is and what it costs them.
