I keep bumping into the same argument shape in places that otherwise have nothing to do with each other, and I can't find out what it's called — or whether it's really one shape at all. (Maths grad, writes software now, reads this stuff on the side, so sorry if I'm reinventing something with a standard name.)

Here's the move. Someone puts forward a deliberately modest principle: not that everything is actually known, just that for any truth you pick, it's possible — in some stretched, idealised sense, not by us and not now — that somebody could know it. Then somebody else shows that if you add two things nobody would think of denying, namely that being in a position to know a conjunction means being in a position to know each half of it, and that you can't know something that isn't true, plus the plain fact that some truth is in fact unknown, the modest principle turns out to collapse into the wild one: every truth is known. The 'merely possible' claim and the 'actual' claim were the same claim all along.

Two things I noticed that I can't place. I tried running the same derivation with 'could be proved' and with 'could be correctly believed' and it seemed to go through just the same, but with plain belief — every truth is such that someone could believe it — nothing happened at all. So I think the guarantees-truth bit is what's doing the work, but I'd like that confirmed or corrected. Also, weakening the 'possible' didn't seem to rescue anything: even reading possible as just 'not logically ruled out', the collapse still came out.

Does this shape have a name? I first met it in an argument about whether every truth is knowable, but it feels more general than that case, and I've seen both 'modal collapse' (which seems to be about all possibilities becoming necessary?) and 'triviality result' used for what might be quite different things. I don't just want a label though — I want to know what the moving parts are, how this differs from an ordinary reductio or from the oddity of saying 'p, but I don't know that p', and where the boundary lies: which principles fall to it, which don't, and why. And if there genuinely isn't a term for the shape, give me one, with a definition and the cases that clearly count as in or out.
