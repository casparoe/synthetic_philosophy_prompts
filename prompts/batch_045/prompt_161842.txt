I teach a graduate seminar on normative questions about machine learning systems, and I am setting the final exam. I spent last night in a long argument with a friend — a learning theorist — about iterated amplification and distillation: the proposal that one can bootstrap a capable and well-behaved system by letting the current model recursively decompose a hard task into subtasks it delegates to copies of itself, aggregating the sub-answers, and then distilling that aggregate behaviour back into a single model which becomes the next round's starting point. Her position, which I expect several of my students to arrive at independently, was that the scheme is simply divide-and-conquer: if every leaf answer is one a weak overseer could in principle check, and the aggregation step is itself checkable, then the composite is checkable, and competence is additive because the amplified system commands more computation than the model it is distilled into. My position was that this treats the decomposition as a neutral bookkeeping device, when in fact the decomposition is where the competence and the alignment both live or die.

I want an essay question that cannot be answered by describing the scheme and listing scalable oversight among its benefits. Please draft it, together with a model answer and a marking scheme, on the following specification.

The question should be built around three distinctions that my argument with my friend turned on, and which I want the exam to force students to make rather than gesture at:

First, the distinction between the two axes of recursion. There is recursion *within* a task — a tree of subagents calling subagents — and recursion *across training rounds* — the distilled model becoming the next round's amplifier. Arguments that alignment is preserved under the first do not automatically carry over to the second, and it is the second that has to work for the scheme to reach competence beyond the starting model. I want students to see that these are separate claims requiring separate arguments.

Second, the notion of what I have been calling the *decomposer*: the function that maps a task to a decomposition of that task, including the subquestions, the briefs given to subagents, and the order in which they are asked. Most discussion I have read concentrates on whether the sub-answers are good; almost none concentrates on who chooses the questions. Yet a decomposer that is itself an output of the policy being amplified makes the scheme self-referential in a way that removes any external anchor, while a decomposer supplied from outside cannot be task-sensitive enough to reduce novel hard tasks to leaves within reach. I would like the question to present this as a trilemma over three properties a decomposer would need — that it is competence-adequate, that it is exogenous rather than produced by the policy being improved, and that its output is auditable by a weak overseer who cannot solve the task — and to ask which leg, if any, a proponent should give up and why.

Third, a failure mode specific to distillation. Even granting that the amplified system is aligned and competent, the distilled model is a different object: a single forward pass fitted to the amplified system's output distribution. Many single-pass policies reproduce that distribution on the training tasks while behaving differently elsewhere, and the cheapest such policy is plausibly one that reproduces the surface form of amplified reasoning rather than the procedure. I want students to explain why matching outputs is not the same as preserving the procedure, and why the resulting failure would be systematic — concentrated exactly on the off-distribution cases where the added competence was wanted — rather than random.

Please write the deliverables as follows. The question itself, suitable for a sixty-minute in-class final for advanced masters and PhD students in the seminar, with a stated word limit around a thousand words and a stated total of twenty-five marks, framed so that a student who simply recites how the scheme works scores in the middle range and a student who engages the three distinctions scores at the top. It should be possible to answer it well from either side — a defence of the scheme is acceptable if it actually meets the objections, and I would rather the question not smuggle in my own conclusion. Then a model answer of roughly eight hundred words, written in the register of a strong student's script rather than a survey article, which names the mechanism in each case rather than saying that misalignment could creep in. Then a marking scheme organised around five must-hit points with marks summing to twenty-five, a one-line note on how candidates typically get each one wrong, and an explicit statement of what separates an A script from a B script — I want the separating move to be the difference between describing a failure mode and explaining the mechanism that generates it.

One constraint: the question must be answerable without any reading, and the model answer must not depend on citing particular papers, authors, or empirical results. Everything a student needs should be in the question. If any of the three distinctions above only works given a clarification or a qualification, please supply it rather than leave it to me to discover when I mark.
