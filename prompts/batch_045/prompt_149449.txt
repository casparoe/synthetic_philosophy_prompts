I teach a graduate seminar on the ethics of the long term, and I want to assign a short piece that separates two readings of a single symbol. In the standard decomposition r = ρ + ηg — where η is the elasticity of marginal utility of consumption and g the growth rate of per-capita consumption — ρ is the rate of pure time preference: a discount applied to a unit of well-being merely because of where it sits on the timeline. But in the long-run cases that give the debate its urgency, the later well-being is borne by people who do not yet exist, and once generational succession is modelled explicitly — non-overlapping generations, no person alive in more than one period — the formalism no longer distinguishes ρ as impatience within a single life from ρ as a distributional weight favouring earlier-born cohorts: the same discounted sum results either way. Please explain (i) why the two readings collapse in the standard framework, and what would have to be built into a model — overlapping generations, a discount factor defined over life stages rather than calendar periods, an explicit survival probability — to prise them apart; (ii) what that collapse does to the familiar charge that a positive ρ is arbitrary, on a par with discounting by spatial distance or by whose welfare is at stake; and (iii) how far a positive observed or policy-recommended discount rate could instead be doing the work of a hazard rate, and what would tell these readings apart. I want conceptual clarity about what the parameter is doing rather than a survey of positions; use a little notation where it earns its keep, and say plainly which of the distinctions you think is merely verbal.
