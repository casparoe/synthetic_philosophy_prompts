QUESTION (take-home essay, 900 words)

Part (a). A philosopher we will call Vega offers the following argument against mechanism. It is not in your reading, so assess it on its merits.

Suppose that the arithmetical output of a working mathematician — the set of sentences of elementary arithmetic she will, in the long run, endorse — could be captured by some consistent, effectively axiomatized theory S containing Robinson arithmetic Q. By the first incompleteness theorem there is a sentence G_S, obtained by diagonalization on the proof predicate of S (or, if you want the result from consistency alone, by the usual Rosser variant), such that S proves neither G_S nor its negation. Presented with a specification of S, our mathematician sees that G_S is true and endorses it. So her output outruns S; and since S was arbitrary, no machine captures her mathematical capacity.

Vega knows the standard reply: that seeing the truth of G_S requires knowing that S is consistent, and that she cannot have the kind of certainty the argument needs about whichever system she herself implements. Her answer is what she calls the Diagonal Gambit. The reply, she says, mistakes the difficulty. Producing G_S from a specification of S is trivial and mechanizable — the map from an index of S to its Gödel sentence is computable — so the achievement Vega is pointing to cannot be the production of the sentence; it must be the recognition of its truth. And recognition is exactly the sort of thing a formal system cannot accomplish for itself: by the second incompleteness theorem S cannot even prove its own consistency, let alone the soundness that would be needed to certify G_S.

Assess Vega's argument. Is the Diagonal Gambit a genuine answer to the standard reply? Does the conclusion follow even if every step up to it is granted? Say carefully what the first and second theorems do and do not license here.

Part (b). A colleague from the physics department, hearing about this, argues that incompleteness shows there can be no final theory in physics. Any candidate theory of everything would be a finite, effectively axiomatized formal system rich enough to encode arithmetic, so it would have its own Gödel sentence, and that sentence states a physical fact which the theory cannot settle — from which it follows that the search for a final theory is misconceived, and that the theorem tells us something about the physical world and not merely about our axiomatizations of it. Assess this briefly.

ANSWER AS SUBMITTED

The first incompleteness theorem says that any consistent, effectively axiomatized theory containing enough arithmetic — Robinson arithmetic Q suffices, with the Rosser construction if one wants undecidability from consistency alone rather than from omega-consistency — is negation-incomplete: there is a sentence G_S such that S proves neither G_S nor its negation. The proof arithmetizes syntax: formulas and finite sequences of formulas receive numerical codes, provability becomes an arithmetical predicate, and the fixed-point lemma then delivers a sentence equivalent in S to an assertion of its own unprovability. This is a limitation of axioms rather than of logic. By Gödel's completeness theorem for first-order logic, the rules of first-order inference already derive everything that follows from the axioms, so nothing is lost in the passage from semantic consequence to proof; incompleteness is not a complaint that our deductive machinery is too weak to capture our logic.

If S is sound, G_S is true but unprovable in S, so arithmetical truth outruns what S can prove. And the point does not stop with S. Any candidate theory can be diagonalized against in its turn, so the real content of the theorem is that there are arithmetical truths which lie beyond the reach of any formal system: for any theory you propose, diagonalization produces a truth it misses, and strengthening the theory does not close the gap, since the strengthened theory has its own Gödel sentence in turn. The result is naturally placed alongside Tarski's theorem on the undefinability of truth, and the two say much the same thing — that arithmetical truth is not something a formal system can get a grip on, not merely something that our present axioms fail to reach.

Vega is right to concede that the production of G_S proves nothing. The map from an index of S to G_S is computable, so a machine can be built to output it, and an argument resting on production alone would beg the question against mechanism. Nor is the objection she spends her time on the forceful one. What the mechanist questions is not whether she can tell which sentence G_S is, but whether she can be in a position to certify it as true for the system she herself implements. Certifying G_S requires certifying S as sound, or at minimum as consistent; and if she is S, then her certification of G_S is in effect an assertion of her own consistency, which is exactly what the second incompleteness theorem says S cannot prove. The move from S cannot prove its own consistency to the mathematician can see the truth of G_S is where Vega's argument quietly assumes what is in dispute. The situation is best put as a dilemma. Either she can identify the system she implements, in which case mechanism is not excluded — she would be a machine supplied with its own specification, and the second theorem shows at most that she cannot derive her own consistency within that system, not that she cannot correctly judge it and not that she is not a machine; or she cannot identify it, in which case there is no determinate G_S of her own for her to recognize and the argument never gets off the ground. Either way, the conclusion that no machine captures her does not follow, and the Diagonal Gambit answers an objection about production while leaving the epistemic objection untouched.

In substance this is the argument made famous by Lucas and Penrose, and it is fair to say that Gödel drew much the same moral himself: the 1931 paper is best read as showing that the mind cannot be a machine, since there is no consistent formal system whose Gödel sentence the mind cannot see to be true, and this is why the theorem has always been thought to bear on the philosophy of mind and not only on the foundations of mathematics.

Part (b). The physicist's argument is sound in outline. Any candidate theory of everything T would be a finite, effectively axiomatized formal system, and it would contain arithmetic, since it must at least describe the natural-number structure of measurement outcomes and the integers that appear in its own equations. So the first theorem applies: T has a Gödel sentence G_T which T can neither prove nor refute. Since G_T is true, given that T is sound, it states a fact which T cannot derive — a genuine question about the world that the theory leaves unsettled. Hence no final theory is possible, and the search for one rests on a misunderstanding of what a physical theory can be.
