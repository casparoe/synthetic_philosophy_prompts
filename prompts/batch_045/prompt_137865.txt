I'm on the market this year and my job paper is on evidential cooperation in large worlds — the view, roughly, that once you stop modelling a decision problem as a fully specified small world in which my act fixes the outcome, and instead let the agent be uncertain about which situation it's really in (uncertain about the world-model, not just about a state within it), then my own deliberation becomes genuine evidence about what the other agent does, and evidential decision theory starts cooperating in cases where everyone assumed it had to defect. My central case is one I made up, the two cartographers: Ada and Bob are mapping the same valley from opposite ridges, they never meet, they can't communicate, there's no predictor, no shared code, and no common cause either of them can observe, and the payoffs are deliberatly asymmetric — she gets 3 from a completed map and he gets 2, sending a survey party over the pass costs each of them 1 — so nothing about similarity or twinhood can do any work. The only thing linking them is that both are uncertain about the same unmodelled structure, i.e. about which of the candidate world-models is actual, where their own acts are among the things that vary across those models, and I argue that this shared uncertainty is enough to make the probability that the other one sends, conditional on my sending, exceed the probability conditional on my staying, and that this is a real correlation in the grand world rather than a superstition about similarity.
