I'm assembling a one-page handout for a graduate reading group on idealizations of judgment, and I want its centerpiece to be a decision tree that sorts readers by what they take HCH to be, starting from the single entry commitment that HCH, or something like it, is a useful idealization of good deliberation. For the handout, HCH is to be understood procedurally and nothing more: a question is answered by a human with a fixed time budget who may either answer directly or decompose it into subquestions, each of which is answered the same way — by another copy of the same human, with the same budget and the same freedom to decompose — and the subanswers are then merged bottom-up; factored cognition is the same picture read as a division of cognitive labor rather than as a picture of one mind. No branch question may presuppose the jargon, since I want people who have never met the model to be able to answer it.

Give me five or six branch questions and eight to twelve leaves, each leaf with a short name and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Build the tree from the logical space first — the questions and the answer-combinations they permit — then locate in that space the positions people actually hold, and mark any combination nobody occupies with a note on whether it is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. The distinctions I care about are: whether amplification preserves content (eliciting an answer the human already latently has) or constitutes it; whether the procedure has a determinate output at all, given that different decompositions and different per-node budgets yield different answers; whose judgment is idealized, a single person's or a plural subject's, and whether the root question and the stopping rule are themselves amplified or given from outside; whether amplification merely adds computation or also corrects error; and whether the fact that HCH would answer a certain way gives the actual human any reason at all, or whether HCH is only an engineering target.

Each question should be a concrete case rather than a label. I want at least these four: a case where the unaided one-shot answer is prejudiced and the decomposed tree's answer is not; two decompositions of the same personal decision that are equally defensible but pull systematically apart, with no budget left to adjudicate between them; the same question run with a five-minute and a five-month per-node budget, giving different verdicts; and a tree whose amplified output is that the asker should want different ends than the ones written into the root question. Leaves that must appear somewhere include: no-fact-of-the-matter proceduralism; HCH as ideal advisor or ideal interpreter in the manner of reflective-equilibrium accounts; HCH as a mere training or evaluation target carrying no claim about deliberation; HCH as degenerate because amplification has no stable fixed point; HCH as the amplification of the seed human's errors; and HCH as a model of distributed cognitive labor rather than of deliberation proper. There is one cell I want you to fill yourself, because I have heard it proposed and cannot tell whether it hangs together: that HCH has no determinate output but remains the right benchmark because any single run is an acceptable draw from the space of good answers, so we may simply pick a run and act on it — call it run-indifferent benchmarking, place it in the tree, and diagnose it. Finally, flag any pair of leaves your questions cannot in fact separate instead of pretending the tree distinguishes them.
