You are a professional analytic metaphysician whose research centers on material constitution and coincident objects. Please think carefully and in stages: begin by brainstorming, in rough and unpolished form, as many candidate precisifications of the slogan as you can generate — including ones that initially sound interchangeable but differ in what they would require of the world — and only afterwards prune, merge, and test them. Precision matters more than coverage: state each candidate so that one could in principle say of it whether it is true, rather than gesturing in its direction.

Context. My first-year metaphysics seminar was working through the now-familiar case of a clay statue and the quantity of clay it is made from. The statue, it seems, can survive some changes the clay cannot: a finger can be remodelled or replaced, and the statue persists while the lump that once constituted it does not. The clay, conversely, seems to survive changes the statue cannot: roll the thing into a ball and the clay is still there, but the statue is gone. A visitor to the seminar said the puzzle rests on a mistake, and that "two things can't be in exactly the same place at the same time": there is one thing there, and 'statue' and 'lump of clay' are two ways of talking about it, two descriptions under which a single object falls. He said it briskly, as though it closed the question, and then moved on. I am confident the line was doing a great deal of unstated work, but I cannot say exactly what, and that is what I want from you.

What I am asking for is a precisification menu, not a survey of the constitution debate, not a defense of your preferred ontology, and not a set of pointers to literature (I have no library access; I want the argumentative content, not references). Specifically:

1. Set out the distinct precise principles the slogan could be expressing. Include at least: readings that turn on spatial regions, with an explicit account of how exact "exactly the same place" is meant to be and of whether a scattered object has a place at all; readings that turn on sharing parts; readings that turn on sharing properties, and on which properties count; readings that restrict the prohibition to objects of the same kind; readings that are really about causal roles, explanatory redundancy, or a parsimony principle rather than about location; readings that are really about how we count or describe rather than about what there is; and readings that amount to the claim that the statue is nothing over and above the clay arranged in such-and-such a way. Where two candidates differ only verbally, say so and merge them. Where a candidate has a hidden parameter — whose parts, which properties, what granularity of location, an instant or an interval, coincidence throughout a career or coincidence now — state the parameter explicitly rather than leaving it implicit.

3. Test the menu against three cases I constructed, because I want to know which clauses of the slogan are load-bearing and which are decoration. Please also flag any pair of readings that are extensionally equivalent in the standard squash-and-remodel case but come apart in one of these; extensional equivalence in the easy case, I take it, is much of why the slogan feels decisive.

   (a) The crated statue. The statue is dismantled for transport: its parts sit in nine crates in three cities, to be reassembled next spring. While it is in pieces, the statue — if it exists at all — and the clay, if the clay exists at all, still share all of their parts, but there is no single connected region that they both fill. Does the "same place" clause do any real work here, or is the work being done entirely by the "same parts" clause? And does a reading that requires a connected region force a verdict on whether the statue survives disassembly — a verdict the visitor surely does not want to be committed to?

   (b) Two titles. A sculptor owns the statue as a work; the supplier who provided the clay retained title to the material and may reclaim it, and everyone agrees that reclaiming the material can only be done by ending the statue. Two parties thus hold, at the same time, rights whose exercise pulls in opposite directions with respect to one occupied region. If there is exactly one thing with two sets of relations to different people, nothing here forces two objects; if the difference in what may be done is a difference in the thing itself, it does. Which readings of the slogan can treat this as a merely relational difference, and is that treatment available to the visitor without cost — in particular, does it generalize to the persistence and survival conditions that the standard argument relies on, or is there a principled difference between the two kinds of difference?

   (c) The grain. If the statue and the clay may coincide, why not the statue and the clay-minus-one-grain, or the clay taken together with the block it was cut from, or the mereological sum currently arranged statuewise, or the surface of the statue and the surface of the clay? For each reading, say whether it blocks the statue/clay pair cheaply — that is, only by blocking coincidences, parts, or countings that even the visitor wants to retain — or whether it draws a principled line, and say where exactly that line falls.
