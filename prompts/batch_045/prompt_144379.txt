I spent thirty years as a field entomologist and now, since retiring, I sit on the volunteer panel that signs off on pest-control requests for the city's parks, school gardens and community orchards — six or seven a season, twenty minutes each, sometimes ten if it's an emergency, and we owe the parks department a one-paragraph written rationale per decision. None of us have any philosophy. Increasingly the requests come to us arguing about insect welfare and we argue back the same way, so I want that argument turned into something we can actually run rather than improvise each time.

The rough principle I keep coming back to (I more or less made it up out of population biology and some reading on invertebrate sentience, so treat it as mine) is: don't count bodies, count foreclosed experience, and count it net. Three corrections go into that. The great majority of juveniles in an outbreak population would be dead within days of something else anyway, so the number killed massively overstates the loss — what matters is the remaining expected experience of the few who'd have made it. For a population sitting at carrying capacity, removing individuals mostly changes who gets to live, so the ledger has to subtract the lives that will now exist because the competition went away. And it cuts the other way too: a defoliated host supports fewer herbivore-days later in the season, so doing nothing can cost more than intervening. There's also an asymmetry I want kept: predators, parasitoids and pollinators are longer-lived and far less replaceable per individual than the pest herbivores, so a broad-spectrum kill can do more damage in welfare terms than the pest kill it was meant to avert.

Turn that into a decision procedure for my panel: the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each with the time and expertise we have, where it stops early with a no, and where it has to hand off to a person because the question is values rather than facts. I specifically want to know what we do when a step needs a number nobody can give us — how confident we are that these animals have anything at all to lose — because that seems to me like a dial rather than a fact and I don't want the procedure pretending otherwise. Then run it on three from last season: a systemic soil drench proposed for green peach aphid on the heritage rose beds; a winter-moth caterpillar outbreak defoliating the street oaks where the options were Bt, barrier bands on the trunks, or nothing; and a yellowjacket nest in the ground next to the toddler play area, which I suspect the procedure simply can't handle and should say so. I expect at least one of these to come out against what I'd vote for on instinct, and I'd rather hear that than have the procedure quietly rigged to agree with me. Also be straight about where it will misfire and what it can't do at all.

One format thing: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — continuous prose, the way you'd write it in an email to a colleague.
