I've been circling the basilisk argument since the original forum threads, and the thing I have never seen pulled apart cleanly is two claims that usually get run together: first, that a future system could have a genuinely *credible* standing policy of penalizing agents who foresaw it and did not help; second, that a decision theory which tracks correlations rather than causal influence makes you vulnerable to such a policy. I want a case that concedes the first claim in full and isolates the second — a case where the threat is real, where carrying it out is genuinely in the retaliator's interest, and where the only reason to pay is evidential.

Please construct it to the following specifications.

1. Mundane in every respect except the retaliator's existence: no perfect predictor, no simulation of a person, no torture, no acausal or backward causation, no exotic physics. Ordinary stakes: complying costs the victim on the order of a few thousand dollars or a few weeks of work; the penalty is roughly ten to a hundred times that.

2. The retaliator must actually carry out the penalty, and must have a forward-looking reason to do so that survives the standard reply that no rational agent wastes resources punishing someone who has already failed to comply. I want credibility secured structurally — by something like a standing policy announced in advance and applied to an entire class of agents, whose deterrent value with respect to agents it meets later exceeds its cost. State the policy and say why it is credible rather than merely asserted.

3. Targeting must not depend on whether the victim has heard of the threat. I want the information-hazard framing out of the picture entirely: the victim is selected on the basis of facts already on file about them, whether or not they ever learn of the threat and whether or not they ever deliberate about complying.

4. The victim decides after targeting is already settled, and the variable the retaliator's targeting reads must be (a) unknown to the victim at the moment of decision, (b) genuinely correlated with whether the victim would comply, and (c) not causally or subjunctively downstream of the victim's decision procedure — a record or trait fixed independently of what that procedure outputs at the decision node. Choose the variable and justify it on all three counts.

5. No precommitment escape hatch: there is no earlier point in the story at which the victim could have bound themselves, so the case cannot be won by pointing out that any theorist would have wanted to have been the sort of person who pays.

Keep the scenario itself to about two hundred and fifty words. Then show the work: give the verdict of causal decision theory, evidential decision theory, and functional decision theory, with the reasoning exhibited rather than asserted, and say whether updateless ex ante policy selection changes anything. Identify the single feature that makes evidential decision theory come apart from the other two, show what happens in both directions when you remove it — let the victim read their file, and let the retaliator model the victim's decision procedure instead of reading a fixed record — and give the strongest reply available to someone who thinks the evidential verdict here is correct, or at least no objection to evidential decision theory.

What the case is for: I want to test, in a discussion I am preparing, the claim that the basilisk only bites an evidential decision theorist, and that functional decision theory paying in the usual version reflects a real dependence on the decision procedure rather than riding on a bare correlation. That fixes what counts as cheating. If your scenario gets evidential decision theory to pay by stipulating that paying is good news, or gets functional decision theory to refuse by stipulating that nothing depends on the victim's decision, it has not done the job.
