I write fiction, and a character of mine — a retired proofreader who has begun keeping a diary in a language she invents as she goes — has backed me into an argument I can't find my way out of, and I need to know what escaping it costs, because I have to decide what she concludes in the last chapter.

She stipulates that a new sign of hers, "thrale", applies to pairs of numbers thus: give their sum, unless both numbers exceed 10^20, in which case give 5. Then she reasons:

(1) Everything she has actually done with the sign — every inscription, every mental application, every disposition she has formed — is finite, and every bit of it concerns small numbers.
(2) For any finite record of applications there are infinitely many rules that fit it exactly while diverging on cases it does not cover: the rule she stated, and the rule that gives the sum unless both numbers exceed 10^20 or the computation is performed after 2031, in which case it gives 5, and endlessly many more besides.
(3) Her dispositions cannot adjudicate, since nobody can be disposed to respond to a pair of numbers beyond 10^20 one way rather than another; her introspective report that she "meant the first rule" is itself just another item in her head, standing in need of interpretation exactly as the sign did; and a community cannot adjudicate either, because she has no community — nobody else speaks her language, which is the point of the diary.
(4) So there is no fact of the matter about which rule she meant, nor about which of her future applications of the sign would be correct.
(5) If there is no fact of the matter about what a sign means, then no sentence containing it says anything definite — it has no content at all.
(6) What holds of her sign holds of every sign, since every speaker's record with a sign is finite in the same way; so nothing anyone has ever said says anything.
(7) But (6) is absurd, and it also devours the reasoning that produced it: if nothing says anything, then steps (1) through (6) say nothing either, and she has established nothing.

I would rather pay almost anything than surrender (1), (2) or (3): the finitude of the record and the absence of a community are what the book is about, and I don't want the final chapter to dissolve into a reassurance she hasn't earned. So tell me which step to reject, why that step looks as though it follows, and what each available way out costs — including whether the community-based answer, the one that appeals to what others would let you get away with asserting, is any use to someone who by construction has no community. Please answer in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, numbered lists, bold text or tables.
