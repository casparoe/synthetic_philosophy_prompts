I am a novelist, and I need a scene built to a specification rather than an illustration of a thesis. The scene is the hinge of a novel I am writing; its job is to make the reader do something the reader will afterwards be unable to justify, and I want to know exactly which bolt in the machinery is bearing the weight before I trust it.

Here is the specification. Write me a short realist scene — a single beat, not a chapter, three hundred words at most — in which two characters make the same gamble for the same reasons, with the same information, the same degree of care, and the same willingness to risk what is at stake, and in which the outcome comes out badly for one of them because of something neither controlled. The reader must end up blaming the unlucky one more. The scene should be written as it will appear in the book: no character may say the word luck, no narrative aside may frame the issue, and the argument belongs entirely to your commentary afterwards, not to the scene.

The hard part is that the shift in blame has to be generated, not stipulated, and it has to survive two standard deflations. First, it must not be the evidential route: the bad outcome cannot be allowed to function as evidence of a worse will, less care, worse judgment, or a more reckless disposition, so the scene must be rigged so that the reader can verify that the two gambles and the two gamblers are indistinguishable in every respect that the control principle counts. Second, it must not be mere contamination — squeamishness, gore, sentiment, the reader flinching at a corpse. The reader has to be able, on reflection, to endorse the difference, or at least to be unable to shrug it off. If those three requirements cannot be met together, say which one has to give, build the strongest scene you can with the other two, and tell me what I lose.

After the scene I want the audit, in four parts. One: state the two versions — write the bad-outcome scene, then give me in a sentence or two the minimal change that yields the good-outcome counterpart — and show that the control-principle verdict is identical across them, and show how the scene has closed the evidential route. Two: tell me what the residual attitude actually is, since I suspect it is neither blame in the ordinary sense nor the regret one feels for a stranger's accident, and say why it is fitting rather than merely caused. Three: run two diagnostics I have been turning over. The ordering test: does the effect survive if I withhold the chance mechanism until after the harm instead of showing it up front? The swap test: if the outcome is transferred to the other character, does the blame follow it? Tell me what each result would show about whether my effect is evidential or constitutive. Four: name the feature of the case doing the work, say what happens to the effect if I remove that feature, and give me the best available attack from someone who thinks blameworthiness cannot vary with what lies beyond an agent's control — the attack I should expect from my most unsentimental reader, not a straw man.

Finally, a craft question rather than a philosophical one: given whatever turns out to be doing the work, should the scene be in first person or close third, and why? I would also be grateful to know which of my instincts here is most likely to produce a scene the reader feels tricked by rather than implicated in.
