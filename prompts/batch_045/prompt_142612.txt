Eighteen months ago my father died and left my sister and me joint control of the small charitable fund he had set up. The deed gives each of us an independent annual allocation — roughly forty thousand pounds apiece — to disburse as we each see fit, provided only that it reaches a registered charity. Over the past two years my sister and I have discovered that we disagree about nearly every grant either of us proposes: I want the money to go where it does the most measurable good per pound, she wants it to go to the hospice and the parish youth work in the village where we grew up. We have been splitting the difference, and both of us now leave every meeting feeling that our half was simply wasted.

A friend who works in philanthropy told me that what we have been groping toward has a name — moral trade — and I have since read just enough about it to think the fit is genuine but not perfect. Next month I have to put the idea to the fund's advisory panel in about forty minutes with nothing but a whiteboard. The panel is my sister; our father's old colleague, a retired actuary; and my cousin, a hospice nurse. None of the three has any philosophy or economics. My sister is quick, allergic to jargon, and will treat any hint of condescension as evidence that the whole scheme is a device for pointing her money at my causes.

One complication runs through all of this and I do not know where in the session it belongs. My sister's objection to my preferred grants is not that she ranks them below hers. She holds that there are things she must not fund — that to direct money to them would be to cooperate in something seriously wrong, and that this remains true whatever she might get in return. I cannot tell whether that closes off the idea entirely or only forces us to look for a different lever, and I would rather find out from you than in front of her.

So please do not explain moral trade to me; plan how I should explain it to them. I want: the order in which the component ideas have to arrive and what each one depends on, including what must be in place before a trade so much as looks available to people who share no scale of value; the single example that should carry the whole thing, what it must contain and what it must carefully not contain, and why that example rather than the vegetarianism-for-bednets sort of case, or rather than our own disagreement; the misconceptions to expect, in the order they will actually arise, with the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off — that it is just compromise, that it is vote-trading, that it is bribery, that a person who will act against her own convictions for a price never had any, that if her view is mistaken I should not be paying her to act on it, that we should simply argue until one of us wins, that if she would have funded the hospice anyway my concession buys nothing, and that both of us have an incentive to overstate how much we would hate conceding; where in the sequence my sister's constraint should enter and what the honest answer is about whether someone with a constraint of that shape can trade at all; one question I can ask at the end that would show the idea had landed rather than merely been endured; and what I should openly decline to explain at this level, naming it as a refusal. I am not asking you to adjudicate our disagreement about which causes are worth funding, and please do not point me to anything to read — I want the shape of the session, not sources.
