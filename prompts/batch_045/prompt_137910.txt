I'm writing an essay for a module on comparative philosophy, and I want to read Mary Shelley's *Frankenstein* through a relational conception of personhood of the sort associated with ubuntu — the idea that being a person is not something one has simply in virtue of being a human organism, but something one comes to hold through one's relations with others.

Please distinguish at least three versions of that claim and test each against the novel: a constitutive reading, on which relations are partly what make someone a person, so that a being wholly outside community is not yet one; a merely recognitive reading, on which relations are how personhood is acknowledged and confirmed, not what creates it; and a counterfactual-relational reading I would like you to try out, on which what matters is whether the candidate could stand in the relevant relations were the surrounding community not failing it — so that a being wrongly excluded does not thereby cease to be a person.

Bring these to bear on specific episodes: the creature's solitary self-education beside the De Lacey cottage, his demand that Victor make him a companion, and the interview with the blind De Lacey that the returning family breaks off. On which reading is Victor's failure a failure to confer personhood rather than to acknowledge it, and does that difference change how culpable he is? Does the novel vindicate the constitutive reading, dramatise its horror while granting it, or refute it by making the creature unmistakably a person whom every human community refuses? Where does the book resist all three readings, and what, if anything, does it add to the philosophical debate that the arguments on their own do not?

Please write your answer in British English.
