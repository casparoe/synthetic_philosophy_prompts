In a reading group I've been going through John Locke's A Letter Concerning Toleration (1689, in William Popple's translation), and we've hit a passage I cannot square with the use later writers make of Locke in debates about church and state. The passage says, in part: "The business of true religion is quite another thing. It is not instituted in order to the erecting of an external pomp, nor to the obtaining of ecclesiastical dominion, nor to the exercising of compulsive force, but to the regulating of men's lives, according to the rules of virtue and piety." A friend in the group objects that if the business of true religion is to regulate men's lives according to virtue and piety, and if the civil magistrate is likewise responsible for outward conduct and for restraining vice, then this passage seems to give the magistrate a warrant to enforce religious morality—which is just what defenders of established churches and state-collected church taxes have argued. I do not think that can be Locke's view, but I cannot reconstruct from the passage itself why not. Can you explain what the passage is claiming about religion, compulsive force, and the regulation of conduct, and whether my friend's objection actually shows that the passage supports a larger role for the state in religion than Locke is usually taken to allow? Please begin your reply by stating your bottom line in the first sentence; then give the reasoning. Use no preamble and do not restate the question.
