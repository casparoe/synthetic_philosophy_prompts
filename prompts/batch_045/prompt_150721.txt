I got into Jain philosophy a few weeks ago through a podcast episode (one of those "ancient wisdom for modern life" ones, which is maybe already a red flag) and I want to know how much of it I should actually keep. The host summarized anekāntavāda roughly like this:

"Reality is infinitely complex, so no single statement can ever capture the whole of it. Every claim is therefore only partly true, and when two people disagree they're both right from their own angle. The Jains built a whole logic around this: instead of saying the pot exists, you say 'in some respect the pot exists,' and in principle any dispute can be dissolved just by attaching 'in some respect' to both sides. The illustration they use is the blind men and the elephant, which shows that every religion is grabbing hold of one part of the same truth — that's why Jainism is sometimes called the most tolerant religion in the world. Technically it's a form of relativism: truth is relative to a standpoint and there's no fact of the matter about who is right. That's also the philosophical root of ahimsā, because if you can never be sure you're the one who's right, you have no business imposing your view on anyone. One more detail: they worked out that there are exactly seven things you can say about any object — that it is, that it isn't, that it both is and isn't, that it's indescribable, and three further combinations of those — and since all seven hold, the doctrine never actually rules anything out."

I liked this a lot, partly because it sounds like a fancier version of "everything is subjective," which is roughly where I already was. But my flatmate, who did a religion module at uni, said the relativist bit is definatly the thing Jain philosophers spend most of their time denying, and now I can't tell which parts of the episode to trust.

Can you go through it claim by claim and sort it into: what someone who actually works on Jain philosophy would accept, what they'd wince at as a simplification, and what they'd reject outright? In particular:

- If it isn't relativism, what is it, and what stops it from collapsing into relativism the moment you press on it?
- Can "in some respect" really dissolve any dispute, or does the view still call some positions simply wrong? If so, which ones, and can you give me an example of a claim it rules out?
- The elephant story — is that even a Jain illustration, and does its moral match the doctrine? I'm confused about how it fits with the Jain idea that some beings have complete knowledge of everything.
- Is the link to ahimsā and tolerance part of the classical doctrine or a later apologetic thing?
- Is the list of seven right, and is "all seven hold" the right way to take it?

The thing I actually care about: a colleague used this at a lab meeting to argue that the statistician saying a drug "works" and the clinician saying it "doesn't work" are both right and we should stop arguing. That felt like an abuse to me, but if the doctrine really is "in some respect, both," I'm not sure what the abuse is. What does the corrected version say about that case — and about a claim where nobody's angle seems to support it, like a flat-earther's?
