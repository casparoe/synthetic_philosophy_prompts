You are a professional moral philosopher who works on decision-making under deep uncertainty and on the ethics of trying to do good with one's life. Please think this through carefully and at length before answering. Begin with a few paragraphs of unstructured brainstorming: what you take the deep structure of my problem to be, what the main candidate answers are, and what the strongest objection to each of them is. Only then give me your considered recommendation.

Here is my situation. I am 32 and have two job offers, and I have argued myself into a state where I cannot reason about the choice at all.

Job A is a forecasting and logistics role at a large agricultural-inputs company. I would be making the distribution of seed and fertiliser more efficient. I have no illusions about the firm: it mostly serves intensive monoculture, and I expect that, other things equal, making that system run more smoothly is mildly bad. It pays roughly twice what Job B pays, and I intend to give away a third of my income. At the margin that money would go to a global-health charity of the kind that distributes insecticide-treated bednets and vitamin A supplements — interventions whose short-run effects are about as well evidenced as anything in the social sciences.

Job B is at a small regional organisation that helps farmers move to lower-input practices. It pays much less, I would give away almost nothing, and whatever good I do would be local, visible, and small. There is also a real chance the post depends on a grant that will not be renewed, in which case in two years I would be job-hunting again, possibly back where I started.

The paralysis comes from a friend who reads a lot of philosophy. She says I cannot compare these options by adding up their consequences. Her argument, as I understand it: anything I do sends ripples outward — the people I affect go on to affect others; the technologies and institutions I nudge shift which innovations arrive and when; the money I give changes who ends up being born — and within a few generations the number of people touched is so enormous that anything I can predict about the next decade is a rounding error by comparison. And those ripples are precisely the part I cannot predict; I cannot even guess whether they are for the better or the worse. So, she says, if I want to choose by consequences, I am choosing on the basis of a quantity whose value is set almost entirely by terms about which I know nothing, and 'adding up the consequences' is simply not an operation available to me.

I have tried to find a way out, and I want you to examine each attempt and tell me where it holds and where it breaks.

First, symmetry. I said: fine, but my ignorance is symmetric. For every way the ripples could turn out good there is a mirror image in which they are bad. If that is right, the far-future part of the calculation has an expected value near zero, and I should simply maximise the near-term, well-evidenced good — which points fairly decisively at Job A. She replied that symmetry is exactly what a clueless person is not entitled to assume, and that my inability to think of a reason for a tilt is not evidence of no tilt. I do not know how to answer her, and I also do not know whether the burden of proof here is mine or hers.

Second, sensitivity. It occurred to me that her point may be worse than she makes it. If the far-future stakes are as astronomically large as she claims, then the expected value of that term is violently sensitive to extremely fine differences in my confidence: a tilt of a hundredth of a percentage point one way or the other would swamp every near-term consideration many times over. On that picture my decision would be determined by differences in probability far finer than I could ever justify holding. That makes consequentialist deliberation look not merely hard but structurally hopeless. But I also cannot shake the suspicion that this argument proves far too much, since it would seem to apply to every decision anyone has ever made.

Third, a distinction I invented to try to rescue myself. It seems to me that cluelessness might not be uniform across choices. Some effects look sign-locked to their near-term effects: if a child survives because a bednet was distributed, the ripples flow through a life that exists because of the intervention, and I cannot see why those downstream effects should be systematically opposed in sign to the good of her surviving. Other effects look sign-free: altering how an institution works, or changing when some technology arrives, could push things in a direction bearing no relation at all to whether the short-run result looked good. If that distinction is real, then Job A and Job B are not symmetric cases, and I would at least know which kind of ignorance I am facing. Is the distinction real, or am I fooling myself — and even if it is real, does it do the work I want it to do?

Fourth, replacement. If I decline Job A, someone else will take it; the applicant pool for that kind of role is deep. Job B, by contrast, may go unfilled, or be filled by someone worse than me. Does that make the difference I make in Job A smaller, larger, or is it simply one more thing I cannot know?

Fifth, a decision rule for ignorance. I have been toying with abandoning expected-value calculations altogether and asking instead which option's ranking is most robust: which option would still look better across the widest range of reasonable ways of filling in everything I do not know, and which one flips depending on guesses I have no grounds for. I like this because it seems to make use of my ignorance rather than pretending to have resolved it. But I worry that it smuggles my assumptions back in through my choice of what counts as a reasonable way of filling things in, and that in practice it may collapse into doing whatever I was already inclined to do.

Please work through these five points — saying where my reasoning is sound, where it fails, and what I am missing — and then tell me how I should actually go about making this decision, and how much confidence I should have in the answer you give.

Do not present a contested view as though it were the consensus, and flag the points where your own recommendation depends on a judgement you cannot fully defend. If the difficulty I have been circling has a standard name in moral philosophy, mention it in a sentence or two at the very end — but no bibliography or further reading, and do not let the name do any of the real work.
