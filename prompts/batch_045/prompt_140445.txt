I'm a journalist — I cover science and medicine — and I'm working on a piece about consent forms, the click-through kind and the signed-in-triplicate kind, and I keep typing the phrase "valid consent" without being able to say what makes it valid. So I did what I always do when I'm lost: I wrote down a pile of cases, marked each one with my gut, and now the pile won't sit still.

- Someone agrees to sex with a condom, partner takes it off halfway through. Definitely not consent, and worse than I expected myself to feel about it.
- Patient signs for a knee operation; surgeon gets in there, finds something else, fixes that too. Low risk, genuinely beneficial, patient would probably thank her. Still not fine to me.
- Patient signs for "robot-assisted" surgery and the actual cutting is done by the system running on its own, nobody's hands on the console for most of it. Not fine. But — and here's the thing that bugs me — I'm fine with the surgeon handing half the operation to a resident the patient never met. Both are a substitution in who does the thing. Am I just defending whatever's normal?
- Nurse practitioner at a community clinic tells a patient the visit is covered; it isn't and she's on the hook for $400. Gut says the consent to treatment is still fine, the billing is a separate wrong. But then: a study enrols people telling them it's about memory when it's about conformity, same risk, same payment. That one I say is invalid.
  Those two look structurally identical to me — same risk, same money, lie about something the person cared about — and I give opposite answers. I have no idea what's supposed to separate them.
- Blood donor is told the fee goes to a charity; it goes to the company's overhead. Annoying. I don't think it voids consent.
- Boss says sleep with me or you're fired. Coerced. Partner says sleep with me or I'm leaving. Not coerced. The partner is also threatening to do something she's entirely entitled to do.
- A phase I trial in a country where $2000 is four months' wages, for a drug with real risk. My gut says "not coercion" and then immediately says "but not fine either," which probably means my gut is useless on this one.
- Drunk yes at a party: not consent. But my dad, early Alzheimer's, signed a letter last year saying he'd hand over the keys when his doctor said so, and I think the letter should win over what he's saying now. Which seems backwards — in one case I throw out the impaired present self in favor of the earlier one, in the other I won't let the earlier self's explicit instruction count.
- 16-year-old getting an IUD without her parents: yes. 17-year-old selling her eggs for tuition money: no. And a 15-year-old refusing a transfusion on religious grounds: no, override her. If there's a shape here it's not just "kids can't consent."
- Signed a waiver to get into the climbing gym. I don't think that's consent to the risk of the wall falling on me because they never inspected the bolts, but I do think it's consent to the ordinary risk of falling off. Same signature.

The principle I keep falling back on is: nobody pushed them, and they'd have said yes anyway if they'd known the whole truth. But that has to be wrong somewhere. The blood donor would have said no if she'd known — so my principle calls it invalid, and my gut doesn't. And there's a version that's worse: a patient who'd have refused if he'd known the valve was from a pig, or that the surgeon was Black. I don't want to say the "would you have said yes if you knew" test makes that consent invalid, but I also don't see how to stop it from saying that.

So: is there one principle — or a short list of them — that gets most of my verdicts, and where does it overrule me? I'd like it stated tightly enough that I can run new cases through it, with a clause-by-clause note on which case forced the clause. And two cases I haven't put on the list because I keep flip-flopping: a woman who said yes to being named in a story while she was hypomanic and now wants out, and a friend who slurred "yeah fine" about a quote at a conference party and then texted "still fine" the next morning. Does a later clear-headed yes repair an earlier broken one, or is that a different question from whether the first yes counted?

One more thing I actually need for the piece: after all this, is there anything left in "she consented" beyond a label we stick on a bunch of unrelated complaints — the pushing, the lying, the not-getting-it — or is there a core that all of them are failures of?
