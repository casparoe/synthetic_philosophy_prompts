I have recently taken charge of a four-person signal-assessment team at a national plant-health agency. Every Monday we publish a single probability, for each of about a dozen notifiable pests, that it is already established somewhere in our region; the deputy director signs the sheet off and it goes to people who decide whether to impose movement restrictions. The inputs are a mixture of hard findings and soft ones — a member of the public's photograph, a grower's suspicion, a partial sequencing run, a press report from a neighbouring country — and until now we have folded them in by discussion and instinct. Last month an external reviewer asked why our figure for one pest moved from 0.15 to 0.6 in a single week on the strength of one photograph, and the best I could offer was that it had felt right. I have since been told that what we are doing has a name and a rule attached to it — Jeffrey's rule for updating on evidence you are not certain of — and that the rule only applies when the shift is what they call rigid. I want a procedure we can actually run, not a tutorial.

Constraints: spreadsheet only, about half an hour per case, no probability training assumed beyond a week's internal course, and every step written down in a form an external reviewer can follow. What I need:

- Start from the partition, not the hypothesis: how to decide what the signal is directly about, at what grain, and how to spread the probability that is left over, since I gather the answer depends on this.
- A check on whether an expert's number is usable as the shift at all — I suspect that when our pathologist says she is seventy per cent sure the photograph shows the pest, she has already folded in what she knew about the region, and that using her number as the shift counts that twice.
- A test for rigidity, and a specified alternative when it fails: what to do instead when the same item that moves our confidence about, say, a laboratory sample also tells us something about how much that sample was ever going to tell us.
- What to do when nobody can do better than "somewhat more likely" or "somewhere between sixty and eighty per cent", and what to publish as the single headline number when the honest output is a band.
- Where the procedure stops and hands the decision to our senior epidemiologist, and an honest marking of which steps are real constraints from the theory and which are judgment calls dressed up as steps.

Please run it on three cases from the last fortnight: (1) the photograph, with the pathologist's seventy per cent; (2) a partial sequencing run from the regional laboratory — the kind of evidence our unit's budget is justified by — commissioned because a grower nearby had already reported symptoms, and produced in the same week the laboratory changed to a more sensitive kit; (3) three soft items arriving together: a machine-translated press report from a neighbouring country, a trap specimen degraded in transit and identified only to genus, and nothing at all found in two hundred traps elsewhere. Give me the unwelcome answer on the second if that is what the procedure gives, and close by saying what the procedure cannot do. Please write in British English.
