For a very long time — arguably since antiquity — the same small set of answers to the paradox of painful art has been in circulation: the negative emotion is converted or transformed by its artistic framing; the pleasure is a second-order response to one's own capacity for pity or fear; the state in question isn't really grief or fear at all but an imaginatively framed analogue of them; or the aversive affect is simply offset and outweighed by formal, cognitive, or moral rewards. Everyone in the field has heard everyone's objections, and I can't see that anyone has moved. I want a diagnosis of that standoff, not a verdict on it.

My hypothesis is that it isn't one disagreement. First, the sides may be explaining different explananda: an occurrent feeling of pleasure during the experience, a disposition to seek such works out and return to them, a retrospective judgment that the experience was worth having, or an absorbed state that is neither straightforwardly pleasant nor unpleasant. Second, the theories may be tacitly scope-restricted. Accounts that lean on representation — fictional objects, imagined outcomes, attitudes toward characters — are built for tragedy and horror and handle them well; they have much less to say about the enjoyment of wordless sad music, where there seems to be nothing to pity or fear. If both are right, the apparent standoff is a mismatch of questions rather than a conflict about mechanism.

If I'm wrong, say what kind of disagreement it actually is: verbal, a dispute about which cases count as data, or a genuinely empirical question about affective phenomenology that we currently lack the tools to settle. Take a definite position and argue for it against the rival diagnoses. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis. I've been playing with three candidate discriminators — repeat exposure to works whose endings are already known; the apparent preference for stronger doses of the very affect that is supposed to be aversive; and the mismatch between sadness in music and sadness in fiction — but I can't tell whether any of them really separates the families of answer, and I'd like you to say which do, which don't, and why. Be committed, but flag where your diagnosis rests on a contested claim about what audiences actually feel, and say what would most quickly change your mind.
