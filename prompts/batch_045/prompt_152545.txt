I'm putting together a grad seminar on formal epistemology for the spring, and the session I'm least comfortable with is the one on logical uncertainty, so let me lay out where I'm stuck. I got into an argument about the logical induction framework with a friend from the CS side over drinks at a conference last month — the setup being a computable sequence of prices over all the sentences of some formal language, where the headline guarantee is that no computable trader running in roughly polynomial time can make unbounded money betting against the prices, and where the prices are, by the framework's own admission, not coherent at any finite stage but only coherent in the limit. He thinks this more or less wraps up the old worry that Bayesianism demands logical omniscience. I keep trying to say why I'm not convinced, and the strongest thing I've got is this: if the prices aren't coherent at a given stage, then the Dutch book theorem applies — there's a set of bets the agent would accept at its own prices that guarantees a loss. So at every finite stage there's a sure-loss contract waiting for the inductor, and a bounded agent that managed its beliefs this way would be systematically loosing money. That looks fatal if the framework is meant to be normative rather than merely useful. He says something like "the bookie can't find the bets," but as I understand the Dutch book theorem it doesn't care whether anyone can find them; incoherence is a property of the credences, not of the bookie's computational situation. Is my objection actually right, and if not, where exactly does it break?

Second thing, which I half believe and half don't: he claimed that because the prices converge to 1 on every true mathematical statement, the framework finally delivers the correct prior over the undecidable cases — he used the continuum hypothesis as his example — so we can drop the whole project of figuring out what the "ur-prior" over mathematical structures ought to be. That sounds too strong to me, but I can't say wich step is wrong, and I'd rather not hand a subtly false claim to a room full of sharp grad students.

Third, a toy example I want to put on the problem set, which we also disagreed about: the claim that the 10^12th digit of pi is 7. My friend says a rational credence there has to be 0 or 1, since the digit has some definite value and the sentence is either true or false, so anything in between — the framework famously lands near 10% on this kind of claim — is just a defect of the setup. I think he's wrong but I'm not sure how to say why without begging the question about what degrees of belief are even for. Any help on all three would be much appreciated, especially on whether my Dutch book argument has a real hole in it.
