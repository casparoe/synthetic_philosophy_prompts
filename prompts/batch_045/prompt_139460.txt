Hey — hoping you can help with something for the discussion group I run. It's a loose crew, six or seven of us in the back room of a pub, and we've spent this term on how thought experiments actually persuade people. We got a bit obsessed with something we started calling shadow-sensitivity: take a case, find its knobs — the incidental details you can flip without touching the philosophically loaded part, like who found the thing and where, how much time passed, whether it was typed or hand-written, whether anyone stands to gain — flip them one at a time, and watch whether the verdict survives. If it flips on a knob that shouldn't matter, we say the pump is leaking: it was never tracking the principle, just someone's reaction to a bit of framing that happened to be in the room.

The case we keep coming back to is one of ours, not from any reading: a novelist dies leaving a finished manuscript and an explicit instruction in her will that it be destroyed unread. In her desk is an unsent letter, written years earlier and addressed to no one in particular, saying that if the book turned out to be any good she'd want it out in the world once she was gone. Should the executor publish? The room was split roughly down the middle, and — this is the part that got under our skin — people's confidence didn't seem to track how many knobs they'd checked. It tracked whichever knob they'd thought about last.

So here's the ask: a sestina (six six-line stanzas plus the three-line envoi; don't name the form in the poem, and please don't stick a preface on it explaining what a sestina is). Use these six end-words, which are also more or less the six knobs of the case: will, letter, drawer, years, burn, silence. Let each stanza be a version of the case with one knob turned, so that the verdict shifts as the words rotate, and let the rotation do the arguing — a reader should come away feeling that whichever word arrives last looms largest, which is sort of the whole problem. "Will" doing double duty as document and as faculty at least once would be a nice touch, if you can manage it. The envoi should bring all six back into three lines and stage the disagreement rather than settle it: our last session ended with two of us convinced the drawer-and-letter business is doing all the work and one of us convinced that's exactly where the principle lives, and I'd rather the poem leave that open than pick a winner. It should read as a real poem for someone who has no idea what we argue about, and be careful enough that someone who does think about this stuff wouldn't wince — so no implying the case has a settled answer, and no quietly assuming a knob is irrelevant just because calling it irrelevant is convenient.

Then, after the poem, a short note (a few paragraphs, prose, not verse): separate what's actually settled among people who work on this from what's genuinely contested, and don't present a contested view as the consensus. We were arguing about whether the instability thing is well replicated at all, and about what it would even show if it is — that case intuitions are noise and should be tossed, that surface noise masks a stable competence underneath, or just that we should be stricter about which knobs we're allowed to touch. I'd also like you to flag anywhere the poem takes liberties with the ideas or leans on a reading that's itself up for grabs, including anything in my own setup above that you think is shakier than I've made it sound.
