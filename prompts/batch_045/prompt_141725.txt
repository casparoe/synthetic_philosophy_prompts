I work in product analytics, and a fairly widely-read substack post that keeps getting passed around internally ("preference is what you do, not what you say", by a data scientist who writes about econ) argues roughly this:

"Preferences aren't hidden mental causes of behavior sitting inside people's heads — they just are the pattern in what people do. You never need to ask anyone anything; you watch what they pick, and with enough observations you can reconstruct the utility function they're maximizing. That's revealed preference theory, and it's why clickstream and purchase logs tell you what people want better than any survey. Nor is it vacuous: choices have to be consistent, so if someone picks x from a menu that also contained y, then in any other menu containing both they can't turn around and pick y — that's roughly the weak axiom — which is a genuinely testable restriction. Sure, people violate it all the time: put a decoy option on the shelf and they flip from the cheap bottle to the expensive one. But the theory's answer is just that their utility function changed between the two menus, so nothing could ever really refute it; it's a redescription of behavior, not a theory of anything. The practical upshot: utility is a number we read straight off the logs, it's the same quantity welfare economics cares about, and a change that raises measured engagement makes users better off by their own lights."
