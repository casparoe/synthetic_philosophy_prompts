I'd like a case analysis rather than a history lesson. Take the enactment of the Fair Labor Standards Act in 1938 — the first durable federal minimum wage in the United States — and analyze it through the economics and ethics of price controls. I know the textbook story: a floor set above the market-clearing wage reduces the quantity of labor demanded, so a minimum wage buys higher wages for those who keep their jobs at the cost of jobs and hours for the least productive, and the magnitude of the trade-off is an empirical matter. What I want is a disciplined mapping of this particular episode onto the available rationales, and an account of what the episode does to each of them.

The facts I am working from, which you may take as given rather than look up. The Act set a floor of 25 cents an hour with higher rates scheduled for later years, attached an overtime-premium requirement, and applied to employees engaged in interstate commerce or in producing goods for it. It exempted agricultural and domestic workers, among others. It arrived two years after the National Industrial Recovery Act's industry codes were struck down and one year after the Supreme Court, in West Coast Hotel Co. v. Parrish (1937), upheld a state minimum wage law and overruled Adkins v. Children's Hospital; its federal constitutionality was confirmed in 1941. Wages in the South were far below those in the North, Southern legislators were pivotal in shaping the bill, the rate was national rather than regionally differentiated, and the exempted sectors were where a large share of Southern — and, above all, Black — workers were employed.

Please do the following.

First, run the competitive price-floor model on it properly. Identify the players, the markets, and the margins: the floor is a single national price in an economy with a large regional wage gap, so it binds in some places and not in others, and the model's employment effect is therefore not one national number. Say what it predicts about hours, effort and hiring standards, about product prices and consumers, about capital-for-labor substitution, and — importantly — about the uncovered sector the exemptions create, since displaced labor has somewhere to go.

Second, run the competing rationales alongside it: the monopsony and efficiency-wage family, in which a floor can raise wages and employment at once because employers have wage-setting power; and the collective-action story, in which jurisdictions competing for mobile firms cannot raise standards unilaterally and a national floor solves a coordination problem, with the corollary that incumbent high-wage firms and organized labor have a private interest in a floor that raises rivals' costs. For each feature of the Act's design — national uniformity, commerce-based coverage, the sectoral carve-outs, the level chosen, the staged increases — say which rationale predicts it, which merely tolerates it, and which is silent. In particular, tell me whether the carve-outs are an anomaly for the anti-sweatshop and anti-poverty rationales the reform movement actually campaigned on, and what they look like from the competition rationale instead.

Third, be honest about what the episode can and cannot test. The economy was recovering from a depression, the NRA had intervened and then vanished, and war mobilization followed within three years. Tell me what evidence one would need, and whether the 1938 case can supply any of it.

Finally, the ethics, which interest me most. Distinguish the candidate justifications — a minimum as a requirement of justice in exchange or non-exploitation, a minimum as social protection for the poor, and a minimum as a remedy for unfair competition between jurisdictions — and say what the Act's actual design commits us to. If the operative justification was the third, the policy is not principally about the worst-off, and the costs of the uniform national rate fall hardest on the lowest-wage region's least productive workers; is that a defect of the instrument, a fair price for solving the coordination problem, or a reason to think the wrong people were asked to pay? And one last thing: minimum wage setting in the United States has since moved sharply toward cities and states, which is arguably the very jurisdictional competition a national floor was meant to end. Does the 1938 episode count against that localism, or does the modern case for local rates break the analogy — and what would have to be true for the analogy to hold?

Please reason from the models and the facts above; I am not asking for a survey of the empirical literature or for citations. Where a rationale has nothing to say, say so rather than forcing it to speak.
