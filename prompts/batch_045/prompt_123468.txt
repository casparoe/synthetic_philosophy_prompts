Hey — I'm a third-year med student doing a clinical-ethics elective, and one of the philosophy PhD students in our reading group has me backed into a corner and I can't find the exit. We were going over why neurological criteria are supposed to be a legitimate way of declaring death, and he gave me this chain, which I think proves way too much but whose bad step I can't put my finger on (he claims it's a version of an argument in some paper he read, but I suspect he's mixing two things up):

(1) A human organism is alive only when its parts are unified into a self-maintaining, self-regulating whole — a system whose activity is directed at keeping itself going from the inside, not a pile of subsystems that somebody is maintaining from outside.
(2) In an adult mammal, the organ that does that unifying is the brain. Destroy it and the whole falls apart in exactly the way you'd predict from (1): no temperature regulation, diabetes insipidus, hypotension, no coordinated response to anything at all.
(3) So death is the irreversible loss of the brain's integrative activity, and the neurological criteria are a legitimate test for it. (I want to keep this one. I've scrubbed in on procurements and I really don't want to conclude we were killing people.)
(4) Now take a patient — call her Aisha, 34, viral cardiomyopathy, on VA-ECMO for three weeks. Her heart is fibrillating and ejecting nothing. The pump makes the pressure, the oxygenator does the gas exchange, and the ICU team does the pH, the temperature, the electrolytes, the volume. Her brain is intact and she's aware, but during those three weeks nothing inside her is integrating her into a self-regulating whole — the circuit and the clinicians are.
(5) If the only thing that made Aisha an organism was something playing the integrative role, then during those three weeks nothing in her was playing it. Nothing in (1) says the integrating has to be done by the brain specifically; what (1) requires is that it be happening at all, from inside.
(6) So for those three weeks Aisha wasn't an organism. She was a collection of artificially supported subsystems — i.e. exactly what (2) says a body with a destroyed brain is.
(7) But Aisha came off bypass, learned to walk again, and remembers a fair amount of it. Something that stops being an organism for three weeks and then starts being one again isn't a biological category, it's a miracle. So (1) or (2) has to go, and the integration story never actually supported (3) in the first place.

When I try to block it at (6) — Aisha kept the internal *capacity* to self-regulate, the brain-dead body has lost it — he says that's the question and not the answer, and hands me a case: a woman declared dead by neurological criteria at 14 weeks of pregnancy, kept on support, who delivers a live baby at 30 weeks, having regulated her own temperature, blood pressure, hormones and wound healing well enough to gestate a fetus for four months. Whatever internal capacity I want to credit Aisha with, he says, that woman has more of, and if I withhold it from her I'm just helping myself to the conclusion. He also made a point about "permanent" vs "irreversible" mattering for the circulatory criteria, but that felt like a side quest.

I want to hold on to (1), (2), (3), and the plain fact that Aisha was alive the whole time. So: which step do I actually give up, why did it look fine, and what does giving it up cost me — especially if, as he claims, dumping (2) lands me on a "higher brain" view where someone in a permanent vegetative state counts as dead, which I like even less? Don't bother with citations or reading lists, I've had enough of the literature; I just want the structure of the thing and the price of each way out.
