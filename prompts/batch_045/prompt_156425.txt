I sit on the clinical ethics committee of a mid-sized teaching hospital, and a case from last month has left me unable to hear a certain sentence without wanting to interrupt. The patient was an eighty-two-year-old woman with advanced Alzheimer's disease: minimally responsive, no longer recognising anyone, no living family, no visitors for something like two years, and all of her care delivered by rotating agency staff who never knew her before her illness. The question was whether to transfer her for aggressive treatment of aspiration pneumonia and start tube feeding, or to manage her palliatively. One member of the committee, a geriatrician who brings a certain amount of African philosophy into our meetings, ended the discussion with the line that a person is a person through other persons, and then drew his conclusion in a single step: she is a person to the extent that we are in relation with her, and so we cannot quietly downgrade her standing by pointing to her solitude. I objected that on that same principle she has become steadily less of a person as her visitors stopped coming and her chart became the only thing that mentions her; he said I had misunderstood him. Neither of us could say what precise claim the sentence was making, and the meeting ended with the vote split along exactly that fault line.

What I want from you is a menu, not a verdict. Set out the distinct theses that sentence could be expressing, the trivial ones as well as the radical ones, and for each say whether it is a platitude about how human beings develop, a substantive metaphysical claim about what personhood consists in, a claim about a social status conferred (and conceivably withdrawn) by recognition, a claim about self-conception and identity, a claim about what we owe one another, an epistemic or evidential claim about how we come to know that someone is a person, or something else again. I have tried to sort the options myself along three axes and keep getting stuck: whether the dependence on relations is constitutive or merely causal; whether the relation that matters runs from the person to others, from others to the person, or must be mutual; and whether what is at stake is moral status or something more like identity and self-understanding. Use those axes if they help and replace them if they do not. I also want the parameters made explicit: whose relating counts (a community, a single other person, an institution, the dead, someone hostile), what has to pass between the parties (care, recognition, the possibility of address and response, mere causal interdependence, someone holding a file with your name on it), whether the relation must be current or whether being remembered is enough, and whether the view makes personhood scalar, so that standing comes in degrees.

Then put each reading to work. I have been testing my own intuitions against some probes, and I would like them used as test cases: a child raised with no human contact and brought into a clinic at fifteen; a hermit who has chosen solitude for forty years; a prisoner in long-term solitary confinement; the last surviving speaker of a language, whose entire community is dead; a newborn infant; a patient in a persistent vegetative state with a large and devoted family; and a conversational AI system that thousands of people treat as a person and that invites them to. For each reading, say what it implies about these, and flag the readings whose implications I should treat as a reductio rather than as a consequence to be accepted. Finally, tell me which reading my colleague needs in order to reach his conclusion that unvisited patients must not be downgraded, whether that reading is one the sentence actually supports or merely one it is compatible with, and whether any reading of it supports the palliative recommendation instead. If no reading both captures the sentence's real rhetorical force and avoids licensing the downgrading of people whom nobody visits, say that plainly, name the best reading available, and tell me honestly what it costs to hold it. Please argue from the structure of the claims rather than from who has defended what: I have no library access from the meeting room, and the committee would not be moved by citations even if I could check them.
