I am closing the final session of a graduate seminar on P. F. Strawson's essay *Freedom and Resentment*, and I would rather end with a poem than with a summary. Would you write me a sestina to read aloud — thirty-nine lines, six stanzas of six, a three-line envoi, unrhymed, with the standard end-word rotation (if the first stanza closes 1-2-3-4-5-6, the second closes 6-1-5-2-4-3, and so on)?

The six end-words should be: **resentment, gratitude, love, forgiveness, excuse, treatment.**

The reason for that set is the reason for the form. Five of them come out of the vocabulary of the participant attitude and one — *treatment* — out of the vocabulary of the objective attitude: the stance in which someone is an object of policy, management, training, therapy, a case to be handled rather than a person to be answered. The rotation means the speaker never gets outside the same six words; they only ever get them in a different order. That is, I take it, close to Strawson's own point: the objective attitude is not a third thing floating free of the reactive attitudes but a suspension and rearrangement of them, intelligible only against a background in which one still stands in relations to somebody. Please let the words change grammatical function across stanzas — *love* and *excuse* as noun and verb, *treatment* as care and as handling — since a word's doing a different job each time it returns is part of what the poem is about.

A rough arc, only if it helps:

Begin inside an ordinary small injury — a friend who promised to come and didn't, a confidence passed on, something too slight to be tragic — with the reactive attitudes in full operation and no philosopher anywhere in the room. Then let the excuses arrive in their ordinary order: he didn't mean it, he didn't know, he couldn't help it, he was under a pressure no one could have been expected to bear. The asymmetry I want registered is Strawson's: each of these is an invitation to name something particular about this person on this occasion, and it mollifies or suspends the attitude without threatening the practice, whereas the philosopher's global version — that nothing anyone ever does is up to them — is a different speech act altogether, not an excuse but a demand that the whole practice be disbanded. I would like the global thesis to arrive in the poem the way weather arrives, not the way a reason arrives.

Then the objective attitude: the speaker trying, almost as an experiment in relief, to see this person as a case. Then try universalizing it — a small settlement, a clinic, a cloister, a colony whose members have all agreed to meet one another that way. What I want shown is why Strawson's complaint is not merely that such a world would be cold: a world in which everyone met everyone else so would not be a world of relationships at all, and there would be nothing left for gratitude or forgiveness to be, no one to whom a promise could be owed, no one who could be excused. If you can make the poem register that the stance would have lost its *content* rather than merely its warmth, so much the better.

Then the double edge, which is where our discussion actually stalled: the objective attitude is mercy when it exempts and an injury when it demotes. It is plainly warranted toward the very young, someone deep in dementia, someone in the grip of an episode — and it is a distinctive wrong to visit it on someone who is fully able to answer for themselves, to be managed when you asked to be answered. I would like *you* in the poem to be doubled: the friend who failed to come, and, by the middle stanzas, the philosopher who says it was never up to you — so that excusing you and refusing to answer to you come to look like the same move. Do not resolve whether they are.

Two accuracy constraints, since I will be reading this to people who have done the reading. First, do not let the poem suggest that Strawson's argument is that the reactive attitudes are innate, universal, or selected for, and therefore immune to argument; that is a stronger and different claim. His is that a general theoretical conviction has no purchase on the particular case, and that the practice is not grounded in a prior metaphysical belief about contra-causal freedom, so it is not hostage to the denial of that belief either. Second, do not let the poem refute incompatibilism or vindicate compatibilism; Strawson's target is the assumption that the question turns on the truth of determinism at all.

Otherwise: no -isms, no named philosophers, no technical terms as props, no footnote-poem. Someone with no philosophy should hear it and be moved; someone who knows the essay should be able to say what each stanza is doing and should not have to wince. The envoi should carry all six words, and I would like the very last word of the poem to be *treatment* — arriving after thirty-six lines in which the participant vocabulary has done all the work, and landing as something the speaker neither chose nor could quite refuse.
