A friend of mine, an ecologist, and I spent most of last night arguing about the menu changes my committee is about to announce. She says that swapping our default burger from beef to chicken is straightforwardly a win for animals, and that moving the hot line from beef chili to a lentil-and-soy chili is a bigger one. I pushed back: chickens are small, so per unit of protein you are killing many more of them; but beef takes far more crop acres per calorie, and those acres contain field animals and machines that maim them; and neither of us could say whether the animals who are never bred because we ordered less belong in the ledger at all, or whether a short and painful life is worse for the animal than no life. We went in circles, partly because we disagreed about the physics of the supply chain: whether an order of our size changes anything upstream, or just rearranges which animals die and where.

What I need is not a verdict on beef. It is a procedure. I staff the food procurement committee for a university dining service: roughly fifteen thousand meals a day, four menu cycles a year, decisions that have to be defensible to students, to the budget officer, and to the ranchers who sell to us. The committee are not philosophers. I get an afternoon per cycle and whatever figures are publicly available, and I cannot audit supplier welfare claims.

Please write me a decision procedure I can actually run on a proposed change to the menu: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure returns a definite no; and where it must stop and hand the decision back to the committee because the unresolved question is one of values rather than fact. I want handled explicitly: how to decide whether a purchase of our size has any upstream effect at all; how to count animals killed or injured during crop cultivation alongside animals slaughtered, given that they die in very different numbers and very different ways per unit of food; how to weigh a large number of short lives against a small number of long ones; whether animals never brought into existence enter the calculation, and in which direction; what to do when published estimates disagree by an order of magnitude, including how I should present a break-even threshold to a committee that wants a single number; and the point at which the procedure should decline to help because the institution's reasons are not purely consequentialist.

Then run it on our two live proposals: the beef-to-chicken burger swap, and the beef-to-lentil chili swap. My friend expects the procedure to bless both. If it does not, say so plainly.

Finally, state how confident you are in each substantive claim the procedure rests on, and single out the one you are least sure of.
