Me and two friends ended up in a proper argument after seeing a new biopic and I need someone to referee, because I honestly can't tell who's winning. (I'm making the film up — it's a stand-in for the general problem.)

The film is called Salt Year, about Ivy Thornton, a real labor organizer from the 1930s. The scene we fought about: Ivy leaves her eight-year-old daughter alone for a week during a factory occupation. It never happened. The director has said in interviews that Ivy did neglect her family and that the scene is emotionally true. Key detail: every line of dialogue in the scene is taken verbatim from Ivy's own letters, rearranged and put in that situation. Also relevant: the film merges three of Ivy's real colleagues into one composite character, and it's already being shown in schools as basically the only thing kids encounter about her. Ivy's granddaughter has publicly objected.

Marisol: Look, Ivy isn't harmed, she's dead, I grant that. The wrong here is to the living. The film edits what we can collectively remember. A vivid film image occupies the same mental slot as a memory and it's way stickier than a footnote, so the fiction doesn't sit beside the truth, it overwrites it — she calls this recollective capture. Showing it in schools makes it the default memory for a whole generation.

Grant: Every biography compresses. Two hours can't hold forty years; any film invents dialogue, merges minor characters, moves events around. Once you accept any of that, accuracy can't be the thing doing the work, it's just degree talk. And empirically films push people toward the record: this one has sold more books about Ivy than anything in twenty years. On the memory worry it might be net positive.

Marisol: Compression and attribution are different in kind. Merging three colleagues is compression — though notice it also quietly deletes real junior colleagues, mostly women, and hands their work to a fictional stand-in. Inventing the abandonment is different: it attributes a morally loaded, character-implicating act to a named real person. And the letters thing makes it worse, not better. She calls it quotation laundering: every sentence is authentic Ivy, and that's exactly what lets a fabricated scene borrow her own voice as a witness against her.

Grant: The composite also *protects* people — you can show a pattern without accusing a named individual. And the scene isn't arbitrary: documented neglect exists, just not that week. If you can't dramatize a pattern you can only ever make a documentary, and almost nobody watches those, so Ivy stops mattering to anyone at all. Emotional truth is a real thing even if you can't falsify it — otherwise you're banning most historical drama ever made.

Marisol: Emotional truth is unfalsifiable, which is why it conveniently licenses whatever plays well. And there's an obvious out that filmmakers refuse: change the names. A thinly veiled version gets you all the emotional truth and costs nobody's memory. Insisting on the real name means they want the borrowed-name premium — the authority of nonfiction plus the freedom of fiction. And her standard isn't documented-or-forbidden: it's don't show a named real person performing an invented act that a reasonable viewer would read as reflecting on her character, unless there's record-level reason to think she did something relevantly like it.

Grant: That last clause is where all the action is, so it's just my degree talk with extra steps. Also, changing the names helps less than it sounds: audiences know who it's about, and it removes accountability — a named biopic can be checked and fought over in public. What the fictional version loses is the film as a real act of reckoning with an actual person.

Marisol: Not a veto for the family, then, but standing — the descendants are the only continuing stakeholders, so at minimum their objection flips the burden of proof onto the filmmaker.

Grant: Her distress is real but the film isn't a eulogy, and families dont get a veto over history.
