I'm on a tiny team building a shared scheduling assistant for small teams — five or six people per workspace, it proposes slots and mostly just books them unless someone overrides — and we've decided the only data we'll use is behaviour: accepts, declines, manual reschedules, and how long people take to respond. No stated preferences, no onboarding questionnaire, no 'tell us about your ideal week' screen. The reason we gave ourselves is that self-reports are cheap talk and behaviour is where the rubber meets the road. I'm writing up the design doc now and I want someone to check whether the commitments we've ended up with are actually jointly tenable, because I have a feeling at least two of them are quietly fighting each other and I genuinely can't tell which. One thing that matters for context: because it schedules a *team*, the thing we're inferring preferences *for* is constantly trading one person's strong dislike of a slot against another person's mild preference for it, so we do seem to need something like comparative strength and not just a ranking.

Roughly where we've landed:

(a) Behaviour only, never self-report. If someone types 'I hate morning meetings' and then accepts morning meetings for three weeks, the accepts are the fact and the sentence is commentary.
(b) We model each person as roughly Boltzmann-rational: they pick the better option most of the time but not always, with a per-person noise/temperature knob that we fit and then mostly treat as a nuisance parameter and ignore.
(c) We deliberately strip out what we call 'artifacts': position in the list, whatever came pre-checked as the default, and last-minute time pressure. The deployed version actively reorders recommendations to counteract the fact that people click whatever is on top.
(d) Non-paternalism. We're trying to give people what they want, not what's good for them — no wellness scoring, no nudging people toward focus time unless they've behaved like they want focus time.
(e) Recency. The last few weeks get much more weight than everything before; if someone's pattern shifts we treat that as their preference having changed rather than as them having been confused earlier.
(f) When a whole family of different reward functions fits the same behaviour equally well, we've decided not to care. We call ourselves instrumentalists about the reward function: everything downstream is just ranking the next action, and every member of that equivalence class ranks the same, so who cares which one we fitted.
(g) Effort matters. A manual cancel-and-reschedule is much stronger evidence than a one-tap accept, and we weight by how much effort the person expended; slow, reluctant responses count as weaker than fast ones.
(h) Online learning is fine. Our own suggestions are part of the environment people are responding to, so we just keep re-fitting on the stream of behaviour the assistant itself helped produce.

One of my friends, who does choice modelling on the econ side, says (c) is straightforwardly paternalistic and I say it obviously isn't, so if you could settle that too that'd help — but what I actually want is an audit: go through these and tell me which pairs are really in tension and how badly, ordered by severity rather than all flagged as equally broken, which ones only look like they conflict and actually dissolve once you're careful, and for each real tension what specifically would have to give and what that would cost us in the product. Don't tell me to just add a questionnaire — we've ruled that out for reasons that aren't philosophical. And if you think one of these is doing more damage than the others, say which one has to go.
