I teach a grad seminar in philosophy of physics and we spent two sessions on Maxwell's demon this term — I thought I had the standard Landauer–Bennett story down cold until one of my students (the one who always asks the question I can't answer) came to office hours with this. Set-up: one heat bath at temperature T, a Szilard-style demon doing feedback control, and a memory. I dont want papers or a reading list, I want the physics and the argument itself.

(1) On the story I teach, every step of the demon's cycle can in principle be done reversibly — the measurement, the sorting, the extraction of work — except one: resetting the demon's memory to a standard state, which by Landauer's principle costs kT ln 2 per bit, and that dissipation is what keeps the second law intact.
(2) Erasure gets into the picture only because we've assumed the demon's memory is finite: after N bits it's full and has to be wiped before it can sort again.
(3) But nothing in physics compels a computer to erase. A machine that writes each new result on a fresh, previously untouched square of an ever-growing tape, and never overwrites or resets anything, performs exactly the same computation, is logically reversible, and by Landauer's own criterion dissapates nothing.
(4) So hand the demon an unbounded tape — or just keep shipping it new blank registers, one per measurement. Now every operation it performs is reversible, no bit is ever reset, and there is no erasure step anywhere in the gas + demon + memory system.
(5) It therefore extracts kT ln 2 of work per bit from a single bath at temperature T, indefinitely. The only net changes in the world are that the bath has given up heat, work has been done, and the tape has gotten longer and is covered in bits.
(6) That is a straight violation of the Kelvin statement of the second law — heat drawn from a single reservoir and converted entirely into work — unless the growing tape counts as an "other effect," in which case the second law is saved only because we quietly smuggled in a resource, and nobody told us which one.

I can feel roughly where to push back — that "blank" tape looks suspiciously like a free lunch, and that a demon plus an ever-growing tape isn't a cyclic device — but I can't turn either of those into a clean diagnosis, and I want more than a slogan about information being physical. Which step actually fails, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular: is Landauer's principle still doing the saving work here, or does the cost land somewhere else entirely — in preparing the memory medium, say, or in the tape itself as a low-entropy fuel? If the tape really is a fuel, does anything interesting follow for the slogan that information is physical — is a register in a known state just another kind of battery? And one more wrinkle: a colleague in HPS keeps telling me the whole Landauer exorcism is circular, that it assumes what it's meant to prove; if that's right, (1) goes, and then where does the argument above end up? I'd rather not give up (1), since half my syllabus hangs on it, and I'd rather not give up (6) either, since I don't want to be caught redefining the second law to fit the case — but tell me honestly which step goes and what I'm paying either way.
