I am writing a paper on the legality of the post-war military tribunals, and I want to press one thesis rather than rehearse the familiar complaint about victors' justice, which strikes me as sociological rather than legal. My claim is that Nuremberg and Tokyo are not on a par: the difference between them is one of legality, not of degree.

At Nuremberg the counts of aggression rested on a norm with a pre-existing textual basis — the 1928 Pact of Paris, in which the parties renounced war as an instrument of national policy — and Germany was among those parties. At Tokyo, by contrast, the corresponding counts reached back to the Manchurian campaign of 1931 and the war in China from 1937, conduct that no instrument binding on Japan had proscribed at the time it occurred; Japan had given notice of withdrawal from the League by 1933, and my understanding is that it was not a party to the instruments the prosecution relied on. I concede that the two charters define crimes against peace in nearly the same words, which is awkward for my thesis, but I think the difference in the underlying conduct matters more than the difference in the drafting. Since nullum crimen, nulla poena sine lege seems to me the load-bearing principle of legality, the conclusion I would like to defend is that one can accept the Nuremberg verdicts while regarding the Tokyo verdicts as legally void: Tokyo punished ex post facto, Nuremberg did not.

To sharpen the argument I have been distinguishing what I call charge retroactivity — the conduct was not denominated a crime when it was performed — from liability retroactivity — international law, whatever it forbade, attached responsibility to states rather than to individuals, so that no official could have foreseen personal punishment. My sense is that Tokyo suffers from the first defect while Nuremberg suffers only from the second, and that the second is comparatively trivial, since a head of government who orders an aggressive war can hardly profess surprise at being called to account for it.

Before I commit to the argument, I would value your verdict on it. Is the asymmetry thesis sound as I have framed it? And how should I answer the objection I expect from a referee — that the Allies' own conduct was never charged, that the Emperor was never indicted, and that the researchers of Unit 731 were granted immunity in exchange for their data? I take that objection to be a political complaint about hypocrisy, and therefore no reply to a legal argument, but I should like to be sure that I am right to set it aside.
