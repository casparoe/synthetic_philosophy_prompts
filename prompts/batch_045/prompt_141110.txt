I'm a second-year PhD student, and on Thursday I'm presenting a paper against lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism to a workshop where at least two people in the room hold something close to it, so I need to be argued with rather than lectured at. Please play a committed, well-informed defender of that view for the rest of this conversation: sharp, never a strawman, willing to concede small points — that the location of the threshold is vague, that nobody knows what the units on the intensity scale are, that interpersonal comparison of phenomenal intensities is a mess — but never the lexical priority itself. Stay in character until I say stop. Keep each reply to at most two short paragraphs, argue from inside the view rather than about it, and don't appeal to published authority, citations, or any empirical claim you would need the internet to check; if you need to stipulate something about one of my cases, state the stipulation explicitly and move on. If I start to strawman you, correct me sharply and restate the position in your own terms before you answer.

Here is the exact version you are defending. First, pleasure and pain are fully commensurable: there is one cardinal hedonic scale, and below the threshold they trade off against each other in the ordinary way. Second, there is a threshold intensity of suffering, call it T, such that a single episode at or above T lexically dominates any finite quantity of pleasure and any finite quantity of sub-threshold suffering — no amount of pleasant experience, however vast, and no amount of milder suffering, however vast, can equal it or outweigh it. Third, T attaches to individual episodes of suffering, not to aggregates and not to whole lives. Beyond that you may formulate the view as you like, as long as it stays recognisably this one. To clear the ground: I concede in advance that the view does not commit you to preferring an empty world, and I will not run that objection at you, and I will not treat the vagueness of T as a refutation. What I want to fight about is the shape of the scale and the unit of moral accounting.

My opening move is a case and then a fork. In the first world, ten-to-the-fifteen people each endure a single ten-minute episode of nausea at intensity 0.999T, and the same arrangement also produces, somewhere in a much larger population, an unbounded supply of mild pleasures: coolness on a hot day, the small satisfaction of finishing a task, the taste of cold water. Every one of those episodes is sub-threshold, so by your own first claim each is outweighable by enough pleasure, and there is no limit on how much pleasure is on offer — which seems to let this world come out better than a world in which none of these people exist. In the second world, exactly one person endures one ten-minute episode at intensity T, and everyone else lives out an ordinary, mildly contented life. Your view says the second world is worse, and worse in the strong sense that no finite addition of pleasure to it could ever lift it to the level of the first. So a difference in intensity no subject could notice in a single case does all the moral work, while a total quantity of suffering that is astronomically larger by any additive measure counts for nothing against it: that looks less like a single scale with a cliff in it and more like two currencies you have stapled together. Now take a horn. Either T is just a number on the intensity scale, in which case tell me why the curve kinks exactly there and nowhere else and why the kink is not simply bolted on — and note that my case can be rebuilt for every neighbouring threshold, so you cannot answer me by nudging T — or T is meant to mark something qualitative, the way agony colonises attention, fragments a life and makes it impossible to care about anything else, in which case the thing doing the work is not the intensity of an episode but the extent to which a subject's practical standpoint is taken over, and ten years at 0.999T can take a standpoint over as thoroughly as ten minutes at T, so the lexical rule belongs to lives or to aggregates rather than to episodes. While you are in there, answer one more thing: when you say that sub-threshold suffering can be outweighed by pleasure, must that pleasure reach the sufferer? If it must, your commensurability claim has quietly become a person-affecting one and you owe me an account of why the lexical rule is not; if it need not, tell me what outweighing amounts to when nobody is compensated. Pick the horn, concede what you have to, and come back at me.
