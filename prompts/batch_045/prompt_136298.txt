I'm in a final-year metaphysics reading group on realism and anti-realism, and we've just spent a session on Fitch's paradox of knowability. I came away uncertain both about how the argument is supposed to work and about how far it reaches, so I'd like a careful audit of a summary I've been leaning on.

Over the break I read a popular book on the realism debates whose chapter on anti-realism treats the paradox as more or less decisive. My reconstruction of the central passage, close to its wording: "The anti-realist's guiding slogan is that every truth is knowable: whatever is the case, it is possible in principle for someone to come to know that it is the case. Fitch's paradox turns this slogan against itself. Take the claim that some truths are in fact unknown. If that claim is true, then it is a truth that can never be known -- because the moment anyone came to know it, it would cease to be true, since it would no longer be unknown. So if there are unknown truths at all, there is at least one unknowable truth, and the anti-realist's slogan collapses. Since of course there are unknown truths, the only way out would be to deny that anything is unknown, i.e. to attribute omniscience to someone; so the knowability principle, and verificationism with it, is refuted. The same argument also kills the more cautious versions of the principle. Suppose you retreat to the weaker claim that every truth about the physical world is knowable in principle, the sort of thing a scientific realist or a physicalist might want. Fitch's construction goes through there too, because the relevant instance is still, in the only sense that matters, a claim about the physical world conjoined with a claim about ignorance, and any domain of truths you care to restrict to is closed under that operation. So no restricted knowability principle is safe either." I can't tell where the author is being careful and where they are overreaching, though two things make me suspicious: our TA hinted afterwards that the argument is sensitive to exactly which proposition you feed into it, and she also said the restricted principles are where the live disagreement now sits.

So: please go through that passage claim by claim and tell me what someone who works on this material would simply accept, what they would wince at as a simplification or a loose use of a technical term, and what is straightforwardly wrong -- and then whether the book's sweeping final conclusion survives once the inaccuracies are repaired. I also have a hunch I can't evaluate: it seems to me that the whole argument works by demanding knowledge of a proposition whose truth the knowing would destroy, and that this is somehow an unfair demand, so that the paradox shows only that some truths are unknowable-by-us-under-that-description rather than that they are unknowable full stop. Is that a real objection with a name, or just a confusion of mine about what 'knowable' is being asked to mean? And concretely: if the knowability principle is restricted to truths about the physical or mind-independent world, does Fitch-style reasoning still get a grip, or does the instance simply fall outside the restricted domain -- and if it falls outside, is that an honest restriction or a gerrymandered one?
