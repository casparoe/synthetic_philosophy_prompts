I never studied philosophy formally — I left university after a science degree and have been picking things up from podcasts and from a reading group that meets above a pub. We recently spent two evenings on whether the fact that our moral beliefs were shaped by evolution gives us any reason to doubt them. One member of the group, Dan, argues that it does: our moral intuitions are the output of selection pressures favouring cooperation within bands of kin and allies, and once you really take that in, you have no remaining reason to think those intuitions track anything outside of that history. When I pushed him on what exactly follows, another member said the whole argument "can't get off the ground, because you can't derive an ought from an is" — the line I've seen attached to Hume's name — and the group treated that as having settled the matter and moved on to the next topic. I don't think it settled anything, and I also don't think Dan was refuted, but I can't articulate why.

So what I want is a precisification of that sentence as it was being used in that room. Please lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise claims "you can't derive an ought from an is" could be making here — claims about what can follow from what in a valid argument, claims about whether moral statements can be analysed into non-moral ones, claims about what a causal explanation of why someone holds a belief does to that person's justification for holding it, claims about the kind of thing moral facts would have to be if there are any, and any others you think belong on the list — and for each one say whether it is close to a platitude, a substantive thesis that many people working on this would defend, or something that would be widely rejected, and whether it is the sort of claim that would actually stop Dan's argument or only change its shape.

Two contributions of my own that I'd ask you to take apart rather than humour. First, my attempt in the room: I said the slogan looked to me like it cuts both ways, since if the gap blocks Dan from getting "our moral beliefs are unjustified" out of "our moral beliefs were selected for", it should equally block the realist in our group from getting "our moral beliefs are roughly true" out of "evolution steered us toward beliefs that made cooperation pay". Dan replied that a debunker only has to remove a justification, not install a verdict of error, and I want to know whether that reply is a real distinction or a way of restating his conclusion. Second, a case I invented afterwards to test the slogan: imagine a population whose central moral convictions were produced by a deliberate, decades-long state propaganda campaign, and imagine they later find out. Almost everyone feels that this discovery tells against those convictions. If something with that same shape is doing the real work in the evolutionary argument, then it looks to me as though the fight between Dan and the realist is about whether evolution is more like propaganda or more like perception — and not about any is/ought gap at all. Tell me whether that diagnosis is right, and, if it is, which of your readings of the slogan survive it and which turn out to be beside the point in this particular debate.

One last thing about how I'd like the answer pitched: please be explicit about which of your claims are uncontroversial among people who actually work on this and which are live disputes. I would much rather be told "this is settled, that is contested, and here is roughly how the disagreement divides" than be handed a confident answer that papers over a fight. Where a reading you list is itself philosophically contested, mark it as contested rather than presenting it as the standard view, and where you are going beyond anything the literature settles, say so plainly. I don't need citations, references or a reading list — I want the distinctions and the verdicts on them, nothing I'd have to look up.
