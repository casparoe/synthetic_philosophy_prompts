I have been a nurse on a surgical ward for about eleven years, and lately I have been going back and forth with my brother-in-law, who is a physician assistant, about something that I think is a philosophy question wearing a clinical costume. I keep landing on the same puzzle. My ward treats a patient's own 0-to-10 pain rating as authoritative: I was taught the definition that pain is whatever the patient says it is and exists whenever the patient says it does, and we chart the number as the fifth vital sign, right next to blood pressure and pulse. At the same time, we do not actually treat it as authoritative at all. If a post-op patient with a small incision says 9 while laughing with visitors, the team starts talking about drug-seeking behaviour; if a stoic older man with a big incision says 3, someone mutters that he is tough and we watch him more closely; we compare patients against each other constantly; and research on our unit reports average pain-score reductions as if the numbers were temperatures taken with the same thermometer in every mouth. Meanwhile, for the patients who cannot give a number at all — the intubated ones, the ones with advanced dementia, newborns, and (I was taught this in school, I hope wrongly) babies who were once said not to feel much — we score pain from the outside, by grimacing, guarding, restlessness, whether they can be consoled. So the ward seems to be committed both to pain being a private magnitude only the subject can access and to pain being a publicly readable condition that behaves the same whether or not anyone reports it. I fell down an internet rabbit hole about qualia and read people arguing that experiences have an intrinsic feel, and other people arguing that the whole idea of an inner qualitative what-it-is-likeness is confused or incoherent, and I cannot tell what my own ward is committed to.

Please answer as a professional philosopher of mind who takes institutional detail seriously rather than as someone delivering a lecture. Before you commit yourself, brainstorm four to six candidate reconstructions of the implicit theory behind this practice — the view of pain experience, of introspection, of what a self-report is, that the practice would only make sense under — and then commit to the one you think is strongest. I want you to separate three things clearly: what the practice strictly presupposes, what it merely fits comfortably with, and where it is philosophically underdetermined so that several rival theories of consciousness would license exactly the same charting behaviour. Then say what would actually have to change at the bedside if the sceptical view were correct and there were no inner qualitative magnitude to be reported: would we stop asking, or just stop pretending the number is a measurement?

Here is the thought of my own that I want you to test rather than flatter. It seems to me that the number is closer to a request or a bid for help than to a report, that what the patient does with an 8 is raise the volume on a signal so that someone will act, and that the real job of the score is to make suffering legible to an institution that can only act on things it can write down. If that is right, the ward's practice is not measuring anything and never was, and the interesting questions become who is allowed to make a claim on the institution and what counts as a good reason to act. I also wonder whether we are conflating two different questions — how much it hurts and how much it matters — and whether a better instrument would ask what the patient would be able to do if the pain were two points lower instead of asking for the number itself. Tell me where my request-not-report idea fails, and give the strongest case for the view you reject: if you defend the idea that there is a genuine felt magnitude being reported, I want the sharpest version of the argument that the practice is incoherent without it, and if you defend the sceptical side, I want the sharpest version of the argument that the practice secretly depends on what the sceptic denies.
