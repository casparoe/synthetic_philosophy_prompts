I'm in the second year of a research master's in history, writing a 5,000-word historiography paper on whether non-human things — pathogens, droughts, volcanic winters — can coherently be treated as actors in historical explanation or only ever as background conditions, and I keep circling around what looks like a purely verbal disagreement that I can't get behind or past.

At a reading group last week I suggested that the epidemic in a city I'm writing about might be doing explanatory work that most accounts relegate to context, and my supervisor replied with a line I've heard her use before: only people act, germs just happen. A visiting environmental historian then replied with what sounded like the opposite slogan: whatever human beings do, in the long run nature has the last word, since climate and disease settle which projects are even available to them. Both of them seemed to think their line closed the question, and I couldn't tell whether they were contradicting each other at all.

What I want from you is a disambiguation of both of those one-liners: the distinct precise claims each could be expressing, arranged from the trivial to the radical — about whether agency requires intentionality or representational states, whether historical explanation must cite reasons rather than causes, whether things that merely happen are noise or difference-makers, whether only agents can bear responsibility, whether long-run causal dominance is a claim about selection over cases or about what gets screened off within a case. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, substantive and defensible, substantive and doubtful, or straightforwardly false, and — this is the part I actually need — which readings would genuinely entitle my supervisor to rule pathogens out of the actor category, and which would entitle the visitor to treat them as the most important actors in the story. If there are readings under which both are true at once, say whether that means the dispute is merely verbal or whether something real survives it (for instance a disagreement about which feature the word actor should track).

To make this useful rather than general, please also test a taxonomy I've been sketching, and tell me where it collapses distinctions that matter. I've been separating: difference-making in a contrastive explanation (did the outbreak make the difference between the rising happening and not happening?); robustness (the factor shapes outcomes across a wide range of what people choose, so it isn't screened off by anyone's intentions); reason-responsiveness (it can be taken up as a reason by agents and can respond to what agents do — quarantine changes what a pathogen does, nothing changes what a volcano does); legibility (whether the factor was available to the historical agents themselves as something to act about — pre-germ-theory epidemics shaped behaviour while being unavailable as reasons); and narrative eligibility (whether it can occupy the grammatical subject of an explanatory sentence without pragmatic strain). A volcano seems to pass the first, second and fifth, fail the third, and vary on the fourth; a pathogen passes the third only in a thin evolutionary sense. So: which of these five is each slogan-reading really turning on, which one does my supervisor need, and is legibility just an epistemic property that has no business in an account of agency at all? Finally, tell me where the line falls between treating a pathogen as an actor and sliding into the environmental determinism I'm trying to avoid — and whether actor is even the word I want, or whether I'd do better with two or three narrower notions. Please don't spend words on citations or a reading list; I'll fill those in myself.
