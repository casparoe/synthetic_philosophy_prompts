I am writing the methodological preface to a book manuscript on climate, grain prices, and demographic cycles in the early modern Mediterranean, and before I commit to it in print I would like an audit of the commitments it rests on. I work, roughly, in the Braudelian frame — longue durée, conjuncture, event — though this is not an exercise in exegesis and I do not want an answer that consists of what Braudel said. The set, as I currently hold it, is this:

(1) Asymptotic primacy — my own label for it. The explanatory weight of a causal factor increases with the timescale over which that factor persists; as the explanatory window lengthens, only geography and climate retain appreciable weight, and everything faster washes out.

(2) The slow layer operates by constraint rather than determination: it fixes the menu, not the meal. It settles what is feasible and at what cost, never which of the feasible things happens. I also take such constraints to be occasionally broken — the reorientation of European commerce toward the Atlantic is my working example — so that near-immobility here means comparative slowness, not immutability.

(3) Event-causation is real but damped. The impulse from a decision, a battle or a failed harvest is largely absorbed within roughly a generation, so biography and narrative are adequate explanations only over short windows.

(4) Scale relativity. Whether a phenomenon belongs to structure, conjuncture, or event is a function of the window one is aggregating over, not an intrinsic property of the phenomenon; there is no absolute assignment of a process to a level.

(5) The conjuncture is the historian's proper object, because it is where mechanisms are visible and where serial evidence is richest.

(6) Methodologically, quantitative series — prices, wages, tithes, shipping records — are epistemically privileged; narrative sources are evidence of mentalités rather than of what happened.

(7) Moral assessment of historical actors survives all of the above, because responsibility tracks what an agent could have foreseen from inside the event, and the longue durée is precisely what is invisible from inside an event.

I cannot locate the fault myself, and I do not want a defence of the package — I want to know whether there is one. Please tell me which commitments are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be; order the genuine tensions by severity; for each, say exactly what would have to be given up and what the least costly revision would cost me; mark the apparent tensions as harmless if they dissolve on inspection; and identify the single revision that would repair the most damage. Two pairs I keep reversing myself on and would like settled rather than gestured at are (1) with (2), and (3) with (7): in each case I can construct an argument that the two conflict and an argument that they do not, and I cannot tell which argument is sound.
