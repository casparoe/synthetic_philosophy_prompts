Hey — I write historical fiction, and I've got a plot-consistency problem that's turned into an ethics problem. A freind of mine in seminary has read both of Augustine's treatises on lying and has been vetting my scenes for me. Her calls so far:

- a false line delivered in an obviously jokey voice — not a lie, no sin
- a false statement the speaker sincerely believes is true — not a lie
- a strictly true statement picked because the hearer will draw the wrong conclusion from it — a lie, and a sin
- a lie told to save a man's life, when she could have said I won't tell you and shut the door — still a lie, still a sin, just a small one with a good motive
- Rahab, the midwives, that whole class of case — God paid them for the mercy, not for the falsehood
- flatly refusing to answer — fine, that's the move you're supposed to make

So here's mine. My new book is a diary supposedly found in a jar, with a preface by an archivist I invented, about real people and real events, and most of the contents are made up. The words a novel do appear, in tiny print, on the copyright page. I didn't pick that frame to fool anybody — I picked it because it just lands harder, and I'm honestly not fussed either way about what readers end up believing. On her scheme, am I lying? And what about the version where I *do* want readers unsure: I don't want them to believe it, I want them not to know?

I'm less after the verdict than the rule she's running. I can read her track record as it's all about whether you want somebody to end up holding a false belief, or as you may never make your words into an instrument of someone's error, whatever you wanted. Those two split somewhere and I want to know where. Best test I can think of: a reader emails and asks straight out whether chapter nine happened, and I say nothing. Does silence in print do the same work silence did for the fugitive, or is staying quiet when you know it'll be taken as confirmation its own lie? Which rule does that separate, and what does each one say?
