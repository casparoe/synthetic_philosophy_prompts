Running a reading-group session on Goodman's new riddle of induction next week, and I'd like to open with a poem instead of the usual five-minute recap. Could you write a sestina — six six-line stanzas plus a three-line envoi — whose six end-words are emerald, green, blue, *grue*, *bleen*, and t?

What I want is for the form to do the arguing. The end-words rotate by a fixed rule, so the same six words keep coming back in a different order while nothing about the observations changes, and that permutation is the point: the difficulty is that the evidence underdetermines which predicate is the projectible one, and moving the words around is a way of showing that. Get the definitions right (grue: examined before t and green, or not examined before t and blue; bleen the mirror image) and dont let it slide into plain *green before t, blue after* — the observation clause is exactly what lets the hypothesis that all emeralds are green and the hypothesis that all emeralds are grue fit the evidence equally well, and I'd rather not have anyone in the room wincing.

The bit I want you to add, which isn't in Goodman: a second family where the cutoff is a place rather than a time. Call it greeb — green if the sample is examined inside a museum, blue otherwise — and have the poem register, without settling it, whether the spatial version is any less threatening than the temporal one, or whether it just makes our dependence on when and where we happened to look visible in a new way.

Keep the envoi genuinely open: all six words have to appear there anyway, so let them end up side by side rather than letting entrenched green win on the last line. It should still read as a poem to someone who hasnt read Goodman.
