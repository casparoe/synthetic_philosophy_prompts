"For most of the twentieth century the standard view was that perception is modular: the visual system takes the pattern of light on your retina and builds up an inner picture of the scene, and only once that picture is finished is it handed over to the rest of your mind, which is where your beliefs, expectations, moods and stereotypes get to work. So what you consciously experience can't be reshaped by what you believe — belief only ever changes what you make of the experience afterwards. That view has more or less collapsed. There are now lots of cases where wanting or believing something changes the experience itself and not just the verdict you draw from it: a hungry person sees a brown smudge as a sandwich; people who've been told a face belongs to someone from a group they're prejudiced against report seeing the expression as angrier; athletes who are playing well say the ball looks like it's moving slower; and in conditioning studies, once people learn that a shape predicts a mild shock they say the shape starts to look different. Since concepts and background beliefs get built into the experience this way, philosophers say perception is 'theory-laden,' and the interesting payoff is epistemic: your experience can't act as a neutral referee over your beliefs anymore, because your beliefs are already inside it. So there's no such thing as the evidence of your own eyes — what looks like a check on your opinions is really just your opinions coming back at you, and people from different backgrounds literally don't see the same world. The old argument from persistent illusions — you can know the two lines in the Muller-Lyer illusion are the same length and they still look different — used to be taken to settle it the other way, but all it shows is that penetration isn't total, and nobody ever claimed it was."
