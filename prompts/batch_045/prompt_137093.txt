Write a satirical Call for Proposals, 600–700 words, in the deadpan house style of a large public research funder — numbered sections, eligibility criteria, an evaluation rubric, a note on eligible costs — announcing a five-year award for the first study to demonstrate that a cultural value is a genuine cause of a macroeconomic outcome.

The humour has to come from the real methodological difficulty rather than from generic academia jokes. Design the call so that each further requirement, added in the funder's own telling in order to raise standards, quietly shrinks what applicants are permitted to call culture. Include at least these: the value must be measured independently of the practices it is supposed to cause, so that a commitment to hard work cannot be inferred from the fact that people work hard; it must be shown to do causal work rather than merely to stand in a relation of elective affinity to the outcome; it must not be identified with whatever the economists' model leaves unexplained — the call should give this residual an official name, and concede in a footnote that most canonical cultural explanations have been of exactly this kind; and it must survive the fact that values are transmitted by institutions that the outcome itself shapes. By the last criterion the eligible value should have been squeezed to almost nothing. The call should then note, in perfect sincerity, that the previous round attracted no fundable proposals, attribute this to the applicants rather than to the criteria, and announce a follow-up call in which the panel will specify the eligible value itself.

Constraints: the target is the practice of explaining outcomes by appeal to culture, not any named person or school. The piece should never state the problem in plain terms, and no character should announce the conclusion, yet a reader should still be able to tell what is being ironized without the author winking. It should be funny to someone who knows Weber's Protestant ethic argument and the literature criticizing it, and readable to someone who does not. Invent no quotations from Weber; the standard concept labels — elective affinity, this-worldly asceticism, ideal type, the iron cage — may be used freely.
