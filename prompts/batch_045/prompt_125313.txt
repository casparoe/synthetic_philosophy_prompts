Hi — I've got to give the keynote at a ceremony in about three weeks and I'm completely stuck, so I'm hoping you can draft it with me.

Setup: I chaired the panel that picked the marker design for the deep geological repository under the ridge — the one that's finally closing. The ceremony is "last cask": the final canister goes down, the shaft gets backfilled, and the above-ground marker work starts for real afterwards. About 500 people in a marquee. Half of them are engineers, linguists, anthropologists and semioticians who've spent years arguing about how you say "don't dig here" to someone ten thousand years from now when you share no language with them. The other half are the couple hundred workers whose jobs finish the day the shaft closes, plus their families, the mayor, and the local farmers who've been quietly suspicious of the whole project for thirty years. Five or six minutes, spoken, to all of them at once.

The idea I want to use — and I want it to actually constrain what the speech can say, not just decorate it — is von Neumann's on technology that outlives the people who made it. The bit I keep coming back to is his design for a self-reproducing machine, as I understand it: the machine carries a description of itself on a tape, and it uses that tape twice — once read and acted on, to build the offspring, and once copied without being read at all and handed over. So the part that survives is exactly the part that never gets opened. That seems to me to be our whole problem in miniature. The stuff that lasts longest (nickel alloy, granite, the shape of the earthworks) is durable for reasons that have nothing whatsoever to do with meaning, and meaning is the most perishable thing we make. Durability and being-understood pull apart.

And here's the wrinkle I've become convinced of, which I've never seen anyone on the panel put this way. Everyone's been optimising for being understood — right pictograms, right level of weirdness, right amount of redundancy. I now think that's the wrong target, because the probability of being correctly understood in ten thousand years isn't something we can move very far. What we can move is whether being misunderstood is survivable. So the real design goal should be: every plausible misreading — a temple, a tomb, a curse, a hoax, a monument to something we don't actually believe — should still produce behaviour that keeps people away. Robustness to misreading instead of accuracy of reading. I'd like the speech to take that as its spine, because I think it's also true of the part of the job that's ending: two hundred people spent careers on something whose entire value is that it's never used.

Which is most of why I'm stuck. I can't tell those workers they'll be remembered, or that the message will still be legible in ten thousand years, or that it's safe — I don't know any of those things. I can't do "our grandchildren will thank us", and I definitely dont want the joke about writing a warning for people who can't read English. I can't pretend the farmers' suspicion is irrational either, when the honest answer is that we're guessing about people we can't picture.

So: what's genuinely left to say that's true, that the semioticians won't find naive, and that the workers won't find cold? Write it as the actual speech, please — British English (the ceremony's in Britain and half the room will notice), five or six minutes, no quotes from von Neumann or anyone else and no reading lists, just what I say at the microphone.
