I have been asked to write a reply to a comment on a draft paper about voluntary safety commitments by AI labs, and I want to get the reply right rather than merely forceful. The commenter's move was to invoke the ring of Gyges, told by Glaucon in Book 2 of the Republic. In the Jowett translation I have in front of me, Glaucon says that with two such rings "no man can be imagined to be of such an iron nature that he would stand fast in justice," and concludes that "a man is just, not willingly or because he thinks that justice is any good to him individually, but of necessity" (360b–d). From this the commenter inferred that Glaucon settled the matter long ago: the only thing that has ever constrained an agent is the prospect of being found out and made to suffer, so voluntary commitments are theatre, the same will hold for any artificial system we build, and the only expenditures worth making are on detection and enforcement.

I am fairly sure that several quite different claims are being run together here, and I cannot tell which one the argument actually needs, or even whether the claim is about how agents behave, about what reasons they have, about what justice consists in, or about whether being just benefits the agent. I would like a disambiguation rather than an explanation of the dialogue, and I do not need any citations or reading lists: I know the text and will supply references myself.

Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise theses that Glaucon's line could be expressing, ordered roughly from the trivial to the radical. For each reading I would like: the thesis stated in one sentence; a verdict on its status (platitudinous, substantive and defensible, substantive and doubtful, false, or not a straightforwardly truth-evaluable claim at all); an indication of what would have to be true, or what kind of evidence would have to be available, for it to be settled either way; and, most importantly, whether that reading would actually support the inference to the commenter's conclusion — and if it would not, what further premise has to be smuggled in.

Some distinctions I want handled explicitly, because I suspect they are where the argument turns.

First, "not willingly" and "of necessity" seem to me to allow two readings: a claim about the agent's felt reluctance (people comply grudgingly, under protest) and a counterfactual claim about what would happen if the constraint were removed. My impression is that the argument needs the second but that the sentence more naturally reads as the first, and I would like that impression checked and the difference spelled out.

Second, the ring as Glaucon describes it grants concealment and impunity together, and the modern case separates them. A lab, or a system, may be fully observable yet unsanctionable, or sanctionable in principle yet opaque to inspection — and there are also sanctions that are not imposed by anyone, such as the agent's own aversion to having done the thing. Please say which readings survive, and which collapse, if only one of the two powers is granted, and which reading is therefore the one doing the work in a governance argument.

Third, there is a gap I cannot close between the claim that agents would defect and the claim that they have no reason not to, and a further gap between both of those and the claim that being just has no value to the agent beyond its consequences. If these are separate theses, say which is needed here; if one of them is only plausible given a controversial view about reasons, say so.

Fourth, I would like you to flag any reading that is self-undermining or otherwise unstable — for instance a reading on which justice just is the current compromise among parties who would each prefer to dominate, which may leave nothing to defect from in a world where everyone has a ring, or a reading on which the line is not an assertion at all but a challenge that shifts a burden of proof onto anyone who wants to defend justice.

Close by telling me which reading you take the commenter to have meant, which one the argument requires, and whether the required one is the sort of thing anyone is in a position to assert offhand. I would like the whole answer in a form I can compress into a short written reply: numbered readings, each with a one-line verdict and a note on what it does or does not entail.
