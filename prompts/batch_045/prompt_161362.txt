I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about Lewis Carroll's 1895 dialogue in Mind in which Achilles tries to force the Tortoise from (A), (B), and the conditional if (A) and (B) then (C), to (C), while the Tortoise calmly accepts every further hypothetical Achilles adds and still declines to accept (C). We agreed on the standard moral — that a rule of inference cannot be marshalled as one more premise without launching a regress — but we could not agree about why the Tortoise nonetheless seems, to many readers at least, to be making a reasonable demand rather than a stupid one.

My half-formed idea is that the dialogue runs together three states that ought to be kept apart: (i) believing, as a piece of content, that a given rule is truth-preserving; (ii) being disposed to infer in accordance with that rule; and (iii) trusting, second-order, that one's own applications of the rule are reliable. On my reading the Tortoise's move is not really the addition of a premise at all but a query about (iii), and reliability questions have a different structure: they cannot be discharged by adding premises, but they are not for that reason immune to argument of a calibrational or bootstrapping kind. If that is right, the regress is a symptom of collapsing (iii) into (i), and the standard moral, though true, addresses the wrong collapse.

I am applying for a nine-month dissertation fellowship and need a two-page proposal built around this idea. Please give me: the question stated precisely enough to have a determinate answer; why the existing responses leave it open; the method, month by month, including why my tripartite distinction is a better way in than the obvious alternatives (treating the regress as a failure of the deduction theorem, going infinitary about the premise set, or treating the whole thing as one more instance of rule-circularity in the justification of deduction); what a finished project would have produced; and what would count as it failing — the specific finding or impasse that would show the framing was wrong. Assume a purely philosophical project: no empirical work, and no literature review by name, since I will add the references myself. Finally, I want the proposal to be honest about its own confidence: mark, for each of its main claims, how confident it is, and single out the one point on which the project is most likely to be mistaken.
