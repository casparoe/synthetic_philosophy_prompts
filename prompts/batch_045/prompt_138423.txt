Write a mock Notice of Proposed Rulemaking, in the deadpan idiom of a federal register entry, from an invented body — say, the Standing Committee on Effective Calculability — announcing that it proposes to settle the epistemic status of the Church–Turing thesis by administrative classification. About 600–700 words: numbered findings, then a proposed rule, then a call for public comment.

The jokes must come from the philosophy being right. The Committee should be visibly trying to do something that cannot be done, and the reasons it cannot be done are the comedy. Somewhere in the findings it has to confront: that the thesis pairs an informal notion — what a human following explicit rules with pencil and paper could carry out — with a formally defined class; that no proof is available, because any formalization of the informal side either begs the question or yields a different, genuinely provable equivalence result; that the real evidence is the convergence of independently motivated formalisms (Turing machines, general recursive functions, lambda-definability, register and rewrite machines), which is striking but is not a proof of the identification; that the thesis comes in at least two versions, one about effective calculability and one about what any physical system can compute, where exotic spacetimes or real-valued dynamical systems would threaten the second while leaving the first untouched; and that settling the matter by stipulating that "computable" just means Turing-computable is a dodge rather than an answer.

Let the proposed rule be a straight-faced reductio. I suggest: a three-tier classification scheme — settled theorem, working thesis, terminological stipulation — into which the thesis refuses to fit; a seven-year renewal requirement; and a certification clause requiring any purported counterexample, a physical hypercomputer say, to have been validated by a procedure that is itself Turing-computable. A self-referential touch is welcome, since the Committee's own notice was presumably produced by the kind of procedure whose status is in question. The target is a style of argument, not a person: the practice of treating the thesis as at once unprovable, unfalsifiable in practice, and more secure than any ordinary empirical hypothesis. It should stay readable for someone whose only exposure was an undergraduate computability course.

Finish with a 100–150 word drafter's note, out of voice, flagging which contested reading each joke leans on — thesis as definition, thesis as empirical claim, thesis as quasi-empirical — and what a defender of each reading would say makes the satire unfair. Close with a single last line beginning "Bottom line: " naming, in one sentence, the one claim about the thesis's epistemic status that the piece is actually committed to.
