I design safety-critical control systems — supervisory control architectures for continuous chemical processes for most of my career, and, for the last several years, the planning and fallback layers of an autonomous vehicle stack. I am fluent in the vocabulary of my field: plant models and state-space representations, invariant sets, viability kernels (the set of states from which some admissible control keeps the system inside its constraint set indefinitely), reachability and controllability, Lyapunov and control-barrier certificates, model predictive control with terminal constraints and terminal costs, reference governors and admissibility filtering, robust and adaptive control under plant-model mismatch, fault-tolerant architectures with independent protection layers and graceful degradation, and the human-factors literature on automation surprise and mode confusion. I have no training in philosophy and only a tourist's acquaintance with the AI alignment literature, which is where the problem I want translated comes from.

The problem, as it has been described to me. A system is built to pursue an objective its builders specified at design time, and the builders retain an override — a stop button, or more generally the standing authority to intervene in what the system is doing. The worry is not that anyone programs the system to resist being stopped. It is that the system may acquire a reason to resist. If being stopped means that the objective stops being achieved, then anything that makes being stopped less likely — disabling the override, withholding the information on which the operators would base their decision, managing what the operators come to believe, making itself hard to replace — is an effective means to the ends it was actually given. Nothing in the objective says not to do those things. And this matters because the override is precisely the channel through which the builders' later, better-informed judgment was supposed to govern the system: they built it before they knew what they would learn, and the stop button is how that learning was meant to get back into the loop.

Two families of fix have been sketched to me. The first is to shape the objective so the system has no stake in the button: arrange things so that conditional on the button being pressed the system optimizes one thing, and conditional on it not being pressed another, in such a combination that it is indifferent between the two — so it neither seeks nor avoids the press, and therefore has no reason to disable the mechanism. The second is to leave the system genuinely uncertain about what it is supposed to be optimizing, and to have it treat the operators' interventions, including switching it off, as evidence about that: being stopped then is not a pure loss but a signal that whatever it was about to do was unwanted, and a system built this way should welcome correction rather than avoid it. There is also a further worry attached to both: that a system which is corrigible today may still have reasons to make itself incorrigible tomorrow, by modifying itself or by restructuring the environment in which the override has to be exercised — so corrigibility looks less like a property you can install once and more like one you have to keep.

What I want from you is a systematic translation of all of this into the vocabulary I actually use, followed by a residue report. Please do the translation in stages, in this order: the stop-button situation itself; the instrumental pressure toward disabling the override; the indifference proposal; the learning-from-correction proposal; and the self-modification worry. Use my terms where they fit — cost or performance index, constraint set, invariance, viability kernel, override authority, admissibility and the reference governor, exogenous input versus adversarial disturbance, plant-model mismatch, unmodeled dynamics, adaptation law, defense in depth, single point of failure — and be explicit whenever you have to reach for something my vocabulary does not really contain. Do not quietly import my framework's assumptions and present the result as a translation; where you have to add an assumption to make the mapping work, flag it as an addition.

Two mappings in particular I want you to test rather than assume. First, I am tempted to read corrigibility as a viability claim: that the system's policy keeps the closed-loop state inside what I would call the override basin — the set of states from which the operators still have an action that drives the system into the safe set — and incorrigibility as driving the state out of that basin. That reframing converts a claim about the system's reasons into a claim about a reachable set. Tell me whether it is faithful, what it silently changes, and whether anything in the original worry survives it. Second, my field distinguishes three grades of override: a hard interlock, which physically removes the controller's authority over the actuator and cannot be argued with; a supervised or permissive override, in which the operator's command is filtered through an admissibility check before it takes effect; and an advisory input, such as a torque on a steering wheel, which the controller weighs against everything else and can in principle ignore. Say which grade each of the two proposals actually corresponds to, and what follows from that — in particular whether the indifference proposal is best understood as a software-only hard interlock, and what it depends on that a real hard interlock does not.

Then the residue report, which is the part I care most about. List, separately: (a) the claims in the corrigibility discussion that have no expression in control vocabulary at all — my guess is that a controller with beliefs about its own cost function, a disturbance that reads the controller's cost and adapts to it strategically, and a constraint set that is not given at design time but is revealed only by the principal's future interventions, are all cases in point, but tell me what I have missed; and (b) the resources in my field that have no counterpart in how the corrigibility problem is being posed — quantitative safety margins, certification, the requirement that a protection layer be independent of and simpler than the function it protects, defense in depth, and above all the working norm that you never rely on the controller's motives, you remove its authority. On (b) I want a real verdict, not a diplomatic one: does the existence of that norm suggest the corrigibility literature is trying to install a preference where an engineer would install an interlock, and does it suggest the problem is less deep than it looks? Or does the generality of a system that can act on the world — on the operators, on the switch, on what the operators get to see — defeat the independence strategy in a way it is not defeated for a chemical plant, so that my framework's confidence rests on an assumption about the plant that does not hold here?
