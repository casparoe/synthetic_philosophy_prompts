I'm a final-year PhD student in political philosophy, and next month I'm giving a paper at an interdisciplinary history and social theory seminar on the other side of campus — I presented there once before and it went badly, mostly because I wrote the paper for philosophers. The paper defends what I'll call the hinge of history hypothesis: that the people alive over roughly the next one to two centuries are among the very small number of generations in human history at which the long-run character of the future is most sensitive to what anyone does, so that shaping the trajectory of that period plausibly dominates, in expected value, any comparably costly improvement to the welfare of people alive right now. I'm deliberately not resting the case on the familiar arithmetic about how many people the future could hold and the opportunity cost of delay; several people in that room have heard that version and switched off. Instead I build it on a distinction between two ways a time can be pivotal, and on the claim that only one of them carries moral weight. Call a period temporally hinged when the causal leverage of what happens in it over later states of the world is unusually high, and modally hinged when the spread of reachable long-run outcomes — the variance rather than the mean — is unusually wide, so that which of several very different futures gets realized is still genuinely unsettled. My first claim is that the significance people attach to living at the hinge really attaches to modal hingedness, and that temporal hingedness alone licenses nothing: it is no argument for shortchanging the present that later generations will be able to edit whatever we build. My second is that modal hingedness is not the boast that temporal hingedness invites, because it is compatible with full acknowledgment that our era will look parochial and its obsessions absurd in a millennium, and because it requires only that we fall somewhere in the tail of a distribution, not that we are uniquely important.

The audience is about a dozen people and is neither a philosophy department nor an effective-altruism group: mostly historians of the early modern and modern periods, two historical sociologists, an anthropologist who works on colonialism and who I expect will be the sharpest questioner, and one political theorist who is sympathetic but skeptical. Their likely commitments: periodization is contested, and talk of "the hinge" smuggles in a Whig or providential story about history going somewhere; quantification of the far future is fake precision dressed up as rigor; and above all, every generation since the Axial Age has told itself that it was living at the turning point, and the recurrence of that self-description is itself the datum to be explained rather than evidence for any particular generation's claim. They will reach for cases — the printing press, 1492, 1789, 1945, the collapse of the North Atlantic cod fishery — and they will phrase their doubts as doubts about my evidence and about my categories rather than as formal moral-philosophy objections. Nobody in that room will attack expected-value maximization in the abstract; that is not where their fault lines run, and a reply that spends its two minutes defending expected utility will simply confirm their suspicion that I have wandered in from somewhere strange.

What I'd like is a Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room, in roughly the order they will come, each phrased the way these people would phrase it rather than the way a philosopher would, and each with a two-minute reply, a note on what I should concede outright, and a note on where the reply is soft. Please also include the objection I'm hoping nobody asks. My candidate is the one about whether the temporal/modal distinction is doing any work at all: if the spread of futures is wide but nothing I might do predictably selects among them, then modal hingedness gives me no more reason to act than non-hingedness does, and I have bought my immunity to the Whig-history charge at the cost of the conclusion. Tell me if you think a different objection is the real landmine, and say why. And please don't just give me the standard published objections to longtermism reassembled — I've read those, they aren't what this room will say, and they are the reason my last paper there went badly.
