I'm a retired sensory neurophysiologist — vision, mostly, thirty-odd years of it — and about three years ago I started sitting in on a philosophy reading group at the university near where I live. Two grad students, a retired GP, and me. The grad students are very patient with me, but I have this habit of asking ''what would you accept as an answer here?'', and for the last eight months we've been going round on the meta-problem of consciousness (Chalmers's term, I think, from a paper a few years back) and I've become convinced that this habit of mine is actually the crux, and nobody will bite.

The shape of it, as I understand it. Everybody now agrees that explaining why we make the noises we do — why we say experience is ineffable, intrinsic, non-relational, why we report an explanatory gap, why we reach for zombies — looks tractable in a way the hard problem itself doesn't. We know roughly what a model of that would need: some story about introspection representing its own states in a way that makes them come out looking simple, brute, indexical, not-further-analyzable. Fine. The illusionists then say: do that properly and you've done everything there is to do, the hard problem dissolves, there was never a further fact to be found. The realists say: you've explained the judgment, not the thing judged; the judgment might be *true*, and the fact that we can't see how the story could be true is itself what needs explaining, not what needs explaining away. And then the two sides just say their bit again, louder. Nobody has moved in eight months and everyone has heard every move.

Here's my hypothesis, and I want you to tell me if it's wrong or if it's just my field's reflexes talking. I think the disagreement isn't about consciousness at all. I think it's about whether a causal-and-functional story about the *production* of a seeming automatically undermines the seeming. That is, whether these seemings behave like perceptions — where an etiological story really does debunk them, as with the Muller-Lyer, where knowing the mechanism is exactly what licenses you to stop trusting the appearance — or whether they behave like rational or logical intuitions, where telling me the causal history of my belief that 2+2=4 doesn't touch it in the slightest. Both sides in my group have an unargued reflex about which of those the explanatory-gap intuition is, and the whole thing then falls out of that. I also suspect there are grades here that we've been running together: predicting and manipulating when people report a gap (which is boringly empirical), modelling the architecture that produces the report (which is the interesting science), and showing that the report is *unjustified* (which is the philosophical payload). Everyone agrees the first two are available and the fight is entirely about whether the third is a free bonus of the second.

Two more things I want. First: I keep thinking this is structurally the same as an old fight in my own field. Everyone agreed on the retinal stimulus, everyone agreed on the report, and they still argued for decades about whether the right explanation was optical/physiological or a misapplied constancy scaling, and as far as I remember it never ended with a decisive experiment, it just changed shape once people started asking a different question. Is that a real analogy or am I kidding myself? Second: I have a test I rather like — suppose someone fully grasps the grade-two explanation, and the intuition that there is a hard problem just stops firing in them, the way an illusion can stop once you've seen the apparatus. Would that settle anything, and for whom? Or would each side just reinterpret it?

So: is this substantive, verbal, or two questions under one label? Is there a hidden empirical question doing the work, or a hidden disagreement about what an explanation has to do? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, an experiment, a change of question — or should we expect no progress, and why? Please argue it in your own words; I'm not after papers or a reading list, I can find those, and I don't trust my ability to seperate the good ones from the noise anyway.
