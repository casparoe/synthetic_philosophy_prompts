I spent my career in theoretical statistical physics — lattice models, phase transitions, the thermodynamic limit — and since retiring I have been sitting in on philosophy, which I am enjoying rather more than I expected. I have agreed to contribute one essay question, with a model answer and a marking scheme, to the final in-class assessment of a second-year interdisciplinary course on infinity in mathematics and physics. The students are mostly physics and mathematics undergraduates with a single philosophy unit behind them. They have all met Hilbert's Hotel, Cantor's diagonal argument, and the potential/actual infinity distinction, and they can recite the standard moves fluently — which is precisely my difficulty. I want a question that separates the students who can recite from the students who have worked out what an argument like this could establish in the first place. It is a forty-five-minute question, worth 25 marks, closed book; the answer should require no references, no citations and no further reading, and it should be answerable by a student with no physics beyond what I set out below.

The material I want the question built around is the following contrast, which I suspect a philosopher will find either illuminating or sloppy, and which I would like the marking scheme to adjudicate rather than assume.

First, Hilbert's Hotel. I do not want the question to be answerable by retelling it. It should force candidates to distinguish at least three things that "impossible" might mean: (a) there is no bijection from the guests-plus-newcomer to the rooms; (b) the bijection exists but no finite instruction specifies it and no procedure with a first step and a last step executes it — in the sequential variant, where the occupant of room n can move only once room n+1 is empty, there is no first move; (c) the rearrangement is perfectly well defined mathematically but physically unrealisable, because it would require infinitely many simultaneous actions, or infinite precision, or infinitely much matter. A good answer should say which of (a), (b) and (c) is actually bearing the weight in the standard argument against actual infinities, and what is left of that argument if only (b) or only (c) is granted. It should also press on whether the real content of the story is simply that a whole can be equinumerous with a proper subset of itself — Dedekind's criterion — so that the argument against actual infinities collapses into the demand that the part–whole principle hold for number, which is exactly what Cantor denied.

Second, my own field's use of actual infinities. In statistical mechanics one routinely works with an infinite lattice of spins or an infinite volume of fluid: N → ∞, V → ∞, with N/V fixed. In that limit the total energy, the total particle number and the total free energy are all infinite and therefore useless, and the meaningful quantities are densities — a point that invites the obvious analogy with the hotel, where the total number of guests is likewise not a quantity one should expect to behave. But the limit also does representational work that no finite system does. At any finite N the partition function is a finite sum, or a finite-dimensional integral, of analytic functions of temperature and external field, so the free energy is smooth; a genuinely sharp transition, a non-analyticity, appears only in the limit. As I remember it the standard argument runs through the zeros of the partition function in the complex field plane, which can reach the real axis only once N is infinite; I have forgotten whose theorem this is, and I do not want anything to turn on the attribution. So physicists simultaneously treat the infinite system as a mere idealisation — shorthand for a sequence of finite systems — and rely on it to represent something no finite system represents: a sharp transition, which is something we do in fact measure.

The crux I want the question to force is whether that is a precedent for the friend of actual infinities or a cautionary tale for the finitist. The finitist can say that the infinite lattice is never asserted to exist, that every prediction is a limit of finite-N predictions, and that the paradoxes of the hotel are exactly what you get when you start treating the limit object as one more system among others — expecting total particle number to be measurable, or expecting "full" to mean what it means for a finite hotel. The reply is that this is instrumentalism rather than finitism: the infinite system is doing irreplaceable work, and if the finitist's answer is "it is only an idealisation," then the finitist owes an account of why the idealisation works, and of whether Hilbert's Hotel might be a similarly benign idealisation rather than a reductio. Candidates should be made to take a side and defend it, and to say what a finitist who is also a working physicist would have to give up, or what a realist about the infinite would have to say about the difference between the hotel and the lattice.

Please give me four things.

The question itself, as it would appear on the paper: no more than about 180 words, phrased so that a candidate cannot earn marks by summarising Hilbert's Hotel or by gesturing vaguely at "densities."

A model answer of roughly 800 words, written in the register of a strong second-year producing it in forty-five minutes rather than of a specialist showing off, and self-contained about the physics.

A marking scheme totalling 25 marks, broken into named must-hit moves with the marks each is worth, and, for each move, one line on how candidates typically get it wrong. My guess is that most will narrate the hotel and then bolt the thermodynamic limit on as a loose analogy without ever saying what the analogy is meant to establish; I would like that to cost them, and I would like credit to be available to a candidate who attacks the premise instead — for instance by pointing to finite-size precursors of transitions in real, finite systems — provided they say what they would have to show.

Finally, an examiner's note of about 150 words appended after the marking scheme. In it, say how confident you are in the claims the model answer makes, and single out the single step or claim you are least confident about, and why. If the physics I have sketched above is wrong, or overstated, or rests on a half-remembered theorem, say so plainly there and say how the question would need to be changed: I would much rather set a slightly different question than one that quietly depends on something false.
