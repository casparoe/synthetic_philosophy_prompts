I'm a novelist, and a scene in the manuscript I'm currently revising has started an argument I'm not equipped to settle. My narrator, a hospice nurse, makes the same promise to two dying patients: that she will personally deliver a sealed letter after they die. She delivers the first letter and burns the second. When another character demands to know what the difference is, she cannot produce one — not in the wording of the promises, not in how they were extracted, not in the consequences — and says only that the two cases are not the same. Two readers in my workshop then went at it:

(1) The first said that if she cannot state a difference, there is none. Moral reasons, she said, are general in their nature: if having promised was a reason in the first case, it was an identical reason in the second, so burning the second letter was simply a wrong, and my narrator is either lying to herself or dishonest with the reader.

(2) The second said this assumes a reason keeps a fixed valence across cases, which is precisely what is in dispute. On her view a feature such as having promised can count in favor here, be inert in another case, and perhaps even count against in a third, depending on what else is present; judgment is not the subsumption of cases under rules, so a demand for a statable difference is the wrong demand.

(3) The first replied that she is happy to grant all of that. No one thinks the bare feature having promised generalizes; the generalist's reason is the fully specified one — a promise freely made, to someone who would not release her, where nothing weightier is at stake, and so on. Specify the base property finely enough and the holism the second reader likes is entirely compatible with generalism.

(4) The second said that escape route collapses into her own position: either the principle is specified so finely that it fits this one case and guides no one in a new one, or its escape clauses — nothing weightier at stake — can only be applied by the very case-by-case judgment the principle was meant to replace. On neither horn is the principle doing any work.

(5) The first said that without general reasons there is nothing for my narrator to be wrong about: no account of moral error, no way to arbitrate disagreement, and no way for a reader who has never faced this configuration to tell what should have been done — yet readers plainly can, and the scene depends on their being able to.

(6) The second answered that all three are explicable without principles: what experience of cases builds is a trained sensitivity to which features are salient, not a stock of rules; disagreement is disagreement about what matters here; and a narrator can be faulted for attending to the wrong things without anyone's producing a principle she violated.

I have no stake in who wins. Please go through the exchange move by move, say where each reader is right and where each overreaches, say who is more right overall, and tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing — I need to know whether the scene is broken or whether the stalemate is the honest answer.
