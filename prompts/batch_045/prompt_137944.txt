I sit on a small working group that has now been asked the same question twice in a year: first by a national correctional service deciding whether its rehabilitation programs should be built around community-facing restorative conferences or around confidential, individually tailored accountability plans, and then by a hospital patient-safety board deciding whether medication errors should be reviewed in open unit meetings or handled through anonymous reporting and private remediation. Both times the discussion collapsed into the same stale fight — the familiar contrast between guilt cultures and shame cultures, with one side treating guilt as the mature, act-focused, internally anchored emotion that protects the agent as a separate moral subject, and the other treating shame as the more primitive and more basic one, the emotion that actually binds people to the standards and relationships they care about — and both times we adjourned without knowing whether we were disagreeing about psychology, about moral education, or about words. We have therefore decided to run the argument properly, as an adversarial collaboration, and I would like you to draft the document it should produce.

Two camps are at the table, and I want both represented at their strongest rather than as caricatures. The guilt-first camp argues that guilt is the better moral emotion because it targets the act rather than the self, leaves the agent's standing intact, and motivates repair rather than withdrawal; it grants that guilt can degenerate into what we have started calling the ledger failure — compensating, offsetting, settling accounts, and quietly licensing oneself for the next lapse — and it grants that guilt alone leaves standards thin. The shame-first camp argues that shame is the better moral emotion because it registers what one has become and not merely what one did, inherits its content from the relationships and roles one actually occupies, and is what makes a standard one's own rather than an external rule; it grants that shame can degenerate into the vanishing failure — concealment, exit, denial, rage at the audience — and it grants that shame can be weaponized by whoever controls the audience. Both camps have already conceded that no verdict should be given about which emotion it is better to feel in some absolute sense; the question is institutional, comparative, and domain-relative.

Three further things have come up in our meetings that I want the document to handle rather than dodge. First, the off-diagonal cases, which neither camp's official theory fits: I mean freestanding guilt — guilt that attaches to nothing one authored, as in survivor guilt, guilt over an omission one never chose, or guilt for one's group's historical wrongs — and uptake-insensitive shame — shame felt in solitude, shame before a spectator whose approval one does not care about, or shame about traits one cannot change. For each of the four combinations of act-focused versus self-focused and audience-present versus audience-absent, I want the document to say which camp's account classifies the case more naturally and what it costs the other camp to absorb it. Second, a stipulated piece of evidence: suppose the two matched towns in a well-run twenty-five-year panel are otherwise alike, and that one has adopted public moral-status hearings while the other uses confidential audits with private accountability plans; suppose the first decade's readout shows lower recorded reoffending and higher victim satisfaction in the hearing town, but a sharp fall in self-reporting of near-misses and in help-seeking, and rising self-reporting in the audit town together with residents describing their obligations as compliance with a checklist. Both camps have agreed in advance that this panel bears on the question, and I want each side's preregistered prediction for the second decade — especially for the unwatched conditions, such as anonymous tax filing or admitting one's own error when no one will ever know — written down before anyone sees further results, together with what each side would count as a result that moves it. Third, and most awkwardly: can a culture choose? Include a section on whether deliberately installing one emotion through institutional design is self-undermining — whether public institutions that try to produce guilt end up producing compliance-shame, and whether attempts to make shame respectable by publishing the standards turn it into rule-guilt — and on whether the exportability of such a design is limited by a selection effect, since societies that sustain a guilt-dominant regime may already be high-trust ones. If some of the dispute turns out to be merely verbal, I want to know exactly which part and why: in particular, whether better means more reliable in producing right conduct, more likely to constitute the right kind of agent, or best to live under.

Please format the deliverable as a numbered section of claims both camps can sign, then a short section disposing of the off-diagonal cases, then a disagreements table with columns for the crux, the guilt-first formulation of it, the shame-first formulation, the evidence or case both agree would bear on it, and each side's prediction. A brief closing note from both parties, explaining what they would each still want to say about the choice our two institutions face, would be welcome. Aim for something around 1,200 to 1,500 words: dense enough that our group can mark it up, but not a survey of the literature.
