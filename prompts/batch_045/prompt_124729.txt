Write a short story set maybe thirty years out, about a team commissioning some big new piece of physics hardware — a collider, a fusion experiment, whatever — who realize partway through that on the Russellian-monist / panpsychist picture that's by then experimentally supported in this world, their machine is going to add a staggering amount of experience to the world. In this setting there's a measurable quantity (give it whatever name the characters would actually use) that tracks how much experience a physical system subserves, and it scales with something like the amount of integrated intrinsic dynamics in a causally closed system, not with computation or information processing.

Two things I want you to build the story around rather than gesture at: (1) the quantity is dirt cheap to crank up — astronomical amounts of experience per joule, and the cheapest way to make more of it is a vast number of tiny causally-closed systems that don't do anything useful at all; (2) there's a serious argument in the in-world literature that micro-experience isn't valence-neutral but mildly negative, roughly because almost all the dynamics available to a small system is dissipative and equilibrium-seeking in a way that's structurally akin to frustrated goal-directedness — so adding experience by default adds suffering.

I want the philosophy done inside the characters' heads: the strongest case for flipping the switch, the strongest case against, and whichever argument the protagonist personally finds hardest to shake. The ending should be a real decision, not an accident or a reveal. No AI, no uploads, no simulations — the whole point is that whatever's going on is in the physics rather than the computation. Nobody should be stupid or evil, and please dont resolve anything with a villain or a last-minute discovery.
