A guy on my course — he does philosophy of tech and is annoyingly good at this sort of thing — handed me an argument about AI and understanding, and I can't find the way out of it, or even find the step I'd want to reject. It goes like this.

(1) Understanding why p isn't just knowing that p. It needs grasp: you have to be able to follow the chain of becauses that connects the grounds to the conclusion, not just land on the right answer.

(2) Grasp is personal. If the chain lives in the model's weights, in your notes, or in your supervisor's head, then you don't understand — you're leaning on something that does. (Which is why copying the proof doesn't teach you the maths.)

(3) Now: more and more, we take the endpoint from systems whose chain we can't follow. Too big, too opaque, not even the same from one run to the next. Call anything like that an oracle.

(4) So oracles hand us the look of understanding without the thing itself. It's basically that old complaint about writing — I think it's in Plato somewhere — that it'll give people the reputation of wisdom without the reality, except the writing now answers back.

(5) Trace anything you think you understand and it bottoms out in stuff you took on trust: the material spec off a datasheet, what the compiler actually does with your code, the numbers your office-mate ran. Nobody has ever walked the whole chain themselves. The chain is longer than a working life.

(6) So if (2) is right, nobody understands anything, and nobody ever has. What we call understanding is a collective business that each of us mistakes for a personal achievement.

(7) And it seems to prove too much whichever way you turn. (6) is absurd. But if you patch (2) by saying that reliable access to the chain through other people or through tools is enough — long-armed grasp, or whatever you want to call it — then the student who copies the answer off the oracle and can't do the same problem with one number changed also understands, and we've loosing the difference between understanding and having a good source.

I tried one move on him: two programmers, both writing with an assistant, neither able to follow its reasoning. One can read the output, spot where it's wrong and fix it; the other shrugs and ships whatever compiles. He says (2) convicts them both — the first one just has an easier route back to the chain, and that's a different virtue. I don't know if he's right.

Someone will say grasp comes in degrees. He's got an answer ready: run the argument comparatively. Every step you hand over leaves you with strictly less grasp than working it out would have, so piling up delegation drives it toward nothing, and that's still the conclusion I don't want.

I'd definately like to keep (1), and I want to hold on to the thought that leaning on these things can erode something real, so if forced I'd rather give up (2) than (1). But I can't see what giving up (2) costs me, or whether the rot is somewhere else entirely — between (3) and (4), maybe, or in (5), or in the step from (5) to (6). Which step would you reject, why did it look fine when he said it, and what do you end up paying? Price at least two of the other options for me as well, and don't just tell me it's all a spectrum.
