At our reading group last night we got onto the split-brain material, and one member kept falling back on a comparison I've heard a lot: a callosotomy patient's two hemispheres are basically like dicephalic conjoined twins — two heads, two brains, one body below the neck — so the honest description of the patient is that there are two streams of consciousness sharing a single set of limbs and a single life, and that the only reason you and I are one subject is that our corpus callosum happens to be intact. He was using it for a fairly big conclusion: that the unity of consciousness philosophers make so much of isn't metaphysically deep at all, just a contingent fact about wiring; cut the cable and you have two subjects; and if you could somehow get a second hemisphere with its own brainstem into a skull you'd have two people in there, with the moral consequences that follow (two wills, maybe conflicting consent, maybe two claimants on the same body).

I dont trust the analogy but I cant say exactly where it breaks, so I'd like an audit. First the mapping: what plays the role of each twin, of the shared trunk, of the shared sense organs, of the private ones, of the shared biography, and of the social fact that we treat the twins as two people and the patient as one? Then the disanalogies, and which ones actually matter for his conclusion. The ones I can see: the twins have two whole brains, each with its own brainstem and its own arousal system — I have a vague memory that one twin can be asleep while the other is awake, but I dont trust that at all, so tell me if it's wrong, or if you just don't know — whereas the hemispheres share one brainstem, one sleep-wake cycle and one motivational background, so they can't be in different global states. Also, the twins each have their own eyes and their own private visual fields, but the hemispheres share a single pair of eyes with the input divided by hemifield, so it isn't two private worlds, it's one world cut down the middle. And only one hemisphere can talk: the two streams claim is inferred from what each hand does, not from anything the patient says, while in the twins case both sides report. The twins also coordinate the shared body remarkably well, which if anything suggests that sharing effectors pushes toward unified agency rather than two agents competing for them. Strongest one, to me: if each hemisphere really were a subject, then the kids who have an entire hemisphere removed for severe epilepsy would be a death plus a survival, but they seem to come out as one fairly normal unified mind — though he waved that off and I couldn't say why it doesn't count.

Here's a case I made up to test it, since the real cases are messy: suppose the early fusion of two fraternal-twin embryos produced a person whose hemispheres are largely drawn from different cell lines, who has one shared brainstem and a completely intact callosum, and who has been that way from birth. On his picture that should be two subjects fused into one body and somehow never in conflict; on the other picture it's just one person with a genetic curiosity. Which is right, and what does the answer tell us about whether the twins comparison was doing real work or whether the conclusion was riding on the separateness of the twins all along? Also, if there's a better comparison for the hemispheres — something that's two-ish without being two people — I'd like to hear it. A table of correspondences would be fine.
