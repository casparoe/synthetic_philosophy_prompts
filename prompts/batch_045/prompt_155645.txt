Hi — I need a case built to order for my providence seminar, and I keep botching it when I try to write it myself.

Background, so you know what the case is for. I teach Molinism as the view that middle knowledge — knowledge of the counterfactuals of freedom, i.e. of what any possible creature would freely do in any circumstance it could be placed in — buys God providential control over a world of libertarian-free creatures without determining them, and, this is the bit my students swallow whole, without taking on any extra responsibility for the evil that ends up in the world. Simple foreknowledge is my contrast view: God knows the actual future exhaustively, has no middle knowledge, and is normally presented as leaving God worse off — He foresees the evil but has no grip on the alternatives.

What I want is a case where that ranking flips, or at least where the Molinist God comes out with more moral exposure than the simple-foreknowledge God. It has to be honest: I'm putting it in front of thirty undergrads and a very sharp TA, so I definately can't have it rest on a cheap trick.

Spec:

1. Realistic human setting, a paragraph or two at most. Two agents — not one, not a crowd — each facing one significant free choice, with ordinary stakes: money, a job, a betrayal, a reputation. No hell, no cosmic stakes; those muddy every intuition I care about.
2. Both views must agree on all of the following: God exists and is necessarily omniscient, omnipotent and perfectly good; both agents are libertarian-free and fully responsible for what they do; God neither causes nor intends the wrongdoing; God does nothing wrong; the world is worth creating. The disagreement has to come from somewhere else.
3. The verdict that has to come apart: on Molinism, God can be said to have allocated the wrongdoing — to have picked, among circumstances whose outcomes He already knew, who the one to go wrong would be; on simple foreknowledge, He merely permits what He foresaw and cannot allocate, because He has no knowledge of what anyone would have done in the circumstances He didn't actualise. I want those verdicts to fall out of what each view says God knows, not from the story stipulating them.
4. Anti-cheat: God's reason for actualising the circumstances He does must be independent of the wrongdoing and its consequences. No greater good drawn from the evil, no lesser-of-two-evils, no version of 'this sin is milder than that one'. If the case is a theodicy in disguise it's useless to me.
5. Anti-cheat: the counterfactual claims and the foresight claims have to be generated from a described character and situation, not asserted. If a student pushes back I want to be able to defend them from the descriptions alone.
6. No manipulation, coercion, deception or engineered psychology, and nothing that makes the agents less than free. No skeptical theism, no appeal to mystery, no 'God's reasons are inscrutable'.
7. If the flip can't be made to work, say so instead and give me the strongest version you can, and tell me which of my constraints is doing the blocking.

What I want back: the scenario; then, for each view, the verdict and the reasoning that gets you there; then a note on which single feature of the case is doing the work; then what happens to the contrast when you remove it (one agent instead of two? God's reason allowed to track the evil? open theism in place of simple foreknowledge?); then the best reply a Molinist in the room would give and whether that reply is question-begging; and finally, honestly, whether you'd actually use this in class or whether it's too cute.

Thanks — I've been going in circles on this for a coupld of days and my own attempts keep collapsing back into the standard theodicy I was trying to get away from.
