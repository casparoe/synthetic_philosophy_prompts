I'm writing my term paper for a seminar on rational choice and the self, and I need a thought experiment built to order.

The framework we've been working with is the account of transformative experience we've been reading (L. A. Paul's), which splits into two dimensions that are normally said to travel together. An experience is epistemically transformative when the only way to learn what it is like is to undergo it: no amount of testimony, description, or imaginative rehearsal beforehand gives you that knowledge, so you cannot assign the outcomes their real values in advance. It is personally transformative when undergoing it changes your point of view — your preferences, values, or practical identity — in a way that makes your pre-choice preferences an unreliable standard for the decision, so that the choice cannot simply be a matter of maximizing expected utility given the preferences you have now.

Build me a case where the two come apart in the direction I think is hard to construct: personally transformative but not epistemically transformative.

Constraints:

- One paragraph, ordinary life: a career, a family decision, a migration, a medical procedure, a religious or political commitment. No science fiction, no memory erasure, no drugs, no neural intervention, no fictional technology.
- The subject knows, before choosing, exactly what the experience will be like — and not by stipulation. Give them a genuine route to that knowledge (they have lived through something relevantly similar, or they have observed it closely from the inside) which does not itself transform them.
- Despite that knowledge, the decision is not settled by maximizing expected utility on their current preferences, and it is not settled by their foreseen future preferences either: they can predict those future preferences perfectly and still cannot make them their own.
- The usual escape route has to be closed. Because there is nothing new to discover, the case cannot be resolved by treating the choice as a bet on revelation or discovery; whatever difficulty remains has to remain.
- The difficulty must not reduce to weakness of will, ordinary risk aversion, or plain ignorance of consequences.
- No stipulating the verdicts. Don't write "suppose she cannot know what it is like" or "suppose her values change"; the features have to fall out of the details of the case.

After the scenario, show that it meets the specification: name the route to knowledge and say why the experience is not epistemically transformative, name the preference or identity change and say why it is personally transformative, and tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work. Then remove that feature and tell me whether the case collapses back into an ordinary, unified transformative-experience case. Finally, give the strongest reply available to someone who thinks the two dimensions cannot come apart — I expect them to say that what I have described is merely an anticipated change of preference, which decision theory already handles by deferring to current or higher-order preferences, and I want to know whether that reply actually works.

What the case is for: my paper argues that the deliberative problem with transformative experience is generated by identity and identification rather than by ignorance of phenomenal character. So a case that gets its effect by sneaking ignorance back in won't help me, even if it is otherwise realistic.
