I've been prepping my lecture on Zeno for tomorrow's ancient philosophy survey, and I got into an argument with a couple of friends the other night (one of them is a mathematician who thinks the whole thing is just a misuse of infinite series, the other is a continental philosopher who insists Zeno is doing something much weirder with the concept of 'arrival'), and now I'm not sure I understand the core move myself. In Aristotle's report of the Dichotomy paradox, the key line is: 'That which is in locomotion must arrive at the half-way stage before it arrives at the goal.' On the face of it, that just says you have to pass the midpoint before the endpoint, which seems obviously true and not at all paradoxical. So why does Zeno (or Aristotle) think this shows that locomotion is impossible, or at least that the journey can't even begin? Is the trick hidden in the idea that there are infinitely many 'half-way stages' (half, then half of the remainder, and so on ad infinitum), so there is no first stage to arrive at, or is it about the impossibility of completing an infinite sequence of arrivals in a finite time?

I'd especially like help seeing how the passage reads if we tweak the example. Imagine a runner who, instead of moving continuously, is only permitted to move after she has first 'reached' each half-way point by a discontinuous jump that takes zero time, but she must also spend a non-zero amount of time acknowledging that she has reached the stage before jumping to the next one—say, one full second of acknowledgment per stage. Now the journey obviously can't finish, because there are infinitely many stages and infinitely many seconds of acknowledgment. But does my 'acknowledgment' version actually illuminate the original paradox, or is it just changing the subject by sneaking in a new empirical cost? More generally, what does the passage presuppose about the structure of motion and the reality of the parts of a journey, and how should I explain to my students why the line is not just a tautology? I'm stuck, and I'd rather understand the reasoning then just tell them 'it's about convergent series' and move on.
