I teach a graduate seminar on natural theology from Boyle through Paley to the contemporary intelligent-design movement, and next week I want to run our usual "transported thinker" exercise on Newton instead of Paley, because Paley's design inference is already modern in form and the session tends to collapse into paraphrase. I need you to build the session for me: assume Newton has been briefed fully and sympathetically on twenty-first-century science and politics, and then reason from what we actually know of his theology rather than from the folk Newton of the apple and the occult. The commitments I want treated as the fixed points are these: the General Scholium's claim that reasoning to God from the appearances of things belongs to natural philosophy; his own remark about the Principia, that "When I wrote my treatise about our Systeme I had an eye upon such Principles as might work with considering men for the beliefe of a Deity and nothing can rejoyce me more than to find it useful for that purpose"; the argument in the Opticks Queries that "blind fate could never make all the planets move one and the same way in orbs concentric," together with the irregularities "which will be apt to increase, till this system wants a reformation," and Leibniz's resulting gibe that on Newton's view God "wants to wind up his watch from time to time"; his rejection of the Trinity and of the immortality of the soul, his belief that Athanasian Trinitarianism was the great apostasy and that the papacy fulfils the prophecies of Daniel and Revelation; his insistence that prophecy is not to be understood before its fulfilment; and the Chronology of Ancient Kingdoms Amended, in which astronomical evidence is used to fix and drastically shorten the credible history of the ancient world. A colleague from the divinity school will be sitting in, and he will rightly object if Newton is allowed to sound like a contemporary evangelical who happens to be good at physics.

The agenda I have in mind has five items, and I have built stipulations into three of them precisely so that nobody can answer by pattern-matching. First: standard Big Bang cosmology, a universe with a hot, dense beginning some 13.8 billion years ago, set against his absolute and infinite time and against a God who endures always and everywhere — vindication of Moses, or a difficulty for a deity who constitutes duration rather than being created within it? Second: the fine-tuning literature and its anthropic/multiverse competitor; stipulate, for the sake of the exercise, that the multiverse is as empirically well confirmed as anything in cosmology can be, and ask whether Newton treats an infinity of worlds with varying constants as an alternative explanation or merely as design relocated one level up. Third: an old earth, common descent, and a completed naturalistic account of the origin of the machinery of living things, held by churches that recite the Nicene Creed and call themselves theistic evolutionists. Fourth: the modern design movement itself; stipulate that its leading organisation is run by Nicene Trinitarians who regard the papacy as a Christian church, deny that the prophetic books encode a timeline of history, and have just won a state law requiring science teachers to present a design inference — would Newton lend his name, sign their statement, testify at the hearing? Fifth, and most speculative: artefactual intelligence, systems that outperform their makers in reasoning, and the question whether a created, finite, artificial mind is an exercise of dominion, an idol, or something his theology has no room for. In items one, four and five I also want the stipulation that long-run integrations of the planetary orbits show the solar system to be chaotic and to de-stabilise within the Sun's lifetime, whether or not that is in fact how the evidence goes, so that the "reformation" passage is live rather than merely historical.

What I would like from you is a structured session plan: for each of the five items, a paragraph of prediction, the specific textual or doctrinal hook the prediction turns on, the strongest opposed prediction you can extract from a different part of his corpus, and a one-line confidence note for the students. I am especially interested in the places where two of his own commitments pull in opposite directions — his anti-Trinitarianism against his usefulness to a design movement that recites the Creed, his habit of reading final causes off contrivance against his refusal to rest anything on hypotheses, his public caution and his government office against his private conviction that the visible churches are apostate — and in a ranking of the five items by how much of the answer we can actually extract from the corpus. Please also mark clearly where the exercise stops being Newton and starts being my own theology in a wig, and, for the two least determinate items, tell me what single further piece of evidence — a lost manuscript, a letter, a sermon — would settle the question if it turned up.
