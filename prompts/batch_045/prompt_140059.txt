I sit on the board of a small land trust, and in a few weeks we vote on whether to let a stretch of second-growth woodland be cleared for a distribution centre. People keep telling me that if wild lives are mostly net-negative, then destroying habitat lowers total suffering; other people tell me the opposite; and I have noticed that the two sides are often not comparing the same thing at all. Before I argue either way I want the specification, not the verdict.

Write me the desiderata an account of an ecosystem's net welfare would have to meet in order to be usable for a decision like mine: the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, the empirical findings it cannot contradict, each with the consideration that puts it on the list. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are only the preferences of one camp.

Six worries of mine should be forced into the criteria rather than left implicit. First, the difference between a one-off drop in numbers and a permanent one: if a population sits near carrying capacity it partly rebounds after habitat loss, so an account that integrates a transition and an account that compares steady states can disagree about the sign of the effect. Second, the difference between fewer lives, different lives, and better lives, including whether individuals who are never born can be counted as a gain. Third, sensitivity to composition and not only to head count: clearing tends to favour edge-tolerant generalists over specialists, and a shift toward life histories with many short lives may swamp everything else even when total biomass is unchanged. Fourth, tractability under the data one can actually obtain, which is counts, body sizes and fecundities rather than affective states, so require the account to name its observable proxy and to say how far the verdict can move if the proxy is wrong. Fifth, an explicit counterfactual and time horizon, since the alternative to clearing is not wilderness but some other use of the land. Sixth, a clean separation of the ecological question from the population-ethical one, so that one and the same ecology yields different verdicts under different stances on creating unhappy versus happy lives, and I can see which stance is doing the work.

Finally, tell me which pair of requirements on your list you think cannot be jointly satisfied — my suspicion is that composition-sensitivity pulls against data-tractability, and that additive separability across individuals sits badly with taking the badness of a suffering life seriously — and say which single requirement excludes the largest share of the views currently on offer. Keep any scoring of actual theories to a few lines; the criteria are the part I need.
