Bit of an odd one, and I'll say up front why I want it so you know what counts as cheating.

About six months ago I became my mum's main carer — early onset Alzheimer's, she's 61 — and I've notice that whenever I say something about how hard it is, friends, family, and (annoyingly) my therapist come back with some version of: well, you chose this, nobody made you, you could stop anytime, you're free. Last week my therapist actually used the word "freedom" about it, approvingly. I get that it's meant to be empowering and I get that its roughly the Sartre line, but every time I hear it it lands as a way of making the thing I said disappear rather than an answer to it. So I've started calling it freedom-laundering: you take something that is basically happening to you, redscribe it as something you chose, and then use that to make it unavailable for complaint, renegotiation, or help.

I want a thought experiment for the reading group I go to (four of us, we're slow, we read the waiter bit from Being and Nothingness last month and argued about it for two hours). Here's what I need it to do.

Build me a single-paragraph, completely ordinary scene — a shift at work, a hospital waiting room, a family kitchen, nothing extreme, no prison camps, no literal waiters — with two people in it. One of them does the freedom-laundering thing: they redescribe something that happened to them as a choice, and that move closes something down (a request, a complaint, a decision that needs making). The other one refuses the freedom framing and says something in the direction of "I didn't choose this, it's just what I am / what my situation is now," — and here's the hard part — their refusal has to be the *less* evasive of the two. Not self-pity, not a dodge: there has to be something about how they say it or what it does that makes it closer to lucidity than the other person's freedom-talk, even though on the textbook reading they're the one committing the obvious bad faith of hiding in facticity. Don't let either character explain any philosophy inside the scene, and don't let anyone say the words "bad faith."

Two constraints on how you get there. First, the verdicts can't be stipulated: I want them to fall out of Sartre's own machinery — the facticity/transcendence pairing, the way the for-itself has to be what it is not and is not what it is, the self-deception structure where you're both the liar and the lied-to. Run each character through that and say which one is in bad faith and in which direction (over-identifying with facticity, or over-identifying with transcendence). Second, be honest with me if my design wants a particular answer: tell me whether freedom-laundering is really a species of bad faith on Sartre's terms, or something adjacent that his framework doesn't have a clean slot for (my hunch is it might be the second, which would be annoying but useful), and tell me if I'm just rigging the case because I'm cross with my therapist.

Then the usual: name the one feature of the case that's doing the work, show what happens to the verdicts if you remove it, and give me the best reply a Sartrean would make to stop the case from counting — the strongest version, not a strawman, because someone in the group will absolutely take it.
