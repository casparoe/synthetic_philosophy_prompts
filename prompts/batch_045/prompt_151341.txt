hey — my brother-in-law and I have been going back and forth about statues and street renamings for like a year now, and I think we keep talking past each other because we mean different things by "honor." Can you lay out the conceptual lay of the land for me? Please keep it in plain American English, and no philosopher names or paper titles — I just want the distinctions themselves, not who invented them.

The thing I can't get straight: what is it even for a bronze guy on a horse, or a green street sign, to be *honoring* somebody? As near as I can tell there are three candidate stories and I can't figure out which one is doing the work:

1. It honors him because of why it was put up — the people who installed it in 1911 wanted to salute him, and that original purpose sticks to the object.
2. It honors him because of why it's still up — somebody keeps paying to maintain it, fix it, refuse to remove it, and each of those is a fresh little act of approval.
3. It honors him because of what it now says to people — a reasonable person walking past reads it as a salute.

Here's where I get stuck. On (2), doesn't it follow that the crew repaving the street in front of the statue is honoring him too? That's absurd, but I can't say exactly why it doesn't follow. On (3), imagine the plaque has weathered blank, nobody in town has any idea who the guy was, and teenagers just use it as a landmark ("meet me by the horse"). My instinct is that it's definately stopped honoring anyone — but then the people who say "it's just history now, leave it up" seem to be right by definition, which can't be the whole story either.

Second thing, and this is the one I actually care about. Street names feel totally different from statues to me and I want to know if that's just vibes. A statue you look at; a street name you *use* — you say the words out loud every time you give someone your address, so the honoring seems to ride along on the use instead of on anyone contemplating it. Also, there are only so many streets: every street named after somebody is a street not named after somebody else, so it feels like a zero-sum allotment of civic credit in a way statues aren't (you could in principle always add one more statue). Is that a real asymmetry or am I making it up?

And connected: renaming costs real money and hassle — new addresses, licenses, reprinting, everyone's GPS. So when a city doesn't rename, that might just be inertia rather than endorsement. Does *that* matter for whether the current name honors anybody? Like, does honoring require that the thing's still being there be explained by the honoring attitude, or is it enough that it currently reads as a salute to whoever hears it?

Last bit, and it's the idea I keep coming back to: suppose every commemoration had an expiry date — say fifty years — and had to be affirmatively re-voted on or it lapses. Would that make the honoring genuine in a way it isn't now, or would re-voting every fifty years turn it into a bureaucratic checkbox rather than an actual attitude? I feel like the answer to that tells me which of the three stories above is the right one, but I can't see how.

So: walk me through what the difference between honoring, commemorating, and merely marking actually is, where each of those three accounts breaks, and whether statues and street names really are different cases — at a gut level, not a technical one.
