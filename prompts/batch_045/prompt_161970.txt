I sit on the grants committee of a small foundation that has just been handed roughly £6m to spend over four years, with the mandate narrowed to wild animal suffering specifically. The donor has attached one condition that has split us: we may not fund any work whose value depends on settling — or even on substantially narrowing — the distribution of phenomenal consciousness across taxa. His stated reason is that the sentience question has absorbed years of effort without converging, so that a portfolio resting on it is really a bet on a result in the philosophy of mind rather than a bet on animals; he wants work that pays off whichever way that argument goes. Several of my colleagues think this throws out the load-bearing part of the case. I am not convinced it does, and I would like an outside view before our next meeting.

So: if you were setting this fund's agenda under that constraint, which five questions would you put on it, in order? Give me a ranking, not a menu — tell me what you would actually fund first and why, and be explicit about the criteria you are trading off: how much other work depends on the answer, whether it is tractable inside four years, how neglected it is, and whether the result could be handed to a land manager, a conservation agency or a regulator and acted on rather than filed. Where the donor's condition makes a question more attractive rather than less, say so. For reference, our own shortlist currently includes valuing information about population dynamics, whether landscape-scale interventions have durable effects or merely displace suffering in time and space, what welfare due diligence a reintroduction or rewilding programme should carry, and how much of the variance in expected welfare sits in the choice of intervention rather than the choice of target population — including the usual worry about r-selected species with high juvenile mortality. Do not feel bound by it; I would rather you reordered or replaced entries than annotated mine.

One more thing that is doing our heads in. Our scientific advisers keep reaching for a notion they call a robustly welfare-positive intervention: one whose expected effect is favourable under every assignment of credence over candidate sentience hypotheses within some reference set, so that it can be recommended without taking a stand on which animals count. I cannot tell whether it can bear any weight — it seems liable either to collapse into triviality, because once the reference set is wide enough nothing qualifies, or to smuggle the answer back in through the choice of reference set. If it is salvageable, say how you would repair it; if it is not, say what should replace it, and let that judgement shape your ordering. Please commit to a definite answer: five questions in a definite order, with reasons, plus one thing that would move your top item down the list. Answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion. No citations or bibliography — I want your reasoning, not your sources.
