I sit on a couple of volunteer committees where any spend above a few hundred dollars needs unanimous sign-off: there's no vote, one person can just kill a proposal, and we talk until nobody objects. I keep noticing a pattern in how that goes and I don't have a word for it.

Two instances. There was a grant we could have made to a group with a hard deadline. Six of us thought it was clearly worthwhile; two people were unsure, mostly because they'd read less about the area than the rest of us; and it died. A month later we approved a much duller, much safer expenditure that nobody was unsure about. I don't think anyone thinks the second one was better than the first — it's just that nobody's uncertainty got triggered. What seems to be going on is that the effective bar isn't our average view, it's the view of whoever happens to be most doubtful, and that person is usually the one with the least information rather than the one who's right. Adding members seems to make blocking more likely, not less.

The second thing: because everyone knows who the blockers are, we've started wearing the same two or three people down, and now what gets through is whatever happens not to bother the member who caves most easily. So the veto seems to drift to whoever is most stubborn rather than to whoever is most cautious, which feels like a different distortion from the first one, and I can't tell if it's even the same phenomenon.

I vaguely remember something from a blog post years ago called the unilateralist's curse, about groups where any one member can act alone — but that's the opposite setup, and I can't work out whether it's the same machinery pointed the other way or a genuinely different thing. Searching turns up "veto player", but that seems to be about comparing political systems, not about what happens to the quality of the judgement.

So: does the version I'm describing have an established name? I don't just want a label — I want to know what exactly the distortion is, whether it's the same mechanism as the unilateralist's curse or a different one, whether the stubbornness part has been noticed as a separate effect, and what the nearest concepts are and why each doesn't quite fit. And whether the usual remedies for the acting-alone version (deferring, asking first, requiring agreement) do anything here or actually make it worse.
