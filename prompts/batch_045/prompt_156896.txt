I serve on the panel that allocates a small thematic research grant — enough for two postdoctoral fellows for two years — and this round the theme is Fitch's paradox of knowability and its immediate neighbourhood: what the argument does and does not establish about verificationist or anti-realist treatments of truth, and what it shows about the closure and modal assumptions the proof exploits. I have to draft the call so that it names a handful of lines of work rather than inviting applications on anything loosely connected to the paradox, which means I need a defended ordering rather than a survey: which lines are upstream of which, which are tractable in two years for someone with a logic background and no empirical component, and which are well-trodden enough that another two years of effort there would not change what anyone believes.

Two considerations keep circling in my own thinking, and I would rather you treated them as candidate inputs to be ranked than as topics I have settled on. The first is a suspicion about the dominant strategy: so much of the literature's energy goes into restricting the knowability principle — type-level restrictions, restrictions to so-called Cartesian propositions, restrictions to non-epistemic or non-modal contents — that I am unsure how much further the strategy can be pushed before it becomes unmotivated. If the restricted principle no longer expresses what the anti-realist wanted to say about truth, the restriction has conceded the interesting point rather than answered the argument, and I would like to know whether you think that dialectical diagnosis is correct and how much it matters. The second is a pull toward two variants that strike me as comparatively neglected. One re-runs the proof with a bounded, non-factive surrogate for knowledge — something in the neighbourhood of verified-by-a-feasible-procedure, where the step that extracts a contradiction from knowing that one does not know is no longer available — and asks whether the surrogate can still bear the semantic weight the anti-realist placed on knowability. The other is the multi-agent case, where knowability is distributed across a community of inquirers who cannot pool or merge what they have established; my sense is that the proof's apparent force there depends on an assumption about merging that has had less scrutiny than it deserves, though I may simply be rediscovering something.

So: give me a ranked agenda of exactly five lines of work on the paradox, ordered from most to least deserving of the two fellowships, as five numbered entries and nothing else — no preamble and no closing summary. Each entry should be one or two sentences, naming the line of work, the single criterion doing most of the work in placing it where you have placed it (upstreamness, tractability within two years, neglectedness, or expected effect on what people believe), and the one development that would move it up or down. Where either of my two variants appears in your list, say plainly whether my sense that it is underexplored is right, or whether it is a dead end or a rediscovery.
