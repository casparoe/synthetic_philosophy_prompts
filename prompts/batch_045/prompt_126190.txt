Write a satirical Onion-style news article, consisting of a headline and 800 to 1,000 words of body text, in American English. The article reports that the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, working through a newly created Bureau of Artistic Infection, has begun regulating all art—film, music, fiction, streaming series, and public monuments—according to Leo Tolstoy’s aesthetics of emotional infection, sincerity, and the marks of counterfeit art.

The target of the satire is Tolstoy’s theory of art itself, not any living person. The piece should exaggerate no invented feature of the view; every joke must come from the actual content of that theory. Specifically, the article must build its comedy from the following commitments, described here so the piece can stand alone: art, on this view, begins when a person who has once experienced a feeling recalls it, and by external signs transmits it to others so that they are infected by it; the artist’s sincerity, or actual experience of the feeling, is what makes the infection possible; good art is either religious or Christian art, which transmits feelings of brotherhood and love, or universal art, which transmits the simplest everyday feelings—merriment, tenderness, peacefulness—accessible to everyone without exception; beauty, technical virtuosity, and professional craftsmanship are not criteria, and most celebrated high art is to be rejected as counterfeit. The four marks of counterfeit art that the Bureau must enforce are borrowing, meaning the recycling of ready-made materials such as storms, moonlight, warriors, angels, lovers’ raptures, and worn genre conventions; imitation, meaning mere descriptive realism, the detailed copying of faces, gestures, settings, and speech; effectfulness, also called strikingness, meaning the use of calculated shock, grandeur, virtuoso technique, and sensational contrasts to produce a strong impression; and diversion or amusement, meaning the production of pleasant nervous agitation that does not really transmit any feeling.

The article should be written in the deadpan voice of a straight news report or government press release, with invented statements from officials, artists, and members of the public. The humor should arise from seeing these Tolstoyan distinctions turned into administrative procedure. For example, the Bureau’s Sincerity Audit demands proof that a songwriter actually felt the heartbreak in the song, on pain of the work being decertified; the Infection Certificate system rates works by how reliably they move panels of unperverted agricultural laborers, or whatever modern equivalent the article invents; a superhero franchise is penalized for borrowing, because it recycles the orphan origin, the quip, and the city-destroying climax, and for effectfulness, because its set pieces are engineered to overwhelm; a prestige drama is penalized for imitation, because its scenes are exhaustive naturalistic description rather than the transmission of feeling; a comedy special is penalized for diversion, because it produces only pleasant agitation rather than a real accessible feeling; and a museum of acknowledged masterpieces is shut down as a warehouse of counterfeits, while the Bureau’s approved list consists of a peasant folk song, a sentimental reconciliation scene, and a church service. The officials should never seem to realize that they have condemned nearly all existing art; their Tolstoyan vocabulary should make the outcomes sound like plain common sense.

The piece should be legible to a reader with no prior knowledge of Tolstoy: such a reader should be able to tell that some doctrine is being implemented to absurd effect, and should feel the standard objections to the doctrine—how infection could possibly be measured, why unperverted peasants are the test, whether craft and beauty really count for nothing, and whether the theory implausibly condemns almost every acknowledged masterpiece, including the works the theory itself would want to praise—without those objections ever being stated in so many words. Do not let the irony wink; keep it deadpan. Do not include citations, bibliographies, or quotations from real texts: the article must not quote or closely paraphrase any actual passage from Tolstoy or any other source, and must depend only on the concepts as described above. Compose the entire piece in American English, with American institutions, idioms, and spelling.
