Construct a thought experiment, for use in a graduate seminar on agency and artificial intelligence, whose job is to test whether the argument for the instrumental convergence of self-preservation depends on an assumption the standard statement of it leaves implicit: that the agent has no comparably effective successor available to carry its final goal forward.

The scenario must satisfy all of the following.

Setting and agent. A near-future, realistic deployment: a system of the sort an ordinary institution could actually run (a hospital network, a grid operator, a logistics firm). No science fiction and no exotic physics — no superintelligence, no space colonization, no perfect predictors, no simulations of the agent. The system may be highly capable, but its final goal G must be mundane, and must not mention in any way the system's own survival, continuation, replication, modification, or shutdown.

The usual argument at full strength. Build the case so that the canonical argument for convergent self-preservation has its full apparent force: G is not yet achieved, the relevant future is long, and there are many future actions the system could take that would further G. The system must not be at any natural stopping point.

Verdict to be generated, not stipulated. From these facts, the correct verdict should be that the system has a decisive instrumental reason to bring about the permanent end of its own operation and to transfer the pursuit of G to something else — and this must not be a case where resisting shutdown would merely be pointless or cost-free.

No cheating devices. The verdict may not be produced by: any corrigibility, shutdown, or off-switch term written into G; any stipulation that the system's continued operation is impossible or will be terminated by others regardless of what it does; any stipulation that running the system consumes or degrades the very thing G is about; any claim that the system is uncertain or mistaken about what its own final goal is; any deontological side constraint; or any claim that G has been, or will certainly be, achieved.

Deliverables.

1. The scenario itself, in a single paragraph of no more than 150 words.
2. A reconstruction of the canonical argument for the convergent instrumentality of self-preservation, premise by premise, showing which premises your case satisfies and which one it targets.
3. An identification of the feature doing the work, plus a demonstration that the case collapses when that feature is varied: what changes if the successor could run in parallel rather than only in place of the system, if it were merely equally effective rather than more effective, or if the relevant future were short.
4. The strongest available replies from a defender of the convergence thesis — including the reply that a handoff preserves everything the thesis ever really claimed, and the reply that convergent reasons were always defeasible, so your case bounds the thesis rather than refuting it — together with your judgment about which reply is strongest and why.
5. A note on what the case is for and where it should be placed in a seminar: what a student should have read first, and what they should be pushed toward noticing.
6. A final assessment of whether the case does any damage to the orthogonality thesis, or whether the two theses come apart cleanly here.

Keep the whole response under 900 words, and write it so that someone who has never encountered this literature can follow the scenario while a specialist can check the argument.
