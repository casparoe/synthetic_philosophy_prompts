I am a senior, and once a term our science and ideas club hands a fifty-minute session over to a student. Mine is on a question I cannot settle for myself and would like to be able to teach: how, if at all, does a thought experiment tell us anything about the world, given that nothing gets measured and no new data arrives? The audience is fifteen to eighteen, unevenly prepared — three or four have had mechanics, a few have only biology, and one or two have read popular physics and will announce within ten minutes that the whole thing is only intuition. They will arrive supposing that thought experiments are either party tricks or else fake science with better publicity, and I would rather work with that than pretend it is not there.

I am not asking for the explanation itself. I am asking for the order in which to build it, with the dependencies made explicit: which idea has to be in the room before the next one can land, and which orderings would actively generate a mistake rather than merely move more slowly. The ability I want them to leave with is this — handed an unfamiliar thought experiment, they can isolate the one premise doing the work and say whether the verdict's authority comes from that premise or from how vivid the picture is. Everything else in the session is in service of that, so please judge the sequence against it.

Three things I have been working with, which I would like you to take seriously rather than substitute your own for.

First, my own sorting of cases into three kinds. Extraction cases: the conclusion was already implied by something we accept, the thought experiment only makes us notice it, nothing is really learned, and yet it feels like learning. Recruitment cases: a premise we never actually assented to gets accepted because the scenario makes it vivid, and that premise then carries real weight in the argument. Calibration cases: the verdict is not the point at all; the point is a demonstration that whatever produces our verdicts is unreliable here. I file the old argument that a stone dropped from a tower lands at its foot and so the earth cannot be moving under the third heading, and the reply about the sealed cabin below decks as an answer in the same kind. Tell me whether that three-way sort carves anywhere near the joints, whether a fourth kind is doing work I have not noticed, and — this is what I actually need — where each kind belongs in the sequence, since I suspect that opening with one of them produces relativism, opening with another produces the view that thought experiments are decoration, and closing with a third produces the view that they never teach us anything new.

Second, a device I rely on when arguing with friends, which I call a mirror case: keep the setup, change one feature, and see whether the verdict follows the change. If the class cannot say which feature carried the weight, the original was persuading rather than showing. My candidate mirror for the cabin below decks is the same sealed cabin on a turntable: if the argument were really about the room being closed, rotation would be undetectable from inside too, and it plainly is not. I want your judgement on that mirror specifically — whether it is fair to the original argument, where a sharper student will say it begs the question by assuming inertia, and what I should do with the person who has read that Einstein took Mach seriously and therefore wants rotation to be relative after all.

Third, the one case that has to carry the fifty minutes. I have been assuming the cabin below decks, but I would rather you told me what a carrier for this question must have and must lack, and then whether the cabin qualifies. My guesses, to be corrected: the students have to be able to reach the verdict themselves rather than be told it; the operative premise has to be statable in one sentence so the whole inference can later be put on the board at once; it has to admit a mirror; and it must not have moral stakes, or the hour becomes about trolleys and violinists, and must not contain an inconsistency, since a student who spots one can derive anything from it and will.

Then the misconceptions, and this is the part I am least able to do alone: what they will predictably form — that a fiction can stipulate anything, that a clash of intuitions ends the conversation, that a vivid enough picture is self-supporting, that thought experiments are cheap experiments and could replace the real ones, and the overcorrection at the end, that they are therefore mere psychology, good for having ideas and useless for defending them — and for each one, the exact point in the sequence where it should be headed off, including whether any of them ought to be invited and then dismantled rather than pre-empted.

Finally, two closing matters. The check: I want something at minute forty that separates genuine uptake from a student who has merely learned to say that a thought experiment is just an argument with a hidden premise, and my instinct is to hand them an unfamiliar case and ask for the hidden premise and the mirror in writing — tell me whether that tests what I think it tests and what a failing answer would look like. And the refusals: what I should openly decline to settle, and how to mark the refusal so that it reads as honesty rather than as a brush-off. I assume whether laws are the sort of thing one can intuit has to be one of those, but I would like to know what else, and where to say so.

Please do not give me readings or a bibliography. I cannot get hold of most of it, and my problem is not that I have read too little.
