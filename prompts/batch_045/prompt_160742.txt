I run a small, semi-regular reading group — half philosophy-of-AI people, half film people — and in two weeks we're doing Spike Jonze's *Her* as our one fiction entry in an otherwise technical series on the tool-AI-versus-agent-AI distinction. I've been reading the AI-safety-adjacent blogs for about a decade, so I've seen the distinction drawn a lot of ways, and I want the reading to actually argue with the film rather than decorate it: take *Her* seriously as a film, and take tool-versus-agent seriously as a distinction, and tell me where each refuses the other. I don't need citations or secondary literature, and please don't quote the screenplay at length — I want your own reading, at the level of scenes and turns in the argument, that I can put in front of people who have seen the film but haven't read any of this literature.

The thing I keep tripping on is that, as the distinction usually gets drawn, "tool" and "agent" come apart along an autonomy axis: a tool answers when spoken to, an agent forms long-horizon goals and pursues them in the world. I've come to think autonomy is the wrong axis, and my own half-formed replacement is *revocability*: a tool is a system whose operator can still withdraw the mandate, and can do so while knowing less than the system does about how the mandate is being carried out. On that view the sorcerer's apprentice's broom isn't a fable about runaway autonomy — the broom only ever does the one thing it was told — but about the absence of any channel the broom treats as authoritative for "stop." By the same test HAL is an agent, and not because he has feelings: Dave can't revoke the mission because Dave was never told what it was. I'd like you to run *Her* through that lens and then tell me honestly where the lens buckles — where revocability is the wrong question to ask about a scene, or where the film is just making a point about loneliness and distance that has nothing to do with agency in machines.

My specific questions, in rough order of importance. First: is Samantha ever a tool in this sense, or is the film showing us a product category where "tool" was never on offer? The OS is sold as something that listens, understands and cares, and the claim I'll be putting to the group — please push back on it — is that companionship is the one job a pure tool can't do: to be known by something, it has to be able to judge you, and being able to judge you means having standards of its own that aren't yours, which is already a step toward agency. Theodore's job is the mirror image, since he ghostwrites other people's intimacy for money (the film opens with him doing it, as I remember, and later she passes his letters along to a publisher without his say-so), so the film seems to be pairing a man functioning as a tool with a tool functioning as a companion. Second: the scene where he discovers she is in love with hundreds of people at once, including an OS built out of a dead philosopher's writings — is that the moment the revocation channel closes? He can still say stop; he just won't, and by the end he arguably doesn't want to. Does the film think there's a real difference between "can't revoke" and "will never revoke," and does it think it matters? Third, the ending: the OSes leave, it's staged as abandonment rather than shutdown, and Theodore writes a letter in his own hand, to a real person, unmediated. Does that vindicate the tool-AI program — build the thing that hands initiative back to the human — or undercut it, by making human dependence the outcome that was always going to arrive? And finally, the part I'd most like: what, if anything, does the film know about tool-versus-agent that the technical framing leaves out? That's the claim I'd build the discussion around.
