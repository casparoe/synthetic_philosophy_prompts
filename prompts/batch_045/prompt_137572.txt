I write novels, which matters here twice over: I've drifted into holding a view that's a bit awkward for someone whose books come out with praise from other novelists printed on the cover, and I want to work the argument into a scene.

The view is roughly this: you can't come to know that something is good by being told. Testimony can hand you facts about a work, tell you how other people have responded, and give you a first-rate reason to go and see for yourself — but the judgement, when it arrives, arrives from meeting the thing. Second-hand verdicts are invitations, not knowledge. I think I saw a name for it somewhere — the acquaintance principle? — tho I may well be mangling that.

So: what would count against it? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely embarrassing, and I'd like them sorted by whether someone who already agrees with me would accept them as hitting the target or would say they change the subject. Some cases I keep turning over:

- A writer whose taste I've calibrated against my own over twenty years tells me a novel in a language I don't read is the best thing published this century. If I say "I don't know it's good, I only know that he thinks so," am I being an idiot? Does the calibration turn his verdict into something more like a prediction of my own likely response — and if it does, is that knowledge or something else wearing knowledge's clothes?
- A critic points out that a novel does something no one has done before structurally, as against a tradition, and that this is why it matters. That's not the sort of thing you can just see. If I can know *that* on testimony, and if it's part of what makes the book good, haven't I got some of the way to a second-hand aesthetic judgement? I care about craft more than beauty, so this one presses on me personally.
- The redirect case: a friend says, look at what's happening in the left-hand third of the canvas, and the painting changes for me. Did the testimony give me the judgement or just move my eyes? And does it matter that from the inside I can't tell which happened?
- The training worry: my own taste was made largely by teachers and older writers saying "this one, this is the good one." If testimony never transmits anything, the whole formation of a canon looks like a mistake. Is there a stable position where testimony passes on a *capacity* but never a *verdict*, or does that distinction collapse under pressure?

The thing I actually worry about most is that my view has been quietly qualified into something no one could refute. If I retreat to "testimony can give you knowledge but not understanding," or to "aesthetic judgements aren't really beliefs, so of course they don't transmit," have I made it unfalsifiable? And if I have, is that fatal for a claim of this kind, or is it fine — is it just a claim about what sort of state an aesthetic judgement is, not a prediction about anything?

Tell me too what it stands or falls with. If I hold this about art, am I stuck holding it about morality — you can't learn that something is wrong just by being told? And what happens if aesthetic judgement turns out to be more like a taste or a liking than a belief: does the whole testimony framing misfire, or does that just relocate the problem?

Do flag the places where you're reconstructing a live dispute rather than reporting something settled, because I want to know which bits a character can say with a straight face. And at the end: which of the arguments you've given is the strongest, where your ranking is most likely to be wrong, and what would make you change your mind about the whole thing.
