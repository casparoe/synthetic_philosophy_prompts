I'm retired now — I was a marine geologist for most of my working life — and a few of us from the village have a reading group that plods through one old book a term. Right now we're on Nietzsche's 1874 essay about the uses and disadvantages of history for life, the one where he sorts attitudes toward the past into roughly three kinds: the one that goes looking for great examples to imitate and to spur you on, the one that lovingly preserves whatever has come down to you simply because it is yours, and the one that hauls the past up in front of a tribunal and condemns it in order to free the present. Sorry if I'm mangling it, we've only been through it once. What I can't get off my mind is an attitude to the past that I keep running into and that doesn't seem to sit in any of the three, or at least not cleanly.

Two cases. First, the town here spent years and a fair amount of money restoring the old guildhall — absurd attention to detail, they hunted down hand-forged nails and the right lime mortar and so on — but there is a century and a half in the middle of the building's life when it was a workhouse, and that part has been quietly dropped: no plaque, nothing in the guidebook, and the two people who drove the restoration get a bit frosty if anyone raises it. And the very same people use the restored building, every year, as the backdrop for a speech about how shoddy and rootless everything built since 1950 is. Second, my old university department: the portrait gallery, the founders' names recited at every dinner, the careful upkeep of the story of how the field began — and the whole apparatus is aimed forward as an accusation, everything since is a falling away. Except that the founders being invoked turn out, when you actually check, never to have held the views attributed to them, so the past that's doing the work has been tidied up.

So it's reverence and prosecution at once, and the preserving is selective in a way that serves the prosecuting: what gets kept is whatever makes the present look bad, what gets dropped is whatever would cost the present something or complicate the story. One of the group said it's just nostalgia, which is definately too big a word, and another said it's obviously the third of Nietzsche's kinds — except that his third kind wants to destroy the past, not to polish it and put it on a plinth. Is there an established name for this exact configuration, or a literature on it, and if there isn't, would you name and define it? I don't want just a label, though: I want to know how the boundary runs between it and ordinary nostalgia, between it and each of Nietzsche's three attitudes, which of those it comes closest to and where it breaks away, and what would count as a borderline case — for instance, does it stop being this thing if the people doing it recieve their tidied-up past in perfect good faith and genuinely believe it accurate? Could you answer as exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, with no preamble and no summing-up at the end?
