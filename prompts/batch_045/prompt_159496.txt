I'm in a physics PhD program and our group has this recurring argument at journal club about thought experiments that I can never settle, and I think part of the problem is that we're using one word for three or four different things. I'd like you to explain the difference, but the explanations I run into are either Galileo-drops-two-balls level or straight into some literature fight, and neither of those helps me.

Here's the case I actually care about. I made it up, though it definately feels like something people must have thought about a hundred years ago — I don't need the history, just the structure.

A box of gas, divided in two by a wall. In the wall is a tiny hole, and over the hole on the right-hand side hangs a little spring-loaded flap, hinged at the top. A molecule coming from the left can push it open and get through; a molecule coming from the right just presses it shut against the frame. Because the spring resists, only molecules above some speed get through from the left. So: fast molecules preferentially accumulate on the right, pressure builds up there, you run a turbine off the pressure difference, free work out of a single heat bath. No demon, no measurement, no memory, nothing computational — it's a door that swings one way.

Everyone in the room is instantly certain this can't work. My problem is that I can't say what exactly they're certain OF, or where the certainty comes from. When I try to write down what the thought experiment shows, I get different answers:

(a) It's a proof that the setup is impossible. But impossible given what — given the second law? Then it hasn't given the second law any independent support; it just restates it, and all I learned is that I do in fact believe the second law.

(b) It's a demonstration that my description of the system was incomplete. The flap is made of atoms, it's in thermal contact with the gas, it jitters, it's part of the system, and once you put it in the model the books balance. That feels like where the real content is. But then the knowledge isn't about the world, it's about my model of the world, and it's a bit of a trick that I get to feel profound about a bookkeeping mistake.

(c) Or it's neither, and the whole epistemic load is carried by an intuition: the thought experiment just gets me to notice what I already believed about a situation I've never seen, and the rest is theatre.

So: what is the difference between these supposed to BE? Concretely, in terms of this case, what would have to be true for (c) to count as yielding knowledge rather than merely producing conviction? And here's the wrinkle I can't get past — whatever intuition does the work in (c), I acquired it in a physics education that already drilled the second law into me, so it isn't independent of the thing it's supposedly testing. Does that collapse (c) into (a)? Is there a version of the case where the intuition being trained isn't fatal?

One more thing I can't tell: if you make the flap lighter and the hinge better, does the problem fade away quietly, get worse, or change character entirely? And does the limiting case of a massless flap tell me anything at all? I suspect that question is where the arguement actually is.

Please don't give me a literature survey or tell me who argued what about this — I can't look anything up from where I'm writing. What would help most: take my flap case and rewrite it three or four ways, small changes to the setup each time, so that it does genuinely different epistemic work in each version, and say for each what someone is entitled to conclude afterwards and why. If one of your rewrites turns it from a thought experiment into a plain calculation, say which one and why that matters. Plain language, no jargon I'd have to look up — or define it when you use it — and under 1000 words if you can manage it.
