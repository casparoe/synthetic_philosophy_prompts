You are a professional philosopher who works on the ethics of consent and who is unusually good at the kind of case-based reasoning where one reverse-engineers the principle behind a body of verdicts and then tests it on a case that the body has not ruled on. Take your time: start by brainstorming two or three candidate principles that fit all the precedents below, and only then commit to a prediction.

I am a TA for our department's medical ethics course, and as a running exercise we have been compiling the consent verdicts of one senior bioethicist here — call her Professor H. — from her seminars, her case commentaries, and the offhand rulings she gives in departmental workshops. Her verdicts so far: (1) a patient consents to a spinal injection by Dr. R., having asked whether he would perform it himself and been told yes, when in fact a supervised fellow does most of it — no valid consent, because who puts a needle in your spine is part of what you agree to; (2) a paid research participant is fully informed about procedures, risks, burdens, and payment, but is not told that the lab is funded by a tobacco company, and says afterwards that she would never have taken part — valid consent, since the undisclosed fact was a reason for refusing rather than part of what she agreed to; (3) blood drawn for a routine test is also used, fully anonymized, in a genetic study with no extra burden and no extra risk — valid; (4) sleep with me or I post your photographs — not valid; (5) a desperately poor man sells a kidney to a wealthy stranger for a life-changing sum, under no threat — valid consent, though she adds that the sale should be illegal anyway; (6) work the weekend unpaid or you are fired, in an at-will jurisdiction where firing is permitted — valid, a hard choice but not a coerced one; (7) a patient with bipolar disorder, hypomanic, consents to an elective cosmetic procedure she has wanted for six years, understands and can recite the risks, and later, depressed, says it was not really her — valid, because competence is about capacity and understanding at the time, not about cross-time coherence with one's values; (8) a patient signs a consent form written in a language she speaks but loaded with jargon she does not follow, and does not grasp a five percent risk of nerve damage she would have refused over — invalid, because signing is not consenting.

Here is next week's case, which we have not put to her. The night before an elective spinal surgery, a nurse who has no stake in the decision and only wants to settle the patient down tells her that the surgeon says it is surgery now or she will probably need emergency surgery within the month. This is invented: the surgeon in fact considers watchful waiting and physical therapy perfectly reasonable, and the patient, who is markedly risk-averse and who has been properly informed by the surgeon about the procedure itself, its risks, its benefits, and her right to refuse, would have chosen physical therapy. She consents in the morning, the surgeon — who knows nothing of the nurse's remark and reasonably takes the consent to be informed — operates competently, and the materialised risks are exactly the ones she was warned about. Afterwards she learns the truth and says she would have waited. Was her consent valid?

My own guess is that her track record is consistent with at least two very different principles: a scope-and-source view, on which only deception by the party who receives the consent or their agents, about a feature within the scope of what was agreed to, invalidates — which predicts valid here, with the nurse's intervention counted as a separate wrong — and a reasons-responsiveness view, on which what matters is whether her will tracked the reasons she took herself to have, so that a fabricated option set makes the consent defective whoever fabricated it, which predicts invalid. A third possibility is a role-based view that treats anyone on the clinical team who induces the consent as corrupting it, which again predicts invalid. Please tell me which of these best explains all eight precedents rather than merely the ones it likes, what each predicts about the new case, your best guess at her verdict with the reasons for preferring it over the alternatives, and — this matters most to me — which single further case would best separate the surviving principles; I had been thinking either the same false claim made by the patient in the next bed, or the surgeon's own careless exaggeration of the alternatives, but I am not sure either is the sharpest test. Finally, say whether you think she should treat validity as all-or-nothing or as relative to a dimension (valid for the incision, say, but not for the choice of treatment), and what would be lost or gained by going graded.
