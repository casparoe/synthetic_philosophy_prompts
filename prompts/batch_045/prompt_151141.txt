I am a novelist, three drafts into a book about eleven survivors of a cargo-ship wreck marooned on an uninhabited island, and I am trying to write the emergence and the collapse of their rules honestly — as something that happens to people, not as a fable with a moral stapled to it. To that end I would like you to read William Golding's Lord of the Flies through David Lewis's analysis of convention in Convention (1969), and then hand the reading back to me as something I can use on my own pages.

As I understand the Lewisian machinery, a regularity counts as a convention roughly when: almost everyone conforms to it in a recurrent situation; almost everyone expects almost everyone else to; each would rather conform on the condition that enough others do, because the situation is a coordination problem with more than one equilibrium; all of this is common knowledge among them; and there is some alternative regularity that would have solved the problem about as well — the arbitrariness condition, which is a claim about what else could have happened and not about how the rule feels from inside. Conventions are supposed to be able to arise without any agreement, by precedent and salience, and Lewis's sender-receiver signalling games are supposed to model how a signal and a response can lock into an equilibrium without anyone intending a code. Correct me if I have that garbled, in your own words.

What I want from you is a scene-by-scene reading. Take the conch as a device for solving who may speak, the election that makes Ralph chief as a device for selecting one equilibrium out of many, the signal fire as a signalling system whose intended receiver may never be in a position to receive it, the hunters' chant and the dance as a state in which no single boy can unilaterally defect, and the clay and charcoal marks as something that alters the game the boys are playing rather than merely expressing an allegiance. For each, tell me whether the scene really depicts a convention in Lewis's sense or only something superficially like one, and how the novel makes the mechanism legible: how does Golding get the reader to feel the arbitrariness of the conch while the boys experience it as holy?

Then I want the residue, and I want it stated plainly. Lewisian conventions are self-enforcing equilibria among people whose preferences are aligned enough, held in place by mutual expectation rather than by threat; Lord of the Flies runs on fear, changed desires, coercion, ritual, and the sheer pleasure of the hunt, and its central events are not coordination failures so much as a change in what the boys want, after which the old regularity is no longer an equilibrium at all, plus a good deal of enforcement by sanction. Tell me where Lewis's vocabulary stops being the right one, where a convention has quietly turned into a norm, a rule, a taboo, or an institution, and where the honest critic has to say that Golding is not writing about conventions in any philosophical sense and the reading is being imposed from outside. I would also like your view on what the novel knows that the argumentative literature does not — my suspicion is that the irreducible part is the phenomenology: from inside, a convention does not present itself as one of several equilibria but as the way things are.

Finally, the part that matters most to me. In my draft the survivors work out, without ever voting, that whoever draws water from the spring must be watched by a second person, and much later a splinter group begins marking its faces and hands with red ochre from the riverbank before it hunts. Give me a Lewisian description of what is happening in each — what coordination problem is being solved, what the alternative equilibria are, what makes the practice stick or stop sticking, and which of my two scenes is genuinely about convention and which is about something the framework cannot say. Then give me three or four diagnostics, drawn from the reading, that I could apply to any scene of mine to check whether I have actually dramatized a norm forming or dying rather than announced it. Please write in continuous prose with whatever headings you find useful, draw only on the novel as it is commonly known, do not quote or cite anything, and flag explicitly wherever you are extrapolating beyond what Lewis himself argued.
