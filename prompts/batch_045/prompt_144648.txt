You are a philosopher of science who also holds a doctorate in quantitative genetics, and for the past eight years you have convened the final-year option 'Causation, Variation and Explanation in the Life Sciences'. I chair the examination board for that option, and I need one essay question on heritability for this year's paper. Please think carefully before you start writing: I would like you to begin with a few paragraphs of brainstorming in which you identify the single crux that separates a candidate who understands heritability from one who has merely memorised the standard warnings about it, and in which you name the strongest purely recitable answer a candidate could give and state, part by part, how your question blocks it. Only then draft the materials.

I am not asking you for a bibliography, a reading list or any citations. Everything is to be built from the definition of heritability plus a dataset that I supply below, so that a candidate with no library — or, for that matter, a language model with no web access — could in principle answer it from first principles alone.

The problem I am trying to solve is that the conventional question on this topic has stopped discriminating. Every candidate can now reproduce some version of four remarks: that heritability is a population statistic rather than a fact about an individual; that it is a ratio of variances in a specified population in a specified environment; that a strongly heritable trait may still be moved a long way by environmental change; and that heritability within groups tells you nothing about the cause of a difference between groups. All four are true, and a candidate who produces them and little else should get a comfortable upper-second and no more. I want a question that is unanswerable by reciting them but that still rewards a candidate who deploys them correctly.

Roughly, these are the cruxes I want a candidate forced to confront. Use them, improve the wording, and if you think one is a red herring or that I have missed a better discriminator, say so in a short note at the end with your reasons.

(i) Indexicality pressed one step further. Heritability is standardly said to be indexed to a population and an environment. I want candidates to see that it is also indexed to an intervention regime, and to say what would have to hold for an estimate made under one regime to carry information about another — specifically, why a figure describing the causes of actual differences in a population does not by itself tell you what a novel intervention would do to the mean.

(ii) The variance-versus-mean gap, demonstrated rather than asserted. Why a trait can be maximally heritable within a population and yet have its mean shifted arbitrarily far by an environmental change; and conversely why a successful programme of environmental equalisation tends to push heritability up rather than down, so that a high value can be evidence of environmental uniformity rather than of genetic fixity.

(iii) Within-group versus between-group as a construction task. Given my numbers, the candidate should show explicitly that the two decompositions are logically independent: specify a case in which heritability within each of two populations is as high as you like and yet the entire difference between the two means is environmental. Then ask what experiment would actually settle whether an observed between-group difference is genetic, and whether, if reaction norms cross, there is any regime-independent fact of the matter to settle.

(iv) Heritability of a response. Whether individuals differ in how they respond to an intervention is itself a trait with its own heritability, and that quantity can be high when the heritability of the trait itself is low, and conversely. So 'how genetic is this trait?' is not a well-formed question until one says which trait, in which population, under which regime, and with respect to which contrast.

(v) Underdetermination by the data. The decomposition presupposes a causal structure: additivity, and the absence of gene–environment correlation or confounding. Where genotypes and environments are correlated, the same joint distribution can be carved into 'genetic' and 'environmental' components in more than one way, each yielding a different heritability. Heritability is therefore model-relative, not something the measurements alone fix.

The dataset I want the question built around is as follows; alter any number that does not work out, but tell me what you changed and why.

Ardmore station: same crop species, single clonal line, planted on a field with a marked soil gradient. Genetic variance negligible; environmental variance large. Mean stem length at maturity 95 cm; within-station broad-sense heritability close to 0.
Bexley station: genetically heterogeneous seed lot drawn from the same breeding programme, on a field levelled and fertilised to uniformity. Genetic variance large; environmental variance negligible by design. Mean 140 cm; within-station broad-sense heritability close to 1.
Transplant evidence: swapping the plants between the two stations' soils moves each group's mean by the full 45 cm, so the entire between-station difference is soil-driven.
Carrow station: the same seed lot as Bexley, on uniform soil of intermediate quality; mean 120 cm, but the best-performing lines at Carrow are not the best-performing lines at Bexley, and the genetic variance there is smaller, so the within-station heritability is lower.
Deveron station, a separate trial of the same species: seed lot and soil quality are both determined by whichever nursery supplied the plot, so better genotypes sit systematically in richer micro-plots, and nothing in the measurements settles whether the resulting covariance should be counted as genetic, environmental, or neither.
