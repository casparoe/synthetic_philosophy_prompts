I am writing the final exam for a fourth-year seminar on paradoxes in formal epistemology, and I need one question on the surprise exam paradox that cannot be answered by running through the dialectic students will have memorized: the backward induction from the last day, the point that "surprise" is a property of the students' epistemic situation rather than of the date, the observation that the announcement is self-referential, the gesture at the knowledge-versus-truth distinction. I want them to have to do actual work on a variant they have not seen, and to show that they can tell a genuine result about the paradox from a redescription of it.

The variant I have been teaching — call it the lottery model — works like this. A teacher addresses a class at the start of a term of N teaching days and announces:

(A1) There will be at most one exam: it will fall on one of the N days, or on none of them.
(A2) On the morning of the exam, if there is one, a student who accepts (A1)–(A3), who has observed that no exam has occurred on any earlier day, and who conditionalizes on this information, assigns probability at most t to its being that day.
(A3) The student knows the teacher's selection procedure, i.e. the joint distribution over {day 1, …, day N, no exam}.

Call a policy t-surprising when it satisfies (A2) on every day on which it can place an exam. Part (a) of the question should ask for which thresholds t a t-surprising policy exists, and for the maximum probability with which such a policy can place an exam as a function of t and N — together with an explicit policy that attains it. Part (b) should then ask whether the lottery model dissolves the classical paradox or merely relocates it, and require the student to take a definite position and defend it: in particular, to say what has happened to the clause that there will definitely be an exam, whether a teacher who delivers no exam with positive probability has kept the promise, and whether a morning credence of at most t is really the same thing as being surprised.

Please write the question itself, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20.

Constraints on what I need from you:

- The question should be doable in 45 minutes and be fully self-contained: it must not depend on any named author, paper, date, or quotation, and students should be told that no citations are expected or wanted. Anything the variant needs must be stated in the question.
- The marking scheme should name the specific moves a top answer must make, with marks attached — I expect something like: a correct conditional-probability formulation of (A2); the backward recursion and the inequality it generates; the bound on the no-exam probability and hence on the probability of an exam; an explicit extremal policy attaining it; and, for part (b), a claim about where the classical paradox sits inside the result.
- The scheme should also name the errors that must cost marks, distinguishing an A answer from a B answer. The ones I most want caught: treating (A1) as if it said exactly one exam and concluding impossibility for every t; running the ordinary last-day deletion and thinking nothing else changes; conflating a credence bound with the claim that the student cannot know; producing a bound like 1 − t or N(1 − t) instead of the correct geometric one; assuming the student's priors must be uniform; and forgetting that on the final day the denominator still contains the probability of no exam at all.
- In the model answer, the recovery of the classical case should be explicit and correct: forcing the probability of an exam to 1 makes the no-exam probability 0, which is compatible with the constraint only at t = 1, where the surprise requirement has become vacuous — so the impossibility is driven by the certainty clause, and the last-day step is the terminal case of the recursion rather than a separate flaw in the announcement.
- The model answer should also register that (A3) is doing substantial work: if the student does not know the teacher's procedure, the constraint falls on the student's actual credences and can be met by keeping the student uncertain about the policy itself, which changes what "surprise" is a property of.
