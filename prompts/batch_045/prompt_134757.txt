I work in pharmacoepidemiology and health technology assessment, and I do some cause-prioritization work on the side. The vocabulary I actually think in: target trial emulation, surrogate endpoints and their validation, transportability and external validity, measurement invariance, quantitative bias analysis and measurement-error models, latent-variable and item-response models, mediation and the identification of direct versus total effects, and value-of-information analysis. Assume I know all of these; do not explain them.

What I want translated into that vocabulary is the scale argument about insect sentience. Stipulate the following as given rather than as something to check or correct: something on the order of ten-to-the-eighteenth individual insects alive at any one time; most of them dying very young, with only a small fraction surviving to reproductive age; an evidence base consisting of observable markers such as nocifensive behavior, modulation of those responses by analgesics and anesthetics, associative learning, and stress physiology; and a probability that insects have valenced experience at all that reasonable people put somewhere between about one in a thousand and one in ten. The argument is that the population term is so large that even at the low end of that probability range, expected insect welfare is one of the largest terms in any accounting of the consequences of what we do — large enough that interventions targeting insects dominate interventions targeting everything else, and that research aimed at shrinking the uncertainty is competitive with direct action.

Do two things.

First, a translation that is as faithful as you can make it and that does not quietly import my framework's assumptions and call them neutral restatement. Set the argument up as an explicit decision problem with a target trial: what is the intervention and the counterfactual contrast, what is the outcome variable, what is the population and the time horizon, and who is in the analysis. Write the conclusion as an expected value over three components — a prior on whether insects are sentient at all, a conditional distribution over welfare capacity per unit of time given sentience, and the population or exposure term — and be precise about which component the astronomical number multiplies. Then give me the variance story. My own sense is that the honest version of the argument is not a point estimate but an expected value whose spread under model uncertainty is wide enough that it spans both insects mattering more than everything else combined and insects mattering not at all, and I want to know whether that is the right way to put it, how you would formalize it, and what it does to the ranking of interventions and of research.

Second, the residue report. The four places I cannot make the translation work are these, and I would like you to take them in turn rather than smoothing them over.

Surrogate validity and transport. Every marker we can measure in an insect is a surrogate for the outcome I care about, which is whether there is something it is like to be that animal. A surrogate is validated against the true outcome in some population, and here the true outcome is observable in exactly one population, by self-report, while the surrogate is being transported across an enormous phylogenetic distance with no possibility of a validation study in the target. Say what the vocabulary of surrogate validation and transportability can and cannot tell me here. I have a suspicion that the framework implies a floor on resolution: that below some degree of surrogate validity, the probability-of-sentience input is not an estimand at all, so that plugging in 0.01 and multiplying is not conservative but meaningless. I cannot tell whether that is a genuine implication of the framework or a way of dressing up my own skepticism in its language, and I want you to adjudicate.

The unit of analysis. Insect populations are dominated by individuals with very short expected lifespans. In my vocabulary the choice between counting lives and counting time-at-risk is substantive and changes conclusions. Say what happens to the scale argument if the unit is insect-years rather than insect lives, whether the choice is forced by anything in the argument, and whether the translation has the resources to say which unit is the right one or must simply take it as supplied.

Identification, not estimation. The intervention is not well defined, and the effect of anything we do on insect populations runs through land use, pesticide application, predator and prey dynamics, and other ecosystem feedbacks whose signs are not obvious and may differ by region. It looks to me as though what the scale argument identifies is at best a direct effect, while what matters is a total effect that is not identified at all. Say whether that is right, whether the standard apparatus for direct and indirect effects helps or whether the mediator here is endogenous in a way that defeats it, and then the further question: is the difficulty that we cannot tell whether insects are sentient an ordinary identification failure, fully epistemic, or is there a version of the worry that is metaphysical — no fact of the matter, or a vague boundary — that the vocabulary of identification cannot express at all?

Aggregation across the uncertain branches. The argument treats a one-percent chance of a subject with welfare capacity W as equivalent to one hundredth of W of actual welfare. My framework aggregates linearly across persons and takes expectations over uncertainty without hesitation, so it has no place for the worry that very small probabilities of very large moral stakes should not be treated this way. Tell me whether that linearity is doing real work in the scale argument or is incidental to it, whether population-level QALY arithmetic carries any analogue of risk aversion that the argument is implicitly rejecting, and whether the residue here is a gap in my framework or a sign that the worry is not coherent.

Finish with a verdict rather than a summary of the above. Having done the translation, do you think the scale argument is structurally sound but evidentially unresolvable at present, or does it depend on at least one step that my vocabulary cannot state and that therefore cannot be checked or improved on by better evidence? If it is the latter, say which step and why it is not just a case of missing data.

Please do not give me citations, references, or a survey of the empirical literature on insect nociception; I have stipulated the evidence base, and I want the structure of the inference rather than its inputs. Aim for roughly twelve hundred to fifteen hundred words.
