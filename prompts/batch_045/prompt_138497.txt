I run a first-year seminar on utilitarianism — 18 students, one 90-minute slot, a mix of philosophy and econ majors who have already done two weeks on population ethics, so there primed to smell any setup that looks like it's marching toward a repugnant-style conclusion. I want an in-class activity that makes them feel the pull of the torture-vs-dust-specks problem, and I want them to walk into it before they see it.

Hard constraints: no framing lecture up front, nothing on the board that says aggregation or imperceptible or even harmless, and they should not hear the name of the famous case or who invented it until after the activity is over. The point has to arrive through what they do, not through what I say.

Here's roughly what I have in mind, but please design it properly and tell me where my instinct is wrong.

Stage one: a switch game. Each student gets an anonymous slip and a pen, everyone decides simultaneously, and each yes adds one tiny unit of cost to a shared pool that the whole class actually bears at the end. The thing I want them to live through is that no individual yes makes any perceptible difference to anyone, and yet the pool is entirely made of individual yesses. You pick the currency and justify it — money out of a real class fund, minutes of some mildly boring end-of-class task, raffle entries — whatever makes the marginal contribution genuinely undetectable while the total is genuinely felt by people in the room. Tell me the exact numbers, what I write on the board, what I say and don't say, and the order of reveals.

Stage two: they build the comparison themselves. I give them one pair — one person getting the very bad thing for a long time, versus N people each getting a tiny thing — and they choose the N, then I keep pushing it up until they refuse to go further. They should be the ones generating the sequence and noticing where they stopped. Then I want two structural variants run through the same procedure, because I think the standard case quietly assumes them away:

- Imperceptible versus merely trivial. One round where the tiny harm is felt but negligible, one round where it is fully real, fully a setback to someone's interests, and never experienced or discoverable by the person it happens to. Does the stopping point move, and what does it mean if it does or doesn't.
- Additive versus shaped. One round where each extra unit of tiny harm is slightly worse than the last one, one round where each is slightly less bad because of saturation or habituation. Keep the total area the same across the two rounds and vary only the shape. If they were really tracking total suffering their cutoff shouldn't move, so tell me what to watch for and what it means if it does.

There's a fourth variant I like but can't place: instead of N people each certainly getting a tiny harm, each of N people gets a 1/N chance of the large harm. Same expected badness, very different feel. Fit it in only if the timing genuinely works, and tell me if it belongs before the other two rather than after.

What I need back from you:

- Rules, materials, a minute-by-minute timeline for 90 minutes including setup and cleanup.
- The actual board script and slip wording, minimal, in the order I write it.
- A debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto specific parts of the problem — the slip decision, the moment the N stops rising, the gap between the two shaped rounds — and that separates the moves available to someone who wants to resist the aggregative answer: deny that small harms sum at all, posit a threshold below which they don't count, deny interpersonal comparability, or go lexical and just bite the bullet. What each one costs, and which of them the activity actually puts pressure on rather than merely naming.
- Where I should be honest with them that the activity is silent: I assume it can't reproduce the astronomical numbers, can't reproduce the torture end at all given real stakes have to stay tiny, and can't even pose the question unless welfare is cardinally measurable and comparable across people. Tell me what else is missing and which of those I have to say out loud in my own voice rather than expect them to discover.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. I'm especially worried about the class joker who flips for fun, someone refusing to play on principle, anchoring on whatever N I offer first, the whole thing deflating into it's just a game, and the confound that in the switch game the students are partly reasoning about whether they'll get caught rather than about the victim. Give me a fallback for each.
- One thing I'd really like your judgment on: does an activity like this actually support the aggregative answer, or does it only show that intuitions are calibrated to harms that are perceptible, comparable, and medium-sized, and that we have no reliable response once we leave that range? Is that a real philosophical result the class should walk out with, or is it a way of dodging?
