I am the graduate-student member of the editorial board of the *Journal of Social Mechanisms*. Our board applies an "explanatory adequacy" standard: a submission may be empirically successful and still be rejected if the explanation it offers is, in the board's judgment, not the kind of thing that explains. Here are six anonymized rulings from the last three years, with the reasons recorded in the minutes.

1. **Accepted.** Residential segregation produced by a Schelling-style threshold model: agents with a mild preference for same-type neighbors and an explicit moving rule on a grid. The minutes praise the fact that the individual-level rules are stated explicitly and independently motivated by survey evidence, that the mechanism that produces the pattern is identified, and that the result survives wide variation in the threshold parameter and in what agents are assumed to know.

2. **Rejected.** Regional variation in suicide rates explained by the degree of "social integration" of each region, measured with aggregate proxies. No account of any individual's situation or action, no relational structure among individuals, and no analysis of whether the pattern survives alternative specifications at the individual level.

3. **Accepted.** A banking crisis explained by the topology of the interbank exposure network: banks as nodes, a threshold cascade propagating along exposure edges. Bank-level lending rules are specified, and the authors then show the cascade recurs across a whole family of such rules, so the individual-level detail does not carry the explanatory weight. The minutes say the relational structure is what does the work.

4. **Rejected.** An investment slump explained by a "confidence" or "animal spirits" index. The index is an aggregate survey measure; nothing links it to individual action, no structure among agents is articulated, and the authors' own checks show the effect reverses when the composition of the survey sample changes.

5. **Accepted.** The failure of a piece-rate pay scheme explained by an anti-rate-buster norm among workers, with an enforcement mechanism (informal sanctions) spelled out. The authors show the outcome is unchanged whether the norm is modeled as internalized by workers or merely as expected sanctions, i.e. across two quite different micro-level specifications.

6. **Rejected.** A housing-price boom reproduced by a richly parameterized agent-based model whose individual rules are fitted to the target series rather than measured independently, whose boom disappears when parameters are perturbed within their measurement error, and whose authors concede they cannot say which part of the model does the work. The board's reason: generative fit with no identified mechanism is not explanation, and predictive success alone does not meet the standard.

As far as I can tell, exactly three rules fit all six rulings. Call them:

- **(M) microfoundations:** an explanation is adequate only if it bottoms out in the states and actions of individuals, and the macro outcome is shown to be generated from them by an identified mechanism.
- **(S) structure:** an explanation is adequate if it cites an articulated structure of relations among agents — a network, a norm, an incentive structure — that does identifiable causal work; it is inadequate when it cites only a global property, an aggregate index, or a climate, with no articulated structure and no mechanism.
- **(I) invariance:** an explanation is adequate only if the macro-level claim is shown to be invariant across the range of micro-level specifications compatible with the evidence — what our board has started calling a *micro-substitution sweep*, replacing the agents' decision rules with alternative or random ones to check that the macro claim does not depend on them.

Now the live case, submission 24-11. It explains a one-quarter spike in defaults on a peer-to-peer lending platform. It cites nothing but the structure of the borrower-referral network: a densely clustered core, a rise in the betweenness concentration of core nodes past a percolation threshold, and delinquency propagating along referral edges. No borrower-level decision rule or psychological state is modeled; the authors say explicitly that borrower choice is not part of the model. They run a micro-substitution sweep in which default timing is replaced by random draws matched on the marginal hazard, and the spike's timing and magnitude survive. About fifteen percent of the core nodes are shared accounts used by households and rotating savings groups rather than by single individuals; the authors argue this is immaterial to their result. Calibrated on platform data, the model predicts the spike out of sample better than two competing agent-based models do.

The thing I cannot fit is the sweep: it is evidence that the pattern is multiply realizable, which (I) rewards, and it is simultaneously an admission that no individual borrower was ever modeled, which (M) punishes. The chair has said in meetings that emergence talk usually hides ignorance, and also that a blanket microfoundations requirement would kill most of network science, which I take to be evidence about nothing at all.
