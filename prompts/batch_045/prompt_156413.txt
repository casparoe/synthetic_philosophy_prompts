I spent rather more than forty years as a radio astronomer, most of them on interferometry and calibration, and since retiring I have been sitting in on a philosophy of science reading group at the local university, which has been both exhilarating and, I confess, faintly embarrassing, since I keep discovering that things I said confidently for decades were not quite things I knew. The occasion for writing is a departmental seminar last month. Our group, like every group I have worked in, reports results as a measured value with a 95 per cent confidence interval, and in almost every talk I attend the interval is glossed for the audience as meaning that there is a 95 per cent probability that the true value lies inside it. I have come to think that this gloss is not something the procedure delivers, and I asked, in a way I now recognise was clumsy, whether it matters. A senior colleague closed the discussion with a line I have heard all my working life, usually laid at Feynman's door although I have never seen a source for it: philosophy of science is to physics as ornithology is to birds. Our own cruder departmental version, which I have heard from three colleagues and can therefore quote, is that philosophy is what you do when the apparatus is in the shop. I have also met the same attitude in its blunter public form, a well-known cosmologist's announcement some years ago that philosophy is dead because it has failed to keep up with physics.

I would like to know what these remarks could actually be asserting. Please set out, as a menu rather than as a single favoured reading, the distinct precise theses they might express, from the trivially true to the exciting and probably false, and for each one tell me three things: exactly what it claims, and about whom and at what time and about what kind of help; whether you take it to be true, and how confident you are; and whether, if true, it really does settle my question about the confidence interval, or whether it settles something else entirely. Please also tell me which reading you think a busy scientist who deploys the line after a seminar is most likely to have in mind, because I suspect it is not the reading that would be needed to do the work. If one of the readings is true and does dismiss my question, say so plainly; I would rather have that than tact.

There are four things I want folded into the answer, none of which I have seen handled and any of which may be muddled.

The first is that the analogy may cut against itself in a way nobody remarks on. Ornithology has in fact altered the lives of birds, through conservation law, the siting of wind turbines, the control of introduced predators and the design of buildings, and it has done so because its findings reach people who are in a position to act and who are not themselves birds. So I want to know whether the intended force of the line depends on the benefit being usable by the scientist himself, at the bench, in the moment. If it does, then it looks like a claim about a division of labour within science rather than a claim about usefulness, and I do not see why a philosopher of science should concede it. Call this, if it is worth a name, the uptake condition: an outside discipline can only help a field to the extent that somebody in that field's causal neighbourhood is positioned to act on what it delivers. Please state the condition precisely and then test it, including the awkward case where the party able to act is a grant committee, a journal editor, a referee or a science journalist rather than the person taking the measurement, and the awkwarder case where the delay between the finding and the act is a generation.

The second is a distinction I have been toying with and cannot get to sit still: help that changes a number going into a paper, and help that changes what the number is taken to mean or what it licences one to say. My suspicion is that the ornithology line borrows its plausibility from the first kind of help and is then applied to the second, where it may simply not hold. Tell me whether that cut tracks anything real, whether some other distinction does the work better, and where the two kinds of help come apart in practice.

The third is the retort I have already made once and immediately felt cheap: that philosophy of science is useless to science is itself a claim of exactly the sort it denies. I would like this handled properly rather than triumphantly. Does it refute the line outright, does it refute only the cruder readings on your menu, or is it a bad objection that I should stop using?

The fourth is a suspicion of my own, drawn from watching two fields age differently. It seems to me that the value of conceptual work may depend on how far a field's central terms are still load-bearing rather than merely bookkeeping, so that the line might be true of a mature, well-posed field and false of one still arguing about what its own quantities are. My own subject spent a decade disagreeing about what counted as a trustworthy image before anyone could sensibly compare results. Say whether that thought survives scrutiny, and how one would tell, from inside a field, which situation one is in.

Please assume that I know undergraduate physics and a good deal of practical statistics but no philosophical vocabulary, and define any term of art you need. I have no interest in who said what first, and I am not asking for a bibliography, a reading list or citations: I cannot chase references and would not know whom to trust, so argue the points rather than sourcing them. Write in British English, and write it as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, signposting the readings in the running text as the first, the second and so on, because I mean to read your answer aloud to two friends from the reading group.
