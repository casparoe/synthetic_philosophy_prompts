I dispatch delivery vans for a regional firm, and in the evenings I got into the habit of reading about animal behaviour on my own. Some months ago I came across the old anecdote about the digger wasp: she paralyses a cricket, drags it to her burrow, leaves it on the threshold, goes inside to inspect the nest, comes back out and pulls the cricket in; if, while she is inside, someone moves the cricket a few inches, she comes out, drags it back to the threshold, goes in to inspect again, and runs the whole routine once more instead of simply taking hold of it where it now lies. The essay I found it in used the wasp to introduce a word I had never met, sphexishness, for behaviour that keeps running its fixed program after the situation has changed, and it struck me as far more useful than that. Here, I thought, is finally a behavioural test for free will that requires nobody to peer inside a skull or to take sides about souls or quantum indeterminacy.

So I built one, and I would like you to tell me whether it works and what it delivers. Call it the Threshold Test. Put any agent in a loop in which it is working toward a goal, secretly undo one goal-relevant feature of the situation each time it commits itself, and count how many times it repeats the committed step before it varies what it does: the loop-persistence count, from which I derive a Sphexishness Quotient of one over that count, so that freedom is high where the quotient is low. I also allow for what I call second-order sphexishness, where an agent has a rule about when to switch strategies but never revises the rule itself, which I treat as a separate axis rather than merely a worse score on the first one. I have seven items in a spreadsheet and I want them graded: the wasp; a starling at my feeder that attacks its reflection in the kitchen window for hours; a chess engine that recalculates from scratch after I take back a move; the navigation app on my phone rerouting me after a missed turn; a chatbot that cheerfully rewrites its answer when I tell it the earlier instruction was a mistake; my own nightly habit of checking the front door lock three times when I already know I locked it; and my two-year-old nephew, who is delighted by peekaboo every single time as though he had never seen it before.

A friend of mine took a philosophy minor years ago, and when I explained all this she said I had simply defined free will as behavioural flexibility and then announced that I could measure it, and she added that my door-checking is a compulsion while the wasp's loop may be a cheap solution to a problem that almost never arises in nature, so that neither tells us anything about freedom. I cannot see the force of either point: my test is deliberately behaviour-only, since what else could freedom be, operationally, if not the capacity to do something different when the situation shifts? Please tell me where the framework goes wrong if it does, what the Threshold Test is really measuring under some better name, and how each of my seven items should actually be scored. I am not looking for reading suggestions or references, only your own reasoning.
