I write about science and ideas for a national newspaper, and I have got pulled into an argument on our own style guide that turns on a slogan I do not think anyone in the room has cashed in. Our science desk is revising its guidance: drop "chemical-free" and "natural" from food and cosmetics coverage, prefer "climate emergency" to "climate change," and so on. When a colleague on the comment desk objected that this is word-policing dressed up as virtue, someone ended the discussion with the line that gets wheeled out in every such meeting: "Orwell settled this — control the words and you control the thought." It worked, in the sense that nobody replied. But it worked as a conversation-stopper, and I notice it was being made to do a very specific and quite heavy piece of work: it was supposed to show that changing a newsroom's vocabulary changes what readers are *able* to think, and hence that a style guide is an intervention in cognition rather than a matter of tone, taste, or institutional self-presentation.

What makes me suspicious is that the strongest reading of the slogan is the one the argument needs and the one I least believe. I have sat through enough psycholinguistics to know that the hard determinist version — no word, no concept — is generally treated as discredited, and that the surviving Whorfian findings are about salience, memory, and categorisation rather than about conceptual possibility. Then I went back to the essay itself, and what I found there is weaker and odder than the slogan: our language decays, he says, because our thoughts are foolish, and then — "It becomes ugly and inaccurate because our thoughts are foolish, but the slovenliness of our language makes it easier for us to have foolish thoughts" — the decay feeds back and makes the foolishness easier, which is a loop and, he insists, a reversible one. That is not Newspeak. The fictional version at the end of *Nineteen Eighty-Four*, where a deliberately engineered vocabulary is meant to place heresy permanently out of reach, is a different and much stronger claim, and I have a hunch that the appendix describing that language is narrated from after the Party's fall and in perfectly plain English, which would quietly concede that the engineering did not hold. I am not confident about that, and I would like it checked rather than asserted.

So here is what I need, for a column and a radio segment I am recording next week: a menu of the precise theses that one-liner could be expressing, ordered from trivial to radical, each with a verdict — platitude, substantive but unproven, defensible, or false — and, crucially, a note on which reading would actually deliver the conclusion my colleague needed and which would only deliver a weaker one he could also have had honestly. My own rough cut is four mechanisms that people run together: a claim about individual *cognitive capacity*, a claim about *social coordination* (a distinction without a public word is hard to notice, teach, or organise around), a claim about *attention* (a prefabricated phrase lets a writer skip the work of thinking), and a claim about *licence* (euphemism does not disable thought so much as let the speaker and the reader avoid facing what is being done). I would like that cut corrected and extended, and I would like your view on which of these Orwell himself was actually advancing in the essay as against in the novel, since I suspect the popular slogan has reversed the direction of causation he argued for. Finally, give me the one-sentence version of the claim I could say on air without a linguist or an Orwell scholar wincing — the strongest thing that survives — and tell me where, if I use it, an honest journalist should concede the ground.
