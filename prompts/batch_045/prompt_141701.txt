Been reading the rationalist corner of the internet since roughly the Overcoming Bias days, so my reflex on any "I can't decide" question is: shut up and multiply, pick the bigger number, and if that still feels bad the problem is with my feelings. A friend of mine — ex-philosopher, now a clinical ethicist at a hospital, she's the one who got me into this — keeps insisting that this only works when the options are actually comparable, and that the interesting cases are exactly the ones where there's no bigger number to go find. I've read some of the parity / voluntarist package (Chang and co.) and it half-clicked, but I've also definitely seen "incommensurable" used online as a stop-word meaning "I don't want to do arithmetic," which I take to be a real failure mode that a good theory should block rather than license. So before I commit to any particular account, I want the spec.

Write me the desiderata an account of hard choices and incommensurable values has to meet to count as adequate. Fix the purpose as mine: adequate means good enough that I could (i) tell the difference between a genuinely hard choice and a lazy refusal to do the math, and (ii) say something non-bullshitty to my friend Wren, who is three weeks into agonising over two job offers and wants to know whether she is *allowed* to just pick — one is more money and status but sixty-hour weeks in a city she likes, the other is worse-paid, more autonomy, near her family, and neither dominates. For each requirement give the case or consideration behind it, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are just the prejudices of one camp. The cases I want covered, at minimum: the tiny-token case (if you add five dollars to one option, does it stop being hard? — a theory whose account of incommensurability survives an arbitrarily small sweetener is doing something different from one that doesn't); Wren's case re-run (same person, same information, ranks A above B on Tuesday and B above A on Thursday, with no new evidence and no self-deception — is that irrational, a symptom, or the signature of parity?); the resolution case (at coarse grain both options look comparable and "fine," at fine grain they don't — is comparability grain-relative or is that just vagueness in disguise); the transformative case (the choice changes who is doing the valuing, so the evaluator after the choice isn't the evaluator before — having a kid versus not, say, or a career you can only enter young); the chaining case (A is slightly worse than B, B is on a par with C, C is on a par with A — can this agent be exploited, and does a theory that permits two eligible options owe us a story about why that isn't a money pump?); the advisor asymmetry (when I'm deciding for myself, "either is fine, just pick" lands as a category error; when Wren is deciding, the very same advice seems fine — that gap should fall out of the theory, not be asserted alongside it); the tragic-remainder case (choosing one leaves something that isn't merely lost but is in some sense wronged); and the collective case (cost-benefit analysis, a statistical life versus a wetland, institutions that have to decide anyway — does incommensurability have any bite for public choices or is it only first-personal?). Also tell me which distinctions the theory is not allowed to collapse: incomparability versus parity versus equality versus mere vagueness versus our ignorance of the true exchange rate versus incompleteness in the agent's own preferences; "the values are incommensurable" versus "the options are incomparable"; no fact of the matter versus a fact of the matter we can't know versus a fact of the matter the agent constitutes by choosing; and hard-because-the-world versus hard-because-of-us.

The part I most want: which pairs of desiderata you suspect can't be jointly satisfied, so that the list itself is the interesting result. My own candidates — argue with them — are (a) "both options are rationally permissible" against "the agent must not be exploitable / must not be money-pumpable," (b) "there is no fact of the matter which is better" against "the choice is nevertheless the moment where value gets created and where the agent can get it wrong," and (c) "the class of hard choices is rare and serious" against "hard choices are everywhere once you look at fine enough grain." Also say which single requirement does the most work excluding popular candidates, and which requirements on this list are really just one camp's aesthetic preferences wearing a lab coat (transitivity of betterness, expressibility in a decision-theoretic formalism, no primitive normative entities, no residue after a good choice, and so on). Then, briefly — a few lines each, not an essay, and no bibliography, I have no library access and can't look anything up, just argue it — score the parity/voluntarist view, the "it's really just vagueness" view, the "we're merely ignorant of the exchange rates" view, and the "everything is commensurable under one currency with diminishing returns" view against the list. If a candidate flunks a requirement you think is non-negotiable, say so plainly.
