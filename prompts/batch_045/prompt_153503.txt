I'm in the final year of my PhD and I'm giving a twenty-minute paper, with what will in practice be nearer twenty minutes of questions, at a joint work-in-progress seminar between my department and the neighbouring one, in three weeks' time. The paper defends a reading of Nietzsche's eternal recurrence as a test of modal robustness rather than as a cosmological doctrine or as a florid way of asking whether one is glad to be alive. The claim I actually want to defend is narrower than that sounds: the demon's question is not asking for a comparative verdict on one's life as against the alternatives available, but for a non-comparative endorsement, and the two come apart. To make the separation vivid I set the standard memorial recurrence — exact repetition, with memory, no novelty — beside two variants I introduce myself: the telescoped recurrence, in which each cycle is phenomenally identical to the last but occupies less objective time, so that a lifetime of experience is compressed into ever shorter stretches of the world's history; and the amnesiac recurrence, in which each cycle is lived without recollection of its predecessors. I argue that the amnesiac variant cannot do the work Nietzsche needs because it collapses into a dispositional pleasure test, that the telescoped variant shows the test tracks phenomenal character rather than objective duration, and that only the memorial variant forces the question of whether one's valuing is essentially novelty-dependent, which is the real target. I then suggest that the demon's question is properly answered with a temporally indexed conditional rather than a bare yes: one may be able to will the recurrence of one's life as the person one now is while being unable to will it as the person one was at twenty, and reading affirmation as indexed in this way explains why the test is not trivially passed by everyone who happens to be content.

The room worries me more than the paper does, so I'd like the prep sheet to be built around it. There will be two Nietzsche specialists: one holds the minority view that the recurrence is a cosmological doctrine Nietzsche took to be supported by arguments from finite force and infinite time, and thinks the thought-experiment reading domesticates him; the other works on the unpublished notebooks and will want to know whether my reading reduces the published passages — the demon passage in The Gay Science and the relevant chapters of Zarathustra — to mere rhetoric. There will also be three people from the decision-theory and formal-epistemology side who know the literature on regret and on choosing under uncertainty about one's own future attitudes, and who will want the test stated as a decision problem with a defined choice set and well-defined attitudes; at least one of them thinks the whole exercise is unfalsifiable moralising. And there will be a psychologist who works on affective forecasting and the impact bias, plus half a dozen graduate students who have read the usual passages once. I will say at the outset that I am setting the cosmological question aside. Given that mix, give me the objections I am actually likely to get, ordered roughly by how likely they are and then by how much damage they would do, each phrased the way the relevant person would phrase it — a decision theorist's objection should sound like a decision theorist's, not like a continental philosopher's — with a two-minute reply for each, a note on where that reply is weakest, and, where it applies, what I should concede before I am asked. Tell me which single reply has to land for the paper to survive, and tell me what the question is that I'm hoping nobody asks.

One last thing about how I want the answers framed. Please separate what is comparatively settled among people who work on Nietzsche — that the recurrence appears both as a cosmological hypothesis and as a thought experiment, that the published and unpublished materials differ in emphasis, that the demon's question is standardly read as some kind of test of affirmation — from what is genuinely contested, and from what is simply my own addition (the telescoped and amnesiac variants certainly are, and so is the temporally indexed conditional). Where a move of mine would strike either specialist as a minority or idiosyncratic reading, say so plainly rather than smoothing it over; where one of my replies depends on a claim specialists would dispute, mark the reply as resting on contested ground; and if you are unsure whether some textual or scholarly claim holds, flag that rather than asserting it. I don't want my reading presented as the state of the debate.
