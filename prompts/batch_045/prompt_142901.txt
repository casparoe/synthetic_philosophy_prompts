I'm a teaching assistant for an undergraduate course on the philosophy of emotion, and I keep running into the same move in section. One of the other TAs, who also does some kind of somatic coaching on the side, leans on a single analogy and gets a great deal of mileage out of it: emotions are perceptions. The mapping he offers goes like this — vision is of colors, emotion is of danger, insult, or value more generally; both are non-inferential and automatic rather than reasoned; and just as a perceptual illusion can persist after you know perfectly well that the two lines are the same length, a recalcitrant emotion — the experienced pilot who still fears flying, the woman who judges her new colleague harmless and yet feels fear — need not be any kind of rational failure. He then draws the conclusion he actually cares about, which is practical: deliberately reappraising an emotion is a category mistake, something like trying to argue yourself out of seeing an optical illusion, and the reasonable response to a recalcitrant emotion is therefore to trust it and change the situation rather than to talk yourself down. He also presents the analogy as a way of splitting the difference between the two views we covered in lecture — the cognitivist line that an emotion just is an evaluative judgment, and the James-style line that it is a feeling of bodily change — on the grounds that perception is neither: not judgment-shaped, but still world-directed and still assessable for correctness rather than only for how it feels. I am doubtful that the compromise is stable, and more doubtful that it carries his practical conclusion.

What I would like is an audit rather than a verdict. Please lay the correspondence out explicitly, as a table if that is clearest: for emotions-as-perceptions, what plays the role of the distal property, of the sensory organ, of the proximal stimulus, of the receptor, of the illusion, of the illusion's remaining even after correction. Then separate the disanalogies that actually bite for the conclusions he wants from the ones that are idle. Two I cannot settle on my own. First, in the perceptual case we have some story about how the system tracks a distal property — receptors tuned to a physical magnitude, a dedicated organ, an ecology of illusions explained by the receptor story — whereas there seems to be no comparable story about how anything in us tracks danger or value; I want to know whether the real work here is being done by the word perception itself, and whether the analogy survives once that is flagged. Second, perceptual illusions are notably resistant to correction by what you know, yet emotions look trainable in a way the geometric illusion is not: exposure, reappraisal, simply having grown up somewhere safer all shift what a person fears. That pushes me toward a different model, on which an emotion is like a calibrated alarm — a smoke alarm that is functioning exactly as designed but whose sensitivity is badly set for the kitchen it has been moved into. On that model the claim that an emotion is wrong splits into two separable claims, that its content misrepresents the situation and that the mechanism's threshold is miscalibrated for the environment it now operates in, and it seems to me that perceptual vocabulary quietly runs these together while the feeling theory can only see the second one.

So please resolve two things. Take my test case: someone raised by a violent parent who now flinches at a raised hand in surroundings everyone agrees are safe, and who has had enough therapy that she no longer believes she is in danger but flinches anyway. Is she best described as misperceiving, as being badly calibrated, or as something for which the analogy has no slot at all? And whichever way you go, does my colleague's conclusion survive — that reappraisal is a category mistake and that the thing to do is trust the emotion and change the situation? Second, if the perceptual analogy cannot carry that conclusion, is there a better analogy for the work he needs done — alarm systems, bodily appetites, habits, skills, something else — or is the search for one governing analogy itself the error? Tell me plainly if you think the conclusion was never supported and the analogy was only ever decoration, and if you think he is right about the conclusion but wrong about why, say which part of the mapping would have to hold for it to be earned.
