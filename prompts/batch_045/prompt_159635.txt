Hey — I run a tiny Marxist reading group out of the back of a used bookstore, six or seven of us, and we're on the machinery part of Capital and going in circles. One guy's line is that automation for Marx means workers get ground down — longer hours, worse pay, reserve army breathing down their necks — and the other guy's line is that automation wrecks profitability because dead labor keeps growing relative to living labor. They argue as if those are one claim with two mouths, and I want a single case that pries them apart, with arithmetic I can check by hand rather than vibes.

So: build me a numerical example in Marx's own categories, everything in hours of socially necessary labor time, with a before and an after, where a sector adopts a labor-saving machine and — at the point where the new technique has already spread through the sector, so no capitalist is getting extra surplus profit from being ahead of the others — four things hold at once: (1) the rate of surplus value rises, (2) the organic composition of capital rises, (3) the rate of profit comes out lower (in that sector, and so, holding the rest of the economy fixed, in general), and (4) the workers are materially better off, real wages up. Constraints, because I know where this usually gets fudged: the working day stays the same length, money wages don't fall, the machine creates no value (that's cheating, Marx is explicit that only labor does), and the machine itself doesn't get cheaper, so the only two things moving are c/v going up and the value of labor power going down because some wage goods got cheaper. I also want you to invent a knob I'll call the pass-through: the share of the workers' actual consumption bundle that gets cheapened by the new technique — park it around a third — and let the four verdicts fall out of the numbers instead of you announcing them. Ordinary industry, please (bakery, textile mill, warehouse, whatever), numbers small enough to be hand-checkable, because I want to be able to scribble the whole thing on a whiteboard in five minutes without anyone taking it on faith.

Give it back as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, no intro and no wrapping-up at the end (item 1 can carry the little before/after table — that's the scenario itself, not extra prose): 1. the scenario and the numbers; 2. the arithmetic for each of the four verdicts; 3. what each of the two readings I described says about this case, and why it says that; 4. which single feature is doing the work, and what happens to the profitability verdict if I crank the pass-through up until basically the whole bundle is cheapened; 5. how someone wedded to the "automation always immiserates" reading would attack the case, and whether the attack lands.
