You are a philosopher of mind and language who also does applied work on classification disputes: you know the literature on concepts, similarity, analogy-making and conceptual slippage in small closed domains, and you know how badly that literature translates into a checklist that non-specialists have to run under time pressure. Think carefully before writing. First brainstorm the conceptual material you will draw on and what has to survive simplification; only then draft the procedure; then run it on the cases; then state plainly where it breaks.

I chair the Design Integrity Panel of a board-game publishers association. About ten times a year we hear a complaint that one studio's game is a copycat of another's. Our panel is three working designers plus one lay member, we have ninety minutes per complaint including deliberation, and we must issue a one-page written finding the same week. Our sanctions are professional, not legal: a public finding, removal from the association's showcase, and in some cases a requirement to credit the earlier designer. We have no legal staff and we have deliberately put copyright doctrine out of scope, including the idea/expression distinction and the abstraction-filtration-comparison machinery that comes with it. Right now we fall back on a holistic total concept and feel test borrowed from law, and our verdicts are inconsistent in exactly the way you would expect: everything looks like a copy to some panel members and nothing does to others, because similarity is being judged against whatever comparison class each person happens to have in mind.

I want a reusable decision procedure my panel can actually run, grounded in what is really known about concepts and similarity in a small domain. Please import these ideas explicitly, and tell me if you think one of them should be dropped:

Fix the microdomain first. In research on computational analogy-making, the letter-string domain is used because it is tiny and the space of alternatives is fully specifiable: given abc becomes abd, what does iijjkk become, and what does xyz become. Notice that the last question has more than one defensible answer precisely because concepts there can slip: successor into predecessor, the letter d into the idea of the next letter, the rightmost position into the final group of a run. I want my panel to begin every complaint by fixing the comparison class for the accused game: the genre, its conventions, the design moves available at the time, the space of games a competent designer in that scene could have sampled. Say how to do that in fifteen minutes with no market research, and what to do when we cannot agree on the comparison class.

Separate forced choices from free choices. A feature forced by the microdomain carries no copying signal; a free choice that matches does. Give us a test for telling them apart, and handle the hard middle case of a feature that was free when the earlier game was made but has since become a genre convention.

Test for slippage, not literal identity. A copy can preserve a structure while every surface description changes: dice for cards, a die for a token, a deck for a bag, single worker for stack of workers, one resource for another. Our current holistic test systematically undercounts this. Give us the mapping step in a form three volunteers can apply without the theory.

Require distinctiveness before counting similarity. A pattern of matches is only evidence if it is rare in the fixed space of alternatives. Give us a coarse way to say how many alternative designs would show this same pattern, including what to do when we genuinely cannot estimate it.

Check abstraction level. Run the verdict one level up and one level down, and tell us what to write when the verdict flips.

Then run the finished procedure on three complaints:

One: a 2023 release with the same rule skeleton as a 2018 card game, different theme, different art, and dice substituted for cards at one point in the turn, plus two balance tweaks the accused studio says took eight months.

Two: two deck-building games released four months apart, independently developed, sharing everything that defines the genre and nothing else that either panel member can point to.

Three: a case where the procedure gives us an answer I will not like. Our complainant is a two-person studio; the respondent is a large publisher that has clearly studied the complainant's game. But the mechanic the complainant calls its signature turns out, under the procedure's own tests, to be a slippage copy of an out-of-print abstract game from the late 1980s that none of us had heard of. Tell us what the procedure says and what the one-page finding should look like.

Finally, give us the honest limits: the kinds of game for which this procedure will misfire, the points at which it must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than fact, and the cases where it should decline to issue a finding at all. Write the whole thing so a volunteer designer can read it once and run it, with the theory kept in a short appendix rather than in the steps themselves.
