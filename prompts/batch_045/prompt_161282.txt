I am assembling a set of case studies for a graduate seminar on rational choice, decision theory, and the philosophy of action, and I want to move beyond stylized textbook setups. One case I keep returning to is the construction of the Sydney Opera House. For anyone who needs the salient facts: an international design competition in 1957 was won by the Danish architect Jørn Utzon on the strength of a visionary but schematic scheme; the New South Wales government authorized construction to begin in 1958, and work started in 1959 even though the geometry of the roof shells had not yet been solved; the structural solution was found only in 1961, after parts of the podium had to be rebuilt; the project was funded in large part by a dedicated series of state lotteries; initial public estimates were on the order of A$7 million with completion expected in the early 1960s, but the building opened in 1973 at a cost of about A$102 million; in 1965 a new state government took office, and in 1966 Utzon resigned amid disputes over fees and design authority, left Australia, and the interiors were completed by others in forms he did not endorse; the finished building is now a UNESCO World Heritage site and one of the most recognizable and economically valuable cultural landmarks in the world.

I want you to analyze this episode through two closely related lenses: the dollar auction, understood as the game in which the highest and second-highest bidders both forfeit their bids while only the top bidder receives the prize, and the broader escalation-of-commitment literature with its usual mechanisms such as sunk costs, self-justification, entrapment, completion effects, and audience or reputational pressures. Please give a careful mapping rather than a slogan. Who, exactly, are the bidders, and what is the prize? Is the prize the completed building, the political credit for it, the architectural legacy, or something else, and who actually receives it? Who is the auctioneer, and what happens to the model given that the government is simultaneously rule-maker, bidder, and financier, while the public lottery ticket buyers are a diffuse party who have already paid? At the key junctures (the decision to start before the shells were solved, the 1961 spherical-geometry solution, the reliance on lottery funding, the 1965 change of government, and the 1966 rupture), what were the realistic options and rough payoffs for the main parties, and which escalation mechanisms best explain why the project continued rather than being redesigned, mothballed, or cancelled?

Then I want the reverse direction: an honest account of where the dollar-auction and escalation framework breaks or has to be stretched. In particular, the textbook dollar auction assumes a fixed, known prize and identifies irrationality with bidding beyond that prize's value, but here the value of the prize looks endogenous: continuing through each crisis may itself have helped constitute the building's later artistic, cultural, and economic worth, so that ex post the enormous overrun was accompanied by an even larger creation of value. Please say whether this turns the Opera House into a confirmation of the standard models, an anomaly, or a case they were never designed to anticipate, and what it would take to extend the models to cover prize-constitutive or value-creating megaprojects. Where the theory is silent, say so instead of forcing it to speak; and do not give me a bibliography or citations, just the analysis itself.
