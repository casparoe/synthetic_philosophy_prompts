Next week's guest on my epistemology podcast defends what she calls competence-scoped Reflection: the claim that Reflection constrains an agent only with respect to propositions the agent now counts her later self as competent about, with the familiar counterexamples — the drug that induces confusion, the conversion experience, predictable memory loss, deference to a corrupt authority — all said to fail that scope condition. Draft eight to ten interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, pressing on: (i) how the scope condition is fixed without circularity, given that judgments about a future self's competence are themselves current credences, apparently subject to the same principle; (ii) whether a diachronic Dutch book argument can be restricted the same way, given that the bookie's strategy exploits conditional credences in the value of her later credence rather than anyone's competence; (iii) fission cases, where an agent anticipates two successors with divergent credences and there is no single thing she will later think; (iv) whether a scoped principle still does the work van Fraassen wanted of the unscoped one, namely underwriting the charge that expecting to be improved by further reflection, or departing from conditionalization, is rationally defective; (v) cases in which the later self will be shown the earlier self's prediction and will defer to it, so that the fixed-point condition looks satisfiable by any credence at all; and (vi) what observation about her own future opinions would lead her to abandon the principle rather than redraw the scope. No question should let her simply restate the principle or its standard motivation.
