I spent my working life in population genetics and have lately begun reading political philosophy, where I keep getting stuck on an institution that looks, from the outside, like a structure retained long after the function that produced it has gone: the hereditary head of state inside an otherwise parliamentary democracy.

Take the British case. Members of Parliament must swear or affirm allegiance to the monarch before they may take their seats; criminal proceedings are brought in the name of the Crown; and where a bill would affect the Crown's own prerogatives, revenues or property, the monarch's consent is sought early in the process, before Parliament may debate or vote on the substance — though it is granted or withheld only on government advice. Sweden's 1974 settlement moved in the other direction, removing the monarch's formal governing powers while keeping the office, leaving a head of state with largely ceremonial and consultative functions. The institution therefore binds together two things I would have expected to be separable: a person who is the object of allegiance and the locus of legal authority, and a person with no discretion at all.

What I want is the implicit theory, stated as a set of commitments rather than as a verdict on monarchy. Does the practice presuppose that the political community is extended in time in a way the living electorate is not, so that officials chosen by today's voters cannot stand for it while someone positioned in a line of succession can? Is the hereditary principle doing the work of mandate-proofing — selecting for an office-holder who cannot cite a popular mandate against Parliament, as an elected figurehead holding the same reserve powers arguably could — or is heredity merely an arbitrary but stable selection rule for a role whose real content is ceremonial?

Here is the test case I have been turning over. Keep every power, duty and ceremony exactly as it stands, but select the occupant by lot from citizens who pass a vetting procedure, for a single non-renewable term, with the holder's family life as public as it is now: call it the lottery crown. A lottery winner is mandate-proof too, and in a sense more transparently so. Which of the commitments above would it fail to honour? Would the difference register in the day-to-day working of the constitution, or only in what people believe about it? And if only in belief, is the hereditary principle best defended as institutional engineering or as something closer to ritual — and should that distinction change how a democrat regards it?
