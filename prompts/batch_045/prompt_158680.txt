ok so last night me and Sam had a massive row at the pub about lying vs misleading and I'm still fuming about it. His position: misleading someone is *worse* than lying to them, because when you lie you at least stick your neck out — you go on record, you can be called out and held to it, and the person knows they've been deceived and by whom. When you mislead you get all the benefits of the lie with clean hands: you can always say you never actually said that, and the person you've misled is left with a false belief, no one to be angry at, and nothing they can point to. He called it deception on the cheap, and he had a load of examples ready, which annoyed me. I think he's onto something real but I think he's also conflating two things — how bad the act is, and how much grief you catch for it — and I couldn't get him to see the difference and bascially I dont think I can state it cleanly myself yet.

Anyway. I've got to submit a two-page proposal by Friday for a nine-month funded MPhil slot (12,000 words, philosophy dept), and I want to write the thesis on exactly this, so help me turn the row into something fundable.

The bit I keep coming back to, and my half-formed idea: everyone runs this debate on cases where the deceiver *says* something — literally true sentences that implicate something false, loaded emphasis, omission under a direct question, that sort of thing. But most of Sam's examples weren't like that at all. They were things like cropping someone out of a photo before you post it, quietly deleting the message that would have corrected a wrong assumption, leaving a document face-up on a desk, not replying to an email you know is being read as agreement. No assertion, no implicature, sometimes no speech at all. I want to call these environmental or artefactual deceptions — you don't represent anything to anyone, you arrange the world so that the other person's own reasoning does the work. My hunch is that this is where the lying/misleading distinction either does its real work or falls apart completely, and that the standard arguments haven't been stress-tested on it. Two things in particular:

- the move that lying is worse because it involves a distinctive commitment — you warrant the truth of what you say, you invite a kind of reliance that the mere misleader doesn't — looks like it has nothing to say about cases with no speech act at all. But if cropping the photo is just as bad as the lie, that suggests the commitment story was never doing the work in the first place.
- Sam's move, that misleading is worse because it exploits cooperation while dodging the costs, seems to get *stronger* in these cases, which makes me suspicious it's really a claim about blameworthiness and criticisability dressed up as a claim about wrongness. I want the thesis to force those apart.

Just lay out the two or three moves people standardly make — the commitment/warrant one, the I never said that one, the you should have asked one, whatever you think is actually load-bearing — and say exactly where each one runs out on artefactual cases.
