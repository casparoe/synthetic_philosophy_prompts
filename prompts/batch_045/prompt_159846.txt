I work in ML infra rather than research, but I've been going to a reading group that mixes alignment papers with more philosophy-flavoured stuff, and last week one of the grad students (very confident, does RL) gave a kind of "let's cut through the hype" summary of the whole field that I've been turning over since. Roughly, his line was: alignment is an ordinary engineering control problem wearing a philosophical costume. There's really only one problem — you write down an objective, the system optimizes it harder than you anticipated, and it finds the gap between the objective and what you meant (reward hacking, Goodhart's law, the paperclip thing, pick your favourite), so the fix is the fix you'd use anywhere else: iterate on the specification until the gaps close. On his telling, what people call inner alignment or deceptive alignment isn't a seperate problem at all, just outer misspecification that hasn't been caught yet — if a model schemes against you, it's because scheming scored well on the objective you actually gave it, so fix the objective and you've fixed the scheming. Interpretability he waved off as debugging for the same process. And he said the philosophical side of it — that human values are inconsistent, or underdetermined, or that we can't say what we want — is a distraction, because nobody building anything needs a complete theory of value; you just need to be able to say no to the outcomes everyone already agrees are bad, which is a judgement call engineers make all the time.

My instinct is that this is too tidy, but I can't locate exactly where, and the bits I'd want to push on are the ones I understand least. The thing I keep coming back to is something I half made up on the walk home: maybe alignment isn't a property a system has or lacks at all, but something more like a constitution — a deliberately underdetermined text, interpreted and amended over time by people and institutions who didn't write it, where the interesting question is who gets to interpret and what happens when they disagree. I don't mean the industry thing where you train a model against a written list of principles; I mean the framing itself. I like it, but I also suspect it's cheating, because the authors and subjects of a constitution are the same kind of thing and an AI system isn't, and it may just be smuggling governance questions in under alignment's name.

So: is his summary fair? Could you go at it more or less claim by claim — what someone who actually works on this would grant, what they'd call a fair compression of a messy literature, and what they'd say is just wrong — and separately, does his conclusion (one problem, one method, iterate on the spec) survive once the wrong bits are corrected? Two things I'd like taken seriously rather than waved away: his collapse of inner into outer alignment, and his "what we want". My girlfriend does political theory and kept insisting after the meeting that the moment you have more than one principal the whole framing changes shape, and I couldn't tell whether that was deep or a non-sequitur. And tell me what you make of my constitution thing: if anything like it already exists, what's the nearest version and how does mine differ; if it doesn't, where does it break?
