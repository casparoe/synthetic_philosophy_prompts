I'm retired — thirty-odd years in field ecology, mostly waders and saltmarsh — and about eighteen months ago I started sitting in on the decision-theory reading group the philosophy people run on Tuesday evenings. Its the first philosophy I've met that has knobs on it I recognise. We did the absent-minded driver last week and it's been rattling round my head ever since, so I want to close our last session of term with a poem instead of a summary. My setting: a mountain road before dawn, someone driving down after an all-night shift at an observatory, the same two turnings, the second one is his, he's driven it four hundred times and his mind is somewhere else entirely. The form I want is a pantoum — Malay, quatrains, and the rule is that lines 2 and 4 of each stanza come back as lines 1 and 3 of the next one, so the poem keeps meeting lines it has already met without telling you whether it has moved on; five or six stanzas, and the last line of the poem should be the first line again.

What I want the form to do is the work of the problem, not decorate it: the repeated lines should be the turnings themselves, so that by the middle of the poem the reader has lost count of how many have gone past, and the closing return should land him somewhere definite — either in his own yard or out on the highway with both turnings behind him — and mean something different the second time. As I make the problem out: 0 for taking the first turning, 4 for the second, 1 for sailing past both; as a plan, going straight with probability two thirds is optimal and worth four-thirds in expectation; adopt that plan and, standing at a turning, you should reckon a 3/5 chance you're at the first; but standing there going on looks better than turning (if you assume you'll take the next one you get something like 2.8 against 1.6) and that is exactly the thought that unravels you down to a payoff of 1. Correct me if I've bungled any of that and use the right numbers, but don't put them in the poem — I'd rather no "two thirds" or "four-thirds" appear in it at all, and let the arithmetic sit in the structure. Two other things: don't make him a joke or a cautionary tale about paying attention (he isn't lost, he knows the road perfectly; what he doesn't know is where on it he is), and don't resolve the fight between the person planning the drive and the person standing at the turning — a couple of the people coming on Tuesday are partners who haven't read any of this, so it has to work as a poem first.

Under the poem, a couple of hundred words at most: which way you've written it — from the planner's end or the driver-at-the-turning's end — where you cheated on the form or the philosophy, and what would make you write a different poem, meaning which argument or consideration would push you to the other perspective and where your reading of the problem is most likely to be wrong. And if you think there's no real tension here at all, that one of the two perspectives is just a confusion and the other is simply correct, say so plainly in the note and then write the poem from that side anyway.
