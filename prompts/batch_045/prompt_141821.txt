I'm putting together the closing two minutes of our school's intercultural evening, and instead of a speech I'd like to read a pantoum. The situation I want it to work through is one I'm stuck in: my closest friend assumes we're rooming together next year, and I've already put in an application to room with someone else. I keep turning over whether saying that plainly is honesty or just a way of handing her the whole weight of it at once, and whether wrapping it up is kindness or just a way of making sure the message is something she has to finish by herself.

Could you write it as a pantoum of five quatrains in which the repeated lines come back changed only slightly — a hedge, a softener, an altered punctuation mark, one extra word — so that each return keeps the same sentence while shifting what the sentence does: the first time it lands as a plain statement of fact, and by its last return it lands as something the hearer has to interpret. The final line should be the poem's first line coming back, and it should land differently the second time.

Two things I'd rather the poem earn than name-check. The first is that politeness doesn't change what is said so much as it changes who does the work: bluntness spends the speaker's discomfort up front and delivers something already finished, while indirectness hands the hearer half the job and leaves it unsettled whether the message ever arrived at all. The second is that I don't want the poem to settle whether the softened version is a lie or the plain version is a cruelty — I'd like it to close on the question of whether she was actually told anything. It should read aloud in about a minute, and it shouldn't sound like a greeting card or a slam poem; if someone in the audience has genuinely argued about whether white lies are lies, I'd like them to feel the poem knows the argument rather than just gestures at it.
