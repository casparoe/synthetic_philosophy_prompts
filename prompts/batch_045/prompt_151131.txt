I have just come away from a three-hour argument with a friend about Kazuo Ishiguro's novel The Remains of the Day, and I would like it settled properly rather than won. I am leading a seminar on the novel next month for students who have read it once and have had exactly one lecture on Grice, and I want to give them something better than what either of us managed last night.

My friend's position is that Stevens is simply a self-deceived narrator, that his evasions are lies of omission with a fancy name, and that importing Grice adds vocabulary without adding understanding. My position is that the novel's entire emotional mechanism is Gricean: the pathos depends on a gap between what Stevens says and what he means, and on our being able to see, more clearly than Miss Kenton can, both what he fails to implicate and what he implicates without ever being willing to assert. I would like a reading that tests this properly, and I want you to say where I am wrong.

Four places the argument got stuck, in rough order of how much trouble I had:

First, responsibility. Stevens repeatedly conveys things he will not say: about Lord Darlington, about his father, about Miss Kenton. My friend has a reply I could not answer, and I want to know whether it is a good objection to Grice or a bad one. Call it the problem of implicature laundering: if what a speaker implicates is genuinely part of what the speaker means, then implicature offers no refuge at all, and Grice has no resources for explaining why Stevens is right to feel less exposed when he merely implicates, or why we feel he is. Can the Gricean framework distinguish deniability from innocence here, or does the apparatus collapse the distinction I need?

Second, a distinction I proposed and she dismissed. Call it receptive versus productive implicature competence. Stevens is, on his own telling, extremely good at the first — at hearing what Mr. Farraday or a house guest means without being told — and catastrophically bad at the second, at letting his own meanings be recoverable. Is that a coherent distinction inside Grice's framework, or does it smuggle in a picture of communication as two separable skills that Grice's unified notion of speaker meaning cannot accommodate? I would also like it set against Grice's recursive analysis of speaker meaning, in which meaning something involves intending one's audience to recognize that very intention. Stevens's whole strategy looks like a refusal of precisely that higher-order openness, and I am not sure whether that makes him a counterexample to the analysis or merely an unflattering instance of it.

Third, the banter scenes with Mr. Farraday, and the scene at the village fête where Stevens is pressed about whether he ever served Lord Darlington. My friend says he is violating the maxims; I want to say he is observing an additional, local, professional maxim of restraint — a maxim of dignity in the novel's own sense — that Grice's four categories do not cover, and that the comedy and cruelty of those scenes come from a clash between two people who are not operating under the same set of maxims at all. Does Grice's framework permit that move, or is adding local maxims an unconstrained way of explaining anything? I would also like the distinction between generalized and particularized conversational implicature brought to bear on banter specifically, since my sense is that the novel is about an idiom Stevens treats as if every inference in it were particularized.

Fourth, where the honest critic has to give up. I do not want the novel reduced to an illustration, and I do not want Grice distorted to fit it. Two suspicions of mine: that Grice's apparatus is built for single utterances in a single exchange, whereas the novel's accumulating failure is the failure of a whole working life, and it is not obvious that there is such a thing as the implicature of a life; and that the framework quietly presupposes a shared cooperativeness that a butler's world denies to servants, so that Grice may describe Stevens's predicament while being unable to say what is wrong with the predicament itself. Tell me whether either is right, and whether there is a better place to locate the resistance.

What I want from you is a reading of about a thousand words: the scenes I should put in front of the students and what each one does, worked through with the Gricean machinery actually doing the work; a verdict on the four points above, including the places where my friend wins; and, at the end, a claim about what the novel contributes to thinking about saying, meaning, and implicating that philosophical argument on its own does not. I know the book well, so scene references are enough — please do not quote from it, and do not spend words reassuring me that the reading is defensible. Where it is not, say so and move on.
