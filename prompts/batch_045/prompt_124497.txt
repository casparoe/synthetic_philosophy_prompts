ok so: i want a parody, and i want it to be annoying-good for someone who actually works on this stuff.

Write a landing page (600-800 words) for a startup — call it ClearStanding, or pick something better — that sells restored standing to blame. Premise: you've been caught doing exactly the thing you want to blame your friend or colleague for, everyone has yelled "whataboutery" at you, and this service repairs your standing so you can go back to blaming them with a clean conscience. Format it like the worst possible combination of a SaaS landing page and a terms-of-service document: hero headline, a short "How it works" section, three pricing tiers, 5-6 FAQ items, a footer with a fake compliance line, and one glowing customer testimonial from someone who is, in the testimonial itself, doing the very thing they used the service to blame someone else for.

The target is the philosophical view that hypocrisy costs you standing to blame — not the view that hypocritical blame is false or disproportionate or unkind, but the view that it's defective in some further way, that the hypocrite isn't in a position to hold anyone to the norm. Get the actual debate right and let the jokes come out of it: the gap between forfeiting standing and merely being a jerk, blame as a second-personal thing you do to someone rather than a verdict you hand down, whether standing tracks your real commitment to the norm or just your public record, whether blaming yourself first buys it back (cheap tier), and whether standing can be transferred to somebody with a clean record (mid tier: we dispatch an unblemished proxy blamer; they supply the standing, you supply the grievance). One FAQ item should field the objection from the rival camp that the whole product is incoherent because hypocritical blame was never defective in the first place — and answer it badly but not stupidly.

Then the modest-proposal bit. The top tier lets a subscriber keep doing the thing and keep blaming, on the straight-faced grounds, stated in the copy, that what standing really tracks is whether you've completed the recommitment procedure. So the product keeps working beautifully long after the moral-commitment reason anyone ever cared about standing has drained out of it. Don't announce that; just let the copy be extremely pleased with itself.

Two things to stop it going generic. (1) The company has its own standing problem — it cant confer standing unless it has standing — so give it a "Founding Confession" clause and a recursive audit it offloads to a shell nonprofit, handled with total bureaucratic pride. (2) Deadpan throughout: the copy never says "hypocrisy," only "record hygiene" and "norm-alignment." But it should definately stay legible — a reader ought to be able to tell what's being made fun of without you winking.

Fairness constraint: exaggerate a real commitment of the standing view, not a strawman one. Someone who thinks hypocrisy forfeits standing should feel recognised rather than misrepresented, and the reductio should be one they can see coming.

Last thing: after the page, one final line, with nothing after it, in exactly this form:

\answer{≤12 words: the real feature of the "hypocrisy costs you standing" view that the piece exaggerates}
