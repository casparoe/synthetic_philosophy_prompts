I'm 71, retired — 35 years as a hydrologist, mostly measuring things in rivers and arguing about error bars, which is about the only philosophy I was ever trained in. Since retiring I've been sitting on the patient and family advisory committee at our local hospital, which is how I got into this. My older brother died last year with fairly advanced Alzheimer's. About fifteen years before that, while our mother was in a nursing home with the same disease, he'd filled out one of those advance directive forms — the tick-box ones about feeding tubes and ventilators and resuscitation — and he'd scrawled in the margin that if he ever ended up like Mum he didn't want to be kept going. Then the actual thing was messier than the form: by the time he was in a home himself he seemed, as far as anyone could tell, cheerful most of the time, he liked the garden and the music sessions, he'd forgotten he'd ever had a career, and when the chest infection came along that eventually killed him the staff were visibly uneasy about following the document, because the man in front of them was smiling and eating his lunch. We followed it anyway and I've been chewing on it ever since.

Here's the bit I can't line up. The hospital's rule — and I get the impression it's basically the same most places — is that a patient who is still competent can tear up their own directive on the spot and nobody argues; the person in the bed wins over the person who signed the paper. The moment the same person isn't competent any more, the paper wins over the person in the bed, even when the person in the bed is content. So the practice seems to flip which of the two gets to speak for him, and the thing that flips it is a capacity test, which as far as I can tell just asks whether he can currently take in information and weigh it up — nothing obviously to do with whether the man who signed the form twenty years ago is still, in the relevant sense, him. It also doesn't seem to be really about capacity: in a psychiatric crisis we seem much more willing to override what someone is currently and loudly demanding if they've signed something in advance saying so, or so I was told.

I've been reading a bit about this business of transformative experience — the idea that for some things you simply can't know what they're like from the outside, and that going through them changes what you want in a way you couldn't have predicted. On that view my brother at 60 had no real idea what he was turning down, and had never had it and couldn't imagine it. But the whole institution of these forms seems to assume the opposite: that you can know well enough from watching your mother and from imagination. So what does the practice actually presuppose? About who a person is across time, about whether the pre-dementia self or the demented self is the one whose verdict counts, about whether contentment you never anticipated is the same thing as the well-being you were trying to protect? I'd like to know what it strictly requires as opposed to what it merely fits well with: does the practice collapse if the "he couldn't have known" view is true, or could someone who held that view still sign one of these forms without contradicting themselves, and where exactly is the practice underdetermined, with several different theories all licensing the same forms? Is the flip at the capacity line genuinely inconsistent, or is there a reading on which the competent patient revoking his form and the demented patient's smiles are being handled on the same principle? And if you built the practice on the other assumption — that lived experience is the only real evidence about what a state is like — what would it look like: throw the forms out, make people write them after a spell in a memory unit, restrict them to people who've actually been through something similar? One last thing that struck me: when something came up the form didn't cover, I was told to decide what he would have wanted, not what was best for him now, and those struck me as two completely different instructions with two different sets of assumptions behind them. Maybe that's really what I'm asking about.
