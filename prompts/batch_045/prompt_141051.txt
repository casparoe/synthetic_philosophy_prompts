Hey — imagine you're a decision theorist who's spent way too much of your life on predictor cases and has heard every version of Newcomb's problem, and please actually think it through rather than pattern-matching: brainstorm four or five candidate diagnoses first, then commit to one and tell me which you'd defend out loud in a seminar. No citations or reading suggestions, I genuinely don't care who said what first, and please don't leave me with "it depends" or "both theories have their proponents."

My decision theory reading group spent about two hours on this last night and got precisely nowhere, and I've rewritten my notes three times, so here it is. Take the case people call Death in Damascus, but with numbers pinned down. Death is already in one of two cities — Damascus, where you are, or Aleppo, which is a hard journey away costing you 10 units of utility. He got there by predicting yesterday which city you'd end up in, and he is 90% reliable, and we stipulated that this reliability holds however his target chooses: 90% of the people he predicts wake up in his city, whichever way they jump. At dawn, same city as Death means 0, different city means 100. His prediction is already sealed on a tablet, so nothing you do today causally changes where he is; it only changes where you are. My group then added a wrinkle: there's a third town, Homs, 15 units away, and Death's predictor is an old two-output model — a trustworthy demon has confirmed that Death is in Damascus or Aleppo and never Homs. Here's the argument we ended up with:

(1) What you should do depends only on what your action causally does. Your choice today can't affect Death's city — that's settled on the tablet — only your own. So you evaluate your options holding your probabilities about Death's location fixed.
(2) Call p your probability that Death is in Damascus. Then staying is worth 100(1−p), fleeing is worth 100p − 10, so flee just in case p is above 0.55.
(3) But the case never hands you p. All you know about Death's location is that he predicted you and is right 90% of the time. If you're confident you'll stay, p = 0.9 and fleeing looks great (80 against 10). If you're confident you'll flee, p = 0.1 and staying looks great (90 against 0). Whatever you settle on, it undermines itself. And note that on either assumption you're 90% likely to die anyway, so this is a case you cannot win.
(4) A theory of rational choice that can't produce stable advice in a case where you have to do something isn't doing its job. Evidential decision theory does give stable advice here — stay — and by the only measure left, the one that says don't burn 10 units on a trip that buys you nothing, it does better. So the causalist core in (1) is wrong in predictor cases, and the real lesson is that you should manage the news.
(5) That generalizes: whenever your action is a symptom of some fixed state you can't causally touch, pick the act whose news is best.
(6) Now add Homs. Both theories tell you to go there: Death can't be, you survive for sure, net 85. But there is always some act a predictor's model didn't cover — stay in Damascus but sleep in a cellar, take an odd route to Aleppo, whatever — and by (5)'s logic you should go hunting for it. Either the 0.9 figure is held to cover those fine-grained acts too, in which case (3)'s instability comes straight back and there's still no advice, or it isn't, in which case rationality is just the trick of finding a description of your options that your predictor didn't model.
(7) So no decision theory can be both action-guiding and stable: in any case where anything predicts you, either rationality is impossible or it consists of gaming how finely you carve up your options.

The conclusion is absurd and the kicker is worse — my friend pointed out that everything predicts us a little, my doctor and the ad algorithms and my partner, so on (5) I ought to be making myself harder to model all the time, up to and including paying to have my own judgement degraded before any prediction gets made. I don't believe any of that, but I can't locate the mistake.

So: which premise or step do you give up? Explain why the step you're rejecting looked fine when we wrote it down, and price the alternatives for me — if I have to choose, I'd rather keep (1) and (2) than swallow (4), I really don't want to end up an evidential decision theorist, and I want to hold on to there being a difference between changing the world and changing the news. Say too what each of the usual positions ends up paying here: the causal view, the evidential view, the "what gets predicted is your decision procedure, not your act" style of view, and the move that says these cases are somehow impossible or incoherent. One guy in the group kept saying this is just Newcomb's problem with camels and we should all go home, which I also don't think is right, so tell me if he's onto something.
