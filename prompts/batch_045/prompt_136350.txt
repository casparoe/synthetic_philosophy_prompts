i'm teaching a first-year seminar on meaning and messages, and we've spent the last two weeks on Hofstadter's three-way split in GEB — frame message, outer message, inner message (i think it's the chapter on the location of meaning, but don't hold me to it). The version I keep pushing on them: the grooves are the frame, the sound you get if you can build the right player is the outer, and the inner is what only arrives for a receiver who is already inside the right culture — and the point is that no amount of staring at the grooves settles the inner message, because its not in there.

We made up our own case to fight about, which the students have named the Turin cards: a cache of loom punch cards pulled out of a collapsed mill archive. Everyone agrees about the holes (frame). Once you know what a loom of that kind does with a card, everyone agrees about the cloth it would weave (outer): a repeating zigzag inside a border of small crosses. The argument is entirely about the inner message. Half the room thinks it's a weaver's signature, or a greeting left for whoever came next; the other half thinks it's a test weave for calibrating the loom, and means nothing at all. Nothing about the cards decides it, and I don't want the poem to decide it either.

Last session is Friday. I want to project a poem and have it read aloud. The room will be the eight students, two or three people from the CS side who wandered in half way through the term and never read the book, and our department administrator, who comes to the last session of everything. So the surface has to work for someone who has no idea any of this is going on; everything else can be a reward for the people who were there.

What I want is a poem of, say, twenty to thirty lines that is itself a three-layer artifact:

- On the page it just reads as a poem — presumably about the cards, the weave, the archive, the mill — no jargon, no name-dropping of Hofstadter, nothing that needs the seminar explained.
- The first letters of the lines, read straight down, spell a plain sentence that a reader who notices it can recover without knowing anything about the seminar. That sentence should be about the cards, but it must not tell you which side of the argument to take.
- What the recovered sentence means, though, should depend on having sat in the room for the fight. And ideally the poem ends by handing that back — by making the point that the thing you can decode isn't the thing that means something — rather than by settling the dispute.

Two things I'd rather you think about than smooth over. First: I keep going back and forth on whether an acrostic in English cheats. Hofstadter's outer message is supposed to be recoverable by just any receiver clever enough to build the player, and a being that doesn't read English can't read my acrostic, so maybe all I've built is an outer message for English-readers and the demonstration is circular. If you think that's a real problem and not a quibble, do it anyway, but add a couple of lines after the poem saying what you'd have done instead. Second, if there's something you can do with the sound of the thing — rhyme or meter carrying the frame/outer work the way the visible letters do — I'd take it, but don't force it.

No "ghost in the machine" lines, nothing about the universe reading itself, and please don't let it turn into a puzzle box where the cleverness is the point.
