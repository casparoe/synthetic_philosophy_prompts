I'm in a small reading group on formal epistemology, and we spent most of a session stuck on the Judy Benjamin problem — the case of the soldier parachuted into a territory divided into two company areas (First and Second) and, independently, two platoon areas (First and Second). Call the four locations C1P1, C1P2, C2P1, C2P2; she starts uniform at 1/4 each. A message reaches her: if you are in the First Company area, then you are in the First Platoon area. Two people in the room left convinced of opposite things, and I'm the one who still doesn't have the conceptual map, though I can do the arithmetic. I've also seen the case described somewhere as a problem for information-minimizing update rules, which we haven't read, so I'd rather not have the answer routed through machinery I'd have to take on trust.

Here is where I got to. If I treat the message as the material conditional and conditionalize, I rule out C1P2 and spread its mass proportionally over the remaining three cells, so Judy's confidence that she is in the First Company area drops from 1/2 to 1/3. That feels wrong: the message looks like news about which platoon she is in, conditional on the company — it should push P(P1 | C1) to 1 and leave the company marginal where it was. But two things then made me doubt that the intuition supports a rival updating rule. First: suppose the messenger had a fixed menu of exactly the two conditionals sharing the antecedent "First Company" — "if C1 then P1" and "if C1 then P2" — and picked uniformly at random among the ones true at Judy's actual location. Then, if I've done it right, conditionalizing on the *report* rather than on its content leaves P(C1) at exactly 1/2 and gives P(C1P1) = 1/2, which is just the intuitive answer; whereas if I enlarge the menu to all four conditionals of that form, the random messenger instead reproduces the material-conditional answer, P(C1) = 1/3. Second: if I model each message instead as a constraint on a conditional probability — hold the probability of each company-cell fixed and redistribute inside it — then with a second message the result depends on the order it arrives in. Taking the second message to be "if you are in the Second Platoon area, then you are in the First Company area" (modeled symmetrically, holding the platoon marginal fixed and redistributing within it), I get (C1P1, C1P2, C2P1, C2P2) = (0.5, 0.25, 0.25, 0) in one order and (0.75, 0, 0.25, 0) in the other, while taking both messages as a single package and conditionalizing on their conjunction gives (0.5, 0, 0.5, 0).

So please explain the underlying issue, and correct my numbers if they're wrong. What is it about evidence whose content is a conditional that makes it underdetermine the update, in a way that a message saying "you are not in C1P2" would not? Is the whole puzzle only a selection-rule effect of the Monty Hall kind, so that once the messenger's rule is fixed there is nothing special left to say about conditionals — or does something survive, a genuine three-way distinction between learning that P → Q, learning that someone reported P → Q, and coming under a constraint on P(Q | P)? If holding a partition's probabilities fixed is a real rival rather than a misdescription, why shouldn't its order-dependence count against it? And if the selection-rule story is the right one, what does it predict about which menu Judy should assume when she has no information at all about how the message was produced? I'd like the gut-level reason why conditionals are different, and then a clean statement of which account you favour and what it costs you.
