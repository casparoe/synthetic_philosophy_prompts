my flatmate and I had a four-hour argument about pain last night and I walked away less sure of my own view than when we started, so I want someone to audit the package rather than tell me I'm right. Roughly where I've landed:

1. What makes a pain bad for the person having it is exactly one thing: how unpleasant it feels. Not what it tells you about tissue damage, not that it wrecks your plans, not what you believe about it. Those things can be bad too, but they're additional bads, not part of the pain's badness.

2. Pain asymbolia (the thing where people still get the full sensory side — burning, stabbing, located in the left hand, intensity 8 out of 10 — but say it doesn't bother them, and sometimes react with the wrong facial expression entirely) I read as a genuine dissociation: the sensation and its localisation are intact, the unpleasantness is gone. So nothing bad is happening to them. I think it's condescending to insist they're secretly suffering and just describing it oddly. I also think the pain can still be *useful* to them — it still steers them away from injury — while not being bad for them.

3. The reason I think 2 is coherent is a theory of what unpleasantness is: for an experience to be unpleasant just is for it to occupy the aversion role — the thing that makes you withdraw, flinch, want it to stop, and learn to avoid the situation next time. The asymbolic has no unpleasantness precisely because nothing is repelling them.

4. First-person report is authoritative about whether something feels bad. If someone sincerely and stably says "it doesn't feel bad to me," then it doesn't feel bad to them, and there's no further fact of the matter that could overrule that.

5. Badness of pain adds up over time: how bad a stretch of pain is for you is just the total of the momentary unpleasantness. Six months of moderate back pain can be worse for you than twenty minutes of agony, and that's a factual question about how much unpleasantness there was in total — not about anyone's attitudes, choices, or how they'd rank the episodes from the outside.

6. A case verdict, from a made-up condition my flatmate and I invented to test this, call it inverted asymbolia: someone who reports zero unpleasantness from the stimulus, insistently and convincingly, but whose whole avoidance system is going off like a person in severe pain — flinching, sweating, will pay a lot of money to skip the test, never misses a follow-up appointment, never puts their hand back in the apparatus. I said: nothing bad is happening to them either, same as the ordinary asymbolic, and it would be treating them like a machine to dope them up just because their nervous system is carrying on. My flatmate said I can't have that given 3, and that my 4 is doing all the work and 3 is decoration.

7. One more, which I don't think is in tension with 1 at all but my flatmate did: I think a pain can be bad for you even when you're completely fine with it happening, and might even choose it again — the climber who's glad to be in agony on the summit, or someone who's glad the injury hurt because it proved the surgery was needed. Being glad isn't the same as it not hurting.

So: is this a consistent set? Go through the pairs and rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, tell me for each what exactly would have to give and what the cheapest fix would cost me, and tell me if any of the apparent clashes (I'm looking at 1 and 7) is actually fake. Don't tell me which philosopher I'm accidentally agreeing with — I don't care, and I can't look anything up right now.
