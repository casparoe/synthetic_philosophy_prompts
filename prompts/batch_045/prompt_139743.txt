Hi — slightly odd request, and it's got personal recently so bear with me.

My brother and I have spent three years and somewhere around £30k of our own money restoring our late grandfather's sailing boat. I dont really sail; neither does he. Two months ago a surveyor told us the keel bolts are shot, it needs another £15k minimum, and there's no guarantee it'll ever be seaworthy. My brother wants to carry on. I want out. And every time I try to say I'm done, what actually comes out of my mouth is: but we've already put so much in. I know that sentence is nonsense and I say it anyway. My wife's suggestion that we just sell it as-is for maybe 3k and stop makes me want to keep going even more, which feels definately backwards and I'd like to know whether it actually is.

Here's the ask. I run a volunteer evening class in a village hall — Decisions and Arguments, fortnightly, open to anyone, 15 signed up at the moment, ages from about 19 to 70, almost none of them with any philosophy or economics. I've got a 90-minute slot and I want to spend it on escalation: why people keep pouring resources into a course of action that's plainly failing. The hall comittee would have a fit about real money changing hands, so it has to be points or play money or something, and three or four of the group have mobility problems, so nothing can require everyone to be physically active.

The bit I can't find anywhere: everyone runs the classic dollar auction — highest bidder pays their bid and takes the prize, runner-up pays their bid and gets nothing — and then announces that this shows irrational escalation. But I think that demo mashes together about three different things and I want them separated:

1. The trap itself. Once you've bid, stopping guarantees you a loss and continuing at least might avoid one, so carrying on is locally sensible even though everyone can see where it ends.
2. The already-spent feeling. The pull of not wanting what's already gone to have been for nothing. This is the one I actually recognise from the boat.
3. Face. Not wanting to be the person who blinked in front of a room full of people.

Can you design me a 90-minute session where people run into all three separately instead of all at once? My guess is each one needs its own rule set — maybe a round where bids are paid immediately and irrecoverably whether you win or not, in some currency that actually costs people something real (time, effort, dignity, whatever you think works, plus a version for anyone who can't do the physical one), so that the it's-already-gone structure is right there in their body rather than on a whiteboard; and maybe a round where the identical game is played once with bids written down privately and once announced out loud, to see how much the audience is doing. But you'll have better ideas than me and I'd rather have yours.

Two more things I want it to get at, which I don't think the standard auction models at all:

- Whether softening the exit helps or hurts. Does giving people a way out that recovers some of what they've spent make them escalate less, or does it just hand them a reason to feel reasonable while carrying on? Is there a version of the game where you can actually see the difference, rather than just asserting it?
- Whether a rival is even needed. In the auction there's always someone bidding you up. With the boat there isn't — my brother and I aren't competing against anyone, we're just continuing. Is that the same phenomenon at all, or is the auction a misleading model for the cases people actually care about? If it's misleading, I'd rather the session ended somewhere honest about that than with everyone feeling clever about having spotted a fallacy.

What I need is something I can actually run: the rules for each round, what to bring, a payoff scheme with no real money, a rough minute-by-minute for the 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the room onto the three things above. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I can already see one person bidding £50 for a £20 note purely to win, and two people quietly agreeing to bid a penny and split it — and how to handle each. And be straight about which part of this the games can't deliver and I'll just have to say out loud myself.

Please write in British English, the class is here and I'd only have to translate it otherwise.
