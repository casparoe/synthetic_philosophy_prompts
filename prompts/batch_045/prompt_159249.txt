Last night I spent a couple of hours arguing with a friend who takes a hard line: that anyone with nothing to hide has nothing to fear, that every genuine privacy complaint is in the end a complaint about harm or about someone acquiring power over you, and that talk of a right to privacy is only shorthand for claims about property, bodies and security. I could not say what exactly is wrong with that, and I realised I do not know which account of privacy I myself am committed to, since I only know the disagreements at the level of labels. So I want something that sorts people by what they say about cases.

I am putting together a short self-assessment exercise for a reading group of about twelve people, half academic philosophers and half privacy engineers where I work; the working title is Which Theory of Privacy Are You Actually Committed To. Please build it as a decision tree of seven or eight branch nodes, each phrased as a question a reader can answer without knowing which position it leads to, with a named leaf at the end of each path. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: begin with the claims that actually separate the views — whether privacy is a matter of the access others have, of the subject's control, of the appropriateness of an information flow against the norms of a context, or of nothing over and above property-, bodily- and security-based interests; whether an infringement requires that anyone be worse off, notice, or be identifiable; whether the subject of privacy can be a group and not just an individual; whether privacy's value is purely instrumental or can also be impersonal — then locate the real positions inside the space those answers generate, and say of any combination nobody occupies whether it is empty because incoherent, because unattractive, or merely because unexplored.

Four cases should do the work at the branch nodes: a houseguest who photographs the entirely banal pages of your diary while you sleep, deletes the images and never mentions it; a pharmacy whose fully anonymised, never-human-read sales data is used to infer that residents of one building have an unusually high rate of a stigmatised prescription; a second-hand hard drive bought in good faith that turns out to contain a stranger's medical scans and letters, which the buyer then opens; and an employer's break-room camera that records to a buffer overwritten every sixty seconds and is never viewed by anyone, but which everyone knows about and which stops the gossip. No branch question should be phrased in the vocabulary of the theories — nothing like is privacy really just control over information. Leaves must include the control account, the access-based account, the reductionist account, the contextual-integrity-style account, and at least one combination you mark unnamed; flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate.

Add a short note on the tree itself: how confident you are in each leaf's placement, which of your readings of the cases you would expect a defender of that leaf to contest, and, singled out, the one branch node you are least confident really does the separating work you have assigned to it.
