A colleague sent me a transcript of a popular-science podcast in which the host argues that "your reasons don't do anything," and I would like to use the passage as a foil in a graduate seminar on mental causation — provided I can first separate what is right in it from what is not. The relevant stretch runs roughly like this:

"Physics is causally complete: every physical event has a physical cause that fully accounts for it, so nothing non-physical is doing any work. When you accept a job offer, what causes the acceptance is a pattern of neural firing; the fact that you wanted the salary and believed the move was worth it does nothing at all. That is the exclusion argument. If the physical cause already suffices, a mental cause would only be a second, redundant cause of the same event, and nature does not permit that kind of systematic overdetermination. So mental properties are not causes — at best they are ways of describing what the brain is doing. Reasons cause nothing, and consciousness is along for the ride. That leaves two positions: eliminate the mental, or accept dualism and give up physics. And in fact the argument refutes itself, because it generalizes: run the same reasoning on a window and it says the window shattered because of the arrangement of its molecules rather than because a rock struck it, or that a population declined because of molecular events rather than because of habitat loss. Higher-level causes are plainly real, so the exclusion premise has to go."

Before I hand this to students I want an accuracy audit, sentence by sentence: what would a non-reductive physicalist grant, what would they say begs the question, and what would they call a straightforward error? Three things in particular worry me. First, I suspect the host has misdescribed his own target: non-reductive physicalists do not postulate anything "non-physical," so the completeness premise is not where the disagreement lies; and I suspect he is running an argument about properties together with an argument about events, which matters because token-identity is available to the non-reductivist. Second, does the slide from "the mental property is excluded" to "reasons cause nothing and consciousness is along for the ride" follow, or does it need a further premise about consciousness that the argument never supplies? Third, is "eliminativism or dualism" really the pair of options left once the premises are granted — what happened to reductionism, and to replies that keep the mental causally relevant without overdetermination, such as causal inheritance or proportionality and difference-making accounts? Finally, is the closing generalization move — the rock and the habitat loss — the refutation he takes it to be, or is there a developed response that lets exclusion stand against the mental while sparing ordinary special-science causes? And is that last move a distinct objection, or just the generalization problem in different clothes?
