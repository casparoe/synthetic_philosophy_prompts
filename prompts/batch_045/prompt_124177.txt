I teach a first-year seminar on uses of the past, and Thursday is the last session of term. We've spent the last two weeks on why analogies from history mislead — Munich, Vietnam, and that thing everyone now calls the Thucydides trap — and I want to close by reading a poem instead of summarising; last year I did Yeats and it landed with a thud, because they had no idea what he was on about. So: a sestina. Six six-line stanzas and a three-line tornada, readable aloud in about three minutes. I'd like the form to be doing the argument rather than decorating it, and I dont want a single footnote or wink at the reader.

You pick the six end-words, but with constraints: at least two of them should be place-names we actually use as analogy-cases (Munich is fair game, so are Athens and Sparta and a Vietnamese place-name), at least two should be abstract nouns, and I have a weakness for "again" as one of them. Choose words that survive being moved around grammatically — nothing that only makes sense in one slot. Then exploit the two things the form does on its own. First, the last end-word of one stanza becomes the first end-word of the next: the conclusion of one case is the opening premise of the next one, which is exactly how this reasoning actually runs. Second, after six stanzas the sequence lands back in its original order — all that churn and nothing has been learned, the lesson has just been recycled. I want both of those felt, not pointed at.

What I want the poem to get right, in no particular order, is this. That the Munich analogy only bites if you've already built into the description of the case the claim that the other side's appetite was bottomless, which is precisely what wasn't known in 1938 — so what the analogy really does is launder uncertainty into risk, borrowing a probability from a case that isn't yours, and nobody ever audits the number. That its real work is dialectical rather than epistemic: it doesn't raise the probability of anything, it just moves the burden of proof, as in, explain to me why this isn't Munich. That Vietnam has licensed flatly opposite policies — stay out entirely, versus next time go in with enough to win — and both camps claim the same war. And the asymmetry I keep coming back to: a lesson about your own past restraint can be self-undoing, since acting on it early means the disaster never happens and everyone then says you cried wolf, whereas a structural lesson like the trap can be self-confirming, since treating the rising power as a mortal rival manufactures the very fear that the story says makes the war unavoidable — which is also why you can't just test these lessons against the record, because the record has already been contaminated by people acting on them. Oh, and that the pool of lessons is drawn entirely from regret: we name them after the losses, and there is no Munich for the crises that never happened. The speaker should be someone who has to decide something, in a present-day crisis you never name and whose outcome you never reveal — the point is that the reasoning runs the same way whatever it is. Then the tornada is where the poem notices the cycle has closed and either breaks it or admits it can't; I'd honestly rather it fail honestly than succeed cheaply. My colleague in political science said at a faculty reception last week that everyone knows the lesson of Munich, and I nearly dropped my drink, so ideally he'd recognise the arguments if he wandered in, while a nineteen-year-old with no IR background can still follow it. Leave it open — these kids are going to be the ones reaching for the analogy in twenty years, and I definately don't want to hand them a bumper sticker in either direction.
