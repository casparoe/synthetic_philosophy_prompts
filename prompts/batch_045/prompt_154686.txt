I've been falling down a rabbit hole about how much work the presumption of independence actually does in heuristic arguments in number theory, and I want to put numbers on it instead of just shrugging and saying "heuristics are heuristics".

Setup, so were on the same page. The naive move is Cramér-style: pretend the event "n is prime" is a coin flip with probability 1/log n, pretend the flips for different n are independent, read off the answer. Obviously they aren't independent — n and n+2 can't both be even, knowing n survives sieving by 3 shifts the odds for n+2, small primes do nearly all the damage. So the real move is: strip out the congruence obstructions from small primes, sweep the leftover bias into a product of local factors, and then presume independence of whatever remains. Call that the formalised version.

Heres what I want. Rate these five cases, one at a time:

- a score from 0 to 100 for how much confidence you'd put in the actual asymptotic or count the heuristic spits out (100 = as confident as you are about the prime number theorem, 0 = you'd bet nothing on it),
- one sentence on what exactly is being presumed independent, and whether that presumption has been converted into something you could in principle check — equidistribution in arithmetic progressions, a Mertens-type product, whatever — or whether its really just "these events look unrelated",
- one sentence on how much of the gap between the naive coin-flip answer and the refined answer is actually closed by the formalisation, i.e. how much unexplained dependence is still sitting there.

Then put all five into tiers by how much you trust them overall, and tell me which case you think independence is doing the most work in and which the least, and why. I specifically want to know if my sense is right that the Mersenne one is the sneaky case — where the naive version isn't off by a constant but by a whole order of growth — and that the digit-reversal case is the one the standard sieve machinery can't even see. And do tell me if Ive got any of the constants wrong, some of these I worked out on the back of an envelope.

The cases:

1. Twin primes. Naive: x/log²x. Hardy–Littlewood: 2C₂ x/log²x with C₂ ≈ 0.6601, the 1.32 factor coming from the product over odd primes of p(p−2)/(p−1)², i.e. from the fact that surviving the sieve at 3 (or 5, or 7) for n makes it likelier that n+2 survives too. Dependence is real, but it looks entirely captured by local congruence factors.

2. Mersenne primes 2^p − 1. Naive: probability ≈ 1/log(2^p − 1) ≈ 1/(p log 2), summed over prime p ≤ x, so the count up to exponent x grows like log log x. Refined: every prime divisor q of 2^p − 1 must satisfy q ≡ 1 mod 2p (and q ≡ ±1 mod 8), so a Mersenne number has far fewer candidate small divisors than a random number of its size. The corrected probability picks up a factor of roughly e^γ log(2p), γ the Euler–Mascheroni constant, which turns the expected count into about e^γ log₂ x — a log, not a log log. Sanity check: 52 known Mersenne primes, largest exponent around 1.36 × 10^8, and e^γ log₂(1.36 × 10^8) ≈ 48.

3. Fermat primes F_n = 2^(2^n) + 1. Same style of restriction on divisors: q ≡ 1 mod 2^(n+2). So by the same reasoning the probability that F_n is prime is inflated by about e^γ log(2^(n+2)), giving roughly 1.78(n+2)/2^n instead of the naive 1.44/2^n. Summed from n = 5 upward I get about 0.9 expected further Fermat primes, versus about 0.2 naive — so the formalisation changes "another Fermat prime would be a mild surprise" into "its close to a coin flip". Also: the Fermat numbers are pairwise coprime, which feels like it should matter somehow but Im not sure it does.

4. Prime pairs by digit reversal (my own toy, so treat it as new): count primes p ≤ 10^k whose decimal reversal is a different prime. Two complications I noticed. First, reversal preserves the digit sum, so p ≡ reverse(p) mod 9; if 3 doesn't divide p then 3 automatically doesn't divide the reversal either, so the mod-3 sieve condition is bought once instead of twice — a positive correlation worth a factor of about 3/2. Second, for the reversal to be an odd prime > 5, the leading digit of p has to be one of 1, 3, 7, 9 — that's a condition on the leading digit, not on a residue class, so no local-factor machinery covers it. I think the leading digits of primes up to 10^k are asymptotically uniform (roughly 1/9 each) rather than Benford, because the top decade contains most of the primes and the density barely varies across it, which would make that factor about 4/9; but Im genuinely unsure and it matters for the constant.

5. Gaps between primes. Cramér's coin-flip model predicts the biggest gap below x is around log²x. But Maier's theorem shows the model is actually false at the scale of short intervals: the number of primes in intervals of length (log x)^A fluctuates more than independence predicts, for any fixed A. As far as I remember Granville's corrected guess raises the naive log²x by a factor of 2e^(−γ) ≈ 1.123, on the basis of exactly the kind of irregularity in primes in arithmetic progressions that Maier's matrix method exposes. So here we have a case where the presumption of independence isn't merely unformalised, it's known to fail, and the community keeps the leading-order answer anyway.

One more thing: if you think some of these five belong to genuinely different kinds of heuristic argument rather than five points on one scale, say so and group them how you like — but still give me the numbers.
