I have been trying, without success, to find a name for a failure mode of argument that I keep encountering in long-running public disputes of exactly the sort that end with each side concluding that the other is not arguing in good faith. I suspect the familiar accusation is not about what it appears to be about, and I want to know whether the thing I have in mind is already a known concept, and if not, how it should be defined.

Two instances. First: a colleague opposes a proposal in a committee meeting. She states the proposal more accurately than its authors did, volunteers the two concessions that are genuinely owed, commits no fallacy, and is plainly sincere — she believes what she says and would be embarrassed to be caught misdescribing the case against her. Yet when asked what would change her mind, she does not name a piece of evidence the discussion failed to produce; she gives the impression of not having granted the discussion any authority over her conclusion at all. Nothing that happens in that room, she more or less conveys, is the kind of thing that could settle this. Second: a participant in an eight-year-old online dispute about housing policy who knows the other side's best arguments better than most of that side does, links them, quotes them at length — and whose view has not moved. What he does with the other side's material is exhibit that it fails; he does not submit anything of his own to it.

What I think I am noticing has two parts, and I do not know whether they are one phenomenon or two. The first is a missing willingness to be bound by the outcome: there is no result the exchange could produce that the speaker has committed herself in advance to treating as settling. The second is that the argument is frequently not addressed to the interlocutor at all, but to onlookers, to a record, or to the speaker's own side. I would like to know whether the second is the mechanism of the first, a symptom of it, or something independent that merely tends to travel with it.

The neighbours I have tried do not fit. It is not insincerity: these people mean what they say. It is not self-deception in the Sartrean sense — they are not hiding anything from themselves. It is not the indifference to truth that Harry Frankfurt ascribes to the bullshitter, who does not care whether what he says is true; my arguer would be distressed by falsity, and may even have arrived at the view by careful attention to evidence. It is not quite dogmatism or closed-mindedness, since the same person may revise readily on things encountered outside the argument; it is specifically the exchange that has no purchase. It is not simply aiming at victory rather than truth, since my arguer may not be trying to defeat anyone — she may be discharging a role or maintaining a position. Costless signalling seems closer, but the defect is not that her words are cheap; it is that nothing has been placed under the authority of the outcome. The internet's vocabulary for related behaviour builds in an intent to harass or a covert allegiance, which I do not want to assume.

So: is there an established term, and if so which one fits best and where exactly does it come apart from what I have described? If there is none, please coin one and define it tightly enough to sort the following cases: a lawyer arguing a client's brief; a minister reading a departmental line; a devil's advocate; a teacher examining a student; a genuinely undecided person who is using the exchange to find out what she thinks and who has therefore staked nothing; someone who stakes a concession she never intended to defend; and someone who has staked something real but only privately, so that no one else can tell.

Finally, I want the epistemic question addressed rather than gestured at. The defining feature seems to be a counterfactual about what the arguer would accept under an outcome that did not occur, which makes the charge difficult to support and impossible to refute inside the dispute — and the accusation itself is made before an audience and stakes nothing, so it looks liable to be an instance of the very thing it names. What, in principle, could count as evidence for or against it, and is "they are not arguing in good faith" a claim that can be responsibly made?
