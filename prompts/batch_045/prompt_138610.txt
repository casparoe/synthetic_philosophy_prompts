I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on experimental philosophy and I am setting the final. I need one essay question on the Knobe effect that cannot be answered by rehearsing the chairman case and the finding that moral valence drives intentionality ascriptions. The crux I want tested is whether students can say what would count as evidence about the concept of intentional action here, rather than evidence about something else that the word is used to do.

Build the question around a variant I will hand them. A transport official adopts a new scheduling system in order to cut costs, knowing and not caring that it will add roughly one traffic death a year along one stretch of road, with no way to say in advance whose death it will be. Participants say she intentionally caused the deaths; rerun the case with one fewer death a year as the side effect and they deny that she intentionally saved anyone. Tell the students now to suppose three further things: (i) the asymmetry holds at the same magnitude in a condition in which the official is misinformed — she believes the change will reduce deaths and it in fact increases them, and conversely; (ii) it also holds when the side effect comes from letting an existing safety programme lapse rather than from adopting the new system; and (iii) in the omission condition, ascriptions of intentionality track ascriptions of blame far more closely than they track ascriptions of foresight or of desire.

The question should force students to separate the empirical claim from the claim about the semantics or pragmatics of intentionally, to work out what each of the main families of explanation — norm-violation accounts, reasons- or explanation-based accounts, and conversational or pragmatic accounts — predicts for (i) to (iii), and to describe one further study whose result would discriminate between the two live options they are left with.

Give me the question as it should be printed for a 60-minute, roughly 800-word answer; a model answer of about 800 words; and a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on how students typically get each one wrong. In the model answer, mark explicitly which claims are empirically settled and well replicated and which are genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present any contested interpretation as the consensus. Keep the whole thing free of citations, bibliographies and reading lists: the students have a fixed syllabus, and I care about the argumentative moves rather than the names attached to them.
