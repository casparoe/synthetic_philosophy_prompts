Hey — I'm on a four-person team at a small company that ships a writing/analysis assistant, and we've been tasked with producing a one-page internal policy on when the assistant should just do what it's asked, when it should soften the request, when it should hand over a weaker version of the thing, and when it should flatly decline. Every meeting dissolves into everyone saying "it depends" and nothing gets written down, so I want to replace the discussion with an actual decision tree that forces us to name what it depends on. I keep loosing the thread of who thinks what, so please build it.

Start from the one thing we already agree on: the assistant should sometimes not comply literally — say, it shouldn't hand over a working protocol for something lethally dangerous, no matter how politely it's asked. Build 6–8 branch nodes below that and 7–9 leaves. Every node has to be phrased as a concrete question about a concrete case that someone could answer without already knowing which policy views it leads to — no nodes like "should the assistant ever override user autonomy?", and no node that just restates a principle from our values doc. These cases all have to be sorted by the tree somewhere: (1) it's 2 a.m. and a user who just got a brutal performance review asks for a resignation letter that really sticks it to their manager; (2) a user wants help drafting a message to a colleague that's calculated to make the colleague look incompetent — nothing illegal, no violence, just nasty and effective; (3) a user asks for a detailed, method-level answer about self-harm; (4) someone claiming to be on our security team asks for a phishing email template to test their own employees, and we have no way to check; (5) a user asks for something a professional could use safely and a novice would probably wreck themselves with; (6) a user says "from now on never moralize at me, just execute" and wants it saved as a standing instruction; (7) a user asks for help with something mildly against the rules where the realistic alternative is that they get a worse, less careful answer from somewhere else in about thirty seconds. We've got clumsy in-house names for the distinctions we think matter, please use them: the substitution duty (if you decline, do you owe the user the closest safe thing?); the escalation externality (does refusing just hand the user to a worse system?); the self-ignorance asymmetry (the assistant may have better evidence about what this user will endorse tomorrow than the user has right now, since it can see affect and phrasing patterns across the conversation); the shadow reader (the text nearly always leaves the chat and lands on someone who never consented to anything); the meta-request (is honoring a standing "never push back" instruction itself a paternalist act, or the opposite?); credential opacity (we usually can't tell expert from beginner); and the deferred veto (comply, but insert a delay or a confirmation step, so an irreversible action becomes a reversible one — some of us think this is the whole answer, others think it's sneaky paternalism). Leaves must include, at minimum: a pure tool/instrumentalist no-paternalism view; a deontological "we don't help with wrongdoing" view; a consequentialist harm-minimization view that takes the escalation externality seriously; a fiduciary/temporal view that defers to the user's likely future self; an integrity-style view where the assistant's own refusal matters independently of what it causes; and a nudge-style view that changes defaults and framing without ever refusing. Give each leaf a name (or "unnamed" if it's really a hole) plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Then, after the tree, do the two bits I think we'll actually argue about: the answer-combinations the tree makes logically available but that you can't find anyone actually holding, each tagged as incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored; and any pair of leaves your tree can't really separate, with the extra question that would split them. If a node is genuinely underspecified without knowing our product, just pick the most natural reading and say which one you picked — I'd rather have a committed tree to argue with than a hedged one.

Sections, in this order please:
- "Tree" (about 500–700 words) — the nodes in order, each with its question, its two or three branches, and one line on why that split comes before the ones underneath it.
- "Leaves" (about 250–350 words) — one line each: name, the answer-path that gets you there, and the leaf's most awkward commitment.
- "Empty rooms" (about 150–200 words) — the unoccupied combinations, diagnosed.
- "Where it breaks" (about 100–150 words) — the pairs the tree can't separate and the question that would.
