I have been trying to write down, carefully and in one place, where I stand on the morality of everyday conventions — queueing, tipping, traffic rules — because I keep getting pulled in different directions and I cannot locate the fault line. What I want is an audit of whether the package holds together, not a survey of what has been said about it.

Here is the set, stated as plainly as I can.

1. Queue-jumping is a genuine moral wrong to the people behind you, not merely impolite or imprudent. Someone who has waited twenty minutes and is passed by someone who arrived thirty seconds ago has been wronged and can properly complain; this is not just a matter of local etiquette or of avoiding disapproval.

2. What makes conventions bind is that people actually coordinate around them. A convention generates reasons because others form expectations on it, plan around it, and would be set back if it were breached — not because of what the content of the convention independently requires. The reasons here are content-independent, and they exist only where the practice is live and mutually recognised.

3. So the when-in-Rome principle is correct for at least some cases. At a bus stop in a city where the operative norm is a cluster rather than a line, a visitor who moves forward with everyone else does nothing wrong and owes the locals nothing; the people standing nearer the door have no complaint against them. Nor does my own lining up, if I chose to, improve anyone's position.

4. Tipping: I tip at local rates even in countries where I think the tipping system is a bad institution, because wages are set on the assumption that customers tip and the particular server would be made worse off if I did not. But where a service charge has already been added to the bill, I add nothing, whatever the local custom is.

5. Jaywalking: crossing against the signal on a visibly empty street, with no car within sight and no one else waiting, is not morally wrong at all — not mildly wrong, not wrong but excusable.

6. Still, when I try to state the general principle, the one I keep reaching for is fair play: a person who accepts the benefits of a scheme of mutual restraint owes compliance with its rules in return, subject to the familiar caveat that the scheme be reasonably just and its burdens and benefits not too lopsided.

7. I am not a relativist about this. That a norm is local, long-standing, and widely accepted does not by itself settle whether I should follow it. Some local conventions are unjust and may properly be flouted, and a visitor is not obliged to be a mirror of local practice.

8. Methodologically, I want an account that mostly vindicates what ordinary people say about concrete cases. I distrust any account on which our ordinary verdicts in this area turn out to be mass error.

One case where I do not know what to say, and which may be diagnostic: a bus stop where the practice is genuinely mixed — a loose line forms, some people observe it, others walk to the front without provoking objection, and there is no way to tell in advance which kind of stop it will be. Does anyone there owe it to anyone else to queue?

One distinction I have found useful and would like you to use: a convention can be assessed on its coordination value, that is, how well it solves whatever problem makes it worth having, and separately on what I will call its distributive profile, that is, which kinds of person reliably end up with the scarce good under it. A scrum at a bus stop may coordinate perfectly well while systematically favouring the physically assertive. I suspect these two come apart somewhere inside my set, but I cannot see where.

What I want from you: go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly, ranked by severity rather than listed as equally serious; for each real tension, say precisely what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the set; identify any pair that merely looks inconsistent and explain why the appearance dissolves; say explicitly whether the mixed-norm bus stop, or some case of your own construction, discriminates between the accounts I am attracted to; and then name the single revision that would clean up the most. If it is my firm verdict in (5) that turns out to be the exposed one, tell me that plainly. I do not know which of these I would give up, and I would rather you did not infer it from the order in which I have written them.
