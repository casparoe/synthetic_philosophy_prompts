I've been asked to write down my real position on animals rather than repeat an organisation's line — I sit on the board of a large municipal animal shelter and do volunteer policy work for a farmed-animal welfare group, and the two roles keep pulling me in different directions. Before I present it to either group I'd like an audit of whether it hangs together. Roughly where I've landed:

1. Speciesism is unjustified. Sentience is what makes a being count morally at all; once a being counts, its interests count equally regardless of species, and psychological capacities rather than species membership are what track the content of what we owe.

2. Full protection against being killed attaches only to persons — beings with an extended self-conception, plans and projects that reach into the future. Non-persons can permissibly be killed painlessly and replaced by another equally well-off non-person when doing so produces more welfare, so a genuinely high-welfare farm is not objectionable in the killing itself, only in how the animals live.

3. It is seriously wrong to kill a human being who is not a person — an infant, or an adult with profound cognitive disability — even where a painless death and a replacement would produce more welfare. I don't want this to rest on the feelings of relatives or on how such a practice would affect third parties; I think the wrong is to the individual killed.

4. Suffering is what makes factory farming a catastrophe, and the sheer numbers involved swamp anything shelters deal with, so the money should go there rather than to companion-animal rescue.

5. Wild-animal suffering is a serious problem, but we have no duty to intervene against predation, and in practice I oppose intervention programmes even where they look safe and cheap. I don't want this to rest on the value of wildness or naturalness, which I think is empty.

6. We owe more to domesticated animals — the ones we bred into existence and made dependent on us — than to wild ones, and this isn't only a matter of how much good we can do per dollar; it is about what we have already done to them.

7. Concretely: I think our shelter is right to spend its budget on neutering and rehoming dogs and cats, and that it would be wrong to shut it down and send the money to a broiler-welfare campaign, even though that would do far more good per dollar.

What I can't locate is where this package breaks. Which of these commitments actually pull against which others, and which apparent conflicts dissolve once the terms are made precise? For each real conflict, tell me what would have to give and what the cheapest repair costs me. Rank the problems by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, and say which single revision would clean up the most.
