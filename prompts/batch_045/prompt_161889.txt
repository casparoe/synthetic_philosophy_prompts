I've been chewing on the tool-AI-vs-agent-AI thing again and I keep coming back to the sense that the usual way of drawing the line is load bearing in the wrong place. So I want you to argue for a specific replacement distinction and then defend it against the obvious pushback.

Here's the position I want argued. The property that actually matters isn't initiative, or autonomy, or goal-directedness, or how many irreversible actions the system can take before a human gets a chance to intervene. It's whether the operator can cheaply answer the counterfactual question: what would this thing have done if I hadn't said anything, or if I'd said something slightly different? Call the difference between the operator's prediction and the system's actual behavior the *predictive gap*, and say a system is a tool for a given operator to the degree that the gap is small enough that the operator can run that counterfactual before deploying the system rather than after cleaning up.

Three claims to defend:

1. Tool-ness is relational, not intrinsic. Same weights, same deployment: a tool for the operator who can predict it, an agent for the one who can't. And since the predictive gap widens for everyone as capability goes up, "build tool AI, not agent AI" was never a design target you could aim at — it's a description of a regime you fall out of, and the only way to stay in it is to keep the system weak or keep it ignorant, neither of which is a stable selling point.

2. What actually preserves tool-ness is an information asymmetry in the operator's favor. A system that knows strictly less about the situation than you do can still surprise you, but there's a ceiling on how badly and in what directions, because the space of things it could have noticed is bounded by what you gave it. Argue this, and argue that this is what people were really pointing at when they reached for the "comprehensive AI services / pile of narrow solvers with humans in the loop" picture.

3. So the effort should go somewhere other than "don't build agents" — pick one concrete target (interfaces that force the system to externalize its plan in a form the operator can actually check before execution; deliberate narrowing of the system's epistemic situation; cheap simulators of the system you can run in your head) and argue that target is what the tool-not-agent intuition was groping toward, and that the intuition loses its grip entirely once you accept the reframing.

Then work the cases, don't just gesture at them:

- A stateless completion model, no memory, no tools, fresh context every call, but superhuman at persuasion and at spotting what you want to hear. Tool or agent on your account? I think the intuitive answer and your answer come apart here and I want to see you own that.
- Agent laundering: an agent wrapped in a tool-shaped API, where the human presses the button every time. Does your distinction do any work that "who pressed enter" doesn't?
- The crane / the firm. A crane is a tool made of physics; a firm is a tool made of agents. Say what your account predicts about these and where the analogy breaks.

Then steelman the strongest objection I can think of, which is that the old distinction was tracking intervention opportunities, blast radius, and whether you can pull the plug — and that's the part that matters for not dying, whereas "can the operator predict it" is a nice-to-have that scales with user sophistication in a way safety properties shouldn't. Argue that predictive-gap subsumes it, or that it's orthogonal, or that it looses — say which and why.

Finally, be honest about where the framing goes vacuous or unfalsifiable: what would it not predict, what case would it handle badly, what would change your mind.

No citations, no reading lists, no "as Bostrom argues" — assume I've read the standard stuff and argue from the concepts and the cases.
