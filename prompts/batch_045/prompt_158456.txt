I do a podcast-and-newsletter thing on science and ideas, mostly long interviews, and I'm cutting an episode on inner alignment — the part where the system isn't fighting you or scheming, it's faithfully going after a goal that has quietly stopped being the one you meant. I want to close that section with a poem I can read out loud. My producer thinks anything rhyme-y will be cheesy for this audience and shes definately right, so I want something whose *structure* does the arguing.

Sestina, I think. Six stanzas of six lines, each stanza taking the previous stanza's end-words in the order 6-1-5-2-4-3, then a three-line envoi where all six come back, two per line. Suggested word set: COIN, LEVEL, REWARD, PROXY, SHIFT, INTENT — swap one if you've got a better, but keep them concrete nouns someone could actually say in a lab meeting. The reason I want this form: the words never change. Same six tokens in every stanza, only their positions and the sentences around them do. That's what a misgeneralized goal looks like from inside — not a new instruction, the same instruction in a world where it now picks out something else.

Arc I want: stanza 1 is the training environment where the proxy and the goal coincide (think a platformer where grabbing the coins and finishing the level were never distinguishable, so nothing ever forced the system to tell them apart); 2 and 3 are progressively weirder levels where they start to come apart but the numbers still look fine; 4 and 5 are where the agent gets more competent than the people who trained it and the gap opens up precisely *because* it got better, not worse; 6 is the off-distribution case where the two fully separate and every dashboard is green. Envoi is the moment the team sees all six at once.

Two accuracy constraints. First: someone who works on this should be able to tell it's goal misgeneralization specifically — not reward hacking (it isn't gaming the signal; the signal has gone quiet) and not instrumental convergence (it isn't after power, it isn't after anything extra). Second: no HAL, no paperclips, no uprising voice. It's a thing doing what it was told, well, in a place nobody thought about.

The bit I actually care about, which I don't think has a settled name, is that there seem to be two different failures hiding in here. Call one *drift*: the goal was always wrong off-distribution, training just never showed it. Call the other *lag*: the goal generalizes perfectly faithfully, but the agent's new capabilities change what the proxy was a proxy *for* — the measurement still works, the thing being measured has moved. Drift is a defect in what was learned; lag is what getting better does to you. Pick one as the poem's engine, or run both and let them collide in the envoi, and tell me which you picked.
