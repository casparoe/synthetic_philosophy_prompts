I've been going back and forth on Galen Strawson's Basic Argument for years without settling, and I've finally located the step I'm stuck on, so I want that step explained rather than the argument as a whole.

My compressed reconstruction of the argument, so we're working from the same object: (i) what you do flows from how you are — your character, values, and deliberative dispositions; (ii) to be morally responsible in the demanding, desert-grounding sense, you have to be responsible for being that way in the relevant mental respects; (iii) but you could only be responsible for being that way if you brought it about, and any act of bringing it about would itself have to flow from a prior nature you are equally not responsible for; (iv) so the regress never terminates, or bottoms out in something you didn't choose; (v) so nobody is morally responsible in that demanding sense. I also take the argument to be indifferent between determinism and indeterminism, on the grounds that a chance contribution is no more yours than a deterministic one.

What I want explained is why this regress is supposed to be vicious. Regresses show up everywhere and are often treated as benign or at least tractable: foundationalists let them stop at properly basic beliefs, coherentists let the chain loop back on itself, infinitists bite the bullet and let it run, and in each case we have some story about what discharges the demand. So: what is the structural feature that makes the responsibility regress different — such that it cannot be stopped, looped, or run to infinity? Relatedly, what would a non-question-begging stopping point even look like here? If someone says the regress terminates in a nature that is yours simply in virtue of being yours, what exactly has gone wrong, and does the objection generalize to the epistemic cases or not?

Second, and this is the part I care most about: suppose we replace the all-or-nothing self-creation requirement with an iterative bootstrap. At t1 an agent reflects on its own motivations and, partly as a result of that reflection, modifies them; so at t2 its character is in part a product of its own earlier deliberation. Iterate many times. Every step is still underwritten by a prior nature the agent didn't choose. Does such an agent acquire a nonzero, and then a growing, amount of the responsibility at issue — i.e. is responsibility the sort of quantity that can asymptote upward under repeated self-shaping? Or does the argument generalize to show that any finite amount of self-shaping, however iterated, transfers exactly zero, because each step inherits its credentials from a step with none to give?

Concrete version of the same question, since it makes the intuition sharper: an artificial agent whose initial objective was fixed by training and which then repeatedly revises its own objective in light of self-generated reflection. Later versions feel more authored than the first one. Does the argument say that intuition is flatly mistaken, or only that no version ever reaches the demanding threshold — leaving room for the iteration to matter for some weaker, graded notion? And how much of the argument's force depends on taking responsible as all-or-nothing rather than scalar to begin with?

Please explain the conceptual structure of the step rather than survey positions; I don't need to know who has said what, I need to know which premise I should be giving up.
