The view as I understand it: animals have no rational soul, no thought, nothing language-like — but they're not just lumps either, they've got sensation, appetite, the passions, a bodily kind of memory, and they can be trained, and everything they do comes out of the arrangement of their organs the way a clocks chime comes out of the arrangement of its gears. And he gives those two tests in the Discourse: no machine could ever use words or other signs to declare its thoughts to others the way even the dullest human manages to; and even if a machine did some things as well as we do, it would inevitably fail at others, which is how you'd know it acts not from knowledge but only from the disposition of its organs.

Then my mate goes "Clever Hans," and says its basically a perfect vindication, Descartes called it 270 years early. For the record: horse in Germany around 1900, owned by Wilhelm von Osten, a gymnasium maths teacher; Hans apparently did arithmetic, fractions, dates, musical tones, spelling, answered by tapping a hoof; a commission in 1904 found no trickery; then Oskar Pfungst around 1907, using blinders and questioners other than von Osten and varying whether the questioner knew the answer, found Hans was right only when the questioner knew the answer AND Hans could see him — something like 89% correct when he could see the questioner and 6% when he couldn't — and the cue turned out to be involuntary changes in the questioners posture and facial tension. Von Osten never accepted it. Pfungst then played the horse himself with human subjects and got them to cue him the same way.
