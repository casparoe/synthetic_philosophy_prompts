ok I think I've argued myself into a corner and I need someone to find the door.

I teach a first-year module on method — how thought experiments (intuition pumps, whatever you want to call them) are supposed to earn their conclusions — and I built this argument for a seminar to show students you have to handle them carefully. The trouble is that taken at face value it seems to show they're worthless, and I dont believe that.

(1) A thought experiment gives you a reason to accept a philosophical claim only if the verdict it produces is a response to the structural features of the case — the ones the argument says are doing the work — and not to features of how the case happens to be told.
(2) Framing and order effects are everywhere in case judgements: first person vs third person, vivid vs clinical detail, which of two cases you read first, whether the bad outcome is described as happening now or next year, whether the agent gets a name — swap any one of those and a noticeable chunk of people flip on cases that are structurally identical.
(3) I tried it on my own tutorial group. I wrote one case about whether you still owe it to someone, after they're dead, to do the thing you promised them, in two versions: "Nana's letters" (first person, sensory detail, deathbed promise) and "the Archivist" (third person, abstract, a stipulation in an estate file). The split was not the same. Twelve students, no controls, no pre-registration — I know that isn't data, I'm not claiming it is.
(4) So for any single telling of a case, the verdict you get is a mixture: part response to structure, part response to the telling, and from that one telling you cannot see the proportions.
(5) And you can't calibrate the mixture out either. To measure how big the framing contribution is you'd already need to know the correct verdict on the case, which is the very thing the case was supposed to tell you.
(6) So a thought experiment, presented once in one wording, gives you no reason at all to believe its conclusion. At best it dramatises a conclusion you reached some other way.
(7) But presenting it once, in one wording, is what using a thought experiment is. Nobody runs the same pump in forty wordings before they'll lean on it.
(8) So no thought experiment has ever given anyone a reason to believe a philosophical conclusion.
(9) Which is absurd, and gets worse the longer you stare at it. My own premise (2) is supported by comparing people's answers to differently worded cases — that is, by treating answers to cases as evidence about something. And (8) doesn't stop at ethics: the falling-bodies and sealed-cabin style arguments, the twin business, the "is that really knowledge?" counterexamples, and every argument of the form "consider the following example" go down with it.

I'm clearly cheating somewhere and I can't say where. My money is on the move from (6) to (8) — something shifting in what "a reason" means — but my supervisor thinks the real work is being done at (4)/(5) and that (7) and (8) are just along for the ride. I'd rather keep (1), (2) and (3) than anything downstream, and if holding those means dropping one of them I want to know what I'm paying. I also want to know whether there's a genuine distinction between a pump that drags out something you implicitly already know and one that manufactures a verdict out of sheer presentation, because if there is I think it belongs inside premise (1) and I can't work out how to write it in.
