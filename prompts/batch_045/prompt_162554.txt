You are a philosopher of mind who specializes in the theory of emotion, and I want a careful answer rather than a survey. Before proposing anything general, work through the cases one at a time and note, for each, what each of the three usual families of accounts is committed to saying — emotions as evaluative judgments, emotions as perceptions of value, emotions as bodily feelings — and where your own verdict diverges from each.

Here are my verdicts, given on gut, held with varying confidence:

- Phobic fear of a spider behind glass, while I sincerely judge it harmless: an emotion, and it is about that spider.
- Diffuse dread at 3 a.m. in a strange hotel room, with no object I can name — call this ambient: an emotion, though I cannot say what it represents.
- Pity for a character in a novel I know to be fictional: an emotion, and directed at her.
- Fury at an airline's automated rebooking system, discharged at the gate agent: the anger is about the system, not the agent, even though it feels aimed at her.
- A flicker of irritation at a slow elevator, gone before I would call it anything — call these thin: still an emotion, a minimal one.
- The heart-pounding jolt after a near miss in traffic, before any thought of danger: not yet an emotion, only a bodily storm.
- Repressed anger: the person denies it, the pattern of their behavior shows it, and in therapy they later recognize it as having been there all along: an emotion.
- The full physiological profile of fear during a sleepwalking episode, followed by avoidance of the room, with no experience and no memory whatsoever: I go back and forth on this one and lean toward no.
- Grief a year on, rarely felt, but still organizing plans, attention and conversation: an emotion, though barely felt.

My own stab at a principle: an emotion is any state that has a valence, is directed at some target (possibly vague, fictional, or mistaken), and has the job of coordinating attention, motivation and bodily response with respect to that target; feelings are optional symptoms, and evaluative judgment is one way, but not the only way, of settling what the target is.

Test that formulation against the list. Does any account of the three families, or some hybrid of them, return my verdicts more cleanly than mine does? Where mine fails, say which clause fails and on which case. Which of my verdicts do you think I should give up, and why? Finally, state what the account you end up defending predicts for two cases I have not listed: being moved by instrumental music, and feeling ashamed of a friend's behavior in which I played no part.
