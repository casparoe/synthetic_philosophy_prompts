I am facilitating a mediation between two members of a city committee, and I need from you the document they should be able to sign at the end of it, even though I do not expect either of them to have changed their mind.

Here is the case. The city has, in its central square, a bronze equestrian statue of Sir Elias Vance, and it has, as one of its four principal arteries, Vance Street, whose name appears on every bus transfer, every piece of post, and every emergency dispatch address in the district. Vance endowed the city's free public library and its first hospital for the poor; he also drafted and steered through the colonial assembly the statute that extinguished the remaining common lands of the district's smallholders, bought a substantial part of them at auction, and defended both in published letters on the ground that the dispossessed were unfit to hold property. The library and the hospital still stand and are still named after him. One member, whom I will call the removalist, holds that the honour has been defeated and should be withdrawn: the statue to a museum store, the street renamed. The other, the recontextualist, holds that both should stay in place with a permanent interpretive installation alongside them, because removal converts a site of contested memory into a site of forgetting and lets the present city treat the wrong as having been done by a country that is no longer itself.

In the sessions so far I have found four distinctions useful, and I want you to use them where they cut and to say plainly where they do not: honourific saturation, meaning how far the honour pervades obligatory daily civic speech, on the thought that you can avoid a square but cannot avoid your own address; the complicity condition, meaning the question whether wrongdoing defeats an honour only when it is connected to the good for which the person is honoured, or whether grave wrongdoing defeats it regardless; the contrast between honouring and preserving as evidence, on the thought that a statue testifies mostly to the taste of those who raised it while a building or a record testifies to what was done; and the default-reading equilibrium, meaning the reading a passer-by forms with no interpretive effort, which the recontextualist thinks a plaque can shift and the removalist thinks it cannot.

Write the joint statement: first the claims both can sign, including at least one that each of them initially resisted, then the residual disagreement set out in wording each would accept as a statement of their own position, then the cruxes in the order of how much of the dispute would move if each were settled. For each crux give a case, an argument, or a piece of evidence that both would accept as bearing on it, with what each side predicts about it recorded in advance. Tell me which part of the dispute you take to be merely verbal and why, and which crux is genuinely empirical, together with the study or comparison that would settle it. Say whether you think the statue and the street name call for the same verdict or different ones, and why; the two members currently assume they stand or fall together.

Write it as one continuous prose document of about a thousand words, addressed to the committee, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables: the agreed claims and the disagreements should be carried by the sentences themselves and by their order, in the way you would write if it were going to be read aloud. Do not cite or name any theorists, and do not survey the literature or the history of similar disputes; work only from the case, the four distinctions, and the arguments the two members actually make.
